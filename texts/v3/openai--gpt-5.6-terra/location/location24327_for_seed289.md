Below the old ground, in a space no larger than a chapel crypt, there is a meadow that does not know it is underground.

Its ceiling is low and white—not stone exactly, though it has been mistaken for stone by every creature that has brushed against it. It is a dense mat of interlocked roots, pale as bone, packed so completely together that no soil can be seen between them. Through the roots shines a permanent daylight. Not sunlight, not lamp-light: a cool, milk-bright radiance produced by the roots themselves, each hair-fine strand giving off a weak glow that becomes overwhelming in aggregate. The ceiling appears woven from a thousand luminous veins. It leaves no shadows sharp enough to hide in.

The room beneath is crowded almost to the point of discomfort. Every available surface bears life. The floor is a shallow basin of dark, damp mineral grit, never muddy, never dry. Thin black stones press together like teeth. Between them grow short silver-green stems, each bearing one round leaf flat against the ground. The leaves are not numerous enough to make a lawn, but they occupy all the small openings the stones allow. Their undersides are furred with white threads, and from those threads hangs condensation in constant, perfectly formed beads.

The moisture does not fall. It remains where it is.

A bead may hang from the tip of a root, bulging and bright, for centuries without dropping. A film of water clings to the smooth wall, halfway along a curve of exposed clay, and never advances or recedes. There is a narrow rivulet around the room’s perimeter, no wider than a finger. It has the polished look of moving water, its bed marked with tiny ripples, but it does not run. Minnow-like creatures occupy it in such density that their bodies make a dark braided band beneath the surface. They hold themselves in swimming poses—fins spread, mouths open, eyes glittering—and do not travel. Yet they are alive. If one touches the water, all their gills pulse at once.

This is the loudness of the place: not movement, but maintenance.

The roots overhead hum. The hum is made of innumerable faint vibrations and has the layered depth of a distant engine, a hive, a crowd speaking through a wall. In it can be heard the ticks of beetle legs, the high, glassy calls of blind frogs, the rasp of tiny teeth on mineral deposits. Every animal makes sound continuously, as if silence would allow it to disappear.

There are frogs here, abundant and oddly shaped: thumb-sized, pearl-gray, with wide transparent throats that inflate like bubbles. They occupy pockets in the wall, crevices in the stones, hollows between the root columns. They do not leap. Their feet remain fixed to their chosen places, but their throats open and close in overlapping rhythms, producing a chorus like wet flute notes blown through reeds. Their eyes are cloudy, their bodies cool, and each has a small crown of green algae around its head.

The insects are more numerous still. Fat pale moths cling folded against the ceiling-roots, their wings patterned in the exact branching diagrams of the roots behind them. Long-legged beetles climb endlessly over one another in knots, though no individual gains ground. Small red mites occupy the dew beads, walking upon their curved surfaces without breaking them. A species of snail has taken up residence in the old masonry fragments scattered through the chamber. Their shells are clear, revealing the slow dark coils of their internal organs. They leave silver trails that never lengthen.

The masonry is the only sign that people once came close to this place.

At the far side of the chamber, half submerged in the mineral floor, stand three squat constructions: little stacked-stone arches, each only knee-high. They may have been drains, shrines, markers, or the beginnings of a tunnel abandoned almost immediately. Their stones are too regular to be natural, but they have been softened by the persistent damp. The arches are crowded with shells, frog eggs, rootlets, and the empty translucent skins of insects. One has a small niche in its center. Within the niche sits a clay cup, intact, filled to the brim with clear water that has never been drunk and never evaporates.

There are no entrances visible at first. The room seems self-contained, sealed into the earth like a thought kept private. But behind the luminous roots, at one low point, is a narrow seam where cool air arrives. It carries the scent of rain on pavement, though there is no pavement nearby, and of crushed fern, though the nearest fern may be far above. The seam is too tight for a person to pass. It breathes only enough to keep the chamber from becoming stale.

Nothing here develops. Nothing decays.

The silver leaves remain at precisely the same height. A beetle damaged by a falling pebble continues to crawl with its cracked carapace, neither healing nor weakening. The clay cup does not accumulate dust. The stones do not settle. The root ceiling does not thicken, and the damp does not spread. The creatures mate, perhaps, but no young are ever seen; they feed, perhaps, but no food is ever missing. Every process has found its exact counterweight. Life is present in overwhelming quantity, packed into a tiny underground room, but it is held at the instant before consequence.

The place feels less frozen than balanced.

To stand in it is to become aware of one’s own changes immediately: breath warming the cool air, a foot sinking slightly into the grit, a sleeve collecting dew. The chamber accepts these disturbances with no visible objection. But after one leaves, it is said, the damp returns to every surface, the grit rises back around each stone, and the chorus resumes exactly where it had been—not beginning again, not repairing itself, but continuing from a note it never truly stopped holding.
