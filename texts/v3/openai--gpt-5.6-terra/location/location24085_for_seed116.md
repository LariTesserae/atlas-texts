At the top of the old waterworks, there is a garden no larger than a train station waiting room.

It occupies a hollow inside the crown of a drowned tower: a circular chamber of riveted iron and green-black concrete, hanging somewhere far above the clouds. The tower itself rises from an inland sea, though “rises” is not entirely right. It has been submerged so long that the water has claimed its elevators, its stairwells, its furnaces, the sealed offices where paper still moves in slow pale shoals. Yet its uppermost maintenance vault, imperfectly pressurized and warmed by deep machinery, remains a pocket of breathable weather.

The garden is flooded to the knee.

Warm water slides continually through it, entering from six brass mouths in the wall and leaving through a grated drain beneath a central footbridge. It is clear only near the inlets. Elsewhere it has the amber-green cloudiness of old aquariums, full of drifting pollen, rust dust, soft shed skins, and flakes of mineral scale from the tower’s pipes. In the light from a row of ordinary industrial bulbs, the water shines with the quiet depth of a canal at evening.

Nothing here is natural, though everything behaves as if it has forgotten that fact.

The floor is made of tiled slopes and service trenches. The walls are plated in overlapping panels, each one stamped with faded numbers, inspection seals, and the names of companies dissolved generations ago. There is no earth. Plants root in sponge filters, cracked cable jackets, wire baskets packed with clinker, and the furry mineral deposits around leaking valves. Ferns emerge from switch boxes. Broad warm-water lilies float in the basin where an obsolete pressure gauge once sat. Vines climb the support ribs of the ceiling, winding around dangling chains and bundles of dead electrical conduit. Their leaves are thick, lacquered, and almost unnaturally dark; they collect the condensation that falls from above.

A few fruiting bodies grow from the wet insulation: pale orange shelves, blue-veined cups, clusters that resemble folded ears listening to the pipes.

The room is sparse despite its abundance. There is space between things. A bridge crosses the flooded floor in one clean arc. Three old pump housings stand like small islands, each densely furred with moss and valve-flowers, each separated by water. A single ladder vanishes into the flooded ceiling hatch overhead. Along the far wall, a narrow steel door is welded permanently shut, its painted warning stripes softened into ribbons by decades of damp.

Beyond it is the vast submerged shaft of the tower.

Sometimes the garden’s water trembles, and the sound arrives: the giant low turning of machinery below, a depthless rotational hum transmitted through iron. Then come the nearer noises—the hard ticking of cooling relays, the wet rattle of leaf-stems against pipework, the sudden exhaust of pressure from a relief valve. Water falls constantly in different measures. It drips from the ceiling in patient single notes, spills from cracked manifolds in bright rushing curtains, and bubbles up through the floor grates with the dense, muttering voice of a buried spring.

The loudest sound is the rain.

It falls inside.

At intervals, one of the tower’s old atmospheric collectors opens high above the chamber and releases a vertical sheet of recovered rainwater. It descends through the central void in a roaring column, hits the flooded floor, and bursts outward in white turbulence. The plants fold and thrash. Small fish are lifted from hiding places beneath the bridge and carried in flashing circles. For several minutes the room becomes a storm contained by architecture: warm rain, electric lights trembling behind spray, the smell of wet metal and green growth sharpened almost to a taste.

Then the collector closes. The rain ceases as abruptly as a switched machine. Leaves rise again. The water settles. The garden continues.

Its animals are everywhere once one learns to look.

Silver fish live in the circulation channels, their bodies narrow enough to slip through gate valves. They gather around warmth and vibration, and will follow a person’s ankles in the shallow water, nibbling harmlessly at loose skin. Palm-sized crabs inhabit the rust cavities behind the panels; their shells have taken on the colors of safety paint—yellow, red, oxidized white—and they emerge at night to harvest algae from the bridge supports. Long translucent eels thread through the pipes. Their internal organs show dimly through their bodies like mechanisms operating behind frosted glass.

There are birds, too, somehow. Small, black, water-loving things with oil-dark feathers and pale feet. They nest in the fan housings near the ceiling, where the air is warmest. During the artificial rain they fly directly through the downpour, shrieking and snapping at the insects drawn out of the vegetation. Their calls are thin and metallic, but multiplied in the chamber they become a bright, unruly clamor.

The insects are mostly moths and midges. They breed in condensation pans and filter wool. Many have wings patterned like corrosion. Some are blind, sensing light only as heat, and circle the old bulbs until they die and fall into the water. Their bodies feed the fish; the fish feed the eels; the eels are occasionally taken by something larger moving in the black throat of the upper hatch.

No one has seen that creature fully.

There are rules in the garden, old operating rules that have become natural law.

The water level must never reach the third red rivet on the bridge rail. If it does, the pumps below begin to wake in sequence, and every living thing in the chamber retreats to the walls. The rain may fall only when the deep engines turn. The bulbs cannot be replaced, but none ever burn out; when one fails, a neighboring bulb gradually brightens until the room has the same modest yellow light as before. Nothing that grows here may be removed while it is green. Cut leaves turn instantly transparent in the hand and dissolve into a clean industrial-smelling water.

The strangest rule is that all rust must be allowed to finish.

No one scrapes the flaking orange blooms from the railings or closes the slow leaks that create them. Rust is not damage in this place. It is a crop, a soil, a record of time made visible. Roots use it. Crabs wear it. The water carries it in dilute clouds and lays it down in red-brown deltas along the floor drains. The tower’s age is not an enemy of its purpose; it is the purpose that has outlived every other one.

Outside the chamber, the world is all height and water.

Through a thick inspection window, set low in one wall, a person can see the outer face of the tower falling away beneath them. It descends through miles of warm green floodwater, its ribs softened by barnacles and climbing weed, until sight fades into suspended dark. Far below, other towers stand with their heads drowned, their lights glimmering faintly through the water like settlements on the floor of another sea.

Above, beyond the roof plates, there may be sky. There may be weather. There may be the broken peaks of mountains, eroded almost flat by an age of rain.

But in the little high chamber, none of that matters much.

The garden has its own tide, its own rain, its own dawn maintained by old lamps and patient current. It is a small, loud, living room inside an immense dead machine: warm water around the ankles, green leaves against iron, fish beneath the bridge, rain waiting in its pipes.
