The high country has forgotten how to be dry.

It is not a mountain range in the ordinary sense, though from a great distance it resembles one: a lifted, dark-green continent wrinkled along its back, its slopes vanishing into a ceiling of gray vapor. Up close, the ground is not stone with plants upon it. Plant life is the ground. It has covered the old bones of the land so completely that rock appears only in rare wet mouths—black faces of cliff, dripping steadily into pools so dark they seem to continue down forever.

Everything grows, but nothing hurries.

The ridges are broad and ancient, worn low by durations rather than storms. Their summits are not sharp. They are soft-backed, folded, and carpeted with forests whose crowns merge into one enormous, uneven hide. The forest has risen so high that it makes its own lower weather: clouds drift among the upper branches; rain can begin at the canopy and never reach the earth; a warm green dusk lies beneath the leaves even at noon. On the oldest slopes, roots have met roots, trunks have folded around fallen trunks, and the distinction between living tree, dead tree, soil, and hill has become mostly ceremonial.

There are places where the land gives slightly beneath a foot. It is not mud exactly. It is a deep, fibrous mattress of moss, rootfelt, drowned leaves, and the fine white threads of fungi. Water lives immediately below this skin. Press down and clear brown water rises through the green. In hollows, entire forests stand in ankle-deep pools, their boles flared and buttressed like the feet of immense animals. Ferns occupy the water’s surface in dense rafts. Reeds grow from floating peat. The occasional open pool is crowded at its margins by pale flowers, carnivorous cups, and broad leaves that tremble when rain collects on them.

The air is warm for such height, warmed by the mass of the vegetation and held close by constant mist. It smells of bark, mineral water, bruised herbs, and the clean sourness of decomposition. Breath condenses only sometimes, usually at dawn, when cool vapor from higher cloudbanks slides down through the basin forests. Then every vine bears droplets. Every leaf has a bright edge. The whole world seems strung with temporary glass.

Rain is frequent, but rarely violent. It arrives as a patient addition to what is already wet: a thousand small taps on leaves, a thickening of the mist, a gradual darkening of trunks. In the deeper valleys, water flows without visible source from beneath the roots. It gathers in channels that have no banks, only gradual transitions from moss to reed to shallow current. These streams move so slowly that fish may hover among submerged roots as though suspended in air.

The old stone is still at work underneath, though it has surrendered the surface. Cliffs weep from seams. Long walls of basalt emerge from the forest in places, rounded by ages of water and furred with liverwort. Their faces are marked with vertical stains, each one beginning at a hanging garden or a root-filled crack. Some of these stones are so ancient and so softened that they resemble ruins, but they are not. They were old before anyone made the few true ruins scattered through the uplands.

Those structures are easy to miss.

A small roof may appear through the fern canopy, pitched steeply against rain and almost swallowed by aerial roots. A line of hand-laid stones may cross a stream, though half its stones are now moss-covered and submerged. There are occasional raised shelters on old hardwood posts, their walls woven from reeds and broad leaves, abandoned long enough that saplings have grown through the floors. In the high marshes, one sometimes finds circular platforms of dark timber, ringed by upright stones. Their purpose is unclear. They may once have been places to wait for the water to fall, or to listen to something beneath it.

Nothing metallic survives here in any meaningful way. Nothing rigid remains rigid. The country accepts constructions only briefly, then fills them, lifts them, roots through them, and teaches them to become terrain.

The animals are everywhere, though the land is mostly quiet.

At first there is only dripping, the soft persuasion of water moving under leaves, and a far-off wind passing through crowns too high to see. Then the smaller sounds resolve: the wet click of climbing insects; a beetle knocking against bark; frogs calling with voices low and hollow as blown bottles; the slow tearing of leaves somewhere nearby. Birds are numerous but restrained. Their calls tend toward single notes repeated at long intervals, each sound swallowed by fog before it can travel far.

In the pools live broad-backed amphibians with frilled necks, patterned in greens and dull gold. Their young drift in the shallows like fallen petals, breathing through feathery gills. In the trees move long, quiet grazers—six-limbed things with soft muzzles, pale eyes, and coats overgrown with algae in the rainy season. They browse on epiphytes and hanging fruit, moving through the understory with the caution of people in a crowded room. Smaller creatures live in the bromeliad basins high overhead, never touching the ground at all. A single giant leaf may hold its own community: mosquito-like pollinators, translucent snails, predatory larvae, and tiny frogs whose eggs are carried from pool to pool in the folds of a parent’s skin.

There are great animals too, but they are not common enough to dominate the place. Their paths are old corridors through the vegetation, sunk deep by generations of passage. Along these trails, the rootfelt is packed firm and dark. Trees lean away from them. Pools are churned at their edges. A traveler may recognize one by the sudden openness of the forest and by the quiet that follows—the insects ceasing, birds lifting without cries, water becoming the loudest thing in the world.

The largest inhabitants are never seen in full beneath the canopy. There may be a shoulder moving behind a screen of foliage, an immense exhalation from the fog, a branch bending downward and not rising again. Sometimes, after days of rain, a whole hillside seems to shift as a sleeping grazer turns in its bed of moss and low trees. By morning, there is only a new trough holding water, already occupied by tadpoles and seed.

The landscape’s chief law is incorporation.

Nothing is cleared. Nothing remains separate. A fallen animal becomes a hummock; a hummock becomes a nursery for trees. A path becomes a stream, then a chain of pools, then a meadow of water-loving plants. A roof catches leaf litter until it becomes a garden. The land does not destroy things dramatically. It covers them with attention. It gives every surface a second purpose and every hollow a use.

Even the light is incorporated.

Day enters weakly, filtered through miles of vapor and foliage. It is not darkness, but a green-brown luminosity without strong shadows. In the upper forest, leaves gleam silver where mist collects. Below, trunks stand close together in layered ranks, their surfaces dimly lit by reflected water. At night, pale fungi shine from rotten wood, and certain flowers open with a bluish inner glow that attracts moths from far beneath the leaves. Pools hold the faintest reflection of cloudlight. A person could travel by these small illuminations if they knew the country well enough.

Above the forest, the weather has another scale. The high green ridges rise through cloud, and on rare clear mornings there are views across an unmeasured expanse of forested uplands, marsh basins, drowned valleys, and slow rivers braided through vegetation. No horizon appears cleanly. The distance is continually softened by rain and vapor. Far ridges resemble waves beneath a dark sea; far clouds resemble more ridges, growing upward.

It feels less like a wilderness than a mature world: a place that has had enough time to make arrangements with itself.

Water has found every low place. Roots have found every crack. Creatures have found niches inside niches. Stone has yielded its angles. The few marks left by hands have become habitats. Nothing is empty, but nothing clamors. The land is dense with life in the way deep water is dense with pressure—quietly, completely, on all sides.
