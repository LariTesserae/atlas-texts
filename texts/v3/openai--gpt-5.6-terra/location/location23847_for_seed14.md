Below the old surface of the world lies a basin without an edge.

It is not a cave, though the ceiling is somewhere above: a drowned country pressed beneath hundreds of meters of black water. The water is cold enough to numb a hand almost at once, cold enough that every exposed stone bears a pale coat of mineral frost—though no ice forms here, only a white, chalky bloom precipitated from the water. The pressure has made the darkness physical. It fills the hollows, settles behind ridges, lies in the folds of the ground like silt.

The floor is an ancient plain, eroded past drama.

Once there may have been mountains here. Now they are low backs of stone, long and bruised-looking beneath layers of sediment. Their granite has been planed smooth by ages of current and sliding grit. Valleys are shallow troughs. Cliffs have become steep, dark shoulders only a few body-lengths high, their fractured faces softened under curtains of mud. Nothing rises sharply except occasional spines of bedrock, exposed where the current has scoured away everything softer. These are black, rounded, polished as old teeth.

The basin is crowded. Not with buildings or trees, but with matter.

Stones overlap stones. Ropes of drowned root drift gather against ridges. Shell fragments, fish bones, mats of bacterial film, pebbles, dark clays, dead weed, and pale mineral nodules occupy every pocket of the terrain. The bottom has no empty places. Even the apparently bare sediment is pocked by worm tunnels and crowded with minute creatures, its surface continually furred with a living dust. There is no clean line between land and debris, between corpse and soil, between bedrock and whatever has been growing over it for a thousand years.

The water itself is busy.

A current enters from the eastern lowlands in pulses, a river moving inside the sea. It comes low to the ground, dense with silt, and rolls across the basin in gray-brown clouds. In one hour a familiar ridge can disappear. In another, its crest is exposed again, swept naked and shining. Fine sediment rises in twisting columns, collapses, then rises somewhere else. Gravel clicks softly as it moves a few centimeters at a time. Whole sheets of mud loosen from slopes and glide downslope with grave, slow certainty, burying the small things that cannot flee and feeding the things that can.

Yet the sound is almost nothing.

There is the faintest hiss of sand traveling over stone. A distant, intermittent knock when two buried boulders settle against one another. Sometimes a low pressure-hum passes through the water, not heard so much as felt in the ribs: the far-off working of tides, or an undersea storm above. But mostly the basin is quiet. Its turbulence is patient. It has no need to announce itself.

Vegetation survives only where the moving water allows it. Along the lee sides of stone ridges, sparse stands of coarse, dark weed grip the ground with rootlike holdfasts. They grow low and hard, their blades short, leathery, and filmed with silt. A few rise in wavering clumps from cracks in the rock, bending constantly in the current, never reaching toward light because there is almost no light to reach for. In calmer hollows, a red-brown moss spreads over pebbles like old blood faded in water. Most of the basin, though, is too unsettled for plants. The current strips anything tender away.

Life is present without being abundant.

A pale eel may emerge from a mud bank, test the water with its head, and disappear again. Flat fish lie half-buried on the bottom, their outlines visible only when their eyes move. Long-legged crabs travel slowly over the stones, carrying clumps of sponge or shell upon their backs; they seem less like animals than portions of the floor that have decided to relocate. Occasionally, in the open water, one large shape passes—a cod-gray predator, spare and scarred, following the current’s edge before vanishing into the black.

The smallest lives rule the place. Cold bacteria bloom in seams where mineral-rich water seeps from the bedrock. They make dull white veils and orange smears, fed by the deep earth rather than the absent sun. Tiny scavengers comb these films. Larvae drift in the water column. The dead are broken down quickly, not by spectacle, but by innumerable quiet mouths.

There are signs of a primitive intelligence in the basin, though no settlement has ever stood here.

Near certain cracks in the bedrock, clusters of shells have been wedged carefully into place, their open mouths facing the current. Small crabs return to these stations again and again, using the shells as crude traps. Silt-borne food collects inside them. Elsewhere, fist-sized stones have been piled around warm mineral seeps by creatures strong enough to move them, perhaps to make sheltered pockets against the flow. The arrangements are temporary. A storm-current will scatter them; the next generation will make them again. Nothing is built to last here, because permanence belongs to the stone alone.

The rules of this drowned country are simple.

Anything tall is cut down by moving water.  
Anything loose is carried until it catches.  
Anything caught becomes shelter for something smaller.  
Anything buried becomes part of the ground.  

The basin has reached an old equilibrium: not stillness, but a form of endless revision. Its mountains have been worn into low shapes; its forests reduced to stubborn tufts; its animals made cautious, pale, and close to the floor. The water overturns the surface continually, but below each disturbed layer lies another, older arrangement—compressed, dark, patient.

Far above, the sea may be storming. Rain may be striking the surface in white blows. Wind may be moving over the drowned land where valleys once opened to the sky.

Here, none of that is visible.

Only a weak blue-gray dilution sometimes filters down from above, enough to place a faint sheen on a drifting cloud of silt. Then darkness closes around it, and the old submerged plain continues changing, grain by grain, without ever seeming to move.
