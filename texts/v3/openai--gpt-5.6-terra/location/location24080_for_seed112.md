At first it appears to be a forest seen from a great height: an unbroken green continent folding toward every horizon, its upper surface broken by pale ribs, dark seams, and miles of wind-combed foliage. But there is no ground beneath the forest. The forest is the ground.

Everything here grows from everything else.

The high country is made of plant matter compacted over ages into strata: thick, dry roots pressed into ocherous boards; old mats of leaf-fiber hardened almost to stone; forests of living columns rising from the dead wood of forests that fell before them. Ravines open between these layers, exposing a dense vegetable geology—charcoal-colored pith, gold cambium, chalky deposits of pollen and mineral salts, bands of red resin crystallized under pressure. The landscape has cliffs, talus slopes, broad plateaus, even mountain ranges, but all of them are botanical in origin. A ridge may be the fused trunks of a species that once grew upright and then slowly bent under its own expansion. A valley may be a centuries-old feeding channel where roots have drawn nutrients from the buried body of the world.

There is no water.

Nothing runs. Nothing pools. No rain falls.

The air is dry enough to take the warmth from skin quickly. It has the aromatic harshness of crushed needles, dust, peppery spores, and hot sap. Moisture, where life requires it, is held deep in waxy tissues, in swollen root bulbs, in sealed galleries inside immense stalks. At dawn, translucent membranes unfold along the lee sides of plants and comb a faint vapor from the thin upper air. By noon the membranes have curled shut, bronze and paper-dry, their captured breath hidden away below.

The altitude is immense. The sky has the hard, rinsed clarity of high places, an almost painful blue deepening toward indigo overhead. Light comes down in broad, white sheets, striking the upper leaf-fields so brightly that they resemble metal filings or the fur of some vast animal. Yet the temperature remains cool. Shadows are cold, and the interiors of the great plant masses are colder still: green-black caverns filled with slow exhalations and the clicking of hidden lives.

The plants are not dense in the ordinary forest sense. They fill the planet in mass, but not in closeness. There is distance here: great empty basins of pale root-lawn; wide corridors where the wind can travel for days without meeting a vertical trunk; expanses of dry green haze in which one colossal organism stands ten kilometers from the next. Each is huge enough to have its own weather. Some rise in smooth, tapering shafts for hundreds of meters, crowned with fanlike leaves that turn silver when the sun hits them. Others spread low and wide in circular colonies, their overlapping fronds forming plains of tissue that flex underfoot. In the far distance, vast red flowering structures open and close with the day, slow as machinery.

The resemblance to industry is inescapable, though no hand has built anything.

On the horizon, groves of hollow stems pulse in sequence, taking in air through slatted openings and forcing it through internal chambers. Their breathing produces a rhythmic mechanical thunder: intake, compression, release. Root fields beneath them shudder as underground nodules contract. Tall seed towers rotate their crowns in the wind, not freely but with a ratcheting, toothed cadence. Resinous gas vents from their bark and catches the sunlight in iridescent columns. A whole mountainside may begin to vibrate as its buried fibers draw against one another, processing dead plant matter into heat, sugars, salts, and new wood.

It is an industrial world with no industry except metabolism.

The noise is tremendous.

Wind is only part of it. The wind comes hard across the exposed heights, tearing through blade-canyons and striking the broad leaves with a sound like surf—though there has never been a sea here. But beneath that roar is the world’s own labor: trunks knocking together, seedpods detonating, dry husks rolling across the plateaus, roots splitting stone-like old tissues beneath the surface. Sometimes a whole stand of reed-towers bends in one direction at once, and their stems scream against their sheaths with the shrill force of rail brakes. The calls of animals arrive in fragments through the noise: whistles, booming throats, clatters, the amplified drumming of wings.

Fauna is abundant because the vegetation offers every kind of shelter and food, though not necessarily gentleness.

Small pale grazers move in long lines over the root plains, chewing the mineral crust that forms on exposed plant flesh. They have broad padded feet and dry, folded mouths, and they travel beneath the shade of moving leaf-sails. Herds of six-legged browsers climb the lower trunks, harvesting tender new growth before it hardens into armor. Their bodies are dust-colored, their eyes protected by clear side membranes against the constant windblown pollen.

Higher up live gliding creatures with ribbed sails of leathery skin, slipping between the crowns like scraps of shadow. They nest in the warm exhaust cavities of the breathing plants. Predators do not chase often; the terrain is too vast and open. Instead they listen. They cling motionless to bark cliffs, sensing the vibrations carried through the wood, then descend in sudden, silent falls through the deafening air.

There are insects—or things close enough to insects—that work the resin fields in clouds. Their wings make a granular, electrical noise. They gather the hard amber secretions of certain plants and carry them into underground chambers, where the resin is broken down by symbiotic molds. At night, when the sky turns almost black without becoming fully dark, these swarms sometimes glow faintly from the chemicals inside them. Seen from a ridge, they form traveling constellations across the vegetation.

The landscape is old, but not quiet in its age. It has reached equilibrium without becoming still.

Every exposed surface bears the marks of weather: leaves clipped into streamlines by abrasive spores, trunks polished smooth on their windward sides, whole hills striped by migrating roots. The erosion here is not caused chiefly by water, but by movement and friction. Seasons of violent pollen storms abrade the living rock. Dry lichen-like films digest the outer layers of woody formations and leave behind loose mineral flour. Herd paths become trenches, then canyons, then stable migration routes where new plants grow in response to the compacted substrate. A fallen giant does not rot in the familiar sense; it is dismantled in decades by heat-producing fungi, root tendrils, burrowing animals, and the slow hydraulic force of expanding cells.

Nothing is abandoned, because nothing is made.

There are no roads, though certain valleys resemble roads from above: long pale strips through the green, maintained by the passage of immense grazing migrations and by the refusal of the surrounding growth to close over them. There are no towers, though some plants rise taller than towers. No machines, though every grove seems to be pumping, grinding, venting, sorting, lifting, and storing. No ruins, though the deep layers are full of the compressed remains of older ecologies.

The world’s turbulence is visible even under clear skies. Whole plant communities migrate by growth: a bright band of young stalks advancing across a plateau, consuming the old root-mat beneath; a dark fungal bloom spreading overnight over a fallen canopy; a windstorm turning the leaves of an entire province inside out until the continent flashes white-green under the sun. The air is crowded with drifting seeds, torn fibers, leaf scales, gliders, pollen, and the powdery remains of last season’s growth. It is bright enough that each floating particle casts a minute sharp shadow.

In the deepest afternoon, when the wind pauses for a few seconds, one can hear the underlying fact of the place.

Not silence.

A low, continuous pressure from below: the immense circulation of sap, gas, heat, spores, and living tissue through a world that has no soil and no sea, only itself. Then the wind returns across the high green distances. The leaf-fields rise and flatten. The hollow stems begin their breathing again. Somewhere beyond the visible ridges, a seed tower bursts open with the sound of a collapsing factory.

And the bright, dry vegetation goes on making the planet.
