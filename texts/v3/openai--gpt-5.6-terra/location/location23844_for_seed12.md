The city is not built beside the water. It is built in it, and because of it.

At this height the sea has no horizon. It has risen into weather, into cloud, into a dim green air whose pressure is felt in the teeth. Mountains stand beneath it like the knuckles of a buried hand. Their upper slopes are only a few dozen body-lengths below the surface, though no one uses the word *surface* often. It is a distant ceiling, moving faintly with the moon, bright only on clear days—which occur perhaps twice a year.

Below, the old valley has become a drowned basin. The mountains are young enough to be raw: their sides are not yet softened into slopes, but split in fresh shelves, dark basalt ribs, pale seams of mineral salt, blisters of glassy stone where heat once forced its way upward. In places the rock still looks unfinished. It rises in blocks with sharp corners, as if the world has only recently decided what a mountain ought to be.

The water is warm. Not pleasantly warm, but womb-warm, mineral-rich, motionless except for slow invisible currents. It carries a haze of golden silt and minute drifting leaves. One can see forty or fifty paces in the clear channels, and only three or four in the gardens, where vegetation has made the drowned air thick with stems.

Great kelps grow from every ledge. They are not brown, but colors of wet copper, bruised violet, and dull jade. Their leaves are broad as doors and fold themselves around the stone in the slow surge. Between them are forests of reed-coral: green tubes with pale, feathery crowns, growing from cracks in the basalt. Some of these crowns open toward the light above. Others turn downward, feeding on the faint warmth leaking from vents in the mountain floor.

The inhabitants cultivate the plants rather than clearing them. Their city occupies a chain of terraces along the valley wall, where the old mountainside descends into the blackness of the basin. Houses are grown and assembled from woven reeds, shell-lime plaster, and the hollow trunks of a buoyant waterwood. They are rounded, tethered things: baskets large enough to live inside, bound to stone with ropes of kelp fiber. A house may drift a little over the years, turning slowly toward or away from a current, and its family will simply adjust the walkways.

There are no streets in the dry sense. There are routes.

Some are ladders of stone fitted into the cliff, their steps worn smooth by webbed feet and handholds. Some are bridges: braided lengths of root and carved bone suspended between terraces. Some are tunnels through vegetation, marked with strings of white shell disks that click gently when touched. Most travel is vertical. People descend from one dim neighborhood to another, passing through curtains of garden-leaves, baskets of cultivated algae, shrines made from stacked volcanic glass.

Above the settlement, near the pale roof of the sea, the roofs of older buildings can still be seen. They belonged to a time when the valley was merely wet and not wholly drowned. Their upper rooms have become fishless ponds of trapped air, sacred and dangerous. Children are forbidden to enter them. The air inside is stale, full of old dust and the dry smell of things that should not still exist. Yet every few generations, workers climb there with stone knives and clay lamps, opening a tile or wall-panel to recover wood, copper, or a relic of the former sky.

Metal is almost unknown. A small blade is inherited like a name. Most tools are stone, shell, obsidian, teeth, fired clay, and careful hands. The city’s finest craft is binding: making cord, net, hinges, baskets, woven pressure-cloths, and flexible walls from the innumerable fibers of its gardens. Nothing is nailed. Everything is tied, looped, cinched, grafted, or allowed to grow around something else.

Light is never direct. It falls down through leagues of water as a tarnished blue-grey glow, strongest near the high ridges and fading toward the valley floor. At midday, the city appears made of shadows within shadows: reeds against stone, bodies passing behind fabric, the soft phosphorescence of cultivated moss caught in every doorway. At night, which is only a deeper variety of dimness, people hang bowls of luminous algae outside their homes. Their light is not enough to push back the water, but enough to give each terrace a floating cluster of green stars.

There are few animals.

Now and then a pale ribbon-eel slips through the gardens, blind and delicate, feeding on larvae in the warm vents. Small transparent crustaceans congregate in the roots of the waterwoods, their shells flashing briefly when a hand disturbs them. Far out in the basin, something large sometimes passes through the dark—never seen completely, only suggested by a change in pressure, a silence among the leaves, a slow immense shadow where there should be none. The city has names for it, but no agreement on whether it is one creature, many, or simply the valley remembering its former shape.

For the most part, the city is quiet.

Speech travels poorly through water unless it is close and deliberate, so people use touch, hand signs, bells, and taps along taut cords. A household can send a message across several terraces by plucking a particular sequence into a rootline. The notes arrive softened and altered by distance: low wooden knocks, shell clicks, a tremor in the palm. At communal hours, drums are struck against the mountain itself. Their vibrations pass through rock and water, calling workers home, announcing births, warning of a broken tether, mourning the dead.

The loudest sound is usually the breathing.

People carry air in bladders sewn from the skins of giant kelp bulbs, or rest at breathing chambers built into the cliff—small domed rooms where trapped gases are tended like fires. The chambers are humid, hot, crowded with sleeping mats and hanging tools. Every village-tier has one, and all life is organized around their patient supply. To own an air chamber is impossible. They belong to the whole terrace, maintained by everyone, watched by elders with the gravity given elsewhere to priests or kings.

The mountains are still rising.

Not visibly. Not in a human season. But new seams open in the stone, and warm water begins to seep from them. A garden changes direction. A cliff shelf broadens by the width of a thumb. Strange white mineral structures appear overnight around vents, then continue growing for years: ribs, cups, thorny towers. The city treats these changes as invitations rather than disasters. A new ledge becomes a future home. A crack becomes a cistern. A slowly tilting stone wall becomes the foundation for a bridge no one has woven yet.

The settlement has no final boundary. At its edges, houses become gardens, gardens become tethered rafts of root and reed, and those become wilderness. Beyond lies the drowned valley, warm and dark, filled with old roads, unfinished rock, drowned trees that have become reefs, and the vast slow pressure of water.

Nothing here has settled completely into its proper form.

The mountain is becoming a mountain. The gardens are becoming forest. The city is becoming a city, one knot at a time.
