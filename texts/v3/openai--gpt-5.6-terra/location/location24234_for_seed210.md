At a high saddle between two unnamed ranges, there is a basin no larger than a village green. It is nearly circular, though not perfectly so: one side is bitten away by a fresh pale cliff, the other rises in black, newly exposed stone ribs. The place looks young in the geological sense, as if the mountain has only recently opened an eye here. Its edges are sharp. The boulders have not yet softened into their surroundings. Splinters of basalt lie in the moss like broken teeth.

Yet nothing in the basin appears to be falling apart.

Rain comes down almost constantly, but it does not accumulate in puddles, nor does it run toward any outlet. It beads on every leaf, pools in shallow folds of rock, and then slowly draws itself back into the earth. The soil is dark and wet to the surface. In some places it gives underfoot with the deep, vegetable softness of a marsh; in others, a thin green skin covers rock that is only inches below. Water rises in clear seams from the cliff faces. It threads down them in narrow silver lines and vanishes into the moss before reaching the ground.

The air is cold enough that every breath feels washed. A low cloud lives in the basin, not moving through it but occupying it, brushing the ground at knee height. Above that cloud, the sky is dim even at midday: a color between tarnished pewter and old glacier ice. There is no visible sun. The light seems to arrive from the wet surfaces themselves—reflected from the standing water, the glazed leaves, the pale lichen, the white undersides of birds circling overhead.

Vegetation is lavish, though scattered.

There are no forests. Instead, small groves stand apart from one another with large reaches of open, saturated ground between them. Each grove is made of a single kind of tree: narrow, dark trunks rising from moss islands; crowns dense with waxy leaves, each leaf tipped with a perpetual droplet. The trees are stunted by altitude but vigorous, wound tightly into themselves. Ferns grow taller than the shrubs. Blue-green grasses spill from cracks in the stone. On the cliff walls, cushions of red moss swell around the springs, and pale flowers emerge directly from wet mineral seams.

Nothing has been planted. Nothing has been cut, fenced, laid, paved, or arranged by hands.

But the basin works.

Along the black stone ribs are rows of natural cavities, evenly spaced with an exactness too regular to be comfortable. From each cavity comes a steady breath of warm vapor and a low mechanical churning. The sound is not quite an engine, though it has the layered rhythm of one: a deep rotational throb beneath a rapid ticking, and behind that a distant continuous hiss. The mountain seems full of machinery. At intervals, metal-bright fluid wells up from hairline cracks and runs for a few feet before cooling into harmless gray scales. Some of these scales have the ridged texture of gears. Others form thin, curled shavings that dissolve when touched by rain.

No factory stands here. No cable crosses the ground. No smoke stack marks the ridge.

The industrialness is in the processes themselves.

Beneath the marsh, something turns. The water trembles in concentric circles without wind. Wet stones pulse with a faint blue light, each pulse passing from one to the next in orderly sequence. Root systems have grown around buried iron veins and use them like conductors. When lightning strikes the upper ridge—which happens often, silently, behind the cloud—the charge descends through the roots, flashes under the moss, and disappears into the ground with a sound like a freight train heard from very far away.

The animals know the timing.

They are abundant, more abundant than the smallness of the basin should allow. White-backed hares sit in the open grass, unafraid of one another, their ears transparent at the edges. Long-legged birds stalk among the shallow pools, lifting their feet with ceremonial slowness; their legs are jointed in too many places, and their beaks open sideways. Dark moths, broad as hands, cling to cliff faces in dense constellations. When they take flight they make a dry, papery roar, briefly covering the dim sky with moving black shapes.

There are also herds of small, shaggy animals with deer-like bodies and faces smooth as river stones. Their antlers are not bone but branching mineral, cloudy and translucent, as though carved from wet quartz. They wander through the basin on paths that have not been worn down by use. The same paths remain visible even after rain: faint pale tracks in the grass, a pattern repeated too precisely across the terrain. Every animal follows one route, pauses at one particular spring, turns at one particular stone. Then, after an interval, it returns by the same route.

They do not seem trapped. They do not seem free.

At the center of the basin is a pond only a few meters across. It is perfectly still despite the rain. Its surface is black in the dimness and reflects a sky that is not present above it: sometimes a bright rust-red noon, sometimes a field of stars, sometimes the interior of a vast room crossed with beams and belts. Looking into it for long makes the surrounding sounds become clearer. The hiss resolves into ventilation. The ticking becomes thousands of wet feet. The deep throb becomes a heart, or a turbine, or the immense slow wingbeat of something under the mountain.

The pond does not reflect anyone who leans over it.

Instead, it shows the basin as it was a moment ago, and as it will be a moment later, with no meaningful distinction between the two. Rain falls in the same places. A moth lifts from the same stone. A hare turns its head. The water runs down the same cliff in the same shining threads. The landscape is not frozen; it is complete. Every change occurs, but has already occurred and will continue occurring without deviation.

That is why the young cliffs never erode. That is why the moss remains vivid but never overruns the open ground. That is why the marsh is wet but does not expand, why the animal trails do not deepen, why no dead leaf decays beyond a certain soft brown stage. The basin has reached a kind of fixed weather, a fixed season, a fixed working order.

It is loud there, once one learns to hear it. Rain on leaves. Water feeding unseen chambers. Wingbeats, bird cries, the bubbling of mineral springs. The continuous subterranean machinery. All of it held within the little high basin by the surrounding rock. From outside, perhaps, the place might sound like an industrial plant operating behind a mountain wall.

From within, it sounds more like a living thing maintaining itself.

The mist never lifts. The cloud never thickens. No human trail approaches the saddle, and no ruins suggest that anyone has come before. Still, the basin feels occupied—not by a maker, exactly, but by an intention dispersed through roots, stone, water, fur, mineral, and rain.

It has no need to grow.

It has no need to finish.
