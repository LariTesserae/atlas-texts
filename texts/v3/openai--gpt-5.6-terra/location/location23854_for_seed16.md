The city is built inside the hollow of a dead salt dome, though “inside” is not quite right. It occupies a pocket in the earth that was once a bubble of something immense and pressurized, a buried lung which cooled, contracted, and left behind a chamber as broad as a small valley. Its ceiling is low in places, high in others: sweeps of black-brown mineral arching over terraces, then sudden shelves and crevices where the darkness gathers. No sky is visible. The air is hot, dry, and thick with a metallic tang.

There is no water here. None falls, pools, seeps, or hides below the ground. The inhabitants have names for moisture in the abstract, inherited from stories, but no word for wetness in the immediate sense. Their plants do not drink. Their broad, wax-colored fronds feed on heat, mineral dust, and the slow exhalations of the surrounding stone. At intervals in the walls, vents release warm streams of pale gas. The plants turn toward these vents with the unconscious patience of roots toward sun.

The vegetation is startlingly lush for a place so dry. It fills courtyards, grows over retaining walls, bursts in crowded crowns from cracks in the paving. There are low forests of reedlike columns whose segmented skins are blue-green and glazed, like fired clay. Their leaves are not thin but thick, flattened blades of fibrous mineral flesh, silver on one side and charcoal-black on the other. When the hot drafts reverse, every leaf turns at once, exposing one color and then the next, making the gardens seem to darken and brighten in a slow breath.

Other plants resemble heaps of folded leather or bundles of glassy ribbons. They coil around chimneys and stair rails. They root into porous brick, old ash, discarded pottery, and the seams between building stones. In the deepest shadows grow flowers without petals: dark red cups that open only when the vents thunder. They release fragrant powder rather than scent, and their pollen drifts through the streets in luminous brown clouds, clinging to hair, cloth, roofs, and door lintels.

The city itself is sparse. It is urban in arrangement, not in crowding. A great many streets exist for very few people. Broad avenues curve around the base of the central stone bulge; narrow steps climb from them to terraces, bridges, kilns, watch platforms, and empty market courts. Houses are low, thick-walled constructions of salt brick, baked mudstone, and scavenged plates of mica. Their doors are short, often draped with woven frond fiber to keep out the grit. Most buildings lean into the rock face, sharing walls with the cavern itself. Their roofs are planted with heat-feeding mosses and squat velvet shrubs, so that from a distance the settlement seems half swallowed by a dark garden.

There is little metal. Knives, chisels, and needles are made from volcanic glass or dense bone-like deposits pried from the mineral seams. Pots are fired in communal kilns. Pipes are ceramic. Wheels, where they exist, are stone disks rimmed with braided plant fiber. The city’s machinery is simple but constant: hand-cranked bellows, foot-treadle grinders, counterweighted lifts, wind vanes set before the breathing vents. Children—though there are few visible people at any given time—are taught early how to read the pulse of a draft by the movements of hanging fronds.

Nothing is still.

The chamber has weather, though it has no rain. Heat gathers in the upper vault and falls in dense invisible sheets. The vents change their pressure with a sound like distant surf, then like cattle, then like a long iron door being dragged across stone. Whole neighborhoods tie their shutters closed when the vent-season begins. Dust devils crawl down the avenues, carefully following old road lines, lifting dry pollen and crumbs of salt into whirling columns. The forest plants bend, click, and strike one another. Their rigid leaves make a continuous clatter, loud enough to cover speech.

Sound is everywhere. Kiln hammers ring from beneath awnings. Dry seed-pods explode in the gardens. Ropes creak over pulleys. Somewhere, at all hours, someone is scraping salt from a wall with a stone blade. The hollow magnifies these noises. A whispered argument can travel along the concave ceiling and arrive, thin but intelligible, in a plaza several streets away. People have learned not to speak certain names beneath the central arch, because the stone repeats them in fragments long after the speaker has gone.

The light is poor, but it is not absent. Pale mineral veins run through the ceiling like submerged lightning. They shine weakly when the chamber heats, a bruised violet-white glow that gives every face the look of being seen through smoke. Buildings collect this light with tilted mica panels, directing it into workrooms and narrow interior gardens. Some alleys are permanently dark except for the orange throats of kilns and the bluish shine of fungusless plant-sap stored in shallow glass bowls.

There are no animals. No insects stir in the leaf litter; no birds nest in the rafters; nothing small scuttles from under overturned pots. The gardens are untouched by teeth, burrows, or hungry eyes. When leaves fall, they lie where they land until they are ground under sandals, gathered for fuel, or slowly broken down by dry crystalline molds. The absence has made the city’s inhabitants attentive. Every moving thing is either wind, plant, machine, or person. A shadow that crosses a wall must have a cause.

At the deepest point of the pocket stands an old civic square, empty except for a grove of tall black reeds and a bell tower made from stacked salt blocks. The bell is not metal. It is a hollow stone vessel, suspended on braided cords. When the strongest vent opens below the square, hot gas rises through channels in the paving and makes the bell sing.

Its note is low, granular, and enormous.

It tells the city that the air is changing. Doors close. Gardeners secure the young fronds. Kiln workers bank their fires, because the coming draft will make them fierce. Along the terraces, the silver-and-black leaves turn themselves toward the wind. The streets empty, but the hollow grows louder: stone groaning, ropes snapping taut, leaves rattling by the thousands.

And above it all, in the dim mineral vault, the old light wakes in the seams and holds.
