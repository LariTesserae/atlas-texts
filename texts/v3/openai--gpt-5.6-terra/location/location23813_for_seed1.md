The land is low enough that the sky seems to rest on it.

There are no hills in the ordinary sense. The country has been worn down past hills, past plains, into a vast wet level where every old contour survives only as a change in sheen: a darker runnel of water, a belt of black stone exposed by wind, a shallow metallic rise clothed in frost. Nothing has been built here. No road interrupts the ground, no tower, no abandoned wall. Yet the landscape operates with the concentration and violence of an industrial district.

Under its thin crust, the earth is full of engines.

They are not machines made by hands. They are processes that have learned the posture of machinery. Long seams in the ground inhale and exhale. Fields of slate plates lift by fractions, settle, and lift again in staggered sequences, passing a slow mechanical shudder across dozens of miles. Fumaroles do not merely vent: they pulse, click, hammer, close their mineral lips, and open again. In the darkness below the peat-colored water, iron-rich currents turn immense buried wheels of sediment and ice. The world produces a continuous laboring noise: piston-thumps, wet grinding, turbine-like whines rising through fog, the high shriek of pressure escaping from cracks that are older than animal life.

Rain comes frequently, though sometimes it is rain only until it lands.

The drops fall cold and clear, then become tiny faceted grains, black threads, or pale beads that crawl uphill before sinking into the earth. Water lies everywhere, but rarely in open pools. It occupies the land densely: in hairline fissures, among piled pebbles, within transparent skins stretched across hollows, in deep pores of the ancient rock. The ground sweats. A person could walk for hours without finding dry stone, and yet never see a lake. At intervals, whole sections of the plain soften and begin to ripple, revealing themselves as a shallow sheet of water carrying a crust of ash, shell fragments, and reflected sky.

The sky is dim even at noon. It has the color of tarnished tin seen underwater. Light filters down through layers of vapor and suspended mineral dust, not enough to make shadows sharp. Distance is measured by sound more than sight. A cracking shelf may be ten miles away and arrive as a clean rifle report. A migrating mass of animals may be heard first as a broad droning roar, then as a fluttering vibration in puddles, then as movement in the fog.

There are no plants.

Nothing roots, leafs, flowers, or reaches toward the weak light. The land has moved beyond that form of persistence. Its living things cling, burrow, flow, molt, condense, and assemble.

The smallest are everywhere: mats of translucent mineral gel in the cracks, veils of pale filament hanging beneath overhangs, colonies of bead-like organisms that gather around warm vents and ring faintly when struck by rain. Some live in the water films and appear as shifting oil colors. Others live in stone, expanding and contracting beneath the surface until entire slabs make soft clicking sounds, like teeth in a skull.

The animals are abundant enough to give the bare world movement.

Flocks of low-flying creatures sweep over the plain in dense gray spirals. They have no wings that remain visible for long; their bodies repeatedly blur, simplify into a line, then recover their animal shape several yards away. They feed on pressure-sounds escaping from the ground, dipping toward active vents and drawing the noise into the hollow chambers of their throats. When startled, they release it all at once. Their alarm calls are stolen industrial noises: chains dragging, steam hammers, factory whistles, the scrape of enormous metal doors.

Along the wet flats move herds of many-jointed grazers, though there is nothing to graze. They lower their broad, shovel-like faces to the mud and sieve it for heat, salts, and microscopic mineral life. Their backs are plated in old enamel, smooth from centuries of abrasive rain. Steam issues from seams between their plates. They migrate not with seasons but with the slow rearrangement of subterranean pressure. A herd can be seen crossing a distant horizon for three days, never seeming to approach, because the land beneath it is moving in the opposite direction.

Predators follow them: tall, narrow things with limbs like folded surveying instruments and heads that turn independently of their bodies. Their eyes shine only when no one looks directly at them. They hunt by listening for rhythm. A sick animal develops an irregular internal pulse, and the predators can hear it through water, stone, fog, and the overlapping thunder of the terrain. When they strike, the sound goes briefly silent in a circle around them, as if silence itself has clamped shut.

There are also the shoreless swimmers.

At night—or what passes for night when the dimness becomes blue-black—the wet ground fills with shapes gliding just beneath the surface. Long backs rise in the marsh-skin, disappear, and rise somewhere else. They may be fish, though they move through mud and gravel as easily as water. They leave bright tracks of cold phosphorescence which do not mark where they have been, but where they will be in a few minutes. Travelers who follow the lights often arrive ahead of themselves, standing in a place they remember having not yet entered.

This is a country where sequence has become unreliable.

Rain may strike the ground before it falls. Footprints sometimes fill with water, then produce the feet that made them. Far-off noises arrive with the physical effects of their cause: a vibration knocks pebbles loose, and afterward the sound of a collapse rolls over the plain. Herds leave bones in their wake that look weathered for millennia, even while the animals are still passing. The ancient erosion of the place is not entirely in the past. The landscape seems to remember having already endured every storm it will receive.

Its rocks are extraordinarily old and remarkably busy. They have been rounded to low humps, ground into shingle, flattened into layers as thin as pages. But they continue to erode, not by losing material so much as by surrendering definition. A boulder may become a wet patch, then a sound, then a flock of small gray animals, then be a boulder again after the fog shifts. The oldest stones are the most unstable. Their age has made them porous to other arrangements of reality.

No one has made rules here, but rules exist.

Nothing may remain motionless for long. A still creature becomes a mineral deposit; a still pool becomes a window into another depth; a still sound becomes visible as a dark shape in the air and falls. Heat is valuable and migratory. It can be tracked, gathered, stolen, carried in the bellies of animals, traded between vents. Straight lines do not last in the wet: they bend toward pressure, hunger, or memory. Any hollow deep enough to hold a person’s reflection will eventually fill with another sky.

The loudest hours come when the buried mechanisms agree.

Then the entire low country begins to work. Cracks open in patterned ranks. Steam surges up in pale columns. The slate fields rattle like conveyors. The small ring-creatures sound their hundreds of thousands of mineral bells. Flocks rise, herd-plates clatter, unseen swimmers churn beneath the skin of the earth. Over everything comes the deep cyclical report of the ground’s hidden labor: not an earthquake exactly, but a colossal press closing somewhere underneath the world.

For several minutes, perhaps several centuries, the plain is all noise and vapor and moving bodies.

Then the pressure releases.

The fog settles back into the hollows. Water resumes its patient crawling through the stones. The animals return to their routes. In the dim light, the land appears empty again—flat, cold, wet, ancient beyond usefulness.

But the silence is never true silence. Beneath it, the country is still running.
