The plateau begins above the weather.

Not above all weather—clouds still happen here, though they happen low and slowly, lying in the basins like old breath—but above the busy kind. There are no storms that arrive with drama. Snow does not fall so much as accumulate from a dimming of the air. Rain is a rare, fine event, almost a mistake: needles of water that vanish on the stone or collect briefly in black seams before freezing.

The land is enormously old. It has been reduced, not ruined. Ridges have rounded into long-backed rises, their exposed strata polished by millennia of windborne grit. Valleys are broad enough to have their own horizon. Their walls descend in shelves of slate, pale basalt, and a reddish rock that flakes into thin, ringing plates under the feet of anything heavy enough to disturb it.

Nothing has been made here.

There are no roads, pylons, mines, fences, ruins, camps, or signs that a hand has ever tried to persuade the land into another shape. Yet the plateau is industrial in its functioning. It is full of engines.

They are alive.

Across the open ground travel herds of iron-bodied grazers, each one perhaps the size of a freight locomotive, though lower and more patient. Their hides are layered in overlapping dark plates, dull with frost. Between these plates grow bristles of white mineral fibers that catch the weak light and turn the animals briefly silver as they move. They feed with broad, slow mouths pressed to the ground, scraping up the black lichen that gathers along fissures in the rock. Inside them, something grinds constantly: stone against stone, or teeth against ore, or a whole furnace-like system of digestion that is never loud enough to hear from a distance.

Their work changes the landscape with the gentleness of geology. Behind a herd, bare rock has been cleared clean. Ahead of it, lichen fields advance at the speed of a fingernail’s growth. They follow routes inherited over generations, but the routes are not trails. The ground heals too slowly and the animals are too scattered. One sees only a faint alignment of disturbed frost, a repeated darkening of stone, a sense that this immense emptiness has preferences.

Smaller creatures attend them.

Thin-legged scavengers, hundreds at a time, move beneath the grazers’ bellies. They have translucent hides and carry their organs close to the surface, where they glow a muted amber in the twilight. They gather heat from the great animals’ joints and feed on the warm metallic salts shed from their mouths. They make no calls. Even their movement is nearly soundless: a shivering, coordinated procession across grit and snow.

There are winged things, too, though they resemble drifting machinery more than birds. They rise from crevices at dawn—if dawn can be said to occur in a place where the sun spends most of the year diffused behind cloud—and wheel high above the valleys on rigid, narrow wings. Their bodies are black and jointed. They do not flap. They unfold, catch thin currents, and hold themselves aloft for hours, listening through their feet to vibrations in the ground. When one descends, it closes its wings around itself and becomes indistinguishable from a shard of dark rock.

The silence is not an absence. It is an active condition of the place.

Wind moves above the plateau, visible in the crawling shift of cloud shadow, but the air near the ground is often still. Snow squeaks under padded feet. Frost expands in the cracks of stone. A distant herd may exhale, and the steam from hundreds of nostrils may drift over a valley floor without any individual breath being audible. Even the creatures with internal grinding organs seem to have evolved ways of keeping their labor contained.

Perhaps sound is expensive here. Perhaps it travels too far.

The largest animals never call to one another. Their communication is subterranean: slow pulses sent through the plateau’s bedrock. At intervals too long for a person to notice at first, a ridge may give a subtle pressure beneath the soles. Miles away, another herd changes direction. Across distances that would take weeks to cross, bodies answer bodies through the stone.

At the heart of some valleys are damp places: no lakes in the ordinary sense, but fields of dark, saturated gravel where meltwater rises from beneath the ground and never quite becomes a stream. Their surfaces tremble under skins of ice. Mineral reeds grow there, sparse and blue-gray, each blade edged with crystalline deposits. In summer, if the temperature briefly climbs toward thaw, these wetlands steam faintly. They become crowded beyond belief.

Millions of small lives emerge from the gravel: bead-eyed burrowers, plated worms, pale antlered insects that climb the reeds and remain motionless for days. The great grazers come then, standing knee-deep in the cold muck while their young shelter among their legs. Predators gather at the margins—not in packs, not with visible hunger, but in loose, watchful geometries.

They are tall animals with long, angular bodies and faces plated over like masks. Their limbs end in broad, soft feet adapted to the crust of frozen mud. They do not chase. Their prey simply slows around them. A weak animal stops, as though receiving an instruction too deep to refuse, and the predators gather without sound. In the dimness, this can seem almost ceremonial, though nothing here appears to possess ceremony. It is only the established order of hunger, carried out with immense restraint.

The sky rarely clears completely. When it does, it is a hard, dark-blue bowl streaked with stars even before evening. The light remains thin. The sun moves along a low arc and touches the upper faces of the stone, leaving the valleys blue and cold. At night there are faint bands of green over the northern rim of the world. They do not shimmer extravagantly; they linger, washed-out and patient, as if the heavens themselves are conserving energy.

The plateau has weather of its own because it is too large to be merely terrain. Its western marches lie under a permanent veil of airborne ice. Its eastern basins are drier, open expanses of red gravel where immense burrowing fauna leave slow spirals in the ground. Far to the south, the stone descends by terraces into cloud, and no creature from the highlands follows that edge willingly. To the north, the land rises again into a region of black escarpments where the old wind has carved entire mountain ranges into fluted walls.

Nothing is sudden here.

A carcass may remain for decades, its armored ribs gradually taken apart by scavengers, frost, mineral bloom, and the slow pressure of snow. A herd’s migration can take a century to complete. A wetland may shift one valley over during a thousand-year thaw cycle. The ridges continue to wear down grain by grain, while beneath them the animals communicate through stone, maintain their vast hidden economies of heat and metal and flesh, and move through an unbuilt world with the solemn efficiency of factories that were never designed.

From a great distance, the land appears empty.

From within it, emptiness is revealed as the space required for innumerable lives to continue without speaking.
