The high valley is entirely built.

There is no bare hillside beneath the structures, no true bank to the streams, no unworked stone showing through. Every slope has been faced, buttressed, stepped, tiled, or mortared into a patient arrangement of walls. The mountains themselves seem to have been persuaded into architecture over centuries: broad ramps rising into blackness, retaining walls as tall as cliffs, narrow stairways vanishing beneath archways, terraces stacked one above another until they merge with the night sky.

It is warm here despite the elevation. The air has the closeness of a conservatory after rain, though no glass encloses it. Heat leaks from the old masonry. The stone holds the day long after daylight has gone—if daylight comes at all—and gives it back slowly through the dark. Dampness beads on walls and ceilings, runs in fine silver threads through mortar joints, gathers in shallow gutters between paving slabs. There is water, but never much at once: a chain of cisterns, seep-fed basins, channels covered by grates of woven root and bone-white branch. After rain, every stair becomes slick. During dry weeks, the valley still breathes moisture from its deep reservoirs.

The construction is not grand in the clean, empty way of monuments. It is crowded with life.

Plants grow from every permitted crack and many forbidden ones. Ferns soften the faces of stair walls. Thick climbing vines cross from balcony to balcony and bind opposing buildings together. Moss fills the lettering on old lintels. Small trees have been trained in stone planters along roofs and parapets, their roots directed down hollow shafts within the walls. At the level of the paths, warm broad-leaved shrubs make green tunnels between courtyards. Their leaves are glossy with condensation; touching one leaves a cool, mineral smell on the fingers.

Nothing is wild in the sense of untouched. Yet the vegetation is abundant enough that this distinction feels unimportant. It has been accepted into the place, accommodated by drains, niches, trellis frames, water ledges, and low walls built specifically to hold soil. The landscape is made, but it is not sterile. It is a city or sanctuary that has been inhabited for so long by growing things that its original boundaries have been softened beyond recovery.

The darkness is practical rather than mysterious. The valley lies under steep enclosing ridges, and its passages are roofed by bridges, galleries, reservoirs, and layered terraces. Even at noon, much of it is shade. At night, it becomes almost complete blackness, interrupted only by pale wet stone, the dull sheen of leaf surfaces, and the occasional square of clouded sky high above an open court. There are no lamps. No fires burn in windows. There is no machinery, no wire, no gleam of worked metal, no sign that the place expects illumination.

The animals do not require it.

They are everywhere, and they make the darkness populous.

Birds crowd the cornices, eaves, gutter channels, and vine-laden roofs. Their calls overlap so densely that a single voice cannot be followed for long. Pigeons and swifts nest in old drainage mouths; heavier, darker birds roost beneath the bridge vaults; small green parrots move through the planted terraces in restless, quarrelsome bands. Bats spill from holes in the upper retaining walls at dusk, making the air above the stairs twitch and swirl. Frogs occupy every wet depression, each cistern rim, each cracked basin where rain has lingered. Insects gather in the warmer sheltered passages: beetles clicking under leaf litter, moths flapping softly against damp plaster, crickets screaming from behind stacked stones.

There are mammals too, though they are harder to see. Goats stand on ledges that appear inaccessible, their hooves ringing sharply on tiled roofs. Dogs move in loose, silent groups through the lower courts, noses low, appearing briefly where a passage opens onto another passage. Pale mice live in wall cavities and grain stores that no person seems to tend. Monkeys sometimes cross overhead along vines and gutter rails, dropping fruit pits, leaves, and the occasional tile into the lanes below.

The sound is deafening not because anything is violent, but because there is never an absence of it.

Water ticks continually in hidden channels. Rain, when it arrives, pounds on roofs, rattles through drainpipes, and pours down stepped spillways with a force that turns whole districts into echo chambers. Birds call from every height. Frogs pulse beneath them. Wings beat in enclosed stairwells. Animals scrape, bark, cough, chirr, and shuffle behind walls. The masonry takes every sound and returns it altered: amplified under arches, broken into repetitions by colonnades, carried far along covered walks. A person could stand alone in a courtyard and still hear a thousand lives pressing close beyond the walls.

Yet the place changes slowly.

No new construction rises. No old quarter is suddenly abandoned. The walls are weathered into equilibrium. Their corners have rounded under centuries of rain; their stairs are hollowed by feet, claws, and flowing water. Repairs, if they occur, are indistinguishable from the original work: a stone settled back into place, a collapsed roof made into a planted terrace, a blocked drain opening another route for water. The valley does not grow outward so much as it thickens inward. Vines cover a wall; animals begin nesting in the vine; soil accumulates in the nest-shadow; a tree roots above an arch; the arch becomes part of the tree’s ground.

Paths lead through it in long ascending loops. One may begin at a wet lower gate, pass through alleys tight enough for leaves to brush both shoulders, emerge onto a broad terrace where warm wind carries the smell of wet limestone and droppings, then climb again into higher courts where the clouds scrape the parapets. Beyond the last visible wall, there are more walls. Beyond those, more terraces, reservoirs, rookeries, and sleeping gardens continue across the mountain’s folded back.

It is a completely made world, but not a controlled one.

Its makers are gone, or have become irrelevant to its daily life. The place belongs now to moisture, roots, feathers, claws, and the immense nocturnal chorus that fills every hollow built into the mountain.
