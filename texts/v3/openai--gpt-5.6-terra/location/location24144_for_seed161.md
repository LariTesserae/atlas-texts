At the highest shoulder of an old mountain range, where the world has already spent most of its violence, there is a settlement called the Dry Garden.

It occupies a hollow no wider than a cathedral nave: a shallow basin in black, reddish stone, ringed on every side by vertical cliffs. The cliffs are not sharp. Nothing here is sharp anymore. Their faces have been worked for ages by wind, by frost, by the tiny dry rasp of lichens and insect feet. They rise in immense folded walls, their ledges rounded and their cracks packed with dust the color of bone meal. Above them there is only a narrow polygon of sky, usually gray-white, so high and far away it seems less like an opening than a ceiling.

The air is warm in the basin, unexpectedly so. The stone stores heat from the long pale days, then gives it back carefully through the night. Yet the light rarely reaches the floor directly. It comes down softened by overhangs, smoke, woven awnings, branches, laundry, platforms, and the huge canopy of the garden itself. At noon the place is amber-dim. At dawn and evening it barely changes: a dense brown-green twilight inhabited by eyes, wings, calling throats, and the faint red glow of cooking pits.

There is almost no water.

A visitor notices this immediately because everyone else does not speak of it. No fountain marks the center. No gutter runs along the paths. The settlement has no wells, no cisterns large enough to name, no river tucked under its stones. Water is a word used for old songs and medicinal mixtures, for tears, for the clear fluid inside a butchered animal’s eye. The people drink pressed sap, fermented pulp, and the sour, mineral-rich liquid stored in the swollen roots of cultivated plants. Their gardens are lush not because the land is wet, but because the plants have become experts in refusing thirst.

The most important trees are not trees in the ordinary sense. They are pillar-plants: thick, jointed columns of green-gray flesh, as broad as cottages at their bases, rising fifteen or twenty meters before spreading into crowns of overlapping paddles. Their skins are waxy and scarred, patterned by old cuts. Every household is responsible for a few of these cuts, sealed afterward with clay and resin. A careful incision yields a cup of bitter sap. A greedy one kills a branch five years later, and everyone knows whose children will go without shade.

Between the pillars grow compact forests of silver-leaved shrubs, broad fungal shelves fed by compost pits, thornless vine mats, and low pompon-like plants whose roots reach so deeply into fissures that they are believed to touch the sleeping stone beneath the mountain. At the edges of the basin, in places where the cliffs sweat only a trace of vapor before dawn, the garden becomes almost extravagant: thick mosses of dry velvet, flowers like paper lanterns, pale fruit clustered against warm rock. The plants do not glitter with dew. They glitter with wax, dust, and tiny living scales.

Every surface is occupied.

Homes have been built into the cliff wall, fitted into gaps among roots, perched on carved stone shelves, and suspended between the pillar-plants on cords of twisted vine fiber. They are made of dry-stacked stone, mud brick, timber brought up from lower valleys generations ago, and panels woven from the stiff leaves of the garden. Their roofs are flat and heavily used: for sleeping, drying food, repairing baskets, keeping grubs, hatching eggs, and watching weather too distant to touch.

The streets are narrow enough that two laden people must turn sideways to pass. They are not really streets; they are polished routes through density. One goes under low bridges, past smoke-darkened doorways, around a communal kiln, through the dark interior of a hollow root, then emerges onto a little terrace where three old women are grinding seed into flour. Another climbs in shallow steps to goat pens built on a ledge, then vanishes into the cliff through a tunnel originally made by some animal larger than anything now living there.

The settlement is urban in the way a nest is urban. It has no grand square, but it has crowds. There are workshops packed together under awnings: bone carvers, hide workers, potters firing thin vessels in dung-fed kilns, makers of stone blades who sit all day striking flakes from the same dense blue-black rock. There are storage rooms guarded by families rather than locks. There is a council porch whose posts have been polished smooth by four hundred years of hands. There are ladders everywhere, and nearly every ladder is occupied by someone, something, or a bundle waiting to be moved.

Metal exists only as a few inherited objects: a bent knife that belongs to the seed-keeper, two copper needles, a small dull bell used during funerals. Most tools are stone, fired clay, horn, shell, wood, and the tough glassy spines of the pillar-plants. The residents are not poor in skill. Their baskets are sealed tightly enough to hold sap. Their rope bridges hold whole pack animals. Their clay pipes carry smoke from indoor hearths up through cracks in the cliff, so that the basin never fills completely with soot. But every object bears the marks of fingers, scraping blades, smoke, repair, and reuse. Nothing is manufactured at a distance. Nothing is thrown away until it has become dust, fuel, mulch, or a story.

The noise is constant.

High in the dim canopy, great dry cicadas pulse in sheets of vibration, their calls rising and falling like machinery too old to stop. Rock doves boom from the ledges. Little thorn-wrens make harsh, metallic arguments from every roofline. Insect-eating bats rustle under eaves by day, annoyed by the light, then leave at dusk in black dense ribbons. The pillar-plants host colonies of clicking beetles with shiny green shells; when disturbed, they knock their wing covers together so loudly that a handful can sound like rain on a roof—though few living people have heard actual rain.

There are animals everywhere: narrow-faced goats picking their way along cliff paths; thick-tailed lizards sleeping in the warm seams of walls; root-mice fed deliberately in some homes and hunted in others; long-legged fowl that scratch among the compost terraces; pale moths with powdery wings gathering around lamps of rendered fat. In the highest cavities live cliff monkeys, small and gray, treated as clever thieves and occasional ancestors. They descend at night to steal fruit, loosen roof thatch, and scream at dogs.

The dogs are lean, reddish, and patient. They do not bark much. They lie beneath the sap pillars in the heat and watch the gardens as if they, too, understand the rules.

There are many rules.

No person may cut a living pillar-plant after sunset, because the sap rises then toward the crown and an injury can drain too much from it. No one may burn old rootwood except during death rites or the coldest week of the year. The lowest garden terraces belong to everyone, but the upper ledge gardens belong to the households that maintain the climbing paths. Animal dung is weighed before it is traded. Fruit seeds are counted in public. A person who wastes drinkable sap must spend a season tending the compost beds, where all the settlement’s discarded matter is returned to the earth in slow, hot layers.

The oldest rule is carved in a polished slab near the council porch:

**Do not ask the hollow to become a valley.**

Nobody agrees precisely what it means. Some say it forbids digging too deeply for water. Some say it warns against expansion, against cutting away the garden in order to build more homes. Some say it was written after an ancestor tried to divert the mountain’s hidden vapor channels and nearly killed the basin. Children are taught it before they are taught their clan names.

Beyond the settlement, there is not much room for wilderness. The basin’s boundary is immediate and absolute. A climb of half an hour reaches the upper lip, where the garden’s warmth fails and the high world begins: fields of bare, ancient stone; low, scattered cushions of thorn; wind-scoured shelves where the ground looks almost lunar. The mountains continue in folded ridges, gray and umber and purple with distance. Their forms are so old that they no longer seem to be rising or falling. They are simply there, settled into themselves.

Far below, perhaps, there are rivers. People in the Dry Garden know this from travelers and from the rare objects brought upward by trade: smooth blue pebbles, fish bones, reed mats, jars with white mineral crust in their seams. But the descent is dangerous and long. The paths cross exposed faces, scree slopes, and ravines where the sun is severe. Most inhabitants are born in the hollow and die there. The mountain is not a landscape around them. It is wall, roof, ancestry, calendar, and limit.

Time moves slowly in the Dry Garden. A new dwelling may take ten years to complete, because its builders wait for a root to grow clear of the intended wall. A pillar-plant planted at a child’s birth may not be tapped until that child has children. The cliff faces change only in the smallest ways: a flake loosens, a ledge rounds, a burrow collapses. Each generation adds another black handprint of soot above the common ovens, another patched stair, another layer of compost beneath the gardens.

And still, the place feels crowded with life rather than old age.

At night, when the narrow sky turns black and stars appear in their rigid unfamiliar abundance, the basin glows in hundreds of tiny points: ember pits, resin lamps, beetle lanterns kept in pierced clay jars. The calls of animals become stranger in the dark. The people speak from roof to roof. A pestle thumps grain. Someone beats a hide clean. Somewhere, the funeral bell gives one low, blunt note.

The sound climbs the cliffs and returns softened.

Nothing in the Dry Garden is abundant without being guarded. Nothing is dry without also being alive.
