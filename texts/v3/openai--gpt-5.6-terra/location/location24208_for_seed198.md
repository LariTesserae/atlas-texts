The city occupies the whole valley.

There may once have been slopes here, a riverbed, terraces of native stone, but nothing bare remains. Every surface has been faced, bridged, roofed, bolted over, or given a purpose. The mountains rise at either side not as wilderness but as retaining structures: long gray walls of quarried rock, patched with concrete and threaded by galleries, freight lifts, ventilation shafts, and steep service roads. Their upper reaches vanish into low cloud. Water comes out of them everywhere.

It runs from seams in the walls, from overflow pipes, from the joints between paving slabs. It gathers in gutters too narrow for the volume they carry and falls through grates with a patient, continuous black sound. The streets shine even when rain is not falling. Moss fills the mortar lines. Ferns thrust from cracked stairwells, from the shaded backs of transformer cabinets, from the mouths of culverts. On old brick facades, green growth has climbed past the second and third stories and hangs in sodden curtains from the rails of abandoned fire escapes.

Nothing is empty. The valley floor is packed with factories, worker blocks, warehouses, pumping stations, depots, substations, kitchens, repair yards, and dense ranks of narrow houses built up against one another. Their roofs overlap like scales. Pipes pass directly through rooms. Catwalks cross alleys at every level. A person can walk a long way through the city without seeing the ground, moving from stair to corridor, loading platform to enclosed bridge, machine hall to canteen, through the insides of the place.

The city is always being altered.

A crane turns above the rooftops, slowly and without drama, carrying a steel panel through the mist. A whole section of wall has been removed from a printing works and replaced by temporary corrugated sheeting. Fresh concrete cures under tarpaulins. Rail spurs appear and disappear beneath piles of cable, pallets, rubble, and wet timber. One district is being raised on stilts because the lower floors flood each spring; another is being cut into for a new pressure tunnel. The work never feels like expansion. There is nowhere to expand to. It is rearrangement under pressure, a constant exchanging of one necessary space for another.

This turbulence does not make the city loud.

Its machinery has been chosen, maintained, and muffled by generations of people who live beside it. Pumps throb behind insulated walls. Electric freight engines whine briefly on the elevated lines, then are gone behind buildings. Conveyor belts move in covered runs. Water is louder than industry: dripping from awnings, sluicing through channels, ticking from pipes onto sheet metal. In the higher streets, wind touches the hanging wires and makes them hum. The human sound is subdued too—doors closing, boots on wet stairways, a cart wheel rattling across a grating, a call passed from one loading bay to the next.

The light arrives late and leaves early. Cloud catches on the valley’s rim, and the close construction keeps the streets in a gray-blue dusk even at noon. Sodium lamps remain lit in workshops and underpasses. Windows glow a weak amber behind condensation. From the upper tramways, one sees hundreds of these lights scattered through wet foliage and metalwork: not a bright city, but a city full of enclosed, occupied warmth.

Vegetation has learned the available shapes. There are no broad meadows, no open woods, but there is abundance in the seams. Wet gardens occupy roof basins, service courtyards, old foundations, and strips between rail lines. Cabbages and dark greens grow in raised troughs warmed by steam pipes. Fruit trees lean from walled yards, their branches tied back from power lines. Ivy swallows unused gantries. In the deepest alleys, where water seeps perpetually from the foundations, there are colonies of liverwort, red-stemmed moss, and pale mushrooms crowding the base of every wall.

The city’s ecology is largely botanical and human. Animals are scarce. Pigeons appear near grain warehouses; a few crows travel the upper air, black marks against the cloud. Rats keep to the drainage system and are almost never seen. Occasionally, on a quiet night, someone spots a fox moving along the edge of a rail yard, wet and thin, passing between tanks before it disappears into the undergrowth beyond the floodlights.

At the center of the valley is the old waterworks, larger than any other building and older than the current maps. It is a field of brick pump houses, settling towers, intake sheds, and covered reservoirs, all connected by iron bridges. The waterworks regulates the city’s basic fact: water must be kept moving. Rainwater, mountain seepage, household water, coolant, runoff from roofs, drainage from gardens—everything is collected, lifted, filtered, redirected. There are signs posted throughout the city:

**KEEP CHANNELS CLEAR.**  
**NO PRIVATE CONNECTION TO THE RETURN MAIN.**  
**FLOW HAS PRIORITY.**

People obey these rules not out of reverence, but because everyone knows what happens when a drain blocks. A basement fills. Then a stairwell. Then a lower street becomes a brown, reflective canal, and the tram line shorts out beneath its casing. In this city, maintenance is not background work. It is the work that allows all other work to exist.

The place has been here long enough to have settled into its own solutions. Its brickwork is rounded by rain, its steel polished at hand height, its stair treads hollowed in the middle. There are old water marks on nearly every wall, carefully dated in faded paint. There are generations of additions: stone foundations, red-brick mills, concrete facings, galvanized roofs, newer plastic ducting, cable bundles tied to pipes that predate the electrical grid. Nothing is elegant in isolation, but together it has a dense, practiced coherence.

Above the industrial roofs, the valley’s green rises wherever it can. It fills abandoned cuts and the backs of retaining walls. It presses against the rail embankments and gathers on the slopes behind the final row of buildings. The city does not seem to have defeated the mountains. It has simply made itself so complete that the mountains have been forced to enter it as water, moss, fog, and persistent roots.
