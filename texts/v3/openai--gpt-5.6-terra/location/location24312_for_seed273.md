The high country has been built over so completely that nobody remembers whether there was ever stone beneath it.

From a distance, the place resembles a mountain valley, though every contour is made: the slopes are retaining walls faced in pale concrete; the ridges are ranks of water tanks, wind baffles, and old factory roofs; the cliffs are the sides of inhabited structures descending in terraces until they disappear into a green basin. It is called a valley because clouds collect there and because all roads, drains, stairways, and slow canals lead downward toward its center. But there is no exposed earth anywhere. The ground underfoot is brick, tile, cast iron, patched asphalt, corrugated decking, the flat backs of buildings, and in the oldest districts, a kind of porous cement darkened by a century of roots.

At this elevation the air is bright and thin enough to make distances sharp. The far sides of the basin seem close, detailed down to individual balcony rails, but walking toward them takes hours. Warmth rises from the workings under the city: boiler galleries, fermentation halls, engine rooms, heat pipes that cross the streets in bundled black rows. In the morning, the sunlight arrives hard and clean over the eastern rim, catches the glazing of greenhouses and the polished sheets of rainwater held in gutters, and turns the entire valley white-gold. By noon, wet shadows have gathered under bridges and beneath the dense foliage. In the afternoon, a bank of cloud may drift through at shoulder height, wetting the upper walkways and hanging beads of condensation from every wire.

Water is never absent, but seldom abundant enough to be taken for granted.

It comes in brief rains, in mist squeezed from the mountain air by tall mesh towers, in the perspiration of cooling pipes, in the overflow from rooftop cisterns. The old drainage system is as important as the road system. Stone-lined channels run beside markets. Copper downpipes pass through apartments. Narrow staircases are built around little waterfalls that only flow after a storm. The city has learned to make a spectacle of dampness: moss is encouraged in the seams of plazas, ferns are planted beneath leaking valves, and every public wall is shaped with ledges where water can pause long enough for something green to take hold.

The vegetation is lavish, though it is all cultivated or escaped cultivation. Vines cross between buildings in ropes thick as wrists. Broad-leaved bananas rise from concrete planters on the lower terraces, while higher up there are tree ferns, citrus courts, bamboo screens, and orchards growing in long elevated troughs. The roofs of warehouses have become jungles of beans, tomatoes, medicinal shrubs, flowering creepers, and small fruit trees rooted in carefully renewed compost. There are gardens on loading docks and gardens inside abandoned freight elevators. One whole district is threaded by old rail lines whose sleepers have been converted to raised beds; the trains no longer run, but squash vines follow the tracks through tunnels of rusted steel.

Every plant has an owner, a keeper, a water allotment, or a disputed history. Even the weeds are catalogued. No one says “wild” except as a joke.

The fauna thrives in the spaces between management. Pigeons nest behind air-conditioner housings; swifts pour from ventilation shafts at evening; geckos live in the cracks around warm machinery. There are colonies of bees kept on upper balconies, their hives painted in municipal colors. Goats are led along service ramps to graze invasive growth from the steepest retaining walls. Dogs sleep under market tables. Cats have a monopoly on the boiler districts, where the warmth and the rats are dependable. In the damp arcades, large green beetles gather around lamps, and moths settle on the painted metal doors in such numbers that the doors appear furred.

The loudness is not violent. It is accumulated.

Fans turn constantly in the greenhouse roofs. Pumps knock and cycle behind masonry. Freight lifts groan up their shafts. Above everything comes the deep, unhurried working sound of the industrial heart: turbines, belts, compressors, old generators maintained past their intended lives. The city is full of metal struck by use—ladles against pots, shutters pulled open, handcarts rattling over grates, tools dropped in workshops. Voices rise from densely packed balconies and courtyards, carrying easily in the thin air. Birds sharpen the morning. Frogs, in the wettest drainage basins, take over after dark.

Yet the landscape itself changes slowly. Its machinery is old enough to have become part of the terrain. Great pipes have sagged into gentle arcs and are now used as pedestrian bridges. A factory chimney, long decommissioned, supports a lattice of balconies, bird nets, and climbing plants; from a distance it resembles a monumental tree. Rain has rounded the edges of concrete stairs. The passage of countless feet has polished certain routes to a dull shine. Repairs are visible everywhere, but they are not signs of urgency. A patched wall may contain five generations of brick and plate steel, each repair respected as another layer of the place.

There are rules, many of them practical enough to become ritual.

Nothing may be discarded below the level at which it was used, because everything downhill eventually enters the water system. No roof may be left bare if it can bear a planter, a tank, a coop, a drying rack, or a solar canopy. The upper cisterns are opened by schedule and bell, not by private demand. Vines are allowed to cross public facades only if they leave windows clear and do not enter the ventilation grilles. At the highest terraces, where gusts can overturn a person, all loose objects are tethered. Children learn knots before they learn maps.

And because the ground is constructed, every citizen understands that maintenance is a form of weather. There are seasons of repainting, seasons of pipe inspection, seasons when the storm screens are lowered, seasons when compost is hauled uphill in electric carts. The city does not grow outward very much. It thickens, layers, reuses. A disused machine hall becomes a school. A school roof becomes a wet garden. A wet garden’s overflow fills a fish tank. The fish tank warms an adjacent apartment block. Nothing is simple, but most things are connected.

At the center of the valley lies a broad reservoir plaza, rectangular and deliberately modest. In dry weeks it is a paved square of pale tiles, used for markets, repairs, games, and public assemblies. After rain, shallow gates are lifted and water spreads across it in a clear, ankle-deep sheet. Children wade through reflections of towers and hanging gardens. Birds gather. The surrounding buildings—factories, dormitories, workshops, temples, clinics, storage houses—appear doubled below themselves.

Then the gates are closed again, slowly, and the water is distributed through the city’s veins.

By evening, high above the reservoir, lights come on in tiers. They are bright, practical lights: sodium lamps over stairs, fluorescent tubes in workrooms, warm rectangles behind kitchen windows, white grow-lamps beneath greenhouse glass. The basin glows against the dark outer sky. Its terraces resemble an inhabited circuit board draped in leaves.

Beyond the built ridges, there may be mountains. There may be open country. On clear days, people claim to see it.

But the city has its own slopes, its own rain, its own forests, its own animal migrations along pipes and wires. It is a complete landscape made by hands and kept alive by patient systems: warm, crowded, green, wet at the edges, and steadily working far above the clouds.
