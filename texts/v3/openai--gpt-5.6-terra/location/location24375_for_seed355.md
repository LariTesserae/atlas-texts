The city lies so low that the sea has forgotten where it ends and the streets begin.

There is no horizon in the ordinary sense. Darkness has filled it. Not night exactly—night implies a sky, stars, distances—but a warm, close darkness in which the upper air sweats and the ground gives off its stored heat. The city extends across a drowned plain of black clay, salt-stained masonry, canals, and broad shallow basins. Water is everywhere without ever becoming a river. It stands in doorways. It films the paving stones. It gathers in the concave roofs of collapsed halls. It beads on the undersides of arches and falls upward, slowly, into the dark.

The buildings are made of mineral things found nearby: pale porous stone, dark kilnbrick, slabs of shell-limestone, layers of baked mud pressed hard as ceramic. They have no glass, no metal fittings, no lamps. Their doors are tall openings cut with a practical severity, though many now lead into water or into walls that have shifted since they were built. Streets run straight for a while, then bow aside around old sinkholes, old reservoirs, or the roots of structures whose foundations descended farther than anyone expected.

It is an urban place, but not a crowded one. The population, if it has one, is absent from the streets. The empty buildings remain arranged for use: terraces facing public cisterns, stairways rising from inundated plazas, raised walkways crossing reedless flood courts, market alcoves beneath long stone awnings. In places, whole neighborhoods have settled by degrees, their lower stories swallowed by the saturated earth. Yet none have fallen into complete ruin. The city is old enough to have learned its own posture. Walls lean against one another. Cracks have become drainage channels. Courtyards have become retention ponds. The place has weathered its losses into a stable, damp geometry.

Sparse vegetation survives where it can. There are no lush margins, no jungle pressing in. Instead there are tufts of bladegrass as sharp and gray as wire, clusters of waxy leaves emerging from mortar joints, and low trees with naked, pale trunks rooted in flooded squares. Their crowns are small, their leaves few and tough. Some stand waist-deep in brine, breathing through raised knots along their roots. Others grow from roofs, their roots descending through rooms and stairwells like bundled cords.

The heat is constant. Even in darkness the stones are hot enough to warm rainwater on contact. Vapor moves through the streets in low drifts, not wind-driven but exhaled from cracks in the ground. The air smells of salt, wet lime, mud, and something faintly sweet from water left too long in enclosed chambers. Every surface is slick. To touch a wall is to feel a thin living coolness over a deep stored warmth.

There are no animals. No insects gather in the lamps because there are no lamps. No birds disturb the rooftops. No fish break the flooded plazas. The absence is not peaceful at first. It gives the city an unfinished quality, as though it is waiting for the smallest movement to authorize all the others.

But the city moves on its own.

A canal may drain into a courtyard for hours, then reverse its flow without tide or rain. A stairway, seen through rain, sometimes ends one step earlier than it did before. Doorways collect darkness more densely than the surrounding streets; one may look through an opening and see a dry hall beneath a bright noon, though stepping closer reveals only a wall slick with algae. In certain districts the water falls in straight, narrow columns from empty air, striking the paving with almost no sound. Elsewhere, puddles hold reflections of buildings that do not stand above them.

The ground itself is unsettled in a patient way. Beneath the city, the water-bearing layers breathe: filling, emptying, lifting old slabs, lowering entire blocks by the width of a hand. Over generations the inhabitants—or whoever established the place—built for this. Bridges are not anchored rigidly but rest in nested stone cradles. Streets are divided by broad seams packed with shell grit. Houses have sacrificial lower rooms designed to flood, silt over, and eventually be abandoned. There are walls built not to keep water out, but to tell it where it may go.

In the deepest parts of the city, old civic basins spread like plazas. Their edges are marked with staircases descending far below the present waterline. Along the steps are carved levels, hundreds of them, worn by generations of wetness. At some point the marks cease to describe measurements and become writing. The script continues underwater. It continues beyond the visible stone. It may be that the city’s oldest laws are recorded not in books or proclamations, but in the changing depth of water.

Nothing makes much noise. Rain comes often, but it arrives as a dense mist, a tapping too soft to separate into individual drops. Water shifts behind walls. Clay releases a bubble of trapped air. Somewhere, an unseen roof drips at a slow, exact interval. Occasionally a low resonant groan travels through the masonry when one district settles against another.

The silence is not empty. It is structured, held between damp surfaces.

At intervals, the darkness thins—not into daylight, but into a bruised blue-gray clarity. The city appears then as a broad low arrangement extending across the floodplain: towers cut short by subsidence, domes ribbed with salt, aqueducts serving basins whose water no longer follows gravity reliably. Beyond its outer quarters are flats of steaming mud, interrupted by old causeways and solitary stone pylons. Farther out, the land becomes indistinct, dissolving into heat and rain.

Sometimes the far pylons seem closer than the nearest streets.

Sometimes a district that was visible an hour ago is absent, replaced by open water reflecting a ceiling of black cloud.

The city does not regard this as disaster. Its architecture has no panic in it. Every wall is thick, every stairway redundant, every plaza capable of becoming a pond. It has existed long enough in this mutable low country to make room for alteration. It has become less a settlement built against the landscape than a set of habits shared with it: sinking, sweating, redirecting, waiting.

And in the warm darkness, with water rising through the joints of the pavement and no creature calling from roof or marsh, it feels possible that the city is the only organism left—an immense, mineral body sleeping lightly beneath the rain.
