The town lies beneath a low ceiling of stone, in a chamber that was never meant to be a town and is still, in some ways, deciding whether to remain a chamber at all.

It is hot down here. Not furnace-hot, not immediately dangerous, but the kind of heat that settles into clothing and makes every metal rail warm to the hand. The rock sweats continuously. Moisture beads in the seams overhead, swells, gathers the faint reflected light, and drops at long intervals into gutters beside the streets. There is no rain exactly, though the inhabitants speak of rain: *the west wall is raining*, *take the dry lane, the roof is coming down there*. Water is everywhere near the surface of things, never quite enough to make a river. It seeps from pale mineral curtains, runs in black threads across the floor, disappears through iron gratings, and rises in humid breaths from unsealed shafts.

The settlement occupies an oval basin perhaps the size of a cathedral nave turned sideways. At one end, the cavern narrows into a raw, unfinished tunnel face, where drills have stopped their work and abandoned steel ribs vanish into red-brown clay. At the other, a freight lift rises through a square shaft into brightness no one below can see. The lift comes down twice a day with crates, lamp parts, flour, fuel, and people whose faces look briefly peculiar from having been outdoors. It goes up carrying bundled ore, old machinery, and sealed bins of animal feed.

Most of the town has been built against the walls, leaving the middle open.

There are corrugated-metal houses raised on short pilings, brick pump stations, concrete service sheds, a depot with an enamel sign still reading **SOUTH CUT NO. 4**, though there has never been a south cut here that anyone remembers. Pipes run visibly everywhere: along walls, beneath footbridges, through windows, across ceilings blackened by old exhaust. Some pipes carry hot water, some carry air, some carry slurry from the processing rooms. Their purposes are marked in fading paint, but the paint has blistered in the humidity and much of the system is now understood by touch: this one is hot, this one vibrates, this one must not be leaned on.

Electricity is dependable only in the practical sense. There is always some light. It comes from caged bulbs, sodium work lamps, lit office windows, welding flashes at the repair bay. The chamber is dim beyond those pools. Its upper reaches are not visible, only inferred from the occasional silver drops and the broad, hollow sound of machinery. In the center of town, where the open floor has been poured with concrete and scored by tire tracks, the lamps make a yellowish island. Beyond it, the cavern turns violet-gray, then black.

The animals own the darkness, and much of the light besides.

They are not exotic animals, merely animals that have found conditions suited to them so completely that they have become the dominant population. Rats move in thick, purposeful routes under pallets and along warm pipes. Pigeons nest in the upper ironwork, pale with cave dust, flapping from beam to beam whenever a motor starts. There are feral dogs, lean and copper-colored in the lamp glow, sleeping beneath the depot stairs in shifts. Goats occupy the drier ledges near the ventilation works; somebody brought the first pair down years ago, and now they are a herd, picking their way among cable trays and mineral crusts.

But the most numerous creatures are the insects.

Moths gather around every lamp in such numbers that the lamps seem to have grown fur. Roaches live behind the boiler cladding and emerge with the evening shift. Crickets sing from cracks in the masonry, though no one can ever locate one. Beetles, glossy and slow, feed on spilled grain and old wood. Pale cave gnats hover over the drainage channels. Their clouds move with a strange gentleness through the warm air. A person walking home after shift will sometimes pass through one and come out glittered with tiny wings.

The town tolerates them because the town must. There are screens on doorways, traps near kitchens, cats in almost every building, and a municipal notice board with a persistent list of sanitation requests. Yet the animals are too many for any campaign to matter. Their presence has become part of the infrastructure. The dogs warn of strangers. The goats clear the fungus from the warm ledges. Pigeons signal changes in ventilation: when they leave a shaft all at once, the workers know the air has shifted. The insects are simply the price of having light.

Vegetation has made only a few claims. Green exists down here in small, stubborn forms: a beard of moss along the wettest wall, a few fern fronds growing from a crack behind the washhouse, algae slick on the lip of settling tanks. Near the lift shaft, where a little daylight must fall from above, there are planters made from oil drums. Their leaves are dusted gray; their soil is carefully hoarded. Someone grows peppers there, and a single fig tree, bent and sparse, wrapped in heat pipe insulation during maintenance outages. It has not borne fruit in years, but no one removes it.

Nothing in the place feels old enough to be permanent. The chamber may be ancient, but the town is not. Its concrete is freshly patched. Its roads end in piles of aggregate. Its newest buildings are built over the foundations of buildings that failed to dry, failed to hold pressure, failed to survive one bad season of seepage. Walls bulge slightly where damp has entered. Rust grows under every painted surface. A stairwell may be complete except for its final flight, ending in air over a muddy excavation. The settlement continually makes accommodations for itself.

Construction proceeds slowly, almost ceremonially. One crew drills a few meters farther into the raw wall each week. Another lays drainage tile in trenches that fill before lunch. Welders repair guardrails which the goats will rub against until they loosen again. There is always a crane standing still with its hook hanging over the street, and always a wheelbarrow half-full of wet cement, its surface darkening as drops fall from the roof.

The noise is constant but unhurried.

Fans turn in great housings, producing a low industrial breathing that can be felt through floors. Pumps knock and throb below the streets. A generator coughs behind the electrical yard. Tires hiss over wet concrete. Metal doors slam. Dogs bark, pigeons coo, goats complain from ledges, and from the unfinished end of the chamber comes the intermittent sharp chatter of a drill biting stone. All sounds are enlarged by the cavern and returned softened, as if the place is repeating them to itself.

There is no weather, but there are weather-like conditions. Warm mist gathers during the long hours after the pumps are serviced. Condensation worsens when the lift has been running. Some days the air becomes so still that exhaust fumes hang in layers under the lights; on those days, people work more slowly, speaking little. Then the ventilation changes and a wind moves through the streets, carrying smells of hot metal, wet limestone, animal bedding, diesel, and the mineral tang of deep water.

The rules of the place are plain and posted in several languages:

Keep to marked paths.  
Do not enter standing water.  
Do not feed the goats near machinery.  
Report new cracks.  
Never trust a dry ceiling.

The last rule is not official, but everyone knows it. A wet ceiling has announced itself. It is shedding its burden visibly, honestly. A dry ceiling may be holding water in silence.

At the edge of the central square, behind a fence of bent wire mesh, there is a shallow pool where seepage has collected in an old foundation pit. The water is warm and perfectly still except for the occasional drop from above. Mineral films make iridescent skins across its surface. Rats drink there. Gnats hover above it. Sometimes the goats come down, carefully, to look at themselves in the dark water.

Workers pass it without much notice on their way to the depot, lunch hall, control room, or bunks. To them it is just the low place, the old pit, the place where the drains back up after maintenance.

But it gathers the whole subterranean town into one small image: lamps trembling on water, pipework reflected in broken lines, insects scattering and returning, a dog standing at the margin, and overhead a roof of unseen stone, still sweating, still slowly making the world below it.
