The city lies low in a basin that has not finished becoming land.

From a distance it appears to be built on a dark plain, flat as poured metal, with factory stacks, apartment blocks, gantries, tanks, bridges, and immense conveyor towers filling the horizon in every direction. But the plain is not stable. It is a skin: gray-black clay, slag, powdered stone, woven roots, and the pale fibrous material of things that have grown too fast beneath the ground. New ridges rise overnight between warehouses. Cracks open beneath tramlines and expose wet, warm-looking strata though the air is always near freezing. Whole foundations are designed to be moved, jacked up, slid sideways, or abandoned with their utilities still humming.

Nothing here is spacious at ground level. Buildings press shoulder to shoulder in long industrial districts, their walls altered by additions, braces, pipes, stairwells, ventilation trunks, and little enclosed bridges. Apartments occupy the upper stories of mills; chemical works occupy the basements of schools; a greenhouse may be built between the retaining walls of two power stations. There are hardly any alleys, only seams between structures, often roofed over with corrugated sheeting or thick cables. Every usable surface has been claimed: rooftop allotments, rail embankments, crane platforms, pipe racks, switchyards, the sills of broken windows.

Yet the city opens outward in its own peculiar manner. Beyond each packed district lie broad, unfinished fields of substrate, cut across by freight lines and drainage channels. These fields are not empty, exactly. They are occupied by low machinery, scaffold farms, flocks of pale birds, heaps of excavated material, and dense swathes of vegetation. But the eye can travel there. One can see a whole weather system moving across the basin, a long gray smear of rain passing over newly risen earthworks, while yellow work-lights pulse in neighborhoods many kilometers away.

The cold is not clean. It settles in the lungs with the smell of wet iron, leaf mold, lubricating oil, and distant electricity. Rain comes often but seldom for long. More characteristic is condensation: water beading on pipes, threading down the undersides of bridges, collecting in the hollows of machinery. At dawn, every railing is furred with frost except where it sweats. Tiny streams form briefly along curbs and disappear into grates that lead not to sewers but to municipal heat exchangers, root filtration chambers, and old geological probes. In places the ground exhales a faint vapor, and ice forms around the vent in delicate, white-boned fans.

Vegetation is unnaturally determined.

Cold-green reeds crowd the drainage canals, tall enough to hide a person, their blades edged with a waxy blue glaze. Moss fattens on concrete, swallowing faded warning labels and climbing brickwork in dense velvet sheets. Vineyards of black-stemmed climbing plants have been trained over old conveyor frames, their leaves broad and almost translucent, catching what little weak sun the basin receives. Between rail tracks, small forests arise in a single season: narrow trunks, soft bark, canopies of silver-green needles. They are periodically cut down by civic crews, shredded, burned for heat, and then grow again from the disturbed soil.

The city’s parks are not places where nature has been allowed in. They are containment sites. Each is surrounded by a low wall and crossed by observation walkways. Some contain thickets whose roots slowly lift the paving stones outside their boundaries. Others cultivate flowering lichens that feed on exhaust residues. The largest park, in the eastern basin, is called the Nursery though no one agrees on what it is nursing. Its interior is a packed wet woodland around a shallow, black reservoir. The trees lean inward, their branches woven through with old signal wire. When the wind moves them, the whole forest clicks softly, like a room full of cooling machinery.

Animals thrive in the gaps the machinery cannot quite seal.

Long-legged canal birds pick through the gray shallows, their feet bright orange against the silt. Feral dogs wear thick coats of moss-colored fur and sleep beneath warm transformer cabinets. In the rail yards, small white foxes run along the tops of refrigerated freight cars, vanishing among rooftop gardens. The rats are enormous but shy, living mostly in the insulated walls of public buildings and emerging only during pressure changes. There are insects too, despite the cold: slow black beetles in boiler rooms, clouds of tiny gnats rising from vent fields, moths that gather around sodium lamps and leave dust like pale ash on the metal housings.

Most feared are the burrowers.

They are not quite animals, not quite machines, and not publicly discussed. They move beneath the embryonic ground and are blamed for the sudden uplift of streets, the tilting of chimneys, the fresh sinkholes that appear in fenced construction sites. At times, a row of houses will wake angled three degrees to the east, all of their doors sticking, all their cups slid to one side of the cupboards. The city maintenance authority marks these events with orange flags and sends engineers to measure the movement. The engineers install braces, pumps, listening posts, and sometimes a small shrine of reflective tape.

There are rules for living here.

No building is considered permanent until it has survived seven thaw cycles, though true thaws are rare.

No one sleeps below the third floor in the southern wards, where the ground has begun producing shallow pulses every few weeks.

All public pipes must be painted with the direction of their flow, even if the direction changes.

When a street begins to buckle, traffic is rerouted before anyone asks why.

If a new hill appears in a residential district, it belongs first to the city, then to the surveyors, then—after a year without collapse—to whoever can reach its summit without crossing a marked hazard line.

People speak quietly, partly from habit and partly because loud noise is said to confuse the sensitive instruments buried throughout the basin. It is a quiet city despite its industries. There is no constant factory roar. The plants have been insulated, sunk into berms, wrapped in sound-absorbing cladding. Freight trains move with a muted magnetic whine. Cranes turn slowly and nearly silently against the white sky. The loudest things are usually intimate: a valve coughing in an apartment wall, rain ticking on sheet metal, the brief clatter of a dropped tool in a covered lane.

Under this hush, however, the place is always in motion.

A refinery is dismantled while still refining. A block of worker housing is winched ten meters inland because the canal has decided to widen. Fresh foundations are poured around the roots of trees that did not exist last month. Freight arrives carrying crushed stone, steel mesh, prefabricated staircases, seed stock, ceramic conduits, insulated tunnel rings, and thousands of identical yellow boots. Neighborhoods are permanently half-construction site, half-ruin, half-garden. It is common to see a family eating breakfast behind a plastic dust curtain while, on the other side, a hydraulic rig lowers a new support column through their former kitchen.

The light is ordinary in the way that makes everything stranger. There are no crimson skies, no eternal eclipse, no dramatic darkness. The sun rises pale and brief above the basin rim, lays a flat silver brightness across wet roofs and smokestacks, and disappears behind cloud by afternoon. In that light, the city’s materials reveal themselves honestly: rust, concrete, glazed brick, tar, ice, leaf, rubber, glass, soot. Yet sometimes the unfinished ground catches the daylight and seems to shine from within, not brightly, but with the matte luster of a newly opened shell.

At the center of the basin stands the oldest industrial complex, a cluster of towers known as the Original Works. It was built before the land began to move, or perhaps it was built to make it move; records disagree. Its lower levels have vanished into soil, roots, and deposited stone. The upper stacks remain active, though no smoke rises from them. Instead they emit slow threads of cold vapor that drift down rather than up, pooling in streets and courtyards. Workers enter through elevated doors and emerge at irregular hours with mud on their boots and fern spores caught in their hair.

Around the Works, the city is densest. Markets occupy old loading bays. Mechanics repair household appliances beneath hanging gardens. A tram line passes directly through a foundry’s public canteen. Children walk to school in bright insulated coats, carrying survey flags in case the pavement changes under them. There is a municipal museum of vanished neighborhoods, continually expanded, although some of the neighborhoods it memorializes can still be found, half-buried, beyond the western freight loop.

People remain because the city provides work, heat, food, and a kind of rough collective expertise. They know how to read a crack in the wall, how to tell harmless steam from soil breath, how to cultivate root vegetables in mineral-heavy rooftop beds, how to tie a house to a moving street without tearing its water line. They know that the land is young, though nothing else around it is.

And on certain still mornings, when the rain has stopped and the frost has softened into clear droplets, the entire basin seems briefly to hold its breath. The stacked buildings, the quiet rail yards, the reed-filled canals, the towers and pipes and wet green walls all sit under a sheet of thin light. Then somewhere far beneath the city, something turns in its sleep.

A district shivers.

A new ridge begins to rise.
