Beneath a flat and heat-blasted plain, where the ground looks from above like baked clay stitched with dead grass, there is a city no larger than a market district. It is not buried in the sense of having been swallowed. It was built down here deliberately, then built upon, and then built into itself, until the original ceiling disappeared behind pipes, roots, rooms, cables, ducts, cisterns, stairways, and the low continuous clutter of habitation.

It occupies a shallow volcanic pocket: a blister in the stone, opened recently enough that the walls still show their making. The rock is black and red-brown, broken in sharp plates and fresh-looking shelves. In places it seems to have been torn apart yesterday. Pale seams of mineral salts run through it. Heat rises from cracks behind iron grilles. There are no grand caverns here, no monumental darkness; the city is too crowded for that. Every hollow has acquired a purpose. Every ledge supports a cabinet, a shrine, a planter, a compressor, a bed, a water tank, a stack of crates. Doors have been cut wherever a person could stand before them.

The streets are narrow, often only shoulder-width. Most begin as old drainage cuts or cooling fissures, then become corridors as people roof them over with corrugated metal, kiln brick, glass blocks, flattened oil drums, and sheets of insulated cloth. They turn sharply around stone spurs and machinery rooms. A stranger can hear the town before seeing it—not as noise, but as a degree of quietness: the dense, inhabited hush of many people trying not to waste energy or disturb their neighbors.

There is a low electrical hum from the geothermal converters. Water moves somewhere inside the walls, measured and patient. Pipes tick as they expand. A fan turns in a ventilation shaft with the slow rhythm of a tired animal breathing. Far off, a press closes once every few minutes in a workshop, making a soft, padded clang. Then the sound is absorbed by damp fabric, moss, bodies, and stone.

The heat is constant but uneven. At the lower levels it is wet and enveloping, a heat that leaves a thin sheen on the skin and keeps walls slick with condensation. Higher up, near the intake galleries, it becomes dry enough for dust to cling to the corners. The citizens know the temperatures of each passage as closely as they know one another’s names. The warmest corridors are used for drying fruit, firing small ceramic parts, fermenting grain mash, curing rubber seals, and sleeping through the coldest hours of the surface night. The cool niches are reserved for medicines, seed vaults, electrical relays, and the dead.

Water arrives in minor ways. A brief rain above filters down through old boreholes. Condensation beads on heat-exchange pipes. A mineral spring wells from a crack beside the lowest turbine chamber. Nothing flows freely for long. Water is caught in gutters and ceramic troughs, run through charcoal filters, passed among households, used for washing, brewing, irrigation, and finally cooling machinery before it disappears into the deep stone. The city smells faintly of wet iron, green leaves, warm plaster, yeast, and the clean sulfur scent of hot rock.

Vegetation has filled every available mercy of space.

Vines climb cable trays and disappear into vents. Ferns emerge from mortar joints. Broad-leafed edible plants grow in stacked channels beneath dim violet lamps, their leaves glossy with condensation. There are fungus beds in old storage vaults, pale shelves layered over black compost. Moss is encouraged along the coolest walls, where it forms a soft green fur over pipes and crumbling stone. In a few hot courtyards, where cracks in the ceiling admit a diluted grey daylight, there are dwarf trees growing from drums of imported soil. Their trunks are tied to metal frames so their roots will not pry apart the infrastructure.

The gardens are not picturesque. They are dense, technical, and beloved. Each leaf has a function. Each patch of mold is either cultivated, harvested, or removed. Children learn to distinguish edible algae from the kind that ruins a water cistern, medicinal mildew from the kind that gives people fever, root fibers that may be cut from those that hold the whole growing wall together.

There are few animals. The city is too hot, too enclosed, and too carefully occupied for much wildlife. Tiny white crickets live around the boiler drains, almost translucent against the mineral crust. Geckos sometimes appear in the upper ventilation levels, where they hunt moths drawn to the lamps. Every few months, after rain, a pale bird may tumble down an intake shaft and spend several bewildered days in the market canopy before finding its way out again. Such events become local news.

The dominant life is human, and green, and mechanical.

The settlement’s industry is small but intricate. It makes things that the dry towns above need: pump valves, ceramic bearings, insulated wire, heat-resistant seals, glass ampoules, repaired motors, distilled water, preserved greens. The geothermal pocket provides steady power, though never much of it. Heavy machines work slowly. A lathe may run all day to produce a single precise coupling. The foundry fires only on the nights when the power allocation permits. In one narrow chamber, women in leather aprons cast brass fittings under greenish work lamps. In another, a man operates a winding machine that coils copper wire onto armatures, feeding it a little at a time to prevent the old machine from overheating.

Nothing is discarded without a second thought. Old fans become greenhouse circulators. Broken pressure gauges become ornaments or classroom tools. Bent sheet metal becomes roofing. Heat-resistant ceramic fragments are set into walkways, making mosaics that record vanished workshops, old family names, or the locations of pipes beneath the floor.

Because there is so little room, privacy has become an architectural art. People hang curtains of woven fiber across sleeping ledges. Doors are padded with old conveyor belts. Conversations take place close to the ear. A household may occupy three levels of a vertical shaft: cooking below, sleeping halfway up, storage near the ceiling. Bridges cross above one another. Staircases end at roof gardens. There are houses whose only window opens directly onto another house’s kitchen.

Yet the city does not feel frantic. Its density has settled into a slow equilibrium. Everyone waits: for water to condense, for seedlings to root, for parts to cool, for the next surface caravan, for a machine to finish its long task. Time is kept by work cycles rather than daylight. The lamps brighten slightly during the common waking hours and dim toward the periods of rest, but the stone remains twilight-colored. Faces are often lit from below by grow beds or from the side by the blue indicator lights of power cabinets.

There are rules, some painted plainly on walls and some understood without being written.

No open flame near the lower gas channels.  
No drilling without listening first.  
Water belongs to its vessel until the vessel is empty.  
Roots have right of way over wiring unless the wiring feeds the clinic.  
When the pressure bells ring, all doors stay open.  
No one sleeps in the old west gallery.

The last rule is rarely explained. The west gallery was a fresh fracture once, a beautiful clean slash through basalt. It was widened during the founding years, then abandoned after the heat changed direction beneath it. The walls there sweat continuously. The condensation gathers in long, trembling lines and drops into a trench where nothing is allowed to grow. People say the stone occasionally shifts in its sleep. Not an earthquake exactly—more a minute rearrangement, a deep settling that makes nails rise from wood and causes cups to rotate on shelves.

At the town’s center is a plaza scarcely wider than a courtyard. It was once a loading bay. The roof is a patchwork of thick glass, mesh, and old solar panels scavenged from the surface. Dim light comes through it in a dusty green wash, filtered by leaves and the stained underside of rainwater. This is where the market forms, though no one raises their voice. Baskets of mushrooms, bottled condiments, repaired tools, paper packets of seeds, lengths of tubing, hand-bound books, and jars of cooling salve rest on cloths spread over the warm concrete.

In the middle of the plaza stands the oldest condenser tower. It is taller than the surrounding roofs, wrapped in vines, patched with welded plates, and always wet. Drops form along its ribs and collect in a ring of small channels at its base. The tower is not beautiful in the way the people above imagine beauty. It is stained, dented, noisy in hot weather, and covered with warning labels from generations ago. But everyone touches it when passing, at least with two fingers. It is the reason the settlement endured its first year.

Above, the surface sun rages over a mostly empty land.

Below, in the close warmth of new stone, a small city persists in shadow: green, metallic, damp, crowded beyond comfort, and quiet enough to hear water finding its way through the earth.
