The high country begins above the last ordinary weather.

It is not a range, exactly, though there are mountains: new black ridges thrusting up through older ice, their faces too sharp for lichen to have softened, their scree slopes shining with fresh fractures. They rise in crowded ranks, ridge behind ridge, packed so tightly that distance is made of vertical surfaces. From one summit, another summit occupies most of the sky. The valleys are not open valleys but seams—deep, luminous cuts where cold vapor accumulates and green things have found a way to live.

Everything is bright.

The sun here is fierce in the thin air, white rather than warm. It gleams from snowfields, wet basalt, ice membranes spread across shaded pools, and the glazed backs of innumerable animals. The ground often looks metallic from a distance, as though the mountains have been fitted with plates of silver and dark steel. Up close it is only mineral: mica, obsidian, iron-rich stone, salt crusts, and clear ice caught in cracks. Nothing has been built. There are no roads, walls, cables, markers, or ruins. Yet the place possesses the atmosphere of a vast industrial district.

Its industry is alive.

Along the sheltered walls of the clefts grow thick bands of blue-green vegetation, soft in appearance but dense as packed bristles. The plants root directly into young volcanic rock. They are mats, towers, fans, ropes, and dark felted sheets that drink condensation from the air. In the mornings, they swell with rime. By noon, under the hard sun, they exhale streams of pale vapor. Their stems are jointed and hollow; when wind enters them, whole hillsides begin to hum.

The hum is not gentle.

It is a low mechanical sound, a continuous gathered note, like thousands of distant turbines turning beneath the earth. It rises from the vegetation, from the ice fields, from the bodies of the animals packed over every ledge. It deepens at dusk, when cold air descends from the upper snow and moves through the narrow places. Then the mountains seem to be ventilating themselves. Every fissure whistles. Every frozen pool clicks and booms. Far below, where meltwater finds a channel, stones knock together with the steady force of a mill.

There is little free ground.

The slopes are alive with herds so large they seem, at first, to be a change in the color of the rock. Pale, many-legged grazers climb the near-vertical faces using hooked pads that bite into mineral seams. Their coats are short, translucent at the edges, and filled with the faint green of the plants they consume. They move slowly, shoulder to shoulder, so densely that each individual is partly carried by the pressure of the others. When a herd changes direction, the mountain darkens in a moving sheet.

Above them circle enormous thin-winged scavengers, almost invisible until they cross the sun. Their wings are ribbed like leaves. They do not flap often. They ride the updrafts rising from the green clefts and make long, silent spirals until a chorus of smaller creatures begins calling below. Then they descend in numbers, folding out of the glare.

The smaller creatures are everywhere.

They crowd the mossy slopes in bands and knots, burrow under the warm sides of animals, cling to stems, rest in the shallow bowls of ice. Some are feathered, though no feathers here are dry; they shine with frost and oily moisture. Some are soft-bodied and armored with shards of translucent shell. Others look like bundles of roots until they uncoil and crawl. The air between the peaks is occupied by swarms: drifting insects with clear bodies, ballooning spores, fluttering mothlike things that beat their wings so rapidly they make the sound of rain.

Where the sun reaches a depression for long enough, water appears.

Not rivers in the usual sense. Water is too precious and too cold for that. It gathers in bright pockets beneath ice shelves, in dark basins under overhangs, in minute terraces where runoff has frozen and thawed over and over. The pools are crowded with life almost immediately: red threads, black fans, transparent larvae, tiny grazing bodies that leave elaborate tracks in the algae. At night these pools give off a dim blue sheen. The light is biological, but it is so regular, appearing and fading in broad linked patches, that it resembles a network of indicators waking across a factory floor.

The resemblance is strongest in the lower fog forests.

There, the vegetation climbs. It rises from the valley seams as huge pale columns, not trees but colonies of fused stalks, each one made from countless hollow tubes wrapped together by frost-resistant vines. The columns grow only where vapor persists. Their upper surfaces catch clouds and comb moisture from them. Droplets run down their fluted sides in silver streams. Within them, pressure changes make a constant knocking—slow, rhythmic, irregularly synchronized across miles.

The sound is like pistons.

When the columns release seed, they do so all at once. Vast portions of the forest open, and clouds of shining filaments pour upward. The filaments are caught by high winds and travel from mountain to mountain. Nothing visibly changes quickly afterward. The seeds settle in cracks; the new rock remains bare for years, decades, perhaps centuries. Then one summer of unusual condensation may darken an entire ridge with fresh growth.

This is a slow landscape despite its noise.

The animals migrate at the pace of weather systems. A herd may take a year to cross a valley only ten miles wide, feeding in a broad advancing front and leaving the vegetation cropped into precise bands. Ice advances by inches, then withdraws by inches. The young mountains continue to rise, but so gradually that their violence can only be inferred from the sharpness of their peaks and the warmth sometimes felt through the soles of one’s feet.

There are rules to the place, though none are written.

Nothing is alone for long. Bare stone is occupied by bacteria, then by crust, then by grazing mouths. Still water is clouded by living things. A dead animal becomes a mound of feeders before it has finished steaming in the cold. The sky itself is thick with wings, spores, vapor, and drifting down. Empty space is treated as an opportunity.

Nothing wastes heat. Bodies press together. Plants form dense carpets. Birds nest in the insulated hollows of larger animals’ discarded coats. Even the mountains hold warmth in their black volcanic interiors and release it into the cracks where green life gathers.

Nothing moves without announcing itself. The thin atmosphere carries sound unnaturally far: the clatter of horned feet on ice, the crack of calving seracs, the droning vegetation, the wet exhalations of great herds. At intervals, from somewhere beyond sight, comes a deep pulsing roar. It might be wind passing through a system of caves. It might be some immense animal calling beneath the glacier. The sound is repeated by the cliffs until direction becomes meaningless.

And nothing here has been made, though everything seems designed for work.

The ice separates mineral from water. The vegetation harvests cloud. The grazing creatures grind stone-soft plants into fertilizer. Scavengers distribute bones and spores between ridges. Hot vents beneath the basalt feed valleys that would otherwise freeze sterile. Each living form is joined to another so tightly that the whole high continent behaves like a single patient engine: bright under the sun, loud in the cold, crowded beyond comfort, endlessly processing rock, vapor, flesh, and light.

From far away it might look vacant: a white and black wilderness under a cruelly clear sky.

From within, there is no wilderness at all. There is only occupancy. Every ledge is taken. Every crack breathes. Every shining slope is working.
