At the high end of a valley too narrow to have a horizon, there is a town built around a thaw that never completes.

The place is called the White Basin by people who come through it, though no one who lives there gives it a name. It is a small place in the way a clenched hand is small: perhaps a few hundred paces from one wall of ice-dark rock to the other, and yet packed with ledges, footbridges, animal pens, water stairs, stacked roofs, shrines, storehouses, drying racks, and chimneys that have been built not upward but sideways into the mountain.

All day it is bright.

The sun does not warm the basin much. It hangs above the southern rim with the hard, glassy clarity of a lamp seen through frozen water. Snow dazzles even where it is old and gray. Every wet surface throws light back: black slate, glazed timber, the pale hide-roofs of the houses, the backs of animals, the thousand shallow channels that carry meltwater between buildings. A person can squint until night and still see the blue lines of veins beneath their own eyelids.

There are no true streets. There are watercourses, and along their margins people have placed their doors.

The mountain leaks everywhere. Water wells from seams in the cliffs, sweats through stone, drips from overhangs in long synchronized threads. It issues hot in some places and so cold in others that it freezes at its own edges. The central thaw is a black pond not much wider than a house, perpetually breathing steam. Its surface is almost never still. Not because of wind—wind cannot settle in the basin, but rushes down from the high passes in sudden white bursts—but because the water is rising, falling, opening, swallowing.

Every few hours, the pond gives a deep, soundless convulsion. Its skin wrinkles. The bridges spanning it lift slightly on their rawhide lashings. Water climbs the stone steps around it and enters the lower rooms. Nobody cries out. The inhabitants have made the first floors of their houses for this purpose: stone chambers with drains, sacrificial shelves, and smooth walls where the flood can pass. Children wear sealskin boots tied high above the knee. Pots, nets, sleeping furs, tools, and infants are stored on pulley shelves beneath the rafters.

Then the water retreats, bearing away whatever was not tied down.

This is not disaster here. This is the town’s clock.

The buildings are close enough that their eaves overlap. They have been made from everything that holds: slabs peeled from the valley walls, driftwood brought down from some unseen forest below, whale-bone beams traded over the pass, turf, hide, mud, woven reed mats, old ribs of boats. Most are half-buried, their roofs white with hard snow and their entrances marked by dark lintels polished by generations of shoulders. A house may open into another house. A ladder may lead from a kitchen to a goat enclosure, from the goat enclosure to a roof, from the roof to a shrine, and from the shrine to a narrow plank laid across a cleft where water murmurs beneath ice.

Metal is scarce enough to be inherited by name. Knives are sharpened until their blades are crescent-thin. Most work is done with bone wedges, stone mauls, horn needles, flint scrapers, and wooden handles repaired so many times that no original wood remains. The town’s most valued tool is a long bronze awl, green at its edges, kept in a covered niche beside the pond. It is used only to puncture the ice when the basin has decided to close itself too tightly.

The people do not own land. In the White Basin, ground changes too quickly for ownership. They own heights: the third shelf above the pond, the dry beam above a particular doorway, the west-facing ledge where meat will not freeze too hard. A family’s wealth is measured in ropes, poles, waterproof skins, and remembered routes through floodwater.

Vegetation has little purchase. There are bent cushions of moss in the rock cracks, their green so vivid they appear almost lit from within. Brown sedges bristle along the warmer streams. At the edges of the basin grow a few dwarf willows, twisted and flattened by weather, their branches no taller than a seated person. They are old beyond reason. Their roots cling inside cracks of stone and drink constantly. In the brief season when they leaf, the town gathers around them to look at the soft green as if watching a miracle unfold slowly enough to be trusted.

But the basin is full of life.

Birds occupy every ledge: white-throated cliff swifts, black crows with blue ice-bright eyes, small round ptarmigan that vanish against snow until they move. They nest in the walls of houses, under eaves, within unused chimneys. Their wings make almost no noise. When they lift all at once, it is like a pattern of ash being shaken through light.

There are goats everywhere, pale and shaggy, their hooves clicking delicately over planks and wet stone. They sleep on roofs, crowd in passageways, and stand with absurd composure on ledges too narrow for a human foot. Their bells have been removed generations ago. The people say the basin dislikes repeated sounds. A bell rung twice in the same place can draw water upward through the cracks.

Below the bridges, in the dark channels, live blind trout with transparent fins. In the heated runoff near the central pond gather red-backed salamanders, even in the deepest winter. They lie in dense writhing knots beneath the clear water, bright as coals. The children catch them gently in woven scoops and release them into stone basins in their homes, because a salamander kept beside the hearth is believed to take the fever out of a child.

Larger things move above the town.

On bright days, mountain deer come down from the ridge in dozens, stepping through the upper ruins and drinking from the mineral springs. Their antlers are furry with rime. Sometimes an ice bear appears—not a bear of snow, but a massive, gray-furred animal with a blunt head and a long, pale muzzle. It comes to lick salt from the old retaining walls. The town leaves it offerings of fish and never approaches. It may stand for an entire day among the roofs while life continues around it, steaming pots carried past its legs, goats squeezing beneath its belly, children watching from doorways.

No one knows whether the bear is one creature or many.

Despite the abundance of movement, the basin is quiet. Water should be loud there, but it is muffled by snow, moss, hide roofs, ice, and the peculiar density of the air. The many channels whisper under their crusts. The animals breathe. Feet tread carefully. Tools strike wood with wrapped heads. People speak close to one another, almost mouth-to-ear. A conversation from across a room looks animated but cannot be heard.

There are louder moments, though none are expected.

A crack will pass through the valley wall like a line of blue fire. A shelf of ancient snow will shear loose from somewhere impossibly high and spill down in a white, soundless cataract, only to arrive seconds later as a heavy concussion that shudders crockery on the shelves. Sometimes the frozen cliff-face calves a slab into the basin. The slab breaks apart among the upper structures, and by evening its pieces have been made into foundations, walls, steps, and blocks for cooling food.

The town responds to change with practiced speed. At the first sign of pressure in the water, every person becomes busy. Poles are lifted. Roof paths are unrolled. Goats are driven upward. Children are assigned to knots and baskets. Doors are dismantled and carried to safer thresholds. The calm of the place is not peace; it is concentration.

In the middle of the pond stands a stone pillar, perhaps natural, perhaps not. It rises only a little above the water. Its top is flat and always bare of snow, even in blizzard weather. It has grooves along its sides, too regular to be caused by water. The inhabitants bind strips of cloth around it during the highest floods: white for a mild rise, red for a dangerous one, black when somebody has been taken.

No one goes out to place the cloth.

The strips appear there after dark, wet and knotted.

Visitors are told this before they sleep, if they are allowed to sleep in the basin at all. They are also told not to count the houses. There are fewer in the morning, more after storms, and sometimes a familiar doorway opens onto a room nobody remembers building. This is said without ceremony, as one might warn a stranger that a certain bridge is slippery.

In the brightest part of the day, when steam rises from the black pond and the upper ice shines nearly blue-white, the White Basin can seem almost cheerful. Smoke threads upward from clustered roofs. Fish dry on racks. Hands turn stone at grinding bowls. Women scrape hides beside open doorways. Men weave reed screens for the spring channels. Children slide on flattened bones over ice. Goats move across the roofs in patient files. Birds nest in the walls. Water runs through everything, under everything, waiting.

The town endures because it does not try to become stable.

It has learned the shape of survival here: lash, lift, drain, mend, remember. Keep close. Keep quiet. Build lightly on what moves.
