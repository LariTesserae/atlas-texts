The land is low and newly lifted from some older, submerged country. It has not yet learned stillness.

It spreads as a broad black-green basin between shallow ridges of raw stone, their faces sharp as broken pottery and still bare in places where the soil has not had time to cling. Nothing here is old enough to be weathered gently. The ground is a confusion of fresh seams, buckled shelves, narrow crevices breathing warm vapor. In daylight—if daylight ever truly reaches the floor of the basin—it must be a crowded place. But now the world is held in a near-continuous dark: not a clean night, but a wet, living darkness, thick with leaves, wings, steam, and motion.

The canopy is so dense it functions as a roof. Huge broad leaves overlap above in layers, catching the faint light that filters through cloud and trapping it as a greenish glow on their undersides. Vines thread from crown to crown in ropes and curtains. Some are fine as hair and trembling with tiny climbing things; others are thick, ridged, and muscular, bearing pale flowers that open only after dark. Ferns, cycads, young palms, and thorny shrubs are packed together below, all glossy with condensation. The vegetation has arrived fast, as if seeds had been waiting underground for the moment the land emerged.

Water is never far away, though there is no great river.

It collects in leaf-cups, seeps through black soil, gathers in brief silver plates on the clay, then vanishes into cracks. Rain comes in short, violent visits: a drumming assault on the canopy, a sudden flood of cold drops and snapped twigs, then a steaming hush that lasts no longer than a held breath. Every low hollow becomes a temporary pond. Every pond becomes a nursery. By the next rain many have changed shape, been covered by vegetation, or drained into the earth through some new fissure.

The ground is soft in some places and treacherously hard in others. Fresh volcanic stone breaks through the root mat in dark teeth. Pale mineral crusts gleam along the edges of seeps. In the deeper cuts, the land exposes its unfinished layers: red clay, ash-gray grit, black porous rock, a band of shells from an ocean that is no longer here. Warm mist rises from the cracks at night. It smells of iron, wet bark, bruised fruit, and the sour, mineral scent of rainwater lying too long in stone.

There are animals everywhere.

Not merely present—everywhere in the fullest, most exhausting sense. The air is filled with wings. Moths as broad as hands batter softly through the vines, their powder catching faint light. Beetles click beneath bark plates. Small luminous flies drift in clusters, blinking not in patterns but in sudden, collective decisions, extinguishing one side of a grove and igniting another. Birds move through the darkness by sound more than sight: harsh-throated night herons, tiny screaming canopy runners, heavy fruit-eaters that land with a crash and immediately begin arguing.

On the ground, the leaf litter seems to stir even when no wind passes. There are long-backed amphibians with transparent skin, burrowing in wet ash; scaled lizards slipping over exposed roots; blind, pale crabs inhabiting the warm mineral channels; rodents with membranous ears pressed flat against their heads. Larger things are suggested rather than seen. A branch bends low and does not rise again. A repeated exhalation comes from behind a wall of giant ferns. Sometimes, in the brief green glow after lightning, a herd of low, many-legged browsers can be glimpsed moving through the undergrowth, their backs armored in plates of leaf-shaped shell.

The sound is immense.

It begins with insects, but that is only the surface. There is a continuous high metallic whine, dense enough to feel in the teeth. Beneath it are the throaty pulses of frogs calling from temporary pools, the clack and chatter of shell-creatures in the roots, the shrieks of birds, the wing-thunder of startled roosts. Distantly, the low ridges crack as they cool or settle. Steam hisses from hidden vents. Rain drums, stops, and is replaced by water falling from leaf to leaf for long minutes afterward.

No creature sings alone for long. Calls are answered, interrupted, copied, swallowed. The entire basin seems to be conducting an argument in darkness.

There are rules to living here, though none are written and none belong to people.

Still water is dangerous because nothing remains still without a reason. Brightness is dangerous because it attracts too much attention. A clear path is dangerous because too many things use it. The safest places are often the narrowest: under root arches, inside the hollow centers of fallen trunks, among thorn tangles where larger bodies cannot follow. Every living thing seeks warmth, moisture, and cover; therefore the warmest, wettest, most sheltered places are permanently occupied.

The plants participate in the traffic of the place. Some blossoms close around insects only briefly, borrowing their heat before releasing them. Certain vines contract after rain, pulling themselves tighter across gaps and altering the routes through the canopy. There are trees with hollow roots that fill with rainwater and hum faintly when the wind crosses their openings. Their hum is not music, exactly, but animals gather near it. Whether they find comfort there or merely become unable to leave is uncertain.

Nothing has been built. No trail is maintained. No firepit, fence, blade-mark, or cut branch interrupts the vegetation’s insistence. Yet the landscape has a primitive industry of its own. Beetles harvest fungus from the undersides of leaves. Ants herd smaller insects through tunnels of woven grass. Birds use hooked thorns as larders. A kind of crab stacks wet stones near its burrow, perhaps to mark territory, perhaps to channel condensation, perhaps for reasons belonging entirely to crabs. Everywhere, living things shape one another with mouth, claw, root, and repetition.

The basin continues beyond the immediate jungle in glimpses: a wide region of low, steaming folds, reed-choked flats, fresh black ridges, and distant stands of tall trees leaning under storm winds. Clouds drag their bellies over the land. Lightning occasionally reveals the whole geography at once—the new rock, the tangled forest, the dark pools shining like eyes—then takes it away again.

In that instant, the place appears young.

Not innocent. Not empty. Young in the sense that it is still deciding what it is allowed to become. The earth is cracking open, roots are entering those cracks, water is testing routes, animals are arriving in numbers too great to count. Each night something collapses, molts, hatches, flowers, floods, or claims a territory.

By morning, if morning comes, the basin will be almost the same.

Almost.
