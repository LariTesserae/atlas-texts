The place is called the Low Yard, though it has no walls in the ordinary sense and no sky that can be trusted. It is a small country of construction: perhaps no more than a morning’s walk across, contained beneath a sagging roof of overlapping clay slabs, wooden ribs, and old sailcloth darkened by years of vapor. Everything in it has been made by hand. The ground, the banks, the pools, the perches, the little bridges, even the apparent stones—each is fired clay, packed ash, woven reed, carved wood, or mortar made from crushed shell and lime.

There is no earth beneath the paving.

The floor is a basin of broad terracotta plates, their joins black with wetness. Some have sunk slightly over the years, making shallow channels that guide water toward a central sump. Water is always traveling here, though rarely with any urgency: slipping under doors, shining in grooves, falling in threads from the roof, gathering at the feet of low walls and then being lifted again in little hand-pumped wheels. The air is cool and wet enough that droplets collect on every overhang. Moss would thrive if it were permitted, but the Yard’s keepers scrape it away whenever it spreads too far. They leave only thin gray-green films on shaded surfaces, and a few deliberately tended patches of reed-root in ceramic troughs.

At first the Low Yard seems empty. A visitor sees long open stretches of tile, a few squat platforms, ladders leading nowhere visible, and a dark pool under the roof’s lowest fold. There are no crowds, no market stalls, no thick vegetation to soften the space. The architecture gives each object too much room around it. A single woven gate might stand forty paces from the next wall. A waterwheel turns alone in a recess. A row of clay jars waits beneath a drip line, each receiving one slow, regular drop.

Then the animals begin to show themselves.

They occupy the construction the way fish occupy a reef.

There are pale, long-legged marsh hens nesting in the open mouths of drainage pipes; they move in groups, their claws tapping sharply on the fired tiles. Small black pigs root through baskets of wet reed pulp, though there is no soil in which to root, only the instinctive labor of their snouts. Slender lizards with wet, shining backs lie under warmed roof-slabs when the dim light finds them. In the water channels, blind silver fish flicker between clay grates. They are fed scraps from the Yard’s kitchens and reproduce in abundance, becoming food for birds, eels, people, and one another.

The loudest residents are the frogs.

They live in every place water can pause: in cracked cistern lips, under raised walkways, in the throats of unused pipes, within hollow roof beams where rainwater has collected for generations. Their calls turn the enclosed space into an instrument. Some make a blunt wooden knock; some whistle in descending pairs; some produce a hoarse, sawlike rasp that seems to come from the walls themselves. When the rains begin above the roof, and the roof begins to leak in a hundred locations, the frogs answer the weather until ordinary conversation is impossible.

There are other sounds beneath them: water clicking through wheel paddles; ropes rubbing on damp pulleys; birds fighting over fish scales; the hollow drumming of someone repairing a tile from above. At intervals, a bell is struck—not cast metal, but a great fired bowl suspended from a timber frame. Its note is deep, blunt, and short-lived. It tells the inhabitants that a channel has blocked, a roof panel has shifted, a predator has entered the Yard, or the water in the central sump has reached the red line.

Predators do enter.

The Low Yard has been built as refuge and trap, and its ecology is old enough to understand the distinction. Otters come through culverts during flood weather, sleek and curious, and raid the fish channels. Feral cats move along the roof-ribs, hunting the marsh hens. A species of white, wingless heron lives in the deeper chambers where the roof descends nearly to the floor; these birds spear frogs with slow, ceremonial patience. The people do not remove them. They say the herons keep the water honest.

No one entirely agrees on what that means.

The people of the Yard use simple tools: bone awls, stone hammers, copper knives gone green at the edges, woven traps, wooden shovels, clay scoops. Nothing is smooth unless it has been polished by hands. Everything carries repair marks. A cracked basin is stitched with wire. A rotten post is sistered with a younger length of wood. A broken tile becomes a wedge, a scraper, a weight for a net, a marker at the edge of a channel. The place has lasted because almost nothing is thrown away, and because every element is allowed to become something else when its first purpose fails.

This continuity has made the Yard strange.

The oldest walls are not straight. They have accepted the pressure of water and shifted. Their surfaces are layered with generations of patches, so that a low bank may contain tiles stamped with symbols no living person can read, reed matting fossilized in lime, shells from a coast that may be far away or may no longer exist, and the impressions of small animal feet caught in mortar before it dried. Some of the floor plates are warm to the touch in winter, though there is no furnace beneath them. Some remain dry even during the heaviest rain. No one digs them up. It is a rule.

There are many rules, most of them practical until they are not.

Do not sleep beside a covered drain.

Do not remove a frog from the water without replacing it with something living.

Do not mend a roof during the hour when the herons stand facing east.

Never sweep a channel clean; leave a little silt for the fish.

If water begins running uphill, close your eyes and follow the sound of the bell.

The last rule is seldom needed, but everyone knows it.

The Low Yard is low not merely in elevation but in disposition. It receives what comes down: rain, runoff, dead leaves blown in through roof gaps, lost birds, broken timbers, silt, drifting insects, stories from the higher settlements. Its builders made it to gather and distribute water, perhaps at first for a larger place around it. But whatever city, village, or estate once depended upon it has diminished. The Yard remains. It has become self-sufficient in the way of old machines and marshes: not independent, exactly, but deeply practiced in surviving interruption.

The dimness never quite becomes darkness. Thin gray light filters through wet sailcloth overhead. At noon, the roof glows like the underside of a cloud. At evening, oil lamps appear in niches along the walkways, their flames reflected in every puddle until the floor seems scattered with small, trembling stars. Steam rises from the central sump, though its water is cold. In that steam, the roof beams vanish and return. Frogs call from invisible ledges. Wings beat somewhere high above.

Standing in the open center, one might feel enclosed by a whole weather system built from hand-sized decisions: one tile placed to catch a drip, one trough shaped to hold a reed, one gap left beneath a door for fish to pass during floods. Nothing in the Low Yard is natural, yet nothing feels entirely controlled. It has been made too long, repaired too often, and inhabited by too many creatures to belong wholly to its makers.

It is a constructed marsh with no soil. A little world of clay, rope, rainwater, and animal noise, held together by labor and habit beneath a leaking roof.
