The place is called a basin only because no smaller word will do. It lies beneath an abandoned freight yard, below the poured concrete and rusted rail switches, in a hollow revealed when the ground split around an old drainage conduit. From above it is no more than a black seam behind a chain-link fence. A person could descend into it by ladder. They would soon lose the sense that they had gone down at all.

It is a dry country the size of a warehouse room.

Its floor is packed mineral dust, pale gray and very old, pressed into fine polygonal plates by cycles of heat that no longer occur. There is no standing water, no dampness in the cracks, no cool vapor at dawn. Every surface has the blunt, matted look of something long deprived of rain. The walls are sedimentary stone, but they have been altered by industry: old conduits disappear into them, ceramic insulators protrude like fossil shells, and lengths of collapsed ductwork cross the ceiling at odd angles. A few lamps remain on, supplied by some buried electrical system that has outlived its owners. Their light is ordinary, yellow-white, unromantic. It reveals everything, including the dust suspended in the air.

The basin is low and close. The ceiling presses down in places, rising only in a central chamber where a broken ventilation shaft opens into the freight yard far above. Daylight comes through the grille in a narrow, shifting rectangle. The shaft does not bring rain. It brings cold air, soot, distant train vibration, and occasional scraps of paper that spiral down and are immediately taken apart.

There are no plants. Not even the stubborn green scum expected around a pipe joint. Life here has another foundation.

The animals eat mineral.

Not exactly the way cattle eat grass, nor the way insects chew wood. They work the dust, the oxidation on iron, the salts crystallized along old concrete, the fine metallic residues trapped in the basin’s layered floor. The smallest are pinhead-sized creatures with six polished legs and blunt, rasping mouths. In great masses they move over a wall or pipe, leaving behind clean tracks of lighter stone. Their bodies are brown, pearl-gray, or the blue-black of oily steel. They do not appear to breathe. When disturbed, they close themselves into hard pellets and tumble away.

Larger animals have learned to feed on the smaller ones.

They crowd every ledge, fissure, and overturned machine housing. There are pale runners with jointed tails, each no longer than a finger, chasing the mineral grazers through the dust. There are flattened, crablike things that cling to vertical surfaces with magnetic pads, waiting beside electrical junction boxes for prey drawn to warmth. There are sleek burrowers that move beneath the dry crust, their passage announced by a trembling line in the dust and a quick eruption of bodies. Their hides are ribbed and dark, as though made of folded rubber. Their eyes are reduced to silver spots. They hunt by vibration.

Nothing in the basin is alone for long.

The animals have filled every available distance. A hand placed on a wall would soon have several kinds of life crossing it: hard-shelled grains, thread-legged hunters, a slow, warm-bodied feeder nosing at a rust bloom. Beneath the hand, inside hairline cracks, smaller organisms are already shifting. The density is not merely visual. It is physical. The air seems busy. The dust seems to have intentions.

Their noises combine into a constant mechanical weather.

There is the dry tick of shells touching stone. The small electric crackle made when some creatures discharge static from their limbs. A continuous rasping from the grazing swarms. Deeper down come dull impacts, as if somebody is dropping tools in a neighboring room: the burrowers striking the hardened floor from below. Every few minutes the entire basin trembles as freight passes overhead. At those moments, all the animals become still except for the ones built to exploit vibration. Those rise from cracks in sudden numbers, combing the floor with delicate sensory limbs.

The old industrial works have become landmarks in their world.

A corroded compressor sits half-sunk near the center, its flywheel fixed in place by mineral crust. The animals nest within its ribs. A vertical panel of cracked switches is occupied by a colony of heat-loving grazers, each one the shape of a tiny shield. They huddle over the low current leaking through damaged wires. Their accumulated bodies hold the warmth, and predators gather around the panel in rings. An overturned steel locker has become a seasonal den for the largest creatures in the basin: blunt-headed, many-legged scavengers with white armored backs. They emerge in the coldest hours and collect the dead, the shed skins, the dry cocoons left by exhausted swarms.

The basin’s ecology is powered by decay, but not a dramatic kind. It is the patience of old metal becoming powder. It is electricity leaking into the wrong places. It is pressure from above, vibration from trains, warmth stored in machinery, carbon dust brought down by air currents. Every source is small. Every source is used completely.

The animals obey a few severe rules.

They cannot cross polished copper. A length of old cable, stripped and bright along one side, makes a boundary more absolute than a wall. At the center of the chamber, a broad copper bus bar lies exposed across the floor, dividing the basin into two territories. No creature touches it. Swarms collect along either edge and turn back in dense, synchronized waves. Predators wait at the crossings where dust has finally covered a corner.

They follow heat, but too much heat drives them into frenzy. Once a week, perhaps, an electrical relay somewhere in the wall closes with a loud, clean clack. The lamps brighten. A coil within the old compressor gives off a faint red glow. Then every creature in the basin moves at once. Grazers pour toward the warmth in pale sheets. Hunters descend from conduits. The burrowers break the floor open in winding ridges. For several minutes the small underground country becomes a riot of clicking legs, snapping bodies, and dust.

Afterward, it settles.

Dust drifts back into the cracks. The trains continue above. The lamps resume their weak, ordinary illumination. The old machinery returns to its long unfinished failure.

At the far end of the basin is a low opening, barely wide enough for a person to crawl through. Beyond it lies another chamber, older and colder, where the stone floor has been worn almost smooth by generations of mineral feeders. There are scattered objects there: a child’s shoe turned gray with dust, a fractured warning sign, a wrench without rust, a row of ceramic jars welded shut by age. The fauna avoids this room except during vibration storms.

No one knows why.

Perhaps the floor carries a current too faint for human instruments. Perhaps something in the jars has altered the mineral chemistry. Perhaps the animals simply recognize a place where too many of their kind vanish.

From the freight yard, none of this is visible. A train can idle above the seam for an hour, diesel rumbling through the concrete, while under its wheels an entire dry civilization hunts, feeds, molts, dies, and is eaten beneath a rectangle of falling daylight.
