The town lies in a shallow cut beneath the earth, not a cave exactly, though people from the uplands call it one. It is a long basin of exposed clay and pale stone, recently opened by a collapse in the old hillside. The ceiling is gone over most of it. Daylight reaches down cleanly, a cool gray-white light that shifts with the weather and is often crossed by the silhouettes of crows from the surface. At either end, though, the basin narrows into dark passages where the original ground still presses overhead.

Nothing here has had time to become rounded.

The exposed walls are raw: layers of blue marl, wet brown soil, broken chalk, seams of gravel that glitter after rain. Roots poke from the upper edges where the surface turf was sheared away, dangling in dense mats before drying and snapping off. Every fresh shower changes the walls. Small runnels appear, tracing the same paths for a day or two, then vanishing beneath new slides of silt. The inhabitants mark the unstable places with forked willow stakes and lines of lime. They do not speak of erosion, because the land has not yet begun to erode in any familiar, settled sense. It is still deciding what shape it will take.

The town has been built quickly in the basin because flat land is scarce beyond it and shelter is valuable. Its buildings are packed shoulder to shoulder against the slopes, each one braced against the next. Timber frames lean into walls of cut sod and clay. Roofs are made from split planks, woven reed mats, scavenged slate, and heavy skins of turf. There are no broad streets. There are lanes, gutters, stairways, passages beneath overhanging rooms, and narrow shelves hacked into the wall. From above, the settlement resembles a collection of crates wedged in a trench.

The central way follows the lowest line of the basin. It is not paved. It is packed dirt, continuously repaired with crushed stone, charcoal grit, and broken pottery. A ditch runs along one side. Most days it holds only a black ribbon of water, fed by condensation from the shaded walls and by seepage from somewhere deeper in the hill. When rain comes, the ditch thickens rapidly. Children and older people leave their work to set boards across low doorways, clear blockages, and guide the water toward the settling ponds beyond the eastern gate.

Water is never abundant enough to ignore, but it is present everywhere in small ways. It beads on iron nails and the rims of clay jars. It darkens the underside of bridges. It sweats from stone in the inner passages and gathers overnight in basins placed beneath the dripping points. Laundry rarely becomes fully dry. Wool smells faintly of smoke and damp earth. Bread cools with a soft crust. In the deepest houses, the air has the mineral taste of a well.

Vegetation is sparse, yet fiercely used. A few small willow trees have been planted at the wet end of the basin, their roots held in cages of stone to keep them from disturbing the embankment. Moss grows in the shaded cracks where roofs meet walls. There are gardens, but they are not gardens of green abundance: narrow beds of onion, cabbage, bitter herbs, and beans; trays of sprouts on windowsills; mushrooms raised in old storage chambers. Along the upper rim, tough grass remains in torn strips, and a few thorn bushes cling to the soil. In late spring, these are briefly dusted with tiny white flowers before the wind strips them bare.

There are animals, though not many. A pair of goats may be tethered in a yard scarcely larger than a room. Chickens move through the alleys with the deliberate confidence of things that know everyone must step around them. Rats exist but are treated as a civic enemy, and every household keeps traps. Cats are prized and thin. Beyond the eastern ponds, where the basin opens toward a low, scrubby valley, frogs can sometimes be heard after rain. Their voices are strange only because the town itself is so quiet.

Quiet is one of the town’s materials.

There are no carts rolling constantly over stone, no market cries rising from wide squares, no metalworks hammering through the day. The people work with simple tools: adzes, stone mauls, wooden shovels shod with iron, wedges, mallets, hand drills, woven baskets. A smith has a small charcoal forge near the western passage, but even that is used sparingly. Its bellows exhale in slow, measured breaths. The sound of the settlement is mostly close sound: a knife scraping a plank, a spoon against a pot, feet in damp soil, a baby coughing behind a wall, the soft crumbling fall of earth from a newly cut bank.

At midday, when work pauses, the basin holds conversations without carrying them far. The dense construction breaks every voice into fragments. A person standing by the common oven may hear a laugh from three houses away and never know who made it. Smoke rises but catches beneath low rooflines and drifts through the lanes before finding the open sky. It smells of peat, damp wood, boiled grain, goat fat, clay, and the faint sharpness of lime.

The people are builders because they have had to become builders. The collapse that made the basin is recent enough that many remember the first sight of it: a portion of hillside giving way after a long wet season, revealing a hollowed seam of workable clay and stone. At first there were only prospectors and shelter-seekers, then families, then workshops, then storage pits, then rules.

The rules are written nowhere, but everyone knows them.

No one cuts into the wall without telling the nearest three households.

No fire is left burning in an unlined room.

Nothing heavy is stored beneath a cracked beam.

The ditch must remain clear.

When the warning clapper sounds, every able person takes a shovel, basket, or hammer and goes to the marked place.

The clapper hangs near the central way: a slab of hardwood struck with an antler mallet. It makes a low, dry note, not loud but unmistakable. It is used for wall slips, sudden flooding, and the rare discovery of a new opening in the earth. These discoveries are treated with care. A fresh fissure may offer a store chamber, a source of water, or a route through the hill. It may also swallow a room.

Every part of the place is under construction, even the parts that appear finished. New retaining walls are always rising in front of older ones. Doors are moved as paths shift. Houses acquire braces, then lean-tos, then upper sleeping platforms. A lane that was wide enough for two people yesterday may have been narrowed by stacked bricks today. The settlement grows less by spreading outward than by thickening: building over, under, against, and through itself.

In the cooler months, mist settles along the basin floor before dawn. It fills the central way to knee height and turns lamps into pale coins. The upper roofs emerge first, then chimney mouths, then the narrow walkways. For a little while the town seems divided into islands. People moving to fetch water or tend fires appear only from the waist up, their lower bodies erased by vapor.

By midmorning the mist is usually gone. Light enters the open basin and shows the whole place plainly: the newly cut walls, the stacked homes, the thin gardens, the tracks of chickens, the wet gleam in the ditch. Beyond the rim, there is only a modest countryside of rough grass, low fields, and gray sky. But down here, beneath the original ground level, the town has made a complete world out of compression.

It is not old enough to have ruins. It has only mistakes that have already been built around. A blocked passage becomes a pantry. A flood scar becomes the line where houses raise their thresholds. A fallen wall becomes the foundation for another wall. The earth is raw, the timber fresh-cut, the roads unfinished, and still the place feels inhabited in every available inch.

At evening, lamps appear one by one behind oiled cloth and horn panes. Their light is ordinary and warm. It touches the damp walls, the smoke-black rafters, the faces bent over bowls and tools. Above the basin, the sky remains visible, narrow but real, carrying clouds eastward over the torn edge of the hill.

The town listens to the weather through the soil. It listens to drip, creak, settling stone, and the occasional far-off thud of something changing deeper in the ground. Then it sleeps closely packed beneath the exposed earth, waiting for morning and whatever new shape the hillside has chosen overnight.
