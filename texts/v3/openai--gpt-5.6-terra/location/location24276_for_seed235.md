The city is built on the inside face of a mountain that has forgotten it is a mountain.

From a distance, across the lower cloudlands, it appears as a dark vertical continent rising out of heat-haze: terraces, walls, rooflines, stairways, aqueducts, balconies, all pressed into a green-black escarpment thousands of meters high. But no one made it in any recent sense. The structures are too old for intention, too intergrown for abandonment. Their blocks are not cut stone exactly. They are slabs of volcanic glass, pale porous limestone, trunks flattened into beams, strata of clay hardened under impossible pressure. Every house seems to have been grown from the slope and then, over ages, persuaded into the shape of a house.

The place is called a city because there are streets.

There are avenues broad enough for crowds, though no crowds pass through them. There are plazas under hanging gardens, market halls with empty niches, gate towers, canals, stairs that divide and divide again up the mountain. There are thousands of doorways: round doors opening into square rooms, square doors opening onto bare cliff, doorways that contain a wall of water held perfectly upright. Nothing has been left behind. No tools, no carts, no cloth, no bones. Yet the city has the concentrated geometry of habitation. It expects inhabitants with such patience that their absence has become one of its building materials.

At this altitude the air should be thin and cold. Instead it is hot.

The heat rises from the stone in great slow exhalations. It radiates from tiled courtyards, from black cisterns, from cracks along the paving where red mineral damp gleams. The high terraces steam continuously. Warm rain does not fall here so much as arrive already everywhere: condensing on railings, gathering in the folded leaves of giant ferns, running down walls in clear ropes. Water lives just beneath every surface. Press a hand to a flight of stone steps and moisture beads around the fingers. Lean against a column and hear liquid traveling inside it.

The mountain is laced with canals. Some run in ordinary beds, green and narrow, thick with floating leaves. Others cross the air from roof to roof, their water held in square transparent channels without visible supports. A few flow upward, mounting the cliff in smooth unbroken sheets, vanishing into gardens high above. They do not splash. Their surfaces are taut, dark, and grave, carrying fallen petals uphill at the same unhurried pace as the current.

Everything is lush because everything is wet, and everything is wet because the city is never allowed to dry.

Ferns grow out of brick joints in dense fans. Vines blanket entire residential facades and leave only rows of dark windows visible, each window shining with green reflected light. Broad-leaved trees rise from courtyards six stories deep; their roots enter foundations, emerge through interior floors, cross rooms, and descend exterior walls as smooth buttresses. Moss consumes the shaded side of every object. There are orchids with wet, waxy mouths; draping curtains of root; giant cups of bromeliad water; red flowers that open beneath staircases where no sun reaches. In the upper districts, where the clouds pass through the streets, the vegetation becomes pale and immense: silver tree ferns, blue-green parasols of leaf, blooms like overturned bells.

There are no animals.

No insect crosses the open water. No bird moves through the high hot mist. Nothing chews, nests, burrows, hunts, sings, or flees. Leaves are whole unless the weather has torn them. Fruit swells, colors, and remains on its branches without falling. In the courtyards, pools have no fish and no larvae, only drifting pollen and a mineral sheen. The city’s plants have learned, somehow, to perform every visible role of life alone. Vines climb without being carried by seed. Ferns appear in sealed rooms. New roots emerge from old stone where no soil exists.

The only motions are water and vapor, but even these are not changes exactly. They are permanent gestures.

A waterfall descends beside the Hall of Seven Arches, and has been descending in that exact shape for as long as the city has stood. Its white folds do not vary. A drop suspended from the carved lip of a gargoyle never falls, though beneath it a continual curtain pours. Rain hangs in the sunlight over the central valley as fine vertical threads, each thread trembling but never reaching the ground. The mist drifts through the city, yet its drifting creates no new arrangement; it is always both departing and arriving in the same pale corridors between towers.

There is a noon here, though the sun is never quite seen.

The light is normal in its color—warm, broad, bright enough to throw shadows—but it does not seem to come from one direction. Every courtyard is lit. The undersides of leaves are lit. The interiors of empty houses receive a calm gold illumination through walls with no openings. Shadows exist beneath stairways and in the mouths of cisterns, but they are not cast by anything nearby. They lie in old, sensible places, as if remembered from another world.

The sound is overwhelming.

Not violent, not chaotic, but total. It is the noise of the city’s water made enormous by the mountain: cataracts roaring through vertical avenues, deep canals grinding under stone vaults, rain hissing on millions of leaves, hot steam venting from unseen chambers. The walls hum with trapped currents. The roots creak and strain in masonry. At every intersection, the sound of one district meets another—plunge pools booming, aqueducts thundering, hidden reservoirs beating like drums—and the result is a continuous pressure against the body.

It is deafening enough that silence becomes visible.

In certain enclosed plazas, where the fountains rise in smooth columns and vanish into cloud, the roar creates a stillness at its center. One can see a loose leaf turn slowly in the hot air. One can watch water crawl over a carved face on a wall. One can look across an avenue and see the far buildings shimmer behind rain. But no small sound survives: no footfall, no breath, no crack of stone. The world has one voice, and it has been speaking for so long that all lesser voices have been erased.

The highest quarter is not on top of the mountain but behind it.

Stairways lead upward through districts of increasingly narrow houses, past hanging reservoirs and moss-covered shrines, until the cliff face itself opens into a vast interior sky. Here the mountain curves inward around an impossible basin. Above is not a roof, but another horizon: cloudbanks, distant green slopes, a sunless blue distance. The upper city clings to this inward-facing landscape in terraces, as though built around the walls of a colossal cup.

Waterfalls rise from the bottom of the basin and pour into the sky.

They vanish among floating gardens and return, elsewhere, as rain on the lower streets. The city has no outside edge in the usual sense. A traveler walking long enough through its wet corridors may find the same plaza from another direction, but higher; may descend a stairway and emerge on a bridge above its own starting point; may look through an arch and see a valley containing a smaller, greener version of the city, turned upside down in the clouds.

The stone accepts these contradictions without strain.

Its erosion is old, generous, complete. Corners have softened. Steps have been hollowed by water that has never changed course. Walls bulge gently under the weight of roots and centuries. There are carved inscriptions everywhere, but their marks have weathered into shallow flowing lines, indistinguishable from mineral veins. The city is not ruined. Ruin implies an event, a before and after. This place has simply reached the shape it was always seeking: saturated, overgrown, monumental, empty, and wholly occupied by its own enduring processes.

Sometimes, on the broadest avenues, the buildings lean close enough that their roofs touch across the street. Between them, the high hot clouds are visible in strips. Water drips from eaves onto paving polished black by ages of rain. The streets go on uphill, downhill, inward, outward, through gardens and towers and caves full of green light.

They do not lead anywhere.

They lead through the city, which is the same thing as leading through the mountain, which is the same thing as walking through a permanent storm that has learned architecture.
