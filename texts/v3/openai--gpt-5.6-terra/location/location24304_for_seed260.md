The plateau is high enough that the sky has stopped behaving like sky.

It hangs close and dark over the land, not with clouds but with a lid of dull violet mineral light. There is no sun visible anywhere. Day arrives as a gradual lessening of blackness, a weak illumination seeping through the upper air until every distant ridge is the color of old iron. The horizon is not crisp. It breaks, reforms, doubles itself. Mountains appear upside down for hours at a time, suspended beneath the sky-lid like roots pressing through ice.

Nothing here has been worn smooth.

The ground is young, or unfinished. It rises in broad shelves of raw stone, pale green basalt, black glassy seams, chalk-white crust, and folded layers of material that seems not yet to have decided whether it is rock or soil. In places the earth has opened shallowly, exposing interiors full of geometric cavities: honeycombs, spirals, clean tetrahedral wells. These are not mines. No tool marks lead into them. They seem to be the land’s first attempts at making caves.

Erosion has scarcely begun. The cliffs have edges sharp enough to cast narrow, precise shadows in the dimness. Boulders lie where they emerged from the ground, angular and clean-faced, their corners untouched by rain because there is no rain. No river threads the valleys. No snow gathers in the gullies despite the cold. The air is dry enough to draw moisture from breath, dry enough that a dropped leaf becomes papery almost at once.

And yet the vegetation is abundant.

It grows not in continuous cover but in immense, deliberate colonies, each separated from the next by distances of bare mineral ground. Dark groves stand in the folds of the plateau: thickets of columnar plants with soft green-black skins, their surfaces velvety with minute silver hairs. They have no leaves in the usual sense. Instead, broad fronds unfurl from their tops like the sails of submerged creatures, holding themselves rigid in the thin air. At dusk—or what passes for dusk, when the violet ceiling deepens toward charcoal—the fronds slowly turn inside out. Their undersides shine with a damp, interior green.

The plants do not need water. Their roots descend into the new stone and draw something else from it: cold vapor, mineral breath, the faint exhalation of the forming world. Around each root system, the rock remains subtly warm and pliable. You can see it bulging upward over decades, lifting entire groves a finger’s breadth at a time.

There are low carpets too, dense mosses in the shelter of stone ribs, luminous gray lichens spreading in circles across the salt crust, and tall grass-like blades that grow from cracks in black glass. When the wind passes over them, they bend all at once, not in waves but in large, coherent movements—as if one decision travels through the entire field.

No animal crosses the open ground.

There are no birds in the metal-colored air, no insects among the fronds, no burrows at the bases of cliffs. The plants make their own small motions: opening pores, flexing stalks, shedding mineral husks. Their stillness is not loneliness exactly. It is a complete ecology with one side of itself missing.

The sound is unbearable.

At first it seems to come from wind. The plateau is swept by a steady current of cold air moving along its shelves and through its unfinished chasms. But the wind is only the carrier. Every stone has a tone. The raw cliffs hum in long bass notes; glass fields tremble with a high, needling whine; cavities beneath the ground open and close by fractions, producing massive groans that travel for kilometers through the bedrock.

The groves add to it. Their fronds vibrate. Their hollow trunks resonate. In calm periods they emit a dense, organlike chord that is felt more in teeth and lungs than heard. Where several groves face one another across a valley, their sounds interfere, creating zones of sudden silence surrounded by walls of noise. In those silences, one can hear smaller things: the crisp ticking of salt crystals forming, the slow snapping of new rock, the papery rasp of a plant withdrawing one root from a failed seam.

The landscape changes, but with a patience that makes change difficult to witness.

A ridge will lean three degrees over the course of a season. A basin will fill not with water, but with white mineral dust flowing in currents too slow for the eye. A grove may migrate across a slope over centuries, its old stalks dying into upright gray pillars while its new growth rises a hundred meters away where the earth has begun to breathe more richly.

There are scattered structures on the plateau.

They are made of stacked stones, hide-thin sheets of beaten copper, braided fiber, and the translucent husks shed by the tallest plants. Some are simple windbreaks built against the lee sides of basalt slabs. Some are round enclosures with no roofs, their interiors filled with carefully arranged stones that have been rubbed smooth by hand. Others are tall marker towers, narrow and awkward, assembled from raw crystal prisms and bound with dark cord.

No settlement is large enough to call itself a village. Each structure stands alone or in pairs, sometimes miles from the next. Their makers seem to understand the noise. Many shelters are built beside places where the stone sings in a lower key. Their entrances face narrow cuts in the terrain where the wind cannot enter directly. Copper plates, greened by dry oxidation, hang from eaves and tremble constantly. They may be charms, instruments, warnings, or simply efforts to make one terrible sound answer another.

Near some of these shelters are gardens.

They are not cultivated in rows. The ground is too young and too unpredictable for that. Instead, stones are positioned around individual plants, creating little thermal pockets and acoustic basins. A person has coaxed certain mosses onto shaded rock. They have tied the young fronds of tall plants away from cliff edges. They have placed shallow bowls under mineral vents, collecting a blue-gray dust that is perhaps food, medicine, pigment, or seed.

The people themselves are seldom seen, if they are still here. The world gives no clear evidence. A fresh handprint might appear in the powder on a copper sheet, yet there are no tracks around it. A lamp made from a hollow stalk may glow weakly inside a stone enclosure. From far away, in the dim mineral day, one may glimpse a figure carrying a bundle across the salt flats—then look again and find only a standing knot of vegetation, black against the pale earth.

At the plateau’s center is an elevation that should not be possible.

It begins as a mountain, a steep mass of fresh black rock driven upward from below. But halfway up, its slopes abandon gravity. Loose shards cling in vertical streams. Groves grow sideways from walls, their roots entering the exposed air before returning to the stone. The summit is not a peak but an aperture: a vast polygonal absence, darker than the surrounding sky.

Sound pours from it.

Not movement, not wind—sound. A continuous pressure rolls down the mountain in invisible sheets, flattening the fronds and making the mineral plains ripple. The noise is so great that nearby rocks levitate a finger-width above the ground, chattering against one another. At rare intervals, the aperture gives a deeper note, and the entire plateau seems to remember a different arrangement. Valleys briefly become ridges. Distant structures are seen standing on their sides. A grove appears as a reflection beneath the salt, though the salt is solid and has no depth.

Then the note fades, and the world resumes its current version.

The high cold remains. The dry air remains. The dark vegetation continues its slow, lush work over a land that is still being made.

Here, growth is not a softening force. It does not tame the stone or bring water to the empty places. It spreads through the emptiness with roots of silver fiber and sails of green-black flesh, holding fast to whatever reality has settled long enough to be inhabited.
