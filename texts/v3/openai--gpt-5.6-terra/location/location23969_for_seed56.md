Below the frostline, below even the rooted soil, there is a chamber that has not entirely decided to be a chamber.

It is roughly the size of a village green, though no open ground remains to measure it by. The ceiling hangs low in some places and rises unexpectedly in others, not as carved stone but as a thickness of roots, pale fronds, fungal-looking leaves, and translucent vascular sheets. The whole enclosure appears to have grown around a pocket of cold air. Rock occurs only intermittently: soft, dark seams swelling through the plant matter like bones not yet hardened. The geology is young here. Mineral clay pushes up in wet folds from beneath the living floor; tiny ridges form, collapse, and form again over seasons. Nothing has been worn smooth. Every surface seems newly extruded, newly rooted, newly learning what its shape is meant to be.

Water is everywhere, but it does not collect in a lake or stream. It beads along the undersides of leaves. It trembles in the hollow spines of broad mosses. It runs through clear capillaries in walls of root-felt. When someone presses a hand to the floor, cold moisture rises around the fingers, as if the ground recognizes pressure and answers it.

The plants are not arranged in species so much as functions. There are climbing stems, though there is nowhere to climb except other stems. There are low black leaves that absorb the faintest available radiance and emit a more useful gray-green glow from their veins. There are bladder-flowers, each the size of a face, floating without visible support in the dense air. Their petals are thin as ice and packed with drifting internal grains. They open only when the chamber is loud.

At first, the sound seems geological: a continuous low thrum felt in the teeth, an engine somewhere far below. But there is no machine. The roots make it. Networks under the ground tighten and release in waves, pumping mineral water between one wall and the other. The walls answer with a deep wet knock. Vents in the ceiling exhale a sequence of fluting notes. Leaves brush against one another in the slow drafts, making a dry, papery applause. Water droplets strike hollow stalks, and each stalk rings at a different pitch.

The chamber is full of animal life, though almost none of it walks on bare ground.

Small white creatures cling to the roots: six-legged things with velvet bodies and heads divided into bright, heat-sensitive facets. They move in columns along warm vascular channels, stopping whenever the subterranean pulse changes. There are slick, transparent burrowers that swim through the saturated plant mat as fish might swim through water. Their organs glow briefly when they turn, illuminating knots of seeds, stranded mineral crystals, and the pale eggs of something larger.

That larger thing can occasionally be heard rather than seen.

A heavy, soft-bodied animal lives within the ceiling foliage. It passes overhead with the slow force of a weather system, folding leaves outward as it goes. Its many feet do not touch the ceiling; instead, it grips the chamber through fields of static tension. Frost collects along its flanks. Every few hours it releases a long, bell-like call, and the entire garden responds: bladder-flowers dilate, root channels accelerate, insects freeze in place, and a pale fluid begins to rise in the walls.

This is the technology of the place, if technology is the right word: not built, not assembled, not inherited from makers. The plants have acquired processes more exact than instruments. Their roots calculate pressure without moving. Their leaf surfaces sort particles from the air atom by atom. The cold is not merely endured but maintained. Beneath the chamber, the vegetation draws heat out of the surrounding earth and passes it upward through invisible lattices in its cells, radiating it away into cracks too narrow to see. The room remains always near freezing, even where the stone beyond it ought to be warmer.

Occasionally, a leaf turns silver and becomes briefly transparent. Through it, one can see not the tissue behind it but a depth of stars: tiny cold points arranged in patterns that do not correspond to any sky. The points shift when the low root-thrum changes. Then the leaf clouds again, green-black and ordinary, dripping quietly.

There are rules here, though they are not posted.

Nothing dead stays separate for long. A fallen body, a shed skin, a broken branch, even a stone dislodged from the ceiling is slowly embraced by fine white roots. In a day it becomes threaded. In a week it becomes part of the floor’s slow circulation. The chamber does not destroy things so much as translate them into growth.

Heat is treated as an intrusion. A warm animal entering the place would be followed first by the small velvet insects, then by a fog of spores released from the wall. The spores would settle gently, drawing warmth away in minute glistening films. No aggression is necessary. The garden’s cold is simply more persistent than anything that brings heat into it.

Sound is rewarded. A loud cry causes nearby leaves to flex inward, exposing reservoirs of faint light beneath. Repeated tones can make passages appear where there had been only dense hanging roots. Silence, by contrast, allows the vegetation to knit shut. The place is navigated by resonance. Its paths are not physical routes but agreements reached with the listening parts of the plants.

At its center is a shallow rise of raw blue-gray mineral, barely protruding from the wet ground. It is perhaps only a few months old. Its surface has the dull sheen of a tooth emerging through gum. Around it the entire chamber is crowded: roots wrapped around it without touching, animals clustered in quiet circles, flowers inclined toward it as though it radiates a light too dim for eyes.

From a narrow fissure in the mineral mound comes the deepest tone in the garden.

It is not loud in the ordinary sense. It enters through the ankles, the sternum, the fillings of the skull. With each long note, the young stone grows by a thickness too small to witness. The plants lean closer. Water gathers. New roots begin in the dark.

The chamber is not ancient, despite its density. It is being born underground, patiently and noisily, making a world from cold, pressure, vegetal intelligence, and the first uncertain gestures of stone.
