At the bottom of a low basin, beneath a skin of black floodwater, there is a place no wider than a small room.

It may once have been a room.

The water has filled it completely, patiently, to the underside of a ceiling that is no longer quite a ceiling. The old walls remain in portions: pale masonry slabs pressed into mud, a doorway standing without a door, the squared corner of something built by hands. But every hard edge has begun to acquire a second, softer intention. Moss seals the joins. Fine root-hairs enter cracks and emerge elsewhere, so that the walls are stitched to the silt. The place is becoming a pond without ever fully ceasing to be an interior.

There is no surface visible from within. Above, perhaps, lies more water: a flooded field, a drowned wood, a channel swollen beyond its banks. The darkness is not absolute. It has the faint blue-black dilution of deep rain. Occasionally a pallid breadth gathers at the upper boundary, as if daylight had remembered the place and laid a hand against the water, then withdrawn.

Everything is close.

Long ribbons of weed grow from the floor and lean against the walls, not drifting so much as holding themselves in the weak, cold current. Their leaves are broad and deep green near the roots, then translucent at the tips. They overlap every available space. Finer plants have colonized the old furniture: a submerged bench, two bent iron frames, a shelf that has collapsed in a heap. A pale fungus blooms in the angles beneath the shelf, although there is no air there for it. Its caps open like small folded ears. Once in a while, their gills give off a dust of silver motes which rise upward and do not stop at the ceiling. They pass directly through it, one by one, as though the stone has forgotten how to be solid.

The floor is fresh mud, soft and dark, laid over a younger wound in the ground. Beneath it runs a narrow tear in the basin’s clay. It opened only recently, perhaps during the flood, perhaps during some other rearrangement of the world. Clear water wells through the seam in slow pulses, cold enough to make the surrounding growth shrink back by a finger-width. The slit has sharp sides. Small flakes of orange clay still fall from them. Nothing has yet rounded or softened this new opening.

At its mouth, the water behaves differently.

Loose leaves sink toward it and pause. Bits of silt circle it for hours. A drowned seedpod may descend in a straight line, reach the level of the crack, and become horizontal. Then it travels sideways into the wall, disappearing between the roots. There is no sound when this happens. In fact, there is almost no sound anywhere in the basin. The water carries every noise away before it can gather itself.

There is the minute ticking of stems touching each other.

There is the occasional crumble of clay.

There is a pressure in the ears, not a hum exactly, but the suggestion that a hum has been waiting for a very long time beneath all things.

The only animals are difficult to be certain of. A single blind fish, thin as a willow leaf, appears on some visits and not on others. Its body is almost colorless except for the red thread of its spine. It moves between the curtains of vegetation with solemn, measured strokes, never startling, never hunting. It seems less like a resident than a thought passing through the water. Sometimes it enters the old doorway and comes out from a crack in the opposite wall. Sometimes it vanishes beneath the bench and is later seen high near the ceiling, where there should be no way for it to have gone.

Small snails cling to the masonry. Their shells are made from grains of brick, mica, and something that resembles milk glass. They do not move often enough for movement to be witnessed. You notice only that a shell which was beside a nail yesterday is now on the far side of a weed-stem, and that its trail is not behind it but faintly shining ahead.

The scattered remains of construction have become landmarks in the dense growth. A brass handle protrudes from the mud, green with oxidation. A glass jar lies upright on a ledge, its mouth open and its interior occupied by a perfectly round ball of water darker than the water around it. The ball does not mix. It turns slowly in the jar, carrying tiny pale roots around its inner surface. Near the doorway, a rung of a ladder rises from the silt and reaches upward for three steps before ending in open water. No one can say whether it descended from above or was built toward a place below.

The basin changes, but only with the slowness of saturation.

Roots lengthen. The mud settles. A wall leans by the thickness of a fingernail in a season. The cold spring in the floor widens its seam. Outside, storms may darken the waters overhead, and somewhere reeds may bend, banks may fail, whole fields may be made into lakes. Down here, the evidence arrives as a slight increase in pressure, a new leaf drifting through a gap, the distant mineral taste of disturbed soil.

Nothing decays quickly. Nothing is wholly still.

The place is packed with life, yet it feels uninhabited. Every leaf, thread, stone, and old human object seems to be waiting for the same event: not rescue, not collapse, but recognition. As though the flooded room has been quietly practicing for a condition in which it no longer needs to choose whether it is architecture, wetland, memory, or wound in the earth.

And at the narrow crack in the floor, the clear water continues to rise from somewhere impossible—cool, steady, and new.
