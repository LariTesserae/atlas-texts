The high country is not mountainous in the usual way. It has the breadth of a plateau, but the ground has risen into soft, vegetative escarpments and long green shoulders, all of them furred and braided over with growth. Nothing here is bare for long. Rock appears only in dark, wet seams where a slope has recently opened, and even those cuts are occupied immediately: red-threaded lichens, pale mosses shaped like small antlers, thick blue leaves folded into the cracks.

The whole region seems to be making soil.

It is cold enough that breath, if there were anyone here to breathe, would hang briefly in the air. A low ceiling of cloud comes and goes across the plateau. Sometimes the sky opens into a hard, distant blue; more often the light is gray-white, evenly dispersed, leaving no deep shadow anywhere. Moisture does not fall reliably as rain. It gathers. It films every blade and leaf. It wells in the cup-shaped centers of plants, condenses beneath overlapping fronds, and drains in clear threads along the undersides of stems.

There are no rivers in the open, yet water is everywhere. Under the vegetation, channels run constantly: black, tannin-stained little flows, sliding over roots and mineral shelves. In hollows between the larger growths lie pools no wider than a room, dark enough to look bottomless, their surfaces hidden by floating rosettes. When wind passes over the land, it does not make waves in grass. It makes whole slopes flex and tighten, because the ground cover is connected beneath itself by ropes of living fiber.

The plants are not separate inhabitants of the terrain. They are the terrain.

Some lie low in broad mats, their leaves overlapping like roof tiles. Some rise into clustered columns, several meters high, built from dense layers of stiff green blades. There are groves of pale trunks that are not wood exactly but bundled stalks, each trunk made of thousands of stems pressed together, growing upward around an empty central shaft. Cold vapor rises from these shafts in the morning.

Across the valley floors are what might first be mistaken for industrial works.

They have the regularity of mills, the scale of processing yards, the purposeful repetition of factory systems. Long ranks of tubular plants lie parallel to one another across the slopes, each tube swollen at intervals, each linked to the next by root-lines that disappear below the moss. Vast fan-leaved growths turn slowly and continuously in the wind, not as individual leaves fluttering but as entire circular crowns rotating around thick, wet hubs. Their motion is nearly silent. At a distance, they resemble turbines.

Other organs stand along the ravines: tall, ribbed towers of yellow-green tissue, open at their tops. During periods of low cloud they draw mist into themselves. Condensation runs down their internal walls, and at their bases the captured water is expelled in pulses into buried channels. The pulse is strong enough to make the surrounding moss jump and shiver. These towers are perhaps the closest thing the country has to chimneys, pumps, or engines.

Nothing has been built. No hand has cut a path or set a stone. Yet the land has evolved arrangements that look unmistakably industrial: collection, compression, circulation, storage, release.

Its labor is botanical.

And it is relentless.

Storms arrive without much announcement. One moment the plateau is still under a thin white sky; the next, wind is moving through every layer of growth. Great banks of foliage overturn, revealing their pale undersides. Stem-clusters bow nearly flat and rise again. The rotating crowns accelerate. Threads of loosened moss lift into the air and travel across the valley in green veils. The soil itself, held in a woven mesh of roots, creeps slowly downhill in broad coherent sheets.

Nothing crashes. The silence remains one of the region’s defining facts.

There is no clatter from the turning fans, no groan from the bending groves, no obvious roar from wind. The vegetation catches and divides sound. Storms are visible as violence but heard only as a pressure in the ears, a soft innumerable brushing, like fingers passing over damp cloth. Even when a whole bank of root-bound earth shears away from an escarpment and slides fifty meters into a ravine, it does so with a muted, saturated heaviness. The landslip settles into the growth below. By the next season, or perhaps by the next week, the exposed place will be threaded over again.

The erosion here is ancient and settled into habit. Valleys have been deepened by countless small failures rather than a few catastrophic ones. The cliffs are rounded, quilted, densely stitched by plant life. Their faces bear long vertical smears of black mineral stain beneath zones where meltwater seeps from the plateau. In places, the vegetation has developed thick buttresses around old fractures, binding the slopes as a body braces a wound.

There are pathways, but no paths.

The largest plants maintain clear lanes between their root-fields—low corridors of packed fibrous litter where water and spores move more easily. These lanes travel for kilometers, joining basins to ridges in looping, deliberate routes. Some are warm enough underfoot to melt frost. Others exhale a faint metallic smell, like wet iron and crushed fern. At certain junctions, masses of bulbous growth swell from the ground and rhythmically contract, pushing clouds of pale pollen into the wind whenever the pressure changes.

The pollen does not drift like dust. It moves in bands, gathering in the lee of ridges, pouring down ravines, then lifting again. In the gray light it can resemble smoke from unseen machinery.

Fauna is scarce, though not absent. A narrow-winged bird sometimes crosses high above the plateau, black against cloud, and vanishes before one can decide whether it was a bird or a loose leaf. In sheltered water pockets, translucent insect forms cling beneath floating leaves, their motion visible only when they disturb the surface tension. There are small, cold-bodied grazers in the deepest thickets—perhaps no larger than a hand—whose trails are found as neat clipped lines in the moss. They are almost never seen. Their existence is inferred from the tidiness of certain leaves, from the occasional tiny print filling with condensation.

The more obvious life is vegetal and fast.

At the edge of a pool, a mat of green disks may close over a black opening in minutes after a gust clears it. A toppled tower-plant sends out pale anchor roots that crawl visibly across the ground, seeking purchase. After a night frost, certain slopes wake white with crystalline growth; by noon the crystals have been drawn down into the leaves, leaving the land dark, wet, and steaming faintly in the weak sun.

The rules of the place are simple, though they take time to recognize.

Nothing remains exposed.

Nothing moves alone.

Every break becomes a seam.

Every seam is fed.

Every channel, no matter how hidden, eventually joins the larger circulation beneath the plateau.

At the far end of the region, beyond several weathered ridges, the vegetation descends into a wide basin. The basin is filled with tall silver-green reeds, so dense that their upper surfaces make a continuous field. Storm winds race across them in traveling folds. Below the reeds, unseen water moves through a network of hollow stems and porous root chambers. At intervals across the basin, the entire field sinks a little, then rises again, as if some enormous submerged mechanism is drawing breath.

In the evening—if evening can be distinguished beneath the cloud—the normal gray light becomes blue at the horizon. Water beads on every surface. The towers stop pumping. The rotating leaf-crowns slow. Yet the land does not become still. It merely transfers its work underground.

The plateau remains awake in its silence: cold, damp, vegetated beyond the possibility of emptiness, manufacturing itself beneath the weather.
