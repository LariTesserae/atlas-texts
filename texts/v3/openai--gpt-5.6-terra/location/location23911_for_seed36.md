The place lies beneath a shallow skin of stone, but it does not feel buried. It feels sheltered under the world’s palm.

There are no caverns in the usual sense. No dark chambers, no dripping ceilings, no mineral teeth. The ground has only recently begun to separate from itself: pale seams in a mass of warm clay, soft shelves of unfired stone, hollows pressed open by roots. Everything is young in shape. Walls are rounded, not eroded. Banks do not slope so much as gather. The whole underland seems to have been made by pressure from living things above and below, plant tissue insisting gently against earth until the earth accepted a few rooms, corridors, and broad green basins.

Light comes down through the surface in sheets.

The roof is thin in places: a translucent crust of root-matted soil, leaf-fiber, and whitish stone. Sunlight enters gold and almost solid, filtered through innumerable layers of green. It strikes the damp air in bright columns. Each root, each tendril, each pale fungal thread casts a small, clean shadow. There is nowhere fully dark. Even beneath the thickest mats, the light persists as a green-gold dusk.

The heat is constant. Not stifling, exactly; it has been absorbed into the plants and breathed out slowly. The clay is warm to the hand. Broad leaves, folded close to the walls, feel almost feverish. Condensation gathers only where the light is weakest, beading along the undersides of roots and in the narrow creases between fleshy stems. At intervals a drop falls into a basin below: a precise, isolated sound, too infrequent to become rain.

Water has not carved this place. It has merely been invited in.

There are shallow pools in certain low places, clear but tea-colored from steeped roots. They have no visible source and no current. Their surfaces are usually unbroken, coated here and there with translucent disks of floating leaf. Around them rise thickets of plants whose stems are braided into pillars. Some are as thin as cords, some as thick as a person’s waist, and their surfaces range from smooth wax to a fine fur like velvet. The vegetation does not grow beside the terrain. It is the terrain.

Paths are made from it. Bridges are made from it. The floor, where it is not clay, is a firm woven pad of roots and flattened succulent runners. A person walking through the underland would soon learn which green surfaces bear weight and which give way into warm, fragrant hollows. There are broad leaf-steps descending into pools, barkless trunks bent into arches, lattices of vine strong enough to cross open chambers. Here and there a whole embankment is made of compressed stems, slowly pulsing with stored water.

The plants are dense enough that distance must be negotiated. A valley may be only a few hundred paces across, yet take an hour to cross because its air is filled with hanging growth: tassels, seed-veils, curtains of tiny leaves that close behind a passerby. Still, the place is open in a larger sense. It extends. Through gaps in the green architecture, one can see other basins shining beyond, other root-roofs bright with noon, other low ridges where the surface has only begun to buckle and split. The underland is not one garden but a regional system of warm pockets, linked by vegetated passages beneath a broad plain.

Nothing here seems to change.

That is its strangeness. The light moves above, perhaps; seasons must pass somewhere beyond the root-roof. But below, every leaf holds the same angle. A fallen petal remains fresh on the floor. A droplet can hang from a root-tip for years before falling. New formations are everywhere—unsealed cracks, soft clay domes, roots gathering in knots that look ready to become columns—but they remain perpetually on the verge of becoming. The land is embryonic without ever maturing.

In one basin, a small hill of ochre mud has pushed upward beneath a mesh of white roots. It is clearly forming a cave or a mound or a springhead. Its skin is unbroken. Warm vapor leaks from its pores, smelling faintly of pepper and wet stone. No one has seen it become anything else.

Scattered among the growth are old constructions, if they can be called that. They are too simple to feel like settlements. A ring of upright slabs emerges from a fern-bank, each slab wrapped in roots so completely that only a handbreadth of pale rock shows. In another chamber, someone once laid a low platform of fitted stones over a pool. The stones are still level, though roots have made a living mosaic between them. There are little niches cut into clay walls, empty except for seeds. A short stair rises to nowhere, ending against a curtain of leaves.

These traces do not suggest machinery or industry. They are gestures: places to sit, to leave something, to mark the edge of water. They might be very old, or they might have always been present, fixed in the same suspended age as everything else.

Animal life is sparse. A few pale moths live in the brighter shafts, their wings thin enough to show the green light through them. They travel so slowly that they resemble drifting scraps of leaf. Near the pools, small eyeless frogs may be heard—not calling, but making a soft click beneath the water when they shift position. There are also long, root-colored insects that move only at the exact boundary between light and shade. Most visitors would miss them entirely.

The dominant sound is the sound of breathing plants: not a literal breath, but the faint pressure and release of leaves brushing one another in the heated air. There is a hushed, crowded softness everywhere. Occasionally a seedpod opens with a papery crack, and the sound carries remarkably far through the warm corridors. The isolated drip of water feels ceremonial. Footsteps are muffled almost at once.

The air is rich enough to taste. Green, bitter, humid, with an undernote of sun-warmed resin. Breathing it makes the lungs feel coated in sweetness. Skin takes on a fine sheen of moisture but never becomes wet. The temperature and the brightness create an odd sense of permanence: the impression of standing inside a noon that has not ended since the world was young.

There are rules, though they are not written.

Do not cut the pale roots that cross a path. They are not merely roots; the paths remain stable because of them.

Do not take water from a pool unless you leave some small weight at its edge: a stone, a seed, a piece of shed bark. The pools do not punish theft, but those who drink without offering find themselves unable to locate the same chamber again.

Do not force your way through a leaf curtain. Wait. The leaves will loosen eventually, provided you are still enough. Impatience tangles the passages.

And do not try to map the place by distance. It does not obey that sort of measure. Two bright chambers may be separated by a few steps one day and a valley’s worth of green the next—not because the land has changed, but because its nearness belongs to attention rather than geometry.

At the farthest visible edge of the underland, beneath a roof so thin it glows nearly white, new ground is gathering. Clay swells under a field of roots. Soft stone rises in smooth, unopened folds. The vegetation has already claimed it: bright leaves lie across the unfinished earth like hands over a sleeping face.
