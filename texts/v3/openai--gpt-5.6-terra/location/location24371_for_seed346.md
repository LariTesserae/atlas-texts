The city occupies a shallow basin no larger than a few districts of an ordinary town, though nobody who lives there would call it small. It has too many interiors. Streets fold into courts, courts open behind walls that were seen from another street, stairwells ascend into the undersides of plazas. Its boundaries are clear only from above: a dark oval of construction pressed into pale, unfinished ground. Beyond it lies a low plain of raw mineral soil, still deciding whether to become clay, stone, or ice.

There are no mountains. The horizon is close and vacant. In winter—which is nearly always—the sky hangs in a level gray sheet over the basin, admitting an honest, workmanlike daylight. Nothing glows dramatically. Nothing is perpetually night. The strange things here happen under light sufficient to read notices, see rust in a railing, recognize a neighbor’s face as it changes expression into somebody else’s for an instant and then returns.

The ground beneath the city is young.

It pushes up in soft geometric swellings between foundations: black silt ridges, wet seams of blue-gray stone, crystalline growths like half-finished brickwork. At the edge of the paved areas, the earth is not eroding so much as assembling itself. A curb may acquire a bedrock continuation overnight. A vacant lot sometimes fills with a low arrangement of hexagonal columns, each one warm at the center and cold at the surface, as if geology has been instructed to imitate municipal planning but has not yet learned the purpose of doors.

Construction crews attend these formations with survey instruments, thermos flasks, and devices that look like transparent tuning forks. They do not demolish the new earth. The city has long since learned that it is easier, and safer, to negotiate with it. White chalk marks appear around promising mineral extrusions. A sign may be posted:

**THIS SUBSTRATE HAS NOT COMPLETED ITS AGREEMENT WITH GRAVITY.  
DO NOT LEAN AGAINST IT.**

The built fabric is dense: narrow residential towers clad in dull composite panels, service alleys roofed over by bridges, ventilation stacks, rail lines that enter buildings without stopping, tanks, ducts, exterior staircases, public elevators, hanging gardens that contain almost nothing alive. Every available vertical surface has been made useful. Apartments are inserted into old pumping stations. Shops occupy the hollows inside structural pylons. A school runs through three joined buildings whose floors do not quite coincide; children learn early which corridors must be crossed without counting the steps.

The technology is everywhere, but it has become domestic and tired-looking. Its miraculous functions are hidden behind access hatches, municipal casings, scratched screens, and the patient habits of maintenance workers. A wall might be holding back not soil but a pocket of repeated afternoon. A tram tunnel may be prevented from entering an older version of itself by a ring of silent machines mounted along the ceiling. Doorframes are fitted with thin black seals that register whether the person passing through is continuous with the person who entered the building.

Most people wear small verification tags at the collar or wrist. They blink green, amber, or, rarely, a soft steady blue. Blue means the registry cannot determine where a person has been, but has determined that they are not dangerous. Usually.

At intersections, the traffic signals are supplemented by weather vanes that indicate the local direction of causality. Their little metal arrows turn only once or twice a day. When they all point inward, citizens are advised not to make promises.

The dampness never becomes rain enough to clean anything. It gathers in the city’s joints: condensation beading beneath elevated walkways, water darkening concrete at the bases of walls, a slick sheen on handrails, cold droplets ticking from cables. There are drainage channels everywhere, some ancient and open, some sealed under transparent covers. Their water runs slowly toward the basin’s center, where it enters a square pool surrounded by administrative buildings.

The pool is called the Reservoir, though it is not used for drinking. It is only a few meters across. Its water is black, flat, and always exactly level, even during tremors. Every so often, a small object rises from its surface: a key, a shoe, a sparrow skull, a component no one can identify, a cleanly folded municipal map printed in a future revision. Retrieval is regulated. There is a booth nearby, heated and staffed day and night by someone with no visible job except to watch what comes up.

Vegetation survives in omissions.

Tufts of silver grass grow where paving has failed to meet a wall. Lichen patterns the north faces of old structures in pale maps. A few shrubs hunch behind sound barriers, their branches hard and black in the cold. The most substantial trees are cultivated in sealed public atria, each one rooted in a cylinder of engineered soil and monitored by slow-moving cameras. Their leaves are sparse, dark, and thick as rubber. In the winter they do not drop; they simply become stiller.

Outside, in cracks between unfinished mineral formations, a moss grows in narrow velvet strips. It appears only after weeks of fog. The city’s residents prize it as a sign that the local reality is settling. Children are told not to touch it, because it remembers the shape of warm hands and may reproduce them elsewhere.

Animals are uncommon. Pigeons come and go in small, uncertain groups, as though the basin only occasionally intersects their migratory route. A few pale dogs wander the service roads without owners. They are tolerated, even fed, because they are said to refuse entry to buildings that will not remain buildings. In the early morning, one can sometimes see a fox on the vacant margins of the city, standing beside a new stone formation and staring into a gap too narrow for its head.

There are no insects except the municipal ones: tiny maintenance machines that emerge in thaw periods to inspect cable insulation, consume corrosion, and vanish into vents. They make no audible sound. If they gather on a person’s sleeve, people stand still until the inspection is complete.

Quiet governs the place, though it is not silence. The city has a low, nearly subsonic pressure of operation: distant pumps, transformer hum, the occasional hollow impact from beneath a street. Condensation taps in pipes. Elevator cables whisper in shafts. Public announcement systems make their messages in a soft human voice, never raised:

“Platform Four is unavailable due to unresolved arrival.”

“Please do not follow familiar voices into utility passages.”

“The north district is experiencing a mild difference in sequence. Continue your day as normal.”

At night, though night is only a dimming of the gray, the city becomes more densely occupied. Not by crowds exactly. By presences. Lit windows stack one above another in every direction. Behind frosted glass, figures stand in kitchens, workshops, infirmaries, offices that should have closed hours ago. Some windows show rooms much larger than their buildings allow. Some show landscapes: a shoreline, a corridor of red trees, a sky with two pale moons. Residents usually draw their curtains when this happens, not out of fear but courtesy. A window is still a window, even when it opens onto somewhere that has not asked to be observed.

The rules are posted in stairwells, station halls, and the entrances to public shelters. They change slowly, with revisions issued after committees have studied events that have not quite happened.

Do not enter an unnumbered floor alone.

If you encounter your own apartment before reaching it, notify Building Continuity.

Do not remove stones from the unfinished ground.

When the basin fog rises above roof level, remain indoors until it chooses a direction.

If a person you know does not cast a shadow, speak to them normally. Do not ask where they have been.

The people here are not heroic about these rules. They complain about them, ignore the minor ones, borrow each other’s tools, leave bicycles in hallways, argue over heating allocations. They shop for root vegetables and machine parts. They take the tram when it is running in the correct order. They make soup. They fall in love with people from adjacent buildings, then find that the adjacency has altered and try to maintain the relationship across revised geography.

The city persists because it is dense enough to witness itself.

Every street is seen by several windows. Every oddity is noticed by someone taking out trash, waiting for a lift, smoking under an awning, checking a pipe. Its buildings brace one another. Its systems overlap. Its inhabitants have learned that a place may be unstable without being unlivable, and that reality, like wet ground, can sometimes be compacted simply by walking the same routes again and again.

At the basin’s edge, beyond the last service road, the raw plain continues. It is cold, damp in pockets, nearly bare. Fresh seams of stone appear there in the mornings, low and tentative. They resemble foundations waiting for instructions.

The city watches them carefully.

Not because it fears expansion.

Because, in this place, whatever begins to build itself may eventually ask to be let in.
