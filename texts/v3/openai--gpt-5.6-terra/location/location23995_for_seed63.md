At the summit of a mountain that has long since stopped being a mountain in any ordinary sense, there is a basin called the Kiln. It is not large. A person could cross it in an hour if there were anywhere to put their feet.

The basin is roughly oval, enclosed by steep walls of black, heat-cracked stone. Their upper edges disappear into a permanent lid of ash-colored cloud, or perhaps into night. No stars are visible. Light does not arrive here in any useful form. The Kiln is warm enough that its rocks keep a dull internal glow in the narrow seams where they have fractured, and hot vapor sometimes blushes red under the crust, but most of the place is black-on-black: polished obsidian against dull basalt, moving bodies against the less-moving earth.

There is no water. There has not been water for a very long time.

The ground remembers it only as geometry: shallow channels cut through old ash, terraces of mineral sediment, a few rounded stones preserved beneath newer falls of grit. But these forms are dry and hard as bones. The air itself is dry enough to sting the membranes of the nose. It smells of sulfur, hot iron, old eggshell, and the dusty mineral smell released when stone splits open.

Nothing green grows in the Kiln. Nothing roots. There are no leaves, no grasses, no lichens softening the walls. In the warmest cracks, however, a dark velvety film gathers—not plant, not exactly fungus. It is made of countless minute bodies that feed on heat and mineral vapor, forming thin black fans along the mouths of vents. When disturbed, they draw themselves flat against the rock and become indistinguishable from soot.

These films are the beginning of almost everything.

The basin is crowded with animals.

At first this is difficult to understand, because the animals are quiet and the darkness conceals their numbers. A visitor might believe the Kiln empty except for the occasional scrape of claw on stone. Then the heat beneath their skin adjusts their sight, or their eyes begin to recognize motion: a fold in the ground breathing; a ridge of ash resolving into a hundred crouched shapes; pale knuckles moving in a wall-crevice. The Kiln is not empty. It is packed beyond reason.

They occupy every available level.

Small, six-legged ash runners swarm through the dry channels, their translucent bellies faintly luminous with stored heat. They scrape the black films from the vents with blunt mouthparts and travel in ceaseless braided streams, never quite touching despite their density. Above them perch the hooked things known, in whatever language names them, as kiln gulls: featherless, long-necked scavengers with broad membranes stretched between their forelimbs. They do not fly often. The hot air is unreliable, and there is nowhere beyond the basin worth going. Instead they drift from ledge to ledge, opening their mouths to taste the heat-radiance rising from the ground.

The larger creatures are almost indistinguishable from the mineral landscape. They have plated hides, dark gray and faceted, with bodies low to the stone. Some are no bigger than dogs; some are the size of carts. Each has a crown of heat-sensitive horns, branching and retractable, which they use to find warm fissures under the ash. They graze there in groups, kneeling over the vents while their mouths work quietly at the black mineral bloom. Their backs are always dusted with pale grit. In the low radiance of the fissures they look like broken statues slowly rearranging themselves.

Among them move predators: slim, blind animals with too many joints in their legs and smooth stone-colored heads. They hunt by vibration. A runner’s footfall, a shifting plate, the crackle of a cooling rock—each sound travels through the basin floor and becomes a map inside them. They are patient. The Kiln is mostly quiet because it has taught every living thing the cost of noise.

Even their violence is restrained.

A herd compresses around a vent. One animal senses danger and stiffens; the others do not scatter but crowd closer, making themselves a single armored mass. The predator waits at the edge of this living wall, motionless for so long that ash settles across its spine. Then, perhaps, a small runner makes the mistake of crossing open ground. There is one skittering rush, one soft impact, a brief dry chittering. Afterward the basin returns to its usual sounds: the ticking of cooling stone, the faint breathing from the fumaroles, the brush of bodies passing bodies in the dark.

The heat is never steady. It comes in pulses.

At irregular intervals the ground deep below exhales. The vents brighten. Warm air surges across the basin in a low, pressure-heavy wave, making the black films ripple and raising little curtains of ash. Every animal responds. The grazers turn their horn-crowns toward the source. The ash runners vanish into cracks. The membrane-winged scavengers climb the walls with their claws and spread themselves flat. From hundreds of throats comes a sound almost below hearing: not a call, more a communal tightening of breath.

Then the pulse passes.

For a moment the Kiln is lit from underneath. The whole crowded basin reveals itself in ember-outline: thousands upon thousands of creatures packed among the stones, every crevice occupied, every ledge bearing a sleeper, every warm seam attended by feeders. There is no room for solitude here. Even death is quickly given a place. Shells become shade. Bones become calcium deposits around vent mouths. Empty burrows become nurseries. A fallen body is stripped, gnawed, hollowed, and eventually incorporated into the floor as a pale angular ridge.

There are a few built things, though no one now living seems capable of building them.

Near the western wall stand three low arches made from fitted slabs of black rock. They are too precise to be natural, their interiors smooth and pale from innumerable bodies rubbing against them. They form a short passage leading nowhere: one enters from the basin and emerges against the cliff face, where the stone is solid. The animals use them constantly. Runners flow through in streams. The plated grazers press their flanks against the pillars. Predators wait outside, but never within.

No animal fights in the arches.

This is the closest thing the Kiln has to a law.

Another law belongs to the vents: nothing may remain directly above an opening once the deep pulse begins. Creatures that fail to move are not burned—though the heat can blister hide and crack shell—but taken by the breath of the mountain. It lifts ash, pebbles, scraps of bone, occasionally a whole small animal, and draws them down into darkness when the pressure reverses. The basin accepts this without ceremony. The survivors close ranks.

The walls bear old scratches far above any present animal’s reach. Some are straight lines. Some make spirals. Some resemble rows of small hands. Heat has softened their edges, ash has filled them, and time has made them uncertain. Perhaps the arches and marks were left by a people who lived in the Kiln before the animals became its true inhabitants. Perhaps they were made by an older kind of animal, one that learned to stack stone and then forgot why. In the dark, the distinction does not seem important.

Above, the ash lid occasionally opens by a degree. Not enough to show the sky, only enough to change the darkness from black to charcoal. Fine mineral dust falls through the gap. It lands on backs, on horns, on the warm slick films, on the ancient arches. Within minutes the packed life of the basin has disturbed it all again, leaving trails, dents, drags, claw marks, and the delicate continuous stippling of many small feet.

The Kiln is active every moment and almost silent every moment. It is a high, hot, airless-seeming pocket of persistence: a small territory sealed by stone, fed by heat, crowded with lives that have adapted not by flourishing outward but by fitting ever more completely into the narrow spaces left for them.

Nothing grows tall.

Nothing leaves.

And under the black floor, the mountain keeps breathing.
