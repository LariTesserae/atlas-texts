The country lies low under a ceiling of weather.

There are no hills in the usual sense. What rises here is only what has resisted—long black backs of stone, islands of compacted shell, ribs of ancient clay that the water has not yet carried away. The land is a broad drowned plain, open for miles beneath a dim, colorless sky. Rain falls often, though not always from visible clouds. Sometimes it seems to gather directly out of the air, beading on every surface until the ground shines. Sometimes the mist itself settles, heavy enough to make shallow new lakes between one hour and the next.

Nothing grows.

There are no grasses along the watercourses, no reeds, no moss in the cracks of rock. The mud is bare and blue-gray. The stone is bare and dark, worn satin-smooth by innumerable seasons. In the hollows lie fields of pale mineral crust—salt, gypsum, something older and duller—that split into broad polygonal plates when briefly dry. Then the rain returns, or the tide pushes inland through channels too flat to call rivers, and the plates soften, dissolve at their seams, sink back into shining slurry.

The whole place has the look of something eroded almost to completion. Not dead exactly, because it is full of movement; rather, finished in its geological gestures. The cliffs have become shelves. The shelves have become banks. The banks have become low curves of exposed strata, their layers bent and polished like the grain of old bone. There are stones here whose edges have been rounded for so long that they seem unwilling to admit they were ever broken.

Water is everywhere, but rarely deep. It lies in sheets across the land, shivering under rain. It threads through channels packed with black sediment. It wells up from the ground in clear, cold eyes, then spreads over the flats in slow, reflective fingers. Beneath the crusted surfaces, a traveler can hear it moving: a faint sucking, a hollow glug, the occasional deep collapse when an old drainage pocket opens.

The air is cool enough that breath lingers, though never as a proper fog. It hangs close to the face in pale strands and is immediately torn apart by wind.

And there is wind constantly.

It comes unobstructed across the plain, because nothing stands high enough to break it. It presses the rain sideways. It combs shallow water into hard, racing lines. It enters the empty holes in the stone and makes them sing—not delicately, but with the force of a great instrument being played without restraint. The eroded rock is full of vents, tunnels, and undercuts. Through some, the wind produces a low animal bellow. Through others it whistles in piercing, alternating notes. At the mouths of the larger fissures it becomes a roar so steady that it ceases to sound like wind and becomes the permanent voice of the region.

The land is deafening.

There is the wind. There is rain striking water, mud, shell, stone, hide. There is the clattering migration of the animals. There are shrieks, drumming calls, throat-rumbles, sudden volleys of snapping beaks and hard limbs. The tide itself makes noise as it moves, not a soft lapping but a restless grinding, dragging immense beds of loose shells across the flats. During storms, the ground trembles with the impact of waves arriving far inland through old channels.

Conversation must be conducted close to the ear. Signals are made with broad gestures, flags of hide, mirrored scraps of wet metal, or by striking specific stones with mallets. Certain rocks carry sound unusually well. A person can kneel beside a low black ridge and hear, through its body, a distant series of knocks from another outcrop a mile away.

Life has not disappeared with the plants. It has simply taken another foundation.

The animals feed on the water, on mineral-rich films, on drifting spores and microscopic flotillas that bloom invisibly in rain pools and tidal shallows. Some scrape the pale crust from the flats with broad, enamelled mouths. Some sift black mud through curtains of bristles. Others hunt them.

The most numerous creatures are the crawlers: low-bodied, many-legged things the size of dogs or small carts, armored in overlapping plates of gray-green horn. They travel in dense, clicking bands through the wetland, their legs making a sound like handfuls of pebbles shaken in a drum. When threatened, they fold their limbs beneath themselves and become almost indistinguishable from the knuckled stones scattered over the plain.

There are larger grazers, if grazing is the word for creatures that vacuum the surface of water. These are tall, six-limbed beasts with narrow heads lowered continually toward the shallows. Their skins are pale, nearly translucent around the throat and flank, and beneath them pulse dark branching organs that filter the water they drink. They move in loose herds, widely spaced, each animal leaving a fan of disturbed silt behind it. At a distance they resemble standing fragments of ruined architecture: posts, arches, pale upright slabs shifting slowly through the rain.

Their young cluster beneath their bodies, calling with an uncanny, flute-like alarm whenever the water rises too quickly.

Predators follow the herds. They are lean, dark things, long enough to bridge a shallow channel in a single bound. Their feet spread into soft, many-toed pads so they do not sink in the mud. They hunt by vibration, freezing with their narrow skulls pressed to the ground, feeling the minute tremor of legs, water, and panic. The plain sometimes goes briefly still around one of them—not silent, never silent, but attentive. Then the predator moves, and the whole wetland breaks into violent motion.

Overhead, there are flyers.

They have no feathers. Their wings are stretched membranes, dark and wet-looking, supported by long jointed struts. In bad weather they fly low, using the wind skimming the flats. In clearer intervals they wheel high enough to vanish into the dullness above, their calls descending as metallic cries. They nest in the rare vertical places: the sides of eroded stone chimneys, the interior ledges of collapsed sinkholes, the upper beams of abandoned human shelters.

The people who cross this country do not try to master it. Their works are too small and too temporary for that.

Here and there, on raised shell banks or rocky spurs, stand huts made from bent driftwood, crawler plates, hide, and slabs of split stone. They are squashed low against the wind, almost buried in mud on their seaward sides. Their doors are narrow and face away from the prevailing storms. A settlement might contain three such huts and a drying rack, though there is nothing vegetable to dry—only strips of pale animal flesh, membrane, bristled filter-organs, and lengths of tendon braided into cord.

Some structures are older: circular walls of unmortared stone, half sunken, their interiors flooded ankle-deep. No one seems to know whether they were houses, tide traps, meeting places, or simply attempts to mark a patch of ground as belonging to someone. Their stones have been rubbed smooth by generations of hands and weather alike.

Paths exist only as sequences of safe surfaces. A traveler learns the shape of the land by memory: the long ridge of hard blue shale; the white basin that must be crossed at low tide; the narrow stone causeway where the crawling bands always pass at dusk; the cracked black shelf that sings before a storm. Footprints do not last. The mud swallows them, rain fills them, or some animal crosses them within minutes.

Tools are simple but carefully made. Stone blades with edges renewed by pressure flaking. Bone awls. Shell scoops. Hammerstones with grooves worn by thumb and palm. Hooks made from sharpened limb-spurs. A little metal exists, traded from somewhere beyond the flats or pried from exposed seams in the old earth: soft, dark metal, beaten into thin plates for cutting, reflecting light, or making the loudest possible signal against stone.

Fire is rare. The wind dislikes it, the rain ruins it, and dry fuel is almost unknown. When a flame does survive inside a sealed hut, it is fed with rendered fat and burns blue in a clay bowl. Its smoke clings low to the roof, making the inhabitants’ eyes red and watery. A single fire in this country is a serious thing: warmth, light, food, danger, and a visible declaration that somebody has found shelter for one more night.

The dimness is not simply cloud cover. Even at midday, the light arrives weakened, filtered through constant moisture and high pale overcast. Colors have learned to be restrained: black water, iron-gray mud, white mineral flats, the bruised blue of distant stone. Animal bodies supply most of the brightness—rust-red throat fans, milk-pale armor, sudden yellow sacs flashed in warning, wing membranes veined violet when lifted against the sky.

At evening, which is difficult to distinguish from afternoon, the water begins to hold what light remains. Every pool becomes a dull mirror. The horizon disappears because sky and flooded land take on the same color. Herds move as dark interruptions in the reflection. The scattered huts glow faintly through hide shutters, their firelight trembling across the wet ground.

Then the ancient channels begin to speak more loudly.

The tide is returning from far beyond sight. It enters the country through routes established before any living creature had names for them, through cuts worn into the stone when the world was younger and the water perhaps deeper. It pushes around the flattened ridges, fills the old basins, rises over the paths. Animals call from every direction. The flyers descend. The crawlers gather on the highest shell bars in heaving masses. People close their low doors, weigh them with stones, and listen to the immense, familiar noise of the land rearranging itself.

By morning, some of the plain will be where it was.

Some will not.
