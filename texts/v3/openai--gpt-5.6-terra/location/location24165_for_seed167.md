The city lies below the tide line, though no one remembers a time when it stood in air.

It occupies a low basin of drowned earth: a broad, warm shelf where old hills have worn down to blunt ridges and the sea has patiently filled every interval between them. The water is not clear. It is black-green in the deeper streets, tea-colored near the flats, full of suspended silt and pale drifting grains from shells ground down over generations. Above, there may be a surface—somewhere beyond the dim reach of sight—but it is rarely visible. The water overhead is too thick with night, plankton, cloud-shadow, and the slow collapse of rain.

The settlement has no lamps. It has no engines, wires, glass windows, forged hinges, or working machines. Yet it is undeniably a city.

Its buildings are grown, piled, inherited, and repaired by hands. Foundations are made from old reefstone and slabs of water-rounded limestone. Walls rise in heavy courses of shell, coral, compacted mud, and the fossil-white ribs of enormous sea creatures. Some houses are simply chambers excavated into soft rock beneath overhangs. Others have been assembled around living columns of sponge and coral, their rooms shaped over years rather than built in a season. A street might begin as a natural channel, then become a lined passage, then narrow between doorways marked by braided reed ropes, then vanish under the swollen mouth of a grotto.

Nothing is quite square. Corners are softened by water and age. Stairways slope rather than climb. Roofs are low domes, shell vaults, or mats of tightly woven sea grass held under stone weights. The city has settled into its element. It does not resist the sea so much as make terms with it.

At every doorway, thin curtains of eelgrass, bead strings, fishbone combs, or dangling strips of cured skin move gently in the current. Inside are nests of woven bladderweed, sleeping hollows padded with moss from brackish shallows, stores of shellfish in netted pockets, racks of dried kelp held in the few chambers that rise close enough to the surface for air to collect. There are no fires. Warmth comes from the water, from bodies crowded together, from volcanic seepage beneath the basin, from vents in the sea floor where mineral-rich heat sighs out of cracks in the stone.

The water is warm enough that bare skin does not ache in it. It carries the heavy, intimate heat of a flooded summer cellar. In the deepest alleys, the temperature changes subtly: a cool current trailing from a fissure, a sudden milk-warm plume from the mud, a ribbon of saltier water sliding along a wall. The inhabitants know these movements as people elsewhere know wind. They choose sleeping places by them. They hang food where currents will keep it fresh. Children learn to close their eyes and identify neighborhoods by the taste of water on the tongue.

There is sparse vegetation, but what grows has made itself important.

Low forests of pale eelgrass bend across the shallows beyond the city. They are not lush; the blades grow in scattered clumps, leaving broad stretches of bare sediment between them. In protected courtyards, dark red weed clings to walls. Small leathery shrubs of mangrove root find occasional purchase on higher ruins, their roots descending like knotted fingers into submerged rooms. Luminous algae bloom in some cracks, no brighter than the green ghost of a distant moon. They are harvested carefully, carried in sealed shells, and used not for illumination exactly, but as a wayfinding presence: a faint stain of life along a threshold, a living mark on the walls of a communal passage.

Most of the city is stone, shell, skin, mud, and water.

And animals.

They are everywhere, close and unafraid in the old parts of town. Silver fish gather beneath arches in dense revolving clouds, splitting around passersby and knitting together again behind them. Eels live in drainage channels and household basins, sleek as cords of polished charcoal. Crabs occupy the lower stories of abandoned buildings, cleaning mortar from the seams and carrying scraps into rooms too small for a human arm to reach. Rays lie half-buried in the silt of public squares, their eyes raised, their wings lifting and settling like slow breathing.

There are larger creatures beyond the city limits. Long-backed sea cows graze the sparse meadows of grass with grave concentration. Schools of narrow-toothed fish move along the old roadways at dusk, following the corridors as though they were migratory rivers. In the upper water, unseen by most, something immense sometimes passes between the surface and the rooftops. Its shadow lowers the darkness by a degree. The whole city feels it. Fish vanish into cracks. Dogs—if dogs they are, web-footed and seal-sleek descendants of land animals—press close to their keepers. Then the shadow moves on without sound.

Predators exist, but the city has become part of the food web rather than its master. Nets are set at the edges of channels. Shellfish beds are tended. Fish are taken in modest numbers from the great turning schools. The dead are given to particular deep wells, where blind pale creatures consume them and leave the bones clean. This is not considered grim. It is a civic arrangement. The wells have names, and each family knows which one receives its dead.

Sound does not travel here in the expected way.

There is almost no clamor. The sea takes sharp noises and folds them into distance. A hammer striking stone is a muffled knock. A voice is not shouted; it is released in low calls that vibrate through the chest and walls. People speak near one another, with faces close, hands often touching an elbow or shoulder to anchor attention in the wavering dark. Through the water come the small domestic noises of the place: shell cups clicking together; the scrape of a knife made from ray spine; the soft, repeated bite of crabs on stone; a child’s laugh rendered strange and bubbling; the wet flap of a net being pulled through a doorway.

At intervals, a whale-like call rolls over the basin from far offshore. It is too low to hear with the ears alone. Doors tremble in their frames. Sediment lifts from ledges. People pause in the middle of whatever they are doing and feel it pass through their ribs.

There are plazas, though they are not open to the sky. One is a broad circular hollow formed from the collapsed dome of an ancient building. Its floor has been worn smooth by generations of feet, fins, tails, and currents. Around it stand communal halls whose entrances are indicated by clusters of carved shells. Markets convene there during the tides that bring clearer water. Fishers arrive with baskets of striped mollusks, salt-flowers from the mineral vents, lengths of shed skin, bundles of medicinal weed, and the polished teeth of things caught in deep nets. Goods change hands by touch as much as sight.

No coins endure in water. Value is remembered.

The city’s rules are also remembered, sung in slow phrases that can be carried through the corridors. Do not block a current-path. Do not take eggs from a nesting wall. Do not cut living reefstone except at the appointed seams. Do not enter the warm wells alone. Return borrowed shells clean. Leave the dead to the deep. When the black tide comes, seal the lower doors and wait without calling out.

The black tide arrives several times each year.

It is not a tide in the ordinary sense, but a migration of water: colder, darker, laden with deep silt and blind drifting organisms. It comes through the outer channels with enough force to overturn boats, uproot grass, and fill whole districts with opaque blackness. The city has been shaped by it for so long that its lowest streets are designed to flood. Walls contain narrow pressure-gaps. Courtyards have sacrificial gates made of brush and reef branches. Families retreat upward into crowded chambers, carrying food, young, and the small living lights of algae inside sealed shell jars.

Then, for a day or two, the city is entirely dark.

Not merely unlit: erased. No roofline, no hand, no familiar door can be seen beyond the reach of a finger. In that darkness, people tie themselves together with braided cords and wait as the water speaks against the walls. The fauna changes. Strange transparent fish drift in from below. Pale worms adhere to windows of shell. Sometimes a creature from the deep is stranded in a square or alley, enormous and soft-bodied, opening and closing some unseen mouth.

When the black tide leaves, it has rearranged little things. A stair is gone. A channel is deeper. A familiar wall has a fresh crack full of new coral. An abandoned room has become a nursery for fish. The city repairs itself, not quickly, not perfectly, but with the unhurried competence of a place that expects to be changed.

Beyond the clustered buildings, the drowned basin extends outward in sections: reed shallows, mud flats, coral shelves, fields of broken masonry, low ridges of worn rock where old land still shows its shape under the sea. Farther away are the drowned roads, broad pale scars leading nowhere visible. They are used by caravans of swimmers and animal-drawn rafts. Some routes follow the backs of old embankments. Others pass through tunnels crowded with sleeping fish. Travel is never entirely safe, but no part of the region is truly empty. There are fishing hamlets in flooded quarries, shrine-caves around warm springs, and families living in the hollow interiors of colossal shells lodged along the coast.

At the edge of vision, when the water briefly clears, the city seems less like a ruin than a reef that has learned to remember people.

Its towers are not tall. Its streets do not reach upward. It spreads laterally through darkness, dense with bodies and doorways, intimate as a cluster of barnacles on a hull. Every surface bears evidence of use: handholds worn into stone, shell mosaics marking safe passages, old ropes furred with algae, scratches where generations have measured children’s height, claw marks where animals climb in and out of shared water chambers.

The inhabitants move through this world with a patience that would look solemn from elsewhere. But there is humor in the city, and appetite, and stubbornness. People race through canals. They train bright fish to return to particular whistles. They decorate their homes with impossible objects recovered from deep places: a perfect spiral of bone, a stone that rings when tapped, a black pearl too large to wear, the intact skull of an animal no one recognizes. They tell stories about the dry world in the way people tell stories about dreams—certain that it happened, uncertain whether its rules could ever have been real.

Above them, the sea continues.

Rain may strike its surface, making a pale distant trembling. Storms pass as changes in pressure and the long flexing complaint of the water. Day exists only as a faint dilution of darkness, a grayness filtering downward for an hour or two. At night, which is most of the time, the city is held in its own warm obscurity.

Still, it is alive. Not despite the flood, not imprisoned beneath it, but made particular by it: a low, crowded settlement of shell and stone, quiet voices and moving shadows, enduring in the dark water where land has become memory.
