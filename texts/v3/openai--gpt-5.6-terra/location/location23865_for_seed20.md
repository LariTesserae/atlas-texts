At a height where mountains ought to be cold and bare, there is a basin full of water with no visible edge.

It is not a lake. A lake has a surface, a shore, some agreement with gravity. This water occupies the whole small world: a bowl perhaps the size of a village square, cupped among fresh black ridges of stone. It rises from the floor in slow, heavy folds and hangs in the air between the cliffs. Sheets of it stream upward. Other sheets descend. Some hold themselves vertical, trembling like glass curtains, and through them the farther walls appear bent, doubled, briefly replaced by depths that cannot belong inside so small a place.

The basin was formed recently. Its rocks still have knife-edges. Splintered planes of dark basalt spear up from below the water, clean enough to look broken yesterday. Here and there a face of pale mineral cuts through the black, bright only by comparison in the perpetual blue-gray murk. The stone has not yet learned to soften. There is almost no soil. What little has gathered lies in pockets beneath overhangs, a black paste threaded with white roots.

Warm water moves over everything. It is warm in the way breath is warm, or blood. Its currents press against the body from several directions at once. They carry a mineral tang, iron and salt, and a sweetness like crushed leaves left too long in rain.

There are plants, but very few. They cling where they can: mats of dark green skin over a seam in the cliff; pale fronds growing upside down from a ledge, their tips continuously combed away by the current and growing back; a single shrub-shaped colony of something red and translucent rooted in a crack in the basin floor. It opens and closes its many blunt leaves according to rhythms that do not match the water. When it opens, the water around it briefly becomes clearer. When it closes, the darkness deepens.

Nothing grows tall. Anything tall would be torn loose.

The sound is absolute.

It is the sound of water under pressure, of a storm heard from inside its own throat. Roaring, hammering, booming impacts arrive through the stone as well as the airless water. The vertical curtains of current slap against the cliff walls with enough force to shake gravel loose. Somewhere beneath the basin a deep pulse repeats at irregular intervals, and each time it does, every hanging sheet of water tightens, as if listening.

Close sounds are swallowed by the greater noise. A hand striking rock makes no audible knock. A cry exists only as a bubble against the mouth. Yet in moments between the pulses, impossible small noises can be heard with astonishing clarity: grains clicking down a slope, the soft tear of algae, a distant bell note from nowhere.

There are remnants of construction around the upper rim, though the word *construction* is too confident for them. Four short pillars stand on one ridge, each worn smooth on the water-facing side. A square opening has been cut into the basalt beneath them. It leads inward for only a few steps before ending in an unbroken wall. On another ledge, a chain of stone bowls has been fitted into the cliff face; water passes through them one by one, overflowing in different directions, though none of the bowls are level.

No tool marks remain. Perhaps they were made by hands. Perhaps the rock simply remembers being arranged.

The ruins do not look old. Nothing here looks old. Even the pillars seem newly delivered by some enormous flood and set carefully down, except that the water has already begun to claim them. Fine green growth circles their bases. In the square doorway, a white root has found the central crack in the wall and is patiently widening it.

Fauna is scarce enough that each sighting feels ceremonial.

Sometimes a long, colorless body slips through a wall of falling water. It has no apparent fins, no visible eyes, only a narrow opening that flares and closes along its underside. It moves against currents that would pulverize ordinary fish. Once it passes, the curtain behind it continues to carry a faint silhouette for several seconds, as though the animal has left an absence in the water.

There are also small black shapes that cling to the sharp rock. They might be snails. They might be seeds. When the basin’s deep pulse comes, they loosen in unison and drift upward into the dimness, where they vanish. Hours later—perhaps moments later—they appear again on entirely different walls.

Above is not sky, exactly. The basin is open, but the opening is filled with water too: a high roof of dark, boiling blue through which dim light filters. Occasionally a brightness moves across it, huge and slow. It could be cloud cover beyond a flooded world. It could be the underside of an ocean. It could be something looking down.

The rules are local and fragile.

Water falls when it remembers falling. It climbs when the stone calls it. Loose fragments of rock tumble downward, then circle the basin once, twice, and lodge themselves higher than where they began. Light collects in corners rather than spreading. The warmer the water becomes, the darker it appears. Things viewed through two curtains of current are seen not farther away but earlier: a pillar whole before it cracked, a red plant not yet rooted, a figure standing in the square doorway with water streaming from its shoulders.

No figure remains when the water shifts.

At the center of the basin is a low island of basalt, scarcely wider than a room. It surfaces only intermittently. During the pauses between great surges, its black crown rises out of the surrounding water and steams in the dimness. On it rests a shallow hollow full of still water, impossibly still, untouched by the violence all around.

The hollow reflects a clear, dry sky.

For a few seconds, it shows birds passing overhead. Thin grass bending in wind. A sun so bright it seems invented.

Then the deep pulse strikes from below. The island sinks. The reflection breaks. The deafening water resumes its argument with the newly made stone, and the entire high basin shudders, submerged in its own weather.
