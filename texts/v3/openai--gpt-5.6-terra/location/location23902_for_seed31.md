The city sits high in a broad volcanic basin, where the ground is still young enough to look unfinished.

Black ridges rise around it in broken arcs, their slopes sharp and only lightly softened by weather. Some are old lava walls, folded and split, with red-brown seams showing through the dark stone. Others are pale, fresh-looking shelves of ash-bound rock, too angular to have belonged to the mountains for long. The basin floor is not flat so much as gently gathered: a shallow country of wet meadows, reedless pools, low knolls, and channels where water has found every crack in the new land.

Water is everywhere, though rarely in dramatic quantities. It lies under the turf, shines in the wheel-ruts, beads on stone, and rises in dark clear eyes among the grasses. The roads of the settlement are raised slightly above the surrounding ground, made from fitted slabs of vesicular basalt and packed gray ash. Along their edges run narrow drainage cuts, always carrying a thin, nearly soundless flow. In places, the channels disappear beneath houses and emerge again on the far side, clear and cold, carrying threads of mountain mineral.

The air is warm enough that people work without heavy clothing, but the altitude keeps the warmth restrained. Mornings begin with mist gathered in the basin. It hangs low over the ponds and between buildings, not obscuring the city so much as making every wall appear a little farther away. By midday it thins. The sky becomes a pale, substantial blue, crossed by long white clouds that seem caught on the black teeth of the surrounding heights. In the evenings, moisture returns to the stones. Roofs darken. The whole place smells faintly of wet clay, smoke, crushed green leaves, and the metallic clean scent of springwater.

Vegetation has come quickly to the basin but has not yet grown dense. Short warm-green grasses cover most of the open ground, interrupted by mats of fern, shining mosses, and clumps of broad-leafed shrubs rooted wherever soil has collected. Slender trees stand far apart along the wetter channels: smooth-barked, pale-trunked things with sparse crowns and long drooping leaves. Their roots are exposed in places, gripping black stone and reaching into water. There are no deep woods here. The land has too recently emerged, or too often remade by heat and ash, to support a forest. Instead there is openness: a great deal of sky between one cluster of trees and the next.

No animals move through that openness.

There are no insects in the grasses, no birds on the roofs, no frogs in the ponds. Water lies without the quick punctuation of feeding things. Leaves stir when wind passes, and rain makes rings on the pools, but the world contains no calls, wingbeats, hooves, or hidden small lives. The people of the city do not speak of this absence as strange. It is simply a quality of the country, like the dark volcanic stone or the water close beneath the ground.

The settlement is built with patience and local materials. Its houses are low, thick-walled, and slightly irregular, formed from stacked basalt blocks held with pale mud mortar. Most have flat roofs made of layered reeds, clay, and stone chips, with shallow gutters leading to cistern jars. A few larger communal buildings rise two stories, their upper walls made from sun-dried brick where stone has become too heavy to lift. Their windows are narrow, protected by woven shutters. There is little glass, little metal, and no polished ornament except where water has worn a stone smooth.

At the center of the city stands a broad paved square surrounding a spring. The spring does not gush; it wells up steadily through a ring of dark stones, fills a rectangular basin, and leaves by four measured channels. People collect water there in fired-clay vessels. They wash fibers, rinse tools, soak roots and grains, and sit on the basin edge in the warmth of the afternoon. The spring is the city’s clock, though it never changes its voice or volume. It has flowed at the same pace for longer than anyone remembers.

Beyond the square are workshops open to the air. Potters turn coarse gray and red clay on hand-driven wheels. Stoneworkers shape blades, bowls, pestles, and paving blocks with harder stone. Basket-makers weave the tall wetland grasses into panels, carriers, screens, and roof underlayers. There are copper objects in the city, but few: a small knife passed between households, a ceremonial pin, a narrow awl kept wrapped in cloth. Most tools are of stone, boneless wood, fired clay, and carefully selected rock.

The streets are quiet because the city has learned to keep its sounds close. Conversation is low. Work is rhythmic but subdued: the tap of stone on stone, the brush of feet over damp paving, the soft slap of wet clay, the occasional creak of a carrying frame. Smoke rises from roof vents in thin blue columns and is taken away almost immediately by the high air. There are no carts rattling over stone, no beasts harnessed to burdens, no market cries carrying from quarter to quarter. Goods move by hand, in baskets, on shoulder poles, and along small water channels where sealed jars can be floated slowly from one district to another.

At the basin’s margins, the city loosens into gardens. These are not enclosed plots so much as long damp beds lifted above the saturated ground with stones and woven retaining walls. Warm-rooted vegetables, leafy herbs, and a grain with pale tassels grow there. Water reaches them by seepage rather than labor. Each bed is cut with narrow grooves that can be opened or blocked using flat stones. The system is old in practice, though the land beneath it is young: every channel follows a natural line of wetness, every wall acknowledges the pressure of water rather than trying to conquer it.

Farther out, the gardens give way to open marsh pasture with no livestock upon it. The grasses grow long and silver-green at their tips. Pools reflect the mountains. Here and there stand simple marker posts, each wrapped with strips of faded fiber, indicating safe ground, buried channels, spring sources, or the limits of a household’s cutting rights. The people harvest reeds, peat-dark soil, clay, and certain tough shrubs from these margins. They leave the rest unclaimed, partly because the ground is too wet to settle, partly because no one sees much reason to fill every empty place.

The surrounding mountains are visible from almost anywhere in the city. Their slopes carry scattered green in the gullies, bare black rock on the faces, and occasional pale streaks where water descends over mineral deposits. Some cliffs seep constantly, their surfaces draped in moss and thin curtains of wetness. The water does not fall loudly from great height. It gathers, runs, and disappears into the basin soil. A traveler walking toward those ridges would find narrow passes, high shelves of grass, steamless warm springs, and areas where the ground feels hollow underfoot, layered with old ash.

Nothing here seems to be advancing toward ruin or abundance. The city does not sprawl, and the marsh does not swallow it. The channels hold their courses. The springs maintain their level. The mountains remain sharp at the edges, but they do not visibly crumble. Rain comes often, gently, predictably enough that roof-building and planting have become settled arts. The people repair walls where they soften, clear silt from drains, renew mud plaster, and replace worn paving stones. Their labor is not a struggle against disaster. It is a continual fitting of hand-made order to a landscape that has, for now, become still.

At night, lamps of rendered plant oil burn behind shutters and in shallow alcoves beside the streets. Their light is warm and small against the wet black stone. Mist returns to the basin, and the city becomes a pattern of dim rectangles, gutter-lines, roof silhouettes, and faintly shining pools. Beyond the last houses, the open ground waits under the mountains. Water moves under it. Grass bends. No creature calls from the dark.

The silence is not empty. It is full of held water, cooling stone, sleeping people, and the long, quiet permanence of a young land that has stopped changing just long enough to be inhabited.
