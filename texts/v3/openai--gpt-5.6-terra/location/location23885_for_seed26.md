Below the city, below even the oldest foundations and their forgotten service tunnels, there is a constructed valley.

It is not called a valley on any official plan. Plans call it the **Condensate Recovery Basin, Sector Nine**, though no one who has stood at its rim would mistake it for a basin. It extends too far. Under a low, ribbed ceiling lost repeatedly in vapor, its floor descends in broad stepped shelves: black slagcrete, sheets of riveted iron, terraces of broken ceramic insulation, and long embankments assembled from the crushed remains of earlier machines. The place is wide enough to have weather.

Water is always present, but never abundant in a comfortable way. It beads on handrails. It shines in every seam of the ceiling. It gathers beneath pipes and drops, one heavy cold drop at a time, into channels that run alongside the roads. In the lower shelves, the channels widen into dark reedless marshes contained by steel lips. Their surfaces tremble from machinery somewhere below. Sometimes a burst of warm exhaust fog passes over them and they vanish completely, concealed beneath a low white skin of vapor; then the fog thins and they return, black and reflective, holding upside-down images of lamps and gantries.

The cold comes through the structure. It lives in the metal.

Some surfaces are furred with pale frost, especially the undersides of stairways and the shaded walls of abandoned pump houses. Other surfaces sweat. A person walking here moves continually between these conditions: boots cracking across ice-crusted grating, then slipping through a film of oily water; breath smoking in the open vaults, then disappearing in a pocket of damp heat beside a turbine conduit. The air tastes of iron filings, wet concrete, ozone, old rubber, and the sour mineral smell of water that has traveled through too many pipes.

Everything has been made.

There is no bedrock exposed, no honest soil. Even the mud at the marsh edges is manufactured: a sediment of corrosion flakes, insulation pulp, pulverized gasket, soot, powdered lime, and the soft black residue washed out of filtration towers. It gathers in drifts where the drainage fails. It has begun to behave like earth. Tracks remain in it. Its surface cracks when the fans run dry for a few hours. In places, thin things grow from it.

They are not plants exactly, though they have learned the posture of plants. Grey-green cable moss grips bolt heads and drinks from condensation. Stiff translucent fronds emerge from splits in foamcrete walls, their blades threaded with copper. Along the warmer water channels, black brushlike colonies spread in close patches, expanding from discarded heat-exchanger mesh. They bend in the drafts. Every few days a maintenance crew burns them back with a strip of blue flame, and the next month they are there again, slightly farther downstream.

The valley is young, or young by underground standards. Its surfaces have not yet settled into use. Fresh cuts reveal pink aggregate beneath the grey facing. Whole banks have slumped where a drainage duct failed, leaving a raw fan of gravel and broken pipe sloping into the basin. A new spillway has bitten a bright, jagged channel through three tiers of old flooring, and its water falls in silver ropes over exposed rebar. The erosion here is not wind or river work alone. It is pressure, vibration, thermal expansion, acid runoff, and the patient sawing motion of cables under load.

At intervals, the ground shudders.

Far across the basin, freight lifts rise and descend through vertical shafts, carrying pallets of cast housings, bundled pipe, or sealed containers without markings. Their warning bells drift through the dimness. Conveyor bridges cross overhead in long mechanical diagonals, their rollers clattering steadily even when nothing rides them. There are pumping stations on the distant shelves, each lit by one or two weak sodium lamps, their windows glowing amber through grime. When their pumps engage, the marsh channels begin to move. Water pulls itself away in strips, revealing slopes slick with mineral scum and the occasional drowned tool.

The loudness is continuous, but not uniform. There is the deep heartbeat of compressors. The hard cyclic hammer of stamping works beyond a wall. The high whine of a saw or bearing that should have been replaced years ago. Steam shrieks from relief valves and fades to a whisper. Water drips close by, individually and insistently, in the brief spaces between larger sounds. Sometimes the whole constructed valley seems to breathe in: fans change direction, vapor folds through the tiers, loose paper lifts from the floor, and every hanging chain begins to swing toward the same unseen current.

People are rare at this scale, and appear small in it.

A lone inspection cart may cross a causeway, lamp rotating, its wheels sending twin wakes through shallow water. Figures in reflective rain capes may gather beneath a gantry around a jammed valve, bright stripes moving in the murk like signals at sea. There are maintenance shelters, but most are empty. Their doors bear layers of painted instructions: KEEP DRAINAGE CLEAR. NO UNMASKED ENTRY. DO NOT FEED THE FILTER GROWTH. In some of the older shelters, the notices have been painted over so many times they have become thick, rounded plaques of language.

There are animals, though no one agrees how they arrived.

Small white insects gather in warm electrical cabinets, folding themselves into seams when the doors open. They have no visible eyes and seem attracted not to light but to the faint vibration of current. Occasionally one sees a pale rat-shaped thing on a pipe run, too still to be confidently called an animal; it vanishes behind a junction box with a soft metallic scrape. In the lowest channels, thin dark fish move through the runoff. They are said to be maintenance sensors that lost their housings generations ago. They are said to be fish. Both explanations are accepted because neither changes what they do.

The old workers say the basin has rules.

Do not stand beneath a dry pipe; dry pipes are preparing to fail.

Do not follow a light reflected in the water, because it may belong to a machine moving in a deeper service level.

If the warning sirens sound once, continue working. If they sound twice, find height. If they sound three times, hold something that is bolted down and do not look up.

And never remove the pale growth from the drainage markers. It is doing a job no one has managed to name.

At the farthest end of the valley, where the ceiling lowers and the industrial lights thin into a faint brown haze, a new district is being assembled. Crane arms move slowly over a field of foundations. Automated welders throw brief blue flashes against the wet walls. The structures there have no windows yet, only open frames and dangling bundles of cable. Water is already collecting in their floors. Frost has already formed on their upper beams. A few cable-fronds have already appeared in the seams.

The place is unfinished, actively becoming itself.

Nothing here is ancient enough to be trusted, and nothing is empty enough to be dead.
