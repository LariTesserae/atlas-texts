Below the city, beneath the packed foundations and the sun-warmed streets, there is a reservoir no larger than a public square. It is entirely made.

Its floor is laid in pale ceramic slabs, each slab broad as a door and slightly uneven, their joins sealed with black pitch. The walls rise straight from the water in stepped courses of fired brick, then curve overhead into a low barrel vault. The whole chamber is flooded nearly to the crown of that vault. There is only a narrow breathing space at the top: a long trapped pocket of hot air that trembles with every movement of the water.

The reservoir was made recently. Its bricks are still sharp-edged beneath their mineral haze. Chisel marks remain visible on the limestone blocks around the intake mouth. Wooden braces, darkened but not yet rotted, hold a few weak places in the walls. There are no ancient ruins here, no soft collapse of age. The place has the raw, clean severity of something finished only weeks ago and immediately put to use.

Light arrives from above through three square shafts. At street level they are covered by lattices of oiled bronze, but the sun comes through in hard white bars, broken by rippling water. The beams descend all the way to the floor. They make moving nets over the tiles; they set the brickwork flashing amber, green, and milk-blue. At noon the chamber is bright enough that every suspended grain of lime can be seen, each one turning slowly in the clear warm water.

There is no animal life in it. No fish in the depths, no insects skating in the air pocket, no silver beetles in cracks between the brick. The water is too fresh from the new channels, too regularly scoured, too enclosed. Its only inhabitants are the things that have been made: clay pipes, woven reed filters, bronze grates, lead weights, the descending limbs of a wooden sluice mechanism.

Along one wall, a hand-built waterwheel turns in a narrow channel. It is not an elegant machine. Its paddles are rough-cut boards, swollen at their seams, bound with copper wire and rope. Yet it turns with stubborn regularity, driven by water entering from a higher cistern somewhere beyond the wall. Each rotation pulls a chain of small earthenware cups upward. At the top of their climb they tip, spilling water into a trough that carries it through a row of punched clay filters.

The sound is immense for such a confined place.

Water pours constantly from the intake, a white, heavy sound. The wheel groans. The cups clack against their guide rail. Air whistles through the narrow throat of an overflow pipe. Every few minutes the sluice gate shudders in its frame with a wooden bang, and the chamber answers it with a deep rounded echo. Sound does not travel cleanly here; it comes back softened, enlarged, as if the reservoir itself has a slow voice.

The warmth is close and bodily. The flooded room holds the day’s heat long after sunset, and its vaulted roof sweats. Beads gather on brick and roll down into the water. In the air pocket, the smell is wet clay, pitch, heated wood, mineral dust, and the faint sour trace of soaked rope.

Vegetation has found only a few places to exist. At the bottoms of the light shafts, where dust and leaf fragments settle in small corners, thin green films spread across the brick. A handful of pale reeds rise from baskets of packed silt wedged beside the filtering troughs. Their roots are not decorative. They are part of the reservoir’s work, drinking impurities from the water before it is passed onward. Their leaves reach into the air pocket and bend under the wet weight of condensation. Nothing grows thickly. Every living green thing appears placed, tolerated, watched.

The rules of the room are simple and visible.

Water enters high and leaves low.  
Light only comes from straight openings.  
Nothing soft lasts unless it is kept moving or kept wet.  
Nothing alive is permitted to multiply.  
Every surface must be reachable by hand.

There are ledges at intervals along the walls for maintenance workers to stand on, though most are submerged now. Iron rings are set into the brick overhead. A person entering would descend by a rope ladder through one of the shafts, lower themselves into warm water, and move through a room where the floor is visible but unreachable without diving. They would have to work slowly, feeling the made geometry around them: square corners, straight channels, the blunt edges of brick stairs fading into blue-white light.

In the deepest part of the chamber, the tiles slope toward a circular drain. The drain is capped by a bronze disk pierced with hundreds of small holes. Fine sediment gathers there in a faint rust-colored halo. Twice each day, the disk is lifted by a simple winch, and the reservoir empties with violent purpose into channels beneath it. For a short time the hidden room is not a water chamber but a shining basin of wet tile, dripping walls, exposed filters, and wheel-paddles hanging uselessly in the air.

Then the gate is shut. The incoming stream returns. Water advances over the floor in a smooth bright sheet, filling joints, swallowing steps, lifting bits of reed and lime dust, climbing the walls until the chamber becomes itself again: a small, loud, brilliantly lit underground sea, entirely built by hands.
