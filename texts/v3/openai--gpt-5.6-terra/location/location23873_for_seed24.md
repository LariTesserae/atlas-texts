The place is a shallow basin of dark stone, no larger than a small field, enclosed on three sides by low, worn rises. Nothing in it has been built. There are no walls, paths, stakes, fire rings, or discarded tools—only the suggestion that a hand with a sharp stone *could* work here, could split the flat black shale at the basin’s edge, could scrape fibers from one of the tough plants that live in the cracks. But no hand has done so.

The ground is mostly mineral: fine, warm dust packed hard as clay; plates of slate-coloured rock; pale crusts blooming around old seams in the earth. The crust is not salt exactly. It has a dull gray-green hue, the color of oxidized metal, and it forms in low irregular shelves where vapor once emerged from beneath the basin. There is no water in any recognizable form. No pools survive in the shadows. No dampness gathers under stones. The air itself is dry enough to make distance seem brittle.

At the basin’s lowest point lies a patch of powdery white earth, smooth and undisturbed. It resembles the bed of a vanished pond, though it has never held a pond in living memory. Its surface is subtly quilted with shallow polygons, each one no bigger than a palm. They do not crack further, do not shift with heat, do not crumble beneath the occasional falling grain of dust. The pattern has finished forming. Everything here seems to have arrived at its final arrangement long ago.

Sparse vegetation clings to the margins.

There are low thornless shrubs with narrow, gray leaves held close to their stems, as if conserving not moisture but darkness. Their roots run sideways beneath the hard ground, following hairline faults in the rock. A few stiff, grasslike fans rise between broken slabs. Their blades are thick and waxen, copper-brown at the tips, and when touched they give off a faint resinous scent: hot stone, bitter sap, something like old leather. One plant, growing alone near the white basin floor, has a swollen base and a crown of folded leaves. It appears almost geometric in the dark, its leaf edges lined with a faint dusty silver.

There are no animals. No insects tick in the grass. No wingbeats cross the hollow. Even the plants make little sound. Their leaves are too tough to whisper much against one another.

The air is warm, though the landscape is dark.

Above, the sky is concealed by an unbroken mantle of cloud or high dust: not storm cloud, not fog, but a ceiling that absorbs the stars and gives back only a diluted charcoal glow. It is enough to distinguish the basin’s contours after the eyes have adjusted. The low ridges become blacker bands against black-gray sky. Pale mineral deposits catch what little light exists. The vegetation is present mostly as interruption: a vertical line, a rougher patch of shadow, a silhouette against the smoother ground.

There is no wind in the basin proper. Sometimes, along the upper lip of the surrounding rises, air passes with a slow, dry breath. It does not enter. The hollow has its own stillness, preserved by its shape. Dust lies where it fell. A shed leaf remains beside the parent shrub until it breaks down into the same powder from which the shrub draws its scant sustenance.

The rock shows erosion, but not violence. Its corners have been softened by innumerable hot days and cool nights, its layers exposed in long, patient curves. A low outcrop at the eastern edge resembles a crouched animal only until one looks closely; then it becomes simply stone, banded and weathered, marked by natural fractures. The basin has no dramatic ruin in it, no active catastrophe. It is old without being ancient in a theatrical way. It has merely persisted.

Underfoot, the dust has a soft top layer over an almost ceramic hardness. A footprint would be visible for a while, especially in the pale central patch. But there are no footprints. The world has not been disturbed enough to need erasure.

At its boundary, where the basin meets the surrounding plain, the land opens into more darkness and more low ground. Yet the basin feels complete, a contained unit: a room without a roof, a dry bowl without water, a quiet place where the earth has stopped trying to become anything else.

Nothing happens here.

Warmth remains in the stones. The sparse plants continue their slow mineral work. The dark ceiling hangs overhead. The quiet is not expectant; it does not imply that something is about to arrive. It is simply the natural condition of this small, dry hollow: stone, dust, stubborn living growth, and the enduring absence of everything unnecessary.
