High in the oldest fold of the mountains, where the stone has ceased to rise or crack and has instead learned the long patience of bearing weight, there is a city inside a hollow scarcely wider than a small valley.

It is not marked on the outer slopes. From outside, the mountain is only a broad gray shoulder cut with dry ravines, sun-blasted and almost empty. Its upper stone is wind-polished, its fractures filled with pale dust. There are no streams. No snow remains long enough to be named. The mountain has been dry for so many ages that even the idea of rain seems foreign to it.

But behind a narrow cleft, reached by a stair of foot-worn basalt steps, the interior opens.

Not far. Not grandly. It is a contained place: a pocket under an overhanging roof of stone, a high enclosed basin whose walls lean close around it. The ceiling is cracked in a hundred places, but little daylight enters. Most of the light is a muted green-gray, filtered through hanging leaves and the pale reflective faces of mineral deposits. At midday, the basin is still dusk-like. At night, it scarcely changes.

The city has grown into that dimness.

Its houses are low and crowded, built from stacked shale, mudless fitted stone, woven reed panels, and slabs cut from the same ancient mountain. Roofs overlap one another. Ladders lean from roof to roof. Smoke-darkened cooking hollows are shared by several households at once. Small bridges cross alleys where the ground falls away into root-filled cracks. Every usable ledge has been made into a platform, pen, workbench, sleeping shelf, or shrine niche.

There is no metal bright enough to catch the eye. Tools are of bone, stone, fired clay, hard wood, and a dark glassy rock that breaks into keen edges. Clay jars stand everywhere, though they hold no water. They hold powdered roots, insect meal, rendered fat, seeds, shed feathers, dried fungus, ash, and the precious pale salts scraped from the cave walls. The people of the hollow know water only as a word for something that may exist down in the impossible lowlands. Here, nothing flows.

Still, the place is lush.

The vegetation has found other arrangements. Thick-leaved vines climb the walls and suspend themselves from the ceiling, their roots sunk into mineral seams. Broad waxy fronds rise from troughs of porous stone, opening their dark surfaces to the faint air that moves through the cleft. Mats of gray-green moss—not wet, never wet—spread across shaded walls and drink the breath of animals, the heat of bodies, the faint vapors released from cooking and compost. There are fleshy shrubs with translucent stems, clusters of dry-sweet berries, thorn trees no taller than a person, and pale curtains of lichen that billow whenever warm wind passes through the chamber.

The plants are fed by dust, droppings, breath, rot, and the slow chemistry of the mountain. Their leaves taste of salt and peppery stone. When cut, they do not bleed sap so much as release a cool, aromatic vapor. In the city’s darkest corners, certain fungi bloom from manure heaps and refuse pits, glowing only a little—enough to lend a bluish rim to a hand or animal muzzle.

Animals dominate the hollow.

They are in the rafters, under the steps, on the roofs, in the vegetable beds, among the piled firewood, in the walls. Flocks of cliff fowl crowd the upper ledges, round-bodied and gray, with hooked feet made for stone. Their eggs are hard-shelled and chalky, their nests built from dry vine fiber, rags, fur, and stolen hair. Small horned grazers move in nervous groups through the lanes, chewing the thorn shrubs down to their tough spines. Their bells are not metal but hollow seedpods strung around their necks; the clatter is wooden, rapid, and intimate.

There are long-eared burrowers in the lower alleys, shaggy insects the size of a hand beneath every overturned slab, lizards that live in warm chimney cracks, and pale night moths that gather around the fungus lamps. Predatory cats, lean and dust-colored, patrol the roofs. They belong to no one, though every household claims to have fed them. Their eyes gleam from gaps in the stacked stone.

Above all are the birds.

Their wings rasp against the ceiling. Their calls bounce from wall to wall until the hollow seems much larger than it is. At dawn—which arrives only as a slight whitening at the upper cleft—the birds begin before the people do: whistles, coughs, rattling throat-songs, harsh territorial shrieks. Then come the hoofbeats of the grazers, the chittering of insects, the whack of stone pestles, the scrape of hide sandals on old stairs, the low human calls passed between roofs. Sound cannot escape easily. It gathers in the basin, overlaps, and becomes part of the air.

The city is loud enough that silence feels dangerous.

Even its calm hours are full of chewing, wingbeats, scraping claws, coughing hearth smoke, seedpods knocking together, children calling to animals, and the dry rustle of leaves rubbing their waxy surfaces. Wind enters in pulses through the mountain cleft. When it does, every hanging vine shifts at once, and the birds rise in a cloud beneath the roof. Dust lifts from the lanes. The whole settlement seems to inhale.

Its age is visible everywhere.

The first walls may have been built against the chamber’s stone thousands of years ago; no one can say. New walls are not really new. They are additions to additions, small repairs fitted into the outlines left by old households. Stairs have been worn into polished hollows by generations of bare feet. Doorways have rounded at the shoulders. Animal paths cut through the vegetation along routes older than the present families. The central paving stones are glossy from use, and the carvings on them—spirals, hoof marks, birds, ladders, hands—have softened almost back into plain rock.

Nothing here is ruined exactly. The hollow has passed beyond ruin. It has settled into a durable complexity in which collapse, repair, nesting, cultivation, scavenging, and rebuilding are all the same continuous act.

At the deepest end of the basin is a dry cistern, though it has not held water within living memory. Its mouth is ringed with smooth stones and flowering succulents. The people keep it clear. They lower baskets into its cool darkness to store roots and eggs. Bats sleep in the upper shaft. The old say the cistern goes down farther than the mountain and that beneath its final floor is a different kind of world, one where water moves openly over the ground and makes a sound like continuous applause.

Few believe this literally. Yet in the hottest part of the day, when the roofs are warm, the air stale, and the animals restless, people sit by the cistern’s rim and listen.

Far below, perhaps only in the stone settling under its immense age, there is sometimes a faint, irregular tapping.
