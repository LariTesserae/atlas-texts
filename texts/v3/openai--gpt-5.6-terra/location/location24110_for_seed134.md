At this elevation the sea has no horizon.

It occupies a circular basin in the crown of a mountain: not a lake exactly, because it does not rest quietly in its bowl, and not an ocean, because its boundaries are visible from almost everywhere. The basin is perhaps a mile across, ringed by black young stone, its walls steep and freshly broken. Some faces are still so sharp that they look cut, though no hand has worked them. Pale seams run through them: salts, metals, trapped bubbles turned to mineral. The water stands nearly to the rim. In places it overflows in thin, continuous sheets, slipping down the outer slopes in curtains too fine to count.

The basin is deep enough that light fails before the bottom.

Above it, daylight is ordinary: a cold white day, clouded but bright, with the sun appearing now and then as a blurred disk through wet air. Below the surface, the same light descends in green-gold columns and is seized, broken, and carried away by moving bodies. The water is crowded. Not merely full of life, but compressed by it. Every span of open water is crossed by something: transparent bell-creatures beating slowly upward; armored, many-legged swimmers working along the rock; ribbons of muscular animals lacing themselves through one another without tangling; shoals of pale, blunt-faced fish that move as though pushed by a single enormous thought.

No plants grow here. The walls are bare except for a living film that may not be living in the usual sense: dark metallic skins that bloom around warm cracks in the rock, feeding on mineral-rich water. They spread in plates and feathery stains, then flake away in silver curls. Their edges glow faintly when disturbed. There are no weeds in the shallows, no forests of kelp, no soft green hiding places. Everything that lives must cling, swim, bore, filter, carry its shelter, or make shelter briefly from its own body.

The ground beneath the water is a field of recent eruptions.

Low cones of black glass rise from the floor, each one no taller than a house, their flanks glazed and rippled as if the stone had only recently stopped flowing. Between them lie drifts of pale mineral sand, constantly rearranged by currents too slow to notice moment by moment. Heat leaks from cracks. Not enough to warm the whole basin, but enough that a diver passing above one feels a sudden mildness against the legs, like exhalation. Around these vents gather the densest assemblies of animals.

They have the organization of machinery without the appearance of anything made.

Great filter-beasts anchor themselves around the vents in rings. Their bodies are segmented cylinders of dark shell, each as long as a small boat, but they are entirely alive: jointed plates flex, porous mouths open and close, soft red interiors pulse behind grilles of bone. They draw water through themselves with a deep, periodic concussion. The sound travels through the basin as a low mechanical thudding: a hundred pumps operating out of sequence.

Smaller creatures harvest what the great filters discard. Crabs with shovel-like faces scrape the mineral skins from the stone. Blind white worms cluster in the warm sediment, rising and sinking together in slow fields. Slick-bodied grazers carry colonies of luminous microbes in folds along their sides, producing blue flashes whenever they turn. Predators come in from the darker central water: flat, black animals with broad fins and mouths that unfold in concentric rings. They do not rush. Nothing here needs to rush. The water has been full for so long that every appetite has learned patience.

Yet the basin is loud.

The mountain holds the noise in. Water strikes the walls and returns in broad, delayed blows. Overflowing sheets hiss against the outer rock. The animals call, scrape, grind, snap, and pulse. Some communicate with voices like struck glass. Others produce tremors by drumming bony knobs against the basin floor. The larger swimmers release long, descending notes that can be felt in the teeth before they can be heard—calls so sustained that their echoes overlap, making the whole water body hum.

At intervals, the sound alters. Everything becomes quiet for several seconds, except for the falling water beyond the rim.

Then one of the deep animals rises.

It comes slowly out of the green beneath the shallowest light: a dense mass of flesh and shell, broad as a room, trailing dozens of folded limbs. Its back is studded with mineral growths that resemble factory chimneys, though they are only organs—heat-exchanging towers, perhaps, or resonating chambers. From these apertures it releases clouds of warm, rusty water. The cloud ascends, spreads under the surface, and rains down in a haze of iron particles. Whole shoals follow it. The smaller creatures feed upon the particles; the grazers feed upon those creatures; the black flat predators trail the grazers. A complete moving industry unfolds around the animal, mineral becoming body and body becoming mineral again.

There are rules to this place, though no one has stated them.

Nothing remains still unless it is stone.

Nothing bright is safe for long.

Warmth belongs to the depths, coldness to the rim.

A creature may pass through another creature’s territory only if it follows the current in the correct direction. Those that resist the basin’s slow circulation become confused, drifting again and again toward the same black wall, as if the water itself has decided they are no longer part of its pattern.

The current rotates around the central deep hole once every several days. From above it is barely visible: a subtle depression in the water, a dark wheel beneath the reflections of clouds. Every animal knows it. Some spend their lives spiraling inward, feeding and breeding as they descend from one warmer layer to the next. Others hatch near the vents and labor outward toward the cold rim, where the falling water carries their eggs over the edge and into lower valleys. Most do not survive that journey. Their bodies are returned to the basin in fragments, swept back over the lip in storm seasons, or consumed in the whitewater below.

The place is young. Its rock has not yet softened. Its slopes have not yet gathered soil. The basin’s rim has not learned how to be a shore. Even the water seems newly claimed by its inhabitants, crowded with experiments in armor, buoyancy, resonance, and hunger.

But it changes only slowly.

A day resembles the day before: cold daylight above, green pressure below; the steady turning current; the long industrial heartbeat of the vent colonies. Over years, mineral towers grow. Black cones crack open and emit a little more heat. The overflow cuts narrow bright channels down the mountain. A wall collapses with the sound of a mountain being swallowed, and for months afterward the basin is milked pale with rock dust.

Then it settles.

The animals return to their rings around the vents. The great filter-beasts resume their pumping. The whole high sea resumes its deep, enclosed labor beneath the sky.
