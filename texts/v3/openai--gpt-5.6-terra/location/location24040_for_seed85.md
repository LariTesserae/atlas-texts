At the top of a drowned mountain there is a town that has forgotten the sky.

It occupies a shallow saddle between two black ridges, no larger than a few streets if streets had been designed for drifting instead of walking. The mountain is young. Its stone still has edges: fluted basalt faces, fresh breaks like dark glass, pale mineral seams exposed cleanly by whatever violence lifted it from the deeper floor. Yet no current wears those edges down. No sand moves. No fronds bow in a tide. The water around the summit is perfectly held, cool and immense, without a visible direction.

The town sits inside it as a collection of dim geometries.

Its buildings are not made from masonry exactly. They are extrusions of a dark ceramic-metal substance, each wall smooth at a distance and intricate when approached: lattices finer than hair, layers of translucent pressure cells, tight seams filled with a dull internal glow. A few structures resemble ordinary towers, square and narrow, rooted directly into the stone. Others are open rings hovering a handspan above the ground, or long low halls with walls that curve around empty water. Nothing is brightly lit. There is no need, perhaps. The town’s illumination appears only in thin strokes: blue-white lines under eaves, a violet numeral floating inside a windowless shaft, the muted green pulse of a conduit running along the roadbed.

Roads cross the saddle in broad, flat ribbons. They do not rise or fall with the terrain so much as ignore it. Their surfaces are dry-looking despite the water, dark as polished slate, and bordered by little rows of metal studs. Some of these still show signals: one amber point, then another, passing slowly toward an intersection where nobody waits. At intervals, a road ends at a platform suspended over a drop. At the edge of each platform stands a vertical black door.

The doors lead nowhere that can be seen.

Occasionally their surfaces contain the faint suggestion of a depth different from the surrounding water: a square of darkness more absolute than night, or a reflection of rooms that do not exist in the town. They are sealed, but not abandoned. Beneath their frames, tiny mechanisms continuously rearrange themselves with the patience of crystals growing. The town’s machines have been maintaining it for an age that cannot be guessed from the stone.

There are no plants anywhere.

No weed has found a seam in the roads. No film of green lies on the towers. Nothing softens the mountain’s new black fractures. The absence is more complete than sterility. Even the usual underwater fur of algae and bacteria seems unable to take hold. The water is clear, almost polished, and carries only suspended specks of pale mineral dust, each one motionless enough to be mistaken for a defect in vision.

The only living things are rare enough to be startling.

Near the outer ridge, a pale animal sometimes hangs in the water: small, transparent, shaped roughly like a bell but divided by stiff radial bones. It has no visible mouth and never swims. It simply occupies its chosen place, a few meters above the stone, faintly lit from within by a cold ember. There may be a dozen in the whole basin. Or it may be one animal appearing again and again in different places, copied by some law of the water.

Far below the town, where the mountain falls away into darkness, something larger passes at intervals too long for a resident to call them intervals. A pressure changes. A distant blue curve moves behind the black slope. Then the water returns to its complete stillness. The town does not react.

Sound is sparse and close.

A visitor hears their own suit, their own blood, their own small disturbances in the water. Then, from somewhere inside the architecture, comes a nearly inaudible chord: not a warning, not machinery in the usual sense, but a sustained arrangement of tones that seem to make the surrounding darkness more orderly. In one plaza, a damaged fountain emits a single bubble every few minutes. The bubble does not rise. It remains above the fountain bowl, perfectly spherical, rotating slowly around an invisible center.

Elsewhere there are soft clicks, brief as stones touching in a pocket. They come from the walls when no one is near them.

The town has no visible inhabitants. There are rooms prepared for them.

Behind certain translucent panels are chambers furnished with objects whose purposes have worn away from understanding: reclining frames, articulated consoles, bowls of silver particulate, loops of black fiber hanging from ceilings. Some chambers hold chairs facing broad windows toward the abyss. Some are arranged around empty circular tables. In a central building, a whole floor contains small sealed apartments, each identical except for the pattern of dim light embedded in its walls. One is lit like a drowned dawn. One has a field of slow, invisible stars. One is nearly black, except for a small white mark that moves from corner to corner without changing speed.

The rooms remain pressurized differently from the water outside, but their doors open freely when approached. The town knows the difference between a visitor and debris. It does not seem to distinguish between visitor and resident.

Above, the mountain’s ridges rise into a ceiling of black water. The summit is high, though high has become an abstract condition here: a fact encoded in pressure gradients, in the faint chemical change near the upper rock, in the distant memory of air. No surface light reaches this depth. There is only the occasional diluted glow from above, too weak to define a direction, like moonlight filtered through miles of sleep.

The place is not ruined. That is the unsettling thing.

Its infrastructure is intact. Its corners are unworn. Its systems continue their careful, silent labor. Fine filaments emerge from the foundations and vanish into stone, measuring stresses that have not changed. Towers exchange data through pulses too subtle to see. The roads still designate lanes for a traffic that never comes. At the center of town, a small circular square holds a map projected into the water: a network of streets, tunnels, stations, and chambers, extending far beyond the visible settlement.

Most of the map is dark.

One small portion glows: this saddle, these buildings, these waiting doors.

The water has been here long enough to become part of the design, or perhaps it was always the intended occupant. Nothing leaks. Nothing floods. The boundary between city and sea has ceased to matter. The built world is simply another mineral formation beneath the water—more deliberate than basalt, less alive than coral, patient as both.

If one waits on the western ridge, beside the platform where the road stops over the abyss, a voice eventually arrives through the metal underfoot.

It is too quiet to be language at first. It comes as a vibration in the soles, a carefully measured sequence of pressure changes. Then a phrase resolves, not in any spoken tongue, but in the body’s sense of being addressed:

**Arrival recorded. Conditions remain unchanged.**

Afterward, the platform’s black door develops a thin vertical seam of light.

It does not open.

It only remembers how.
