At the top of the world there is a city that has not decided whether it is a mine, a furnace, or a mountain range.

It occupies a broad high basin between black, unfinished ridges. The ridges are not old enough to be graceful. They rise in blunt shelves and fractured spines, their faces raw with dark basalt, pale slag, and seams of metal that show through the stone as if the earth has been opened before its skin was complete. There are almost no slopes. Everything occurs in steps: colossal terraces, retaining walls, gantries, switchback roads, cuts made by drills and then abandoned halfway through the work. Beyond the city, the basin goes on for many miles, full of vents, spoil hills, cooling fields, and platforms whose purpose is no longer immediately visible.

The elevation makes the sky feel close and hard.

At midday it is an ordinary blue, though unnaturally deep over the distant ranges. The sun has no softness here. It strikes the pale mineral surfaces until they glare white; it warms the black stone until it radiates through boot soles and building walls long after sunset. But the thin air is not entirely dry. At certain hours, when hot vapors rise from cracks in the basin floor and meet colder air descending from the high peaks, moisture forms abruptly.

The city sweats.

Condensation gathers on the undersides of pipes, in the ribbed throats of ventilation shafts, along the shaded faces of concrete towers. It does not fall as rain often. Instead it collects into dense, bright beads and trembles there for hours. A network of gutter chains, ceramic channels, and polished catch-plates carries this water toward cisterns set beneath every street. In the lowest parts of the basin are a few reservoirs: black, steaming, intensely still. Their margins are crusted with salts, sulfur-yellow and green-blue mineral deposits, arranged in delicate geometries where the water has retreated and returned.

No grass grows around them. No reeds. No moss softens the masonry.

The absence of plants is not a result of neglect. It is a fact the city has been built around. Wind may carry dust from distant lands, spores may land on a roof or settle in a drain, but nothing roots. The ground is too hot in its shallow layers, too metallic, too newly made. The old municipal planting schemes remain visible only as rows of empty stone planters along certain avenues, and as iron frames intended for shade canopies that were never fitted with anything living.

In their place, the city has developed other kinds of green.

Oxidized copper stains walls turquoise. Cooling towers are streaked with lime-white evaporate. On surfaces near the vapor wells, mineral films grow in pearly bands—silica, salt, and unknown precipitates laid down one micron at a time. From a distance these deposits resemble lichen. Up close they are hard, glassy, and sharp enough to cut a finger. Every season—or what passes for a season in this elevated heat—the maintenance crews scrape them from critical machinery and sell the flakes by weight to glassworks and chemical guilds.

The buildings are dense, even where the landscape is open. They cluster around heat sources and water collectors: warehouses jammed beside pumping halls, worker dwellings stacked against foundries, transit pylons threaded through apartment blocks. The oldest districts are made of dark volcanic brick, their corners melted slightly, as though they were assembled while still warm. Later additions are concrete, corrugated metal, ceramic tile, and heavy lattices of rust-red steel. Pipes pass everywhere: overhead in bundled bridges, beneath grated walkways, through windows and kitchens, down the middle of alleys where children once might have played.

The streets have been designed for machines more than people. Slow crawler cranes move on broad tracks between quarries and processing yards. Cable cars drift from terrace to terrace, carrying ore carts, water tanks, workers in heat veils, and sometimes entire sections of prefabricated housing. Freight elevators climb the cliff faces in open cages. They are so large that a person standing inside one sees the city unfold beneath them as a field of roofs, chimneys, radiators, and black reservoirs.

Yet it is a quiet place.

There is no constant roar. The engines are insulated, or buried, or run on cycles too slow to hear. Most machinery is designed to work for decades without urgency: piston pumps making one deliberate stroke every few minutes, quarry drills pressing into stone over the course of weeks, conveyor belts moving at the pace of walking. From a rooftop, the dominant sounds are subtler: the dry ticking of cooling metal, a far-off cable humming in the wind, the occasional pressure release from a deep steam line. Water dripping into cisterns has become one of the city’s principal sounds. People know the quality of those drips: the clean sharp note of roof-condensate, the dull mineral-heavy sound of a pipe well, the dangerous irregular patter of a leak from the heated underworks.

The basin is still being formed beneath the streets.

This is understood, not feared. Surveyors mark it on public maps. Entire neighborhoods rest on foundations designed to rise, tilt, or be unbolted and moved. New fissures appear in paved courtyards. A wall that was vertical last year may be leaning by the width of a thumb this year. Sometimes a fresh vent opens beneath an unused rail yard, releasing hot breath and a smell like struck flint. The authorities do not rush to seal these things. They build a railing, put up reflectors, send a team to measure the heat, and wait to see what the ground intends.

The city’s greatest industry is patience.

It extracts not merely ore but time from the earth. Deep furnaces use the basin’s own heat to refine metals. Vast sealed chambers collect vapor and separate it into water, acids, and rare salts. In the eastern works, machines feed powdered black rock into long ceramic tunnels and produce sheets of volcanic glass, each one dark enough to reflect the sky. In the western yards, the glass is cut into solar baffles, pressure windows, and the glossy tiles used on the exterior of high-altitude homes.

At night, those tiles make the city seem full of dark water.

The lights are practical: amber lamps over stairways, white work strips under pipe bridges, red warning beacons on cranes and tower tops. Their reflections gather in the roofs and windows. The steaming reservoirs hold them too, so that the basin appears to contain scattered pools of molten color. Above, the stars are numerous and painfully clear. There is little haze at this elevation except near the vents, where vapor drifts through the streets in low, warm veils.

Rare animals survive in the margins of this constructed mineral world.

There are ash swifts, narrow-winged birds with pale, almost translucent feathers. They nest in the warm cavities behind condenser arrays, where the temperature remains steady through the night. They do not sing much. Their calls are brief, dry clicks, easily mistaken for debris striking metal. There are also the cistern moths: broad gray insects that emerge after condensation events and gather around the reservoir lamps. Their larvae live in the sediment filters, feeding on whatever trace organic material the city’s water system catches from the air.

Some workers insist there are larger things in the hot sublevels: white, sightless creatures moving through flooded maintenance tunnels, their skins hardened by mineral scale. The city archives contain no official record of them. But in certain old pumping stations, hand-painted signs advise workers not to enter alone when the pressure gauges begin to rise.

Most residents live above the heat, in high stacked quarters where the wind can find them. Their homes have thick walls, small shaded windows, and rooftop water plates that unfold each morning like silver leaves. The plates are not beautiful in the way gardens are beautiful, but people decorate them anyway: with colored wires, prayer tags, stamped metal birds, pieces of blue glass. On the rooflines, every surface that can collect a drop has been given a use.

Nothing is wasted because little arrives from outside.

Food comes by rail from lower regions, packed in sealed crates; much of it is grown in artificial rooms beneath the administrative terraces, under lamps and in imported soil. These gardens are restricted, protected, almost ceremonial. To enter one is to smell wet earth and leaf matter so unexpectedly rich that visitors often stand silent. The gardeners wear clean gloves and speak of pests with the solemnity of border guards.

The people of the high basin have rules shaped by their landscape.

No one may block a drainage channel, even temporarily. No one may build directly over a fresh crack without a geological warrant. Water theft is treated more severely than theft of metal. Every household must maintain a basin of clean reflective surface on its roof, both for condensation and as a signal to aerial surveyors. When the warning bells sound three low notes—not often, perhaps once in several years—everyone must open the heat shutters at the base of their buildings. This allows the city to breathe when the earth beneath it exhales.

Sometimes, in the late afternoon, the entire basin fills with a pale vapor. The towers disappear from their lower halves. Cable cars slide through blankness. The raw mountains become floating black shapes, and the industrial city, with all its pipes and rails and slow engines, seems less built than precipitated: a structure crystallized out of heat, pressure, and need.

Then the vapor thins.

The city returns, dense and deliberate, still climbing its unfinished mountain.
