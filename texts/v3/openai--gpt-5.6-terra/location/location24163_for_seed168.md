The high country begins without a foothill.

One moment there is only a pale, level plain of powdered stone; the next, the ground has risen into a broad tableland, not sharply, but with the slow confidence of something lifted from beneath and then left unfinished. Its slopes are raw. They have not yet acquired gullies, ledges, or the softened vocabulary of age. Great faces of ocher mineral stand exposed in sheets, scored only by seams where the earth has opened and then stopped opening. The plateau extends for many miles under an ordinary, bright sky.

Nothing here looks ruined, because nothing has ever been built.

There are no roads, pylons, fences, foundations, tracks, or discarded things. Yet the place has an industrial character so complete that it is difficult not to expect machinery beyond every rise. The machinery is geological.

Along the plateau’s upper reaches, black mineral ribs emerge from the crust in evenly spaced rows. They resemble conveyor belts half-buried in the ground, though they do not move. Some run for kilometers, vanishing into stands of vegetation or entering the walls of low, unformed hills. Their surfaces are dark, matte, and warm to the touch, made of a carbon-rich stone that has arranged itself in layered laminations like compressed belts of rubber and ore. At intervals, wide circular deposits of iron salts lie in the dust, perfect as turntables or loading bays. They are natural—not merely shaped like industry, but made by a chemistry that seems to favor repetition, alignment, and large-scale work.

The plateau is dry beyond ordinary drought. There is no pond hidden in a cleft, no moisture beneath the roots, no silver thread of river in the distance. The air has a faint mineral weight, not humidity but vapor: thin exhalations of ammonia, sulfur, and warm metallic gases rising from hairline cracks in the ground. They do not drift far. In the stillness, they gather close to their vents, blurring the stone with a low, wavering haze.

This is how the green things live.

Vegetation grows in lush, deliberate islands around the vents and mineral seams. It does not have leaves in the familiar sense. The largest growths are fans of flat, dark-green tissue, stiff and glossy as enameled sheet metal. They rise waist-high from the dust in dense rosettes, their edges tinted copper or violet. Among them stand pale, jointed columns, each one hollow and ribbed, drawing gases through their interiors. When sunlight strikes the columns, a faint internal amberness appears, as though chemical fires burn in their cores.

Farther across the plateau, the vegetation becomes stranger and more abundant. Broad meadows of low green mats fill shallow basins that have never held water. Their surfaces are velvety from a distance, but up close they consist of innumerable little plates, overlapping like scales. Each plate has a central dark pore. At dawn and late afternoon, the pores open to the air. By midday, they close, and the whole meadow takes on the sealed, lacquered appearance of a factory floor.

There are groves as well: tall mineral-fed trunks rising from bands of green. Their “bark” is white and finely ridged, almost crystalline. Instead of branches, each trunk divides high overhead into broad, folded blades that catch the sun. The blades are not moved by wind. There is scarcely any wind here. They remain held in their poses for years at a time, creating a canopy that seems less grown than manufactured, a roof of green fins above the empty land.

No creature crosses beneath it.

There are no insects in the blossoms, no birds in the open blue, no small motion in the mats. The absence of animal life is not eerie in the immediate way of a forest gone quiet; it is more fundamental. The plants have never expected pollination, grazing, nesting, or pursuit. Their flowers—if flowers they are—open directly toward mineral vapor. They are heavy cups of red-black membrane, filled not with nectar but with dry, sparkling crystals. When they mature, they split cleanly along their seams and release a light dust that rolls low over the ground until it finds a warm crack.

The sounds are too small to be called sounds.

There is the occasional delicate ping as a salt crystal contracts in the heat. There is a muted ticking from the black mineral ribs when the sun reaches a certain height. Near the vents, one can hear the softest continuous fizz, like a distant electrical line heard through a wall. It is possible to stand in a green basin for an hour and hear nothing else—not a wingbeat, not a branch moving, not water, not weather.

The light is clear and warm. It gives the raw rock a honey-colored brightness and turns the distant vegetation almost blue. Shadows are ordinary, dependable things: beneath the blade-groves, under the folds of the mineral fans, along the east sides of newly risen stone. There is no twilight mystery to the place. Its strangeness is fully visible.

Across the horizon, more land is still forming.

Low mounds of pale ash-colored material stand scattered over the plateau, each with a thin, dark fracture at its crown. They may be the beginnings of hills. Around their bases, the first green mats have already established themselves, arranged in rings according to the chemistry of the ground. In a few places, sheets of crust have buckled upward into arches only a few meters high, exposing glowing ocher minerals below. The arches have not yet weathered. Their edges remain sharp, almost soft-looking, as if they could still be pressed into another shape.

But they do not change. Not visibly. Not season by season.

The land is held at a point of becoming. Gas rises at the same patient rate. Salt blooms and remains. New stone pushes infinitesimally upward, but no rain comes to cut it, no river carries it away, no roots break it apart with the violence of thirst. The vegetation advances only where its mineral conditions already exist, filling its allotted spaces with astonishing richness and then stopping exactly at their borders.

It is a landscape of processes without events.

From a height, the whole region resembles a vast industrial site after all workers have gone—not abandoned, because it has never been occupied; not unfinished, because its incompleteness is its final condition. Green reactor-fields spread among raw terraces. Dark seams run like power lines through unmarked ground. Circular iron deposits gleam in the sun. White blade-groves stand over empty basins.

And everywhere, between these presences, there is space: warm dust, pale stone, clean sky, and the enormous quiet of a world that has learned to make life without water, motion, or witness.
