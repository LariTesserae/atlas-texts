Below the ordinary ground, beneath all known root-depth and bedrock certainty, there is a country made almost entirely of plant tissue.

It is not a cave in the familiar sense. There are no long mineral chambers, no dependable walls of stone. The place has been hollowed inside an immense buried organism—or perhaps a civilization of organisms grown together so completely that distinction no longer matters. The ceiling is a pale, fibrous vault far overhead, stretched in whorls and ribs like the underside of a leaf magnified to the size of weather. From it descend curtains of root-hair, white and green, so fine at a distance they resemble rainfall. They do not reach the floor. The floor, in most places, is too far away.

Vast empty basins open under the living ceiling. A person can stand on one of their shores and see no opposite wall, only the slow upward tilt of cultivated-looking green darkness becoming luminous haze. The hollows have their own climates. Cold winds pass through them in low, sudden streams, bending forests of mossy stems that grow from every surface. Clouds form below the roof: thin, milk-colored drifts of spores, steam, and pollen, caught in the chambers’ circulating breath. Sometimes they condense as a silver dew, beading on the leaves and dropping a few cold grains at a time. Then, for hours, nothing falls.

The brightness comes from the plants themselves.

Some of the great membranes overhead glow with a diffuse green-white light, as though they are collecting a sun on the other side of the world. Their veins brighten, dim, and brighten again in broad travelling pulses. There are meadows of bulbous lantern-fruit along the lower slopes, each fruit cold to the touch and filled with slow blue fire. Pale lichens cover the exposed ribs of the land in such abundance that a whole escarpment can shine like ice. In the clearest basins there is no night. There are merely intervals when the living illumination weakens enough for distant forms to become indistinct.

The air is cold, not sharply but thoroughly. It gets into clothing, hair, the seams of wooden tools. Breath hangs low over the ground. Every leaf bears a dark wet edge, though there are few streams. Water is held everywhere in the body of the place: in swollen stalks, in the soft walls, in veined sheets beneath transparent skin. Now and then a root ruptures somewhere high above. A narrow cataract spills from it, falls for an impossible distance, and scatters into mist before it reaches the basin floor. The wound closes quickly. In a day, the waterfall is gone, replaced by a glossy knot of new green growth.

The land is young in the way a fresh scar is young.

Whole regions appear to have pushed up recently from beneath the floor: angular towers of compacted stems, sharp folds of pale cambium, raw terraces where the green skin has not yet spread. Some are split by crevasses whose edges curl inward, actively knitting themselves as one watches. The terrain changes with tremendous, unhurried violence. A ridge may buckle over the course of a morning. A valley of fern-trees may sink when the spongy substrate beneath it drains into a deeper organ. Giant root-cables, thick as towers, flex in the walls and drag curtains of vegetation across the skyline.

There are storms, though they are not storms of rain.

At irregular intervals the entire interior begins to move. The ceiling’s luminous veins race with light. Warm vapor rises from cracks in the ground and meets the colder air overhead. Wind gathers around the basins, first stirring the grasslike fronds, then flattening them. Pollen lifts in yellow sheets. Seed-pods burst by the millions, cracking like distant brushfires. The air becomes full of spinning husks, white fluff, damp petals, needle-shaped leaves. Nothing is destroyed exactly; it is uprooted, carried, replanted elsewhere. A grove can migrate a mile in one turbulent hour, its mats of roots rolling over one another like the legs of a slow animal.

Afterward, the chamber is quiet again, though not still. New shoots rise visibly from disturbed soil. The smell is crushed green matter, cold sap, wet bark, and an almost metallic tang from the luminous molds.

Animal life is sparse and easily overlooked. There are pale long-legged browsers that emerge only at the edges of the bright fields, feeding on the hanging root-curtains with slow deliberate bites. Their bodies are nearly leaf-thin, their bones faintly visible in the glow. Small insects exist in warm clusters around certain flowering vents, but no birds cross the open spaces, no large herds mark the plains. Most movement belongs to the plants: tendrils seeking dampness, leaves turning toward a changing light, trunks shifting their roots before the next convulsion of the earth.

People have entered some of these hollows, or lived beside them long enough to make use of them.

Their structures are rare: low shelters woven from dead root-fiber and sealed with resin; narrow bridges of braided vine stretched across fissures; small platforms raised above the wet floor on pale wooden stilts. There are watch-huts at the mouths of passages where the chamber winds are strongest. Their inhabitants use stone blades, bone awls, fire-hardened digging stakes, and knives flaked from the hard, glassy shells of ancient seed-pods. Metal, if it exists here, is too precious to leave exposed. Most things are repaired rather than replaced. A rope is re-braided until it becomes thicker than the original rope. A shelter gradually becomes part of the vegetation around it, its walls swallowed by climbing leaves and reinforced with living stems.

The people do not cut the great plants without ceremony, not because the plants are sacred in an abstract way, but because the place reacts. A trunk felled in the wrong season can cause nearby root beds to tighten. A patch of luminous lichen stripped too bare will dim an entire route. A cut in a water-vein can flood a basin, or dry another basin a day’s walk away. The rules are practical, inherited, and never complete.

Do not sleep beneath a pale root that has begun to twitch.

Do not follow fresh green growth into a passage: it is often sealing behind you.

When the high leaves turn their undersides upward, find an anchor before the chamber wind begins.

If the light becomes blue, do not drink from open pools.

If the floor is warm in a cold region, leave it. Something below is waking or moving.

The greatest spaces have no permanent paths. Trails are marked with little hand-carved stakes, each topped with a bundle of dry, nonliving grass. Even these markers are unreliable; the vegetation incorporates them, the storms carry them away, or the ground rises and buries them to their necks. Travelers navigate partly by the slow circulation of air, partly by the color of the lamps overhead, partly by listening to the strain of roots in the walls. In the quiet, one can hear it: a deep vegetative creaking, miles of fiber tightening under tension.

At the center of some chambers lie open plains covered in a short, silvery plant that bends all in one direction, though there is no visible wind. Beyond those plains stand forests of translucent stalks. Inside each stalk, cold fluid rises and falls in glimmering columns. Farther still, a cliff may be visible, bright as dawn, where the buried world has split open and exposed a fresh interior layer of green-gold tissue.

No one agrees whether the land is growing upward toward the surface, or downward into deeper dark.

It feels less like a place that has endured for ages than a place still deciding what shape it will take.
