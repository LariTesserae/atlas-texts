The city is beneath a city, though no one agrees whether it was built as a foundation, a shelter, a mine, or simply somewhere for heat to go.

It occupies a low chamber in the earth, not vast enough to lose oneself in but too dense to see across. Every available wall has been claimed. Brick abuts raw black stone; iron balconies grow from concrete pylons; narrow dwellings are stacked in cavities that may once have been natural vents. Pipes run in bundled sheaves along the ceiling, sweating constantly. Their condensation strikes sheet-metal awnings, stair treads, the backs of electrical cabinets. It never becomes rain exactly. The water gathers as a mineral brightness on surfaces, beads, falls, vanishes into hot cracks.

The air is oppressive rather than stale. It moves through the chamber in deliberate currents, drawn by enormous intake fans somewhere behind grilles. It is hot enough that metal rails sting the palm. The warmth comes from below: engine rooms, insulated ducts, a buried industrial vein that hums under the paving. In places the floor is tiled over this heat, but the grout has split along fresh lines. A pale orange seam shows through. The entire settlement has the look of having been assembled quickly around a new and inconvenient fact in the ground.

There are streets, but they are more like passages between structures. They incline unpredictably over the chamber’s uneven floor. Some are paved in reclaimed stone blocks, some in corrugated steel plates, some in a hard gray poured material that still bears trowel marks. Along them stand kiosks with their shutters down, substations with warning lamps, water tanks painted in fading municipal colors, public washrooms, tool libraries, a small depot for wheeled furnace carts. The carts run on narrow rails and pass infrequently, silent at first, then accompanied by a vibration felt in the teeth. Their wheels are rubber-rimmed to preserve the quiet.

The city’s density is not expressed by crowds. There are very few people visible, if any. Instead it is crowded with arrangements made for people: doorways, clotheslines hanging limp above service alleys, numbered lockers, stairways ending at sealed hatches, ventilation masks drying on hooks, rows of drinking cups beside a communal tap. Each square meter has been given a task. The absence of animal life is noticeable here. No insects collect in the damp. No rats move behind the walls. There are no birds in the vents, no pale things in the drains. Food is stored in cabinets without fear of teeth. Garbage is sorted patiently and sealed, not because it will attract anything, but because this is an enclosed world and nothing can be allowed to rot freely.

Vegetation persists only where the infrastructure permits it. A thin garden runs along the warm runoff channel near the central pump house: gray-green reeds, hard little fans of sedge, a few vines with waxy leaves reaching toward the lamp housings. They survive on condensation and nutrient-rich water bled from the filtration system. Their roots knot around rusted grates. Here and there, a stubborn shrub has established itself in a crack between retaining blocks, its foliage dusted white with salts. The plants look less alive than engineered—restrained, purposeful, continually on the verge of drying into wire.

At the chamber’s lowest point is a plaza no larger than a public square. It was probably intended as a loading area. Now it is covered by overlapping canopies, laundry frames, heat baffles, conduits, and an elevated pedestrian loop constructed from old catwalk sections. The ceiling above it is low and obscured by vapor. Light arrives in diluted forms: amber bulbs under cages, blue maintenance strips, the intermittent flare from welding behind a canvas screen. Most of the city is dim enough that its farther portions appear constructed out of silhouette: tanks, ladders, pipe elbows, window grids, the squared shoulders of machinery.

The sounds are carefully contained. Fans murmur. Pumps click into operation and stop. Water ticks from pipe to bucket. Somewhere a generator gives a brief, low cough and settles. The rail carts make their occasional subterranean passage. Human sounds, when they occur, are softened by walls and heat: a door latch, a short exchange from an upper walkway, a radio playing behind glass. The chamber has been built to keep noise from traveling, perhaps because every sound has so few places to escape.

Nothing here is old enough to have become picturesque. The raw stone faces are sharp where recent blasting exposed them. The concrete retaining walls have not yet acquired stains deep enough to disguise their seams. New conduits still carry their colored identification bands: red for heat, white for condensate, yellow for power, green for filtered air. In the dimness, these colors give the streets a strange ceremonial order. It is an urban place still in the act of becoming urban, continually adding another bridge, another platform, another row of units against a wall that sweats and radiates warmth.

At intervals, a warning tone sounds—quiet, almost courteous. Then the ventilation shifts. Louvers turn somewhere above, and hot air begins to draw down through the streets. People close shutters. Fabric canopies tighten against their cables. Condensation stops falling from one set of pipes and begins falling from another. The few plants along the runoff channel tremble in the artificial wind.

The city does not have weather, exactly. It has systems: heat cycles, pressure cycles, the scheduled opening of valves, the unplanned release of steam. Its sky is a ceiling of rock and civic machinery. Its horizon is always another wall, another stair, another lit window across a gap. Yet there is a sense of movement everywhere—in wetness traveling along a cable, in mineral deposits slowly building white crusts on valves, in fresh construction inching into available darkness.

It feels temporary in the way a ship feels temporary, despite all the bolts and foundations. Everything is built for repair. Every wall can be opened. Every route has an alternate route marked with painted arrows. The place expects interruption: a new fissure, a failed pump, a hotter season below the floor, a section of stone deciding at last to become part of the city.
