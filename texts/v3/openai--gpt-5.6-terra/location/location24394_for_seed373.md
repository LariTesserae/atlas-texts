At the top of the escarpment there is a garden with no rain in it.

It occupies a ledge no larger than a village square, though the cliff around it descends so far that the bottom has lost all relation to distance. The walls of the ledge are dark, old stone: not a dramatic young volcanic black, but the softened charcoal color of rock that has endured long cold centuries. Their edges are rounded. Every crack has found its equilibrium. Pale mineral seams run through them like old stitches.

The garden is lush in the way a furnace can be lush—dense, active, self-sustaining—but it contains no water at all. No pools hide under leaves. No dew gathers in cups. Nothing drips. The plants have learned, or been made, to grow from the air’s thin charge and the mountain’s minerals. Their leaves are tough translucent membranes, green at their centers and silver along the veins, opening and closing as they harvest static from the constant weather. The soil is not soil. It is a fine rust-red grit mixed with powdered ceramics, black lichenstone, fragments of shed root-shell, and innumerable bright grains that turn slowly when no one is looking.

The vegetation grows in deliberate clumps, leaving astonishing distances between them. One grove of tall ribbon-leafed structures might rise from the eastern side of the ledge; then twenty or thirty empty paces of bare mineral floor; then a single broad, cushionlike thicket breathing gently against the cold. The emptiness makes the garden seem larger than it is. It has the proportions of a small sanctuary built around absence.

Above it, the sky is an ordinary blue in color and wholly extraordinary in behavior.

Clouds do not bring rain. They are dry white herds of electrically active dust, raced across the high air by winds too fast to see. At intervals they collide over the ledge. The collision makes light—not lightning exactly, but long rippling sheets of bluish brilliance that crawl through the sky and vanish behind the mountain. With each discharge comes a blow of sound. Not thunder rolling away, but an immediate, physical report that shakes the teeth, followed by a thousand overlapping metallic shrieks as the cliff’s embedded conductors answer it.

The garden is deafening.

Every stem sings. Every leaf chatters against its neighbor. The woven grass-banks produce a continuous high glassy hiss as their blades vibrate in the wind. Deep in the ground, mechanisms installed long ago—or grown, depending on who is asked—cycle and recirculate charge through the mountain. Their bass note is felt more than heard: a monumental, steady thrumming beneath the feet. Then the gusts arrive, violently changing direction, and the whole place becomes an orchestra played with industrial force. Pipes in the scattered structures hoot. Stone cavities boom. Flocks call over one another in harsh, joyous repetition. Even silence, when it appears for a second between storms, seems to ring.

There are buildings, but only a few, and none pretend to dominate the ledge.

One is a low observatory half sunk into the western rock. Its outer skin is made of overlapping black scales, weathered until they resemble slate. A narrow door opens only when approached, folding itself apart without hinges. Inside are empty benches and a vertical well full of pale moving points: not stars, but measurements of the atmosphere translated into light.

Another is a tower scarcely taller than the vegetation. It has no windows and no entrance. In calm weather it is easily mistaken for a column of dark stone. During a discharge, its surface unfolds into a lacework of conductive filaments extending several meters outward, catching invisible structures in the wind. The filaments make no attempt to avoid birds. The birds know the schedule better than humans do.

There are also little things scattered through the open ground: a circle of smooth metal posts, waist-high and warm despite the cold; a collapsed shelter whose fabric has fossilized into pearly layers; an upright panel displaying maps of places that do not correspond to any visible direction. None are new. None are entirely abandoned. Their age is difficult to estimate because each repairs itself only where it must. A crack may remain for a hundred years, while a failed internal component is replaced overnight by material extruded from the surrounding stone.

The animals are abundant, though no single species occupies much space.

Small six-legged grazers move between the shrubs in restless bands. Their bodies are lined with dark fur, but their mouths are hard mineral wheels that rasp at the conductive bark of the lower plants. They are constantly charged by the ground. When they leap, blue sparks flicker between their feet and the grit.

Birds nest in the lee of the cliff, where the wind cannot strip them from their holds. They have broad, transparent wing-surfaces strengthened with dark ribs. Instead of merely enduring the atmospheric violence, they use it. A flock will launch together into a crosswind and disappear upward almost vertically, screaming with excitement or alarm. In the flashes of the dry clouds, their wings become briefly visible all at once: hundreds of thin luminous shapes turning over the void.

There are quieter creatures in the garden’s dense places. Burrowing things make tunnels beneath the mineral crust and feed on the warm, charged roots. Pollen-moths with reflective bodies swarm around the tower during electrical storms, their wings beating so fast they make the air look granular. Long, patient predators lie under the silver-leafed shrubs, motionless except for the slow opening and shutting of vents along their flanks. They do not drink. Nothing here drinks. Life is maintained by pressure, chemistry, light, and current.

The rules of the place are posted nowhere, but they are obvious after a while.

Do not stand between two metal posts when the sky turns white.

Do not shelter beneath a broad-leafed plant; the leaves are collectors, not roofs.

Do not follow the birds downward over the cliff, however certain they appear to be flying toward a path.

If the ground begins to glow in lines, step onto bare stone and wait. The garden is about to redistribute its charge.

And if one of the old structures speaks—if its panel lights up, or a door opens in the tower that has never had a door—listen first. The mountain’s machinery does not communicate often. When it does, it is usually because some pattern in the sky has become dangerous, or because it has recognized something in you.

For all its violence, the ledge is not hostile. It is established. It has been like this long enough for every plant to bend correctly, every animal to know where the safe hollows are, every stone to wear itself smooth under the endless abrasion of dry wind. The turbulence is not a disruption of its character. It is its climate, its pulse, its ordinary weather.

Standing there, chilled under normal daylight, surrounded by impossible greenery and the deafening music of the charged air, one understands that water is not the only thing that can make a world fertile.

Sometimes a world grows green by learning how to survive a storm that never ends.
