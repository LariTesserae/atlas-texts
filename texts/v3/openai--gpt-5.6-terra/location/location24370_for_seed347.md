The country lies in a shallow world beneath the world: not a cavern, exactly, but a broad collapsed basin whose rim is hidden by reeds, alder thickets, and the low gray shoulders of wet ground. From above it might look like an overgrown marsh. Inside it is a valley, wide enough that weather has room to happen in it, though the surrounding earth rises close on every side. Cold air collects here. It sits over the water as if in a bowl.

The floor is never wholly dry.

Black peat gives underfoot in every open place. Water shines between clumps of sedge, runs in thin clear rills over pale stones, or seeps darkly through turf. The rain has passed often enough that the landscape seems built from its aftermath: leaves bowed under droplets, exposed roots lacquered brown and red, walls of clay striped by fresh trickles. Even on days without rain, moisture gathers. It beads on moss. It hangs from the fringes of fern leaves. In the morning, a low mist forms over every channel and does not quite clear until noon.

The basin is young. Its slopes are still raw where they have slumped: shelves of yellow subsoil, ribs of gravel, sudden cuts where a whole patch of ground has recently slid away. Stones are sharp and only half-settled. Small cliffs rise from the wetland with no graceful weathering, their broken faces carrying the marks of last season’s frost. Yet life has moved quickly into every crack. Moss has found the rock. Willow roots grip the exposed banks. Dense beds of horsetail and marsh marigold crowd the shallowest water. There is no spare ground; even the fallen branches are occupied by fungi, beetles, lichens, snails, and seedlings.

At the basin’s center stands what resembles a city.

It is not made by hands in any recognizable way. No cut timber, worked stone, iron, glass, lamps, or engines exist here. But the living things of the hollow have made a settlement through repetition, appetite, shelter, and the steady pressure of bodies. Lodges of mud, woven roots, and reed-stems gather along the canals in dense districts. Some are low and round, some built up against the sides of old stumps, some rise in layered heaps from the water like brown, wet-roofed houses. Their passages run beneath the surface, connecting pools and chambers through bank and peat. Beaver families maintain the deeper channels; muskrats take the reedier margins; water voles inhabit the crumbly slopes; otters keep to the cold main runs. Each species has its ground, but the boundaries are practical rather than hostile. A dam is a street. A fallen log is a bridge. A narrow shelf of firm soil becomes a busy crossing.

The channels are everywhere.

They divide the basin into a compact archipelago of sodden islands, root-hummocks, willow stands, and roofed burrows. Water moves slowly through most of them, checked by low dams and screens of branches, then gathers speed where the basin spills toward a concealed outlet beneath the eastern rim. The main watercourse is deep enough to look almost black. Its banks are trampled smooth by paws, hooves, claws, and soft feet. In the cold hours, a skin of ice sometimes forms in the quieter corners, thin and silver-blue; by midday it breaks around emergent stems and drifts into the reeds.

The vegetation is rich, but never tropical in feeling. Everything is green under cold conditions: dark green, blue green, brown green, moss green made bright by wetness. Alder and willow lean above the channels, their roots knuckled into the water. Birch grows on the higher slopes, white trunks lifting through the damp air. Beneath them are ferns, wood sorrel, bog rosemary, crowberry, rushes, liverworts, and broad colonies of sphagnum that make the ground look soft enough to sleep on and prove otherwise when stepped upon. In late seasons, cranberries redden close to the moss. Fungus shelves climb the rotting wood, pale as bone or orange as a fox’s flank.

Animals make the place feel full without making it loud.

A heron stands motionless at the mouth of a side-channel. Moorhens vanish through the reeds with barely a ripple. Frogs pulse beneath mats of vegetation, but their calls are subdued by the cold. Water shrews work the margins. Field mice thread through the roots. A fox comes down one of the raw slopes at dusk, its paws darkened by peat. Above, owls use the rim trees; below, in the denser growth, wrens and reed buntings keep to short hidden flights. Fish hold in the clearer channels wherever gravel has collected: small pale trout, sticklebacks, minnows flashing briefly in the dark water.

The great abundance of life does not produce a great noise. The basin absorbs sound. Peat swallows footfalls. Moss takes the fall of rain. Reeds mutter rather than rattle. The loudest recurring sound is water finding its way through obstacles: a patient sip beneath a dam, a faint overflow over packed mud, the occasional ticking collapse of a thawing bank. Animals announce themselves mostly by movement: the V-shaped disturbance on a canal, a tremble in a willow branch, a sudden parting in a wall of sedge.

There are rules here, though none are written.

Water must always be given a path. If it is blocked entirely, it rises, softens the banks, and takes the path for itself. Every wall is temporary. Every lodge must be repaired. Every island is slowly becoming something else—either a firmer hill of accumulated roots and silt, or a drowned patch of floating green. The residents do not attempt permanence. They reinforce what holds, abandon what sinks, and build again beside it.

The cold establishes another law: nothing may waste warmth. Burrows descend into bank and peat. Entrances face away from the prevailing wind. Nests are deep in dry chambers lined with grass, down, and stripped bark. In winter, the surface city becomes a field of low humps beneath snow, its canals bridged by ice; life continues underneath, moving through black water in rooms warmed only by bodies pressed together.

Under ordinary daylight, the basin is neither gloomy nor bright. Light reaches it sideways, especially in the morning and late afternoon, catching on wet alder trunks and making the innumerable pools shine like fitted pieces of dull metal. At noon the sky appears as an oval of cloud above the rim. Rain descends into it cleanly, with no horizon visible until the mist lifts. When that happens, one can see the full extent of the place: the packed wet settlement in the center, the crowded vegetation around it, the raw encircling slopes, and beyond the upper rim, only the suggestion of the larger land from which this hidden valley has sunk away.
