The valley is underwater, though it has the proportions of a high country.

Its slopes rise away into black water: long shoulders of stone, shelves cut by old weather, ridges that once stood in wind and snow and now disappear beneath a ceiling of ice-dark fluid. The basin may have been a mountain valley before the flood, but the flood has lasted too long to be called an event. Water has become the climate. It hangs in every distance, presses softly against every surface, and carries the cold from the upper ice fields down into the drowned hollows.

There is no sky in the ordinary sense. Above, far above, a dim skin of pale blue moves behind layers of ice and suspended silt. Sometimes it brightens for an hour, not enough to make day, only enough to reveal the valley’s breadth: terraces covered in waving green, black cliffs furred with moss, and the pale trunks of drowned groves standing upright along the old riverbed. Their branches are gone. Their bark has softened into fibrous coats, from which long ribbons of weed trail in the current.

The vegetation is rich but dispersed. It does not make a forest. It gathers where the land permits it: around spring mouths, in cracks of basalt, over ruined walls, along the sheltered sides of boulders. There are broad fans of cold-water fern, their fronds translucent as smoked glass. There are cushions of red moss that glow faintly when disturbed, not with warmth but with a mineral, bruise-colored light. Long kelp-like stems root in the sediment and climb hundreds of feet before bending over in the slow river of the flood. Their leaves are narrow, tough, and pale, adapted less to sunlight than to whatever meager luminosity filters through the ice.

In places, the plants have made meadows.

They cover the former pasture floors in knee-high swaying fields, though there are no knees here, no grazers, no herds. The grass bends all in one direction when the current passes, then rises again. It seeds into the water, releasing clouds of silver threads. Those threads drift through the valley and settle into sheltered seams. Nothing eats them. Nothing tears the greenery down except cold, current, rot, and the occasional collapse of stone.

No fish move through the dark. No insects skate the upper water. No bird calls from the drowned branches. The absence is so complete that it becomes a presence: every open volume of water seems waiting for an animal that will never arrive.

The loudness comes from the land itself.

Water moves through fissures in the mountains with the deep, continuous roar of distant furnaces. Springs surge from the valley walls, mineral-heavy and colder than the surrounding flood, spilling in white plumes over ledges. Ice calves somewhere overhead with muffled artillery cracks. Whole groves of hollow trunks knock together in the current: wood against wood, slow and enormous. From beneath the silt comes a periodic boom, as if the valley floor is breathing through a sealed throat.

Near the western slope stand the remains of a settlement.

It is not large. A handful of low houses cling to a terrace above the old river course, built from fitted stone and black timber. Their roofs are weighted with flat slabs. Their doorways are narrow and round-topped, perhaps designed to hold back weather, perhaps water. Some have collapsed inward. Others are intact, their interiors packed with clear, motionless water and pale sediment. On the walls are marks cut with simple tools: spirals, ladders, branching lines, circles with downward-pointing strokes.

A bridge crosses the riverbed nearby, though the riverbed is now indistinguishable from the valley’s greater water. Its span is narrow, made of stone slabs laid without mortar. Weed grows between them. At either end are small cairns, each bound with lengths of copper-green wire or beaten metal. The metal has endured better than the wood. It gives off a dull gleam when the upper ice briefly admits light.

There are other structures scattered across the basin: a watchtower reduced to a stump; a line of stakes rising from a meadow; a stone platform built around a warm spring; a bell frame on a ridge, holding three green-black bells. The bells have no ropes. Yet in strong currents they sound.

Their notes travel farther underwater than they should. They do not ring sharply. They bloom—low, wavering, immense—and remain in the bones of the valley after the metal has ceased moving. Sometimes one bell answers another from miles away. Sometimes all three speak at once, and the plants lean down as though under wind.

The people who made these things understood the water, or expected it. Their tools were simple: stone mauls, bone wedges, hammered copper, braided fiber. But their construction is patient. Many walls include narrow channels, deliberately carved, so water can pass through them rather than destroy them. Some homes are built around springs. Some stones bear polished handholds along routes leading upward toward the dim ice. There are baskets of river reeds preserved under overturned bowls. There are lamps with mineral wicks, their bowls still fixed in niches, though no flame could survive here.

Perhaps the lamps were for before.

Or perhaps fire exists in this valley only as a memory encoded in objects.

The world has its own rules now. Sound climbs uphill. Loose silt falls upward in thin gray veils whenever the bells ring. Water flowing from the highest springs is warmer, while the lower reaches are numb with glacial cold. The brighter the light above becomes, the darker the depths turn; at rare intervals, the entire valley is eclipsed by a whitening glow from the ice, and every shadow beneath the terraces deepens until it becomes solid-looking.

There are paths through it all, though no traveler uses them.

Old roadways wind along the slopes, their paving stones softened by moss. They pass between standing trunks and over cliffs where ropes of weed have replaced guardrails. At their edges, small marker stones bear the same downward-pointing symbol. The paths lead not toward safety but toward places where the water speaks differently: vents, caves, submerged fields, the quiet mouths of wells that once opened onto air.

The valley is settled in itself. Nothing is newly ruined. The stone has learned its wetness; the wood has learned its slow undoing; the plants have learned to live on dimness and minerals. Even the erosion is gentle, almost domestic. Corners round off. Walls gain velvet coatings. Fallen beams become gardens.

Yet it is never still.

Currents pass through the basin in great invisible weather systems. Cold fronts descend from the ice shelf. Green clouds of spores drift up from the fields. Springs pulse, bells murmur, cliffs shed pebbles that rise in slow spirals before settling elsewhere. In the deepest hour, when the light above has gone nearly black, the red mosses wake across the valley floor.

Then the drowned highland appears in fragments: a bridge, a roof, a meadow, a stairway vanishing into water; all of it lit from below by a dim and patient crimson, as if the land remembers a sunset it never again expects to see.
