The city occupies a shallow basin no larger than a few districts across, a low place gathered around water that has nowhere in particular to go. It is not a river town, though a river arrives at its edge: a broad, slow brown channel slips beneath a concrete lock gate, divides into drainage canals, cooling trenches, and factory ponds, then seems to disappear into the soil. The ground is so wet that every excavation fills. Water shines in wheel ruts. It sweats from retaining walls. Beneath cracked paving, it presses upward in dark seams.

Nothing rises very far. The horizon is a rim of freight sheds, refinery stacks, gantry cranes, and compact apartment blocks built on concrete pilings. The buildings are close in function rather than in position: each is set back behind a yard, a fenced transformer pad, a strip of white gravel, or a service road broad enough for trucks. Much of the town is open space, but it is not empty. Pipes cross it on low racks. Cables sag between short poles. Pallets wait under tarps. Rusting drums collect rainwater. A narrow rail spur curves through reeds and comes to an end beside an unloading shed.

The terrain is young in a quiet way. A new industrial district has been laid over recently reclaimed mudflat, and the land has not yet learned to look permanent. Drainage ditches have clean-cut banks. Fresh concrete channels show their aggregate. The edges of the basin are scored by raw embankments, their slopes held with wire cages of stone. Where rain has bitten at these slopes, pale clay is exposed in sharp little ribs, almost geological in miniature. Every few months the workers add more rock, more culvert, more crushed slag, but the wet ground remains soft beneath the town’s hard surfaces.

It is hot even before noon, a weight rather than a blaze. The air is full of moisture and industrial warmth: heated metal, diesel exhaust, wet timber, stagnant canal water, mineral dust damped into paste. The sky is commonly overcast, not with storm clouds exactly but with a low gray ceiling formed by marine haze, steam from cooling towers, and the vapor lifted from the saturated ground. Light arrives diluted. At midday it is the color of old aluminum. Lamps under factory awnings remain on throughout the day, casting yellow pools on wet concrete.

The water determines the materials. Brick would mildew; untreated wood would blacken and soften. So the place is made mostly of galvanized steel, concrete, corrugated panels, painted handrails, ceramic insulators, black rubber hose. Every metal surface is either new and bright or already streaked with orange. Roofs are low-pitched and wide, built to shed heavy rain into gutters big enough for fists. Downpipes empty directly into open channels, where the water runs for a short distance and then vanishes beneath grated covers.

There are few plants, but those that persist are tough and opportunistic. Reeds gather in every neglected ditch, their blades gray-green beneath dust. A kind of salt-tolerant grass makes thin islands along the canal margins. Small umbrella trees stand at bus stops and yard entrances, each planted in a square of soil hemmed by curb stones; their leaves are sparse, leathery, and permanently coated with grit. Creepers begin at fence bottoms, reach for a season, and are cut back when they interfere with signage or wire. Beyond the outer service road, a flat band of sedge and mud extends toward the river, insect-loud and almost treeless.

Animal life finds more room here than plants do. Egrets stalk the drainage channels, stepping between floating plastic bottles and oil-sheened leaves. Black-backed gulls stand on warehouse roofs and cry above the machinery. Rats travel the pipe runs after dark, and small frogs live improbably in warm culverts, their calls pulsing beneath the traffic noise. At the canal locks, fish surface in the brown water, taking insects brought down by rain. Dragonflies hover over settling ponds. In the heat, lizards lie flat against the pale concrete walls, retreating into expansion joints when forklifts pass.

The loudness is continuous but not chaotic. A refinery pump gives a deep, steady throb. Fans mounted high on factory walls churn with a broad mechanical roar. Compressors start and stop at regular intervals, though their pattern has become so familiar that residents scarcely hear it. There is the metallic clank of rail wagons coupling, the reverse alarms of delivery trucks, the slap of rain on sheet roofs, the hollow rushing sound inside drainage culverts. From open windows come radios, cooking pans, and the occasional shout across a yard. At night the insects take the upper register, and the industrial hum becomes more apparent, like an engine heard from inside a ship.

The town’s principal rule is simple: water must always be given a route. No wall is built without weep holes. No yard is paved without drains. Doors have raised thresholds. Electrical boxes stand on plinths. The lowest rooms are storerooms, workshops, pump stations, never bedrooms. Every resident knows which canal rises first in heavy rain and which underpass fills knee-deep even during an ordinary storm. There is little romance attached to this knowledge. It is maintenance, inherited and practical.

A second rule is that heat is endured collectively. The busiest hours begin before sunrise, when the concrete still holds a little coolness. By late morning, shutters come down on the sunward sides of shops. Workers gather beneath shed roofs, in the shade of loading bays, beside vending machines that dispense bottles sweating cold water. Even the animals seek the margins: birds beneath bridge decks, dogs under trucks, frogs in pipes.

Yet despite the weather and the machinery, the place is stable. The canals hold their levels. The pumps are serviced. The same freight arrives, is processed, leaves. The same stains spread only until they are pressure-washed away. The same reeds return after clearing. There are no dramatic floods in memory, no sudden booms, no expanding skyline. The town has found an equilibrium between its wet ground and its hard industry. It remains a small hot basin of steam, mud, concrete, and sound: dim at noon, loud at midnight, held together by drains, routine, and the patient refusal of water to be ignored.
