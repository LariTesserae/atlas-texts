The city occupies a high basin where the mountains have forgotten how to be mountains.

It is not perched dramatically on a single summit. Instead, it spreads across a broad, cold upland: a country-sized shelf of dark stone, cupped between long ridges and broken at its edges by ravines. From a distance it seems at first to be a pattern in the earth—gray lines, terraces, retaining walls, roofs the color of wet slate. Only as one comes nearer does its size become clear. There are streets down there, courts, thousands of stepped houses fitted shoulder to shoulder, reservoirs, arcades, stairways climbing and descending through neighborhoods that have been rebuilt so many times they have become part of the terrain.

Nothing in the city rises very high. The wind is too steady for slender towers, and the winters too stern. It has grown outward and inward instead: undercrofts beneath plazas, rooms made from old cisterns, alleys roofed by the rooms above them, staircases entering a block and emerging three streets away. Buildings lean against one another with the patience of old animals. Their walls are built of local rock—blue-black basalt, pale seam-stone, greenish slabs that shine when wet—and the mortar between them has darkened almost to the color of soil.

Water is everywhere, though there is no river grand enough to name.

It comes down from the ridges in thin bright threads after rain; it seeps through joints in the bedrock; it gathers under paving stones and shines in the low places. Gutters run along every lane, not hidden but made beautiful by use: shallow channels worn smooth by generations of feet and flowing water. Some are lined with pebbles. Some pass beneath little footbridges. Some are simply deep grooves in the center of a street, and the residents step over them without thought.

The wetness does not make the place lush. The air is cool enough, the soil too thin, the winds too persistent. Plants survive in chosen places. Grass grows in the cracks of broad stairways and along the edges of the reservoir walls. Tough, silvery shrubs huddle in abandoned courtyards. Moss thickens on north-facing stone, particularly under eaves and on the shaded sides of statues whose faces have long since softened into weather. A few wind-shaped trees occupy protected hollows: low rowans, junipers, bent willows at the margins of pools. Their branches rarely reach higher than a roofline.

Above the city, the slopes are mostly bare. They are striped with old sheep paths, scree fans, and the low dark runs of water. Clouds cross them constantly. In clear weather the plateau can see for immense distances: ridge after ridge, blue in the afternoon, then gray, then erased by weather. But clear weather never lasts long. Rain may begin in one quarter of the city while sun remains on another. Mist rises from the basin floor and threads through the lower streets. In the evening, condensation forms on railings and doorframes; by morning the stone seems to have been freshly washed.

The city is dense, but not hurried.

People have arranged themselves around the water and the slope. In the upper districts, homes are smaller and more closely pressed, their entrances opening straight onto flights of stair. In the central terraces, market courts lie between covered halls, though the markets are quiet by the standards of lower lands: baskets of roots, salt, wool, dried herbs, river-stone, pottery, lamp oil. At the basin’s low center, broad communal reservoirs reflect the sky. Their edges are ringed in worn steps. Children sometimes gather there, but even their play seems softened by the damp air and the surrounding stone.

There are no wheels rattling over cobbles, no metal engines, no wires strung between roofs. Things move at the pace of shoulders, hands, animals, and water. Loads are carried in frames or dragged on sledges over the smoother paths. Donkeys and shaggy pack goats appear occasionally in the steep lanes, their bells muffled with cloth so their passage does not startle anyone. Most labor makes only a low, local sound: the scrape of a door, cloth shaken from a window, the small knock of masonry work, a basin being filled.

From a high overlook, the city has almost no audible voice at all.

Rain makes more sound than its inhabitants. It ticks on roofs, travels through channels, spills from one terrace to another. In the drying intervals, water drips from carved spouts shaped like plain animal heads. The wind moves softly along the ridges and enters the streets only as a cool breath. Somewhere, often too far away to locate, a person calls once across a courtyard. The voice is received by walls, diluted, and gone.

Animal life is sparse. Pale hawks circle on the updrafts above the basin. Small black birds live under roof tiles and in the drainage arches, appearing in quick nervous flocks whenever the weather breaks. At dusk, foxes sometimes come down from the higher slopes and vanish through the outer gardens. In winter, when the pools freeze at their edges, the city is visited by narrow-legged birds from the north, standing in the unfrozen shallows as if they have arrived by mistake.

The place has rules, but they are practical rather than sacred.

No one blocks a runoff channel. No one builds over a spring without leaving it an opening to breathe. Roof water belongs first to the house beneath it, then to the lane, then to the cistern downhill. Stones taken from a wall must be replaced before the next hard rain. Doors facing north are painted in mineral colors so they can be found in snow or fog. Every neighborhood maintains its own stairs, because a broken stair is not an inconvenience here; it is an interruption in the city’s circulation.

The city is old enough that these rules have entered the architecture. A wall may be elegant only because it has diverted water for six hundred years. A public square may exist because the ground beneath it is a roof over a reservoir. A shrine may stand at the meeting of three drainage channels, its threshold always damp, its steps polished by water and feet alike.

At the outer edge, construction becomes less certain. Streets turn into retaining paths, then into trails across wet grass and exposed stone. The last houses cling to the folds of the land. Beyond them are small enclosures, grazing grounds, abandoned quarries filling slowly with rain, and long empty stairways leading toward settlements that have diminished or disappeared.

Yet the city does not feel ruined. It feels settled into a difficult abundance: enough water, enough stone, enough shelter; too little soil to waste, too much weather to ignore. It has learned not to conquer the upland but to inhabit its seams.

When clouds lower over the basin, the whole place nearly vanishes. Roofs, walls, streets, and reservoirs become variations of one wet gray material. Then a break opens in the sky. Ordinary daylight spills across the terraces, catching every channel and puddle. The city shines briefly, not brightly, but completely—an immense built watershed, alive with the slow movement of rain.
