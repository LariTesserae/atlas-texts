The town is built inside a root.

Not beneath a tree, exactly. The root is too large for that comparison, too porous and deliberate. It rises from the black lowland like the buried rib of some continental plant, its outer skin folded into buttresses, curtains, vaulted chambers, and long wet corridors. At a distance it might be mistaken for a hill, except that it breathes: the walls dimly swell and settle, and pale vapor leaves the seams in slow exhalations.

No one has found the trunk.

The settlement occupies one of the root’s hollows, a space no larger than a small cathedral from end to end, though its interior keeps revising the measure. There are streets of iron grating suspended over dark drops. There are platforms bolted to living wood. Narrow houses, built from corrugated panels and stripped machinery casings, cling to the root’s inner folds. Their windows are mostly covered: not against weather, but against the dark outside, which is not always outside.

The light comes from the plant itself. Greenish threads travel under translucent bark, faint as moonlight through old skin. At intervals, bulblike organs open high in the vault and spill a blue-white glow over the roofs. This is enough for work. It is enough for moss to grow in the mortar and for the black, waxy vines to find every abandoned cable. It is not enough to see a face clearly unless one is very close.

Beyond the last railings there is mostly room.

That is the unnerving part. The root is full of vegetation—vegetation so complete that stone, soil, and even the air seem merely temporary states of it—yet the inhabited chamber remains spare. A few buildings. A smokestack. A water tower that has never held water. A switching yard for small ore carts. Empty walkways crossing expanses of shadow. Great distances exist between things that should be near each other. A door may stand across a plaza for years, then one night be found opening directly into the apartment opposite.

The town has industrial habits despite its smallness. There is a generator house where three obsolete turbines turn whenever the root’s pulse is strong enough. Their belts shudder and flap, and their copper windings make a smell like rain on coins. There is a pump station built around a fissure in the inner wall. It gathers condensation from the root’s cold breath: water beading on metal fins, collecting in enamel basins, running down pipes to a communal cistern. Rain comes rarely, but when it does it falls inside the chamber as a fine warm drizzle from the ceiling, carrying pollen, spores, and the soft husks of things that were once leaves.

Nobody wastes that water. Nobody calls it clean.

All surfaces are alive. The paved streets are padded with a resilient green felt that draws back from boot soles and closes over wheel tracks within an hour. Rail ties sprout thin red fans. The brick foundations of the oldest homes have been replaced, gradually and without permission, by woody tissue. In the market square, a flowering column has grown through a crane. Its stem has swallowed the engine block but left the operator’s cab untouched, hanging thirty feet above the square with its door still open.

The workers use it as a lookout.

They mine nothing. There is no rock to mine. Instead they harvest the root’s shed material: dry bark plates, hardened amber nodules, cables of vascular fiber, black gum that burns slowly and smells medicinal. Every season the plant produces new internal structures. A wall of pale pith may arrive overnight, filling an alley. A staircase may soften and fold itself into a sloping tongue. The town’s maintenance crews are part carpenter, part gardener, part surgeon. They cut only where the root marks a line in silver sap, and never after the third bell.

There are three bells, though no one agrees where they hang.

The wildlife is everywhere, but it does not crowd the space. It prefers edges, ledges, ducts, and the unreachable upper dark. Long-legged moths cling to the lamps and drink their heat. Pale foxes move along the railings with leaves growing from their shoulders. Schools of tiny, silver fish swim through the mist that gathers near the floor, turning together whenever a person coughs. In the dampest passages, enormous snails carry whole gardens on their shells: ferns, fungal cups, miniature trees, sometimes the remains of old signage. A person who loses their way may follow the snails home, if the snails are traveling in the right season.

There are birds, although the root has no sky. They nest in the turbine housings and make no sound. Their wings are broad and translucent, veined like leaves. They fly only when nobody is looking directly at them. At night—or what passes for night when the green glow fades—they descend to the streets in silent flocks and clean the moss from the rails.

The predators are heard less often than they are inferred. Something large moves behind the walls, pacing parallel to the settlement. Its passage causes tools to tremble on hooks. In the oldest district, residents can place a hand against the bark and feel a slow second heartbeat. Children are warned not to tap back.

The town has rules.

Do not sleep under an open flower.

Do not take a cutting from a wall that has begun to remember your name.

If a corridor becomes longer while you are walking it, stop. Sit down. Do not turn around until the corridor has shortened.

If you find rain falling upward, cover your mouth and wait for it to finish.

The root does not like fire, but it tolerates engines. It does not like shouting, but it accepts songs with work in them. It does not distinguish between buildings and nests, machines and parasites, citizens and beneficial insects. The settlement survives because it has learned to make itself useful in ways the plant can understand: clearing rot, carrying seeds, opening vents when pressure builds, feeding warm exhaust into the cold cavities where new tissues are forming.

There is no horizon here. The chamber’s curves conceal distance; the darkness drinks the far ends of every road. Still, the place is active. Conveyors carry bark to the pressing room. Water ticks into barrels. Repair crews walk in pairs under hanging vines with their lamps hooded. Animals cross the bridges. Leaves unfurl with audible patience. Somewhere below, something is assembling a new room from pulp and root hair.

The town is not expanding, exactly.

It is being grown around.
