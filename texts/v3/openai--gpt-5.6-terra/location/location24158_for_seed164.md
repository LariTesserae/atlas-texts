The high country begins above the weather.

Below it, storms move across the world in ordinary systems: fronts pass, rivers rise, cities fog, forests drink. But on the plateau the atmosphere has become a fixed substance, layered and cold. Clouds arrive from the western rim, climb the black escarpments, and are caught there. They do not disperse. They hang in their accustomed shelves, year after year, pouring fine water through the same gaps in the rock. The rain never becomes a storm and never quite ceases. It is a permanent condition of the altitude: a silver vertical grain in the air.

The land is vast enough that its horizons are meteorological rather than visual. A person standing on one of the rail embankments may see, at an impossible distance, the pale rectilinear glow of another industrial district. Between here and there lies a breadth of wet volcanic ground, newly made and already ancient in its stillness: basalt fields folded like black fabric; steep cinder ridges with raw red interiors exposed; towers of pale mineral crust around vents that no longer emit anything but warmth and vapor. The mountains look unfinished. Their edges are sharp, their screes have not softened into soil, their lava flows retain the ropes and buckles with which they congealed.

Yet nothing is changing.

Loose stones remain forever poised midway down slopes. Water gathers along a fracture, spills over a ledge, and maintains the exact same white thread of falling for centuries. Steam curls up from fissures but does not drift with the wind; it rises straight, reaches a certain height, and becomes cloud. Ice occupies the shaded sides of every broken wall, neither advancing nor retreating. The place is not frozen in a dead sense. Its engines run. Its water falls. Its lights burn. But all motion is circular, every process caught in a completed arrangement.

The plateau’s surface is wet nearly everywhere.

Rain pools in shallow black basins. It gathers in the seams of new stone, in rail trenches, on the flat roofs of pumping stations. The ground has little soil, but where water has had even a narrow interval to linger, vegetation has taken hold with extravagant insistence. Dark green mats blanket the sheltered lava. Moss fills drain channels and clings to bridge pylons. Low thickets of broad, waxed leaves gather around warm vents, their branches heavy with condensation. In the industrial quarters, pale grasses rise from ballast and concrete cracks. Their blades are stiff with frost at dawn and wet with rain at noon, although dawn and noon differ more in light than in temperature.

There are no animals.

No birds cross the suspended cloudbanks. No insect takes advantage of the flowering wetland gardens that have accumulated in the geothermal runoff. There are no hoofprints in mud, no nests beneath eaves, no distant calls to complicate the air. The vegetation grows in an enormous silence of intention, turning itself toward heat, water, and the gray light without being grazed, carried, disturbed, or observed by anything living.

This makes the mechanical sounds seem less like an interruption than a substitute ecology.

The cities are scattered rather than crowded: immense industrial settlements separated by hundreds of kilometers of empty plateau. Each has been built around a source of heat, a reservoir, a mineral seam, or a point where the highland rail lines can be held level. From a distance they resemble clusters of ships moored in a dark inland sea. Their towers are narrow, their foundations deep. Pipes rise in bundled ranks from the ground and vanish into elevated galleries. Tanks, cooling fields, substations, ore silos, and waterworks stand among wet expanses of moss and volcanic stone.

The constructions are practical but not modest. There are conveyor belts running for miles over open ground, supported on repeating steel frames. There are turbine halls broad enough to contain clouds of their own. There are lift shafts cut into young cliffs, their doors opening onto ledges above vapor-filled chasms. Railways cross the plateau in raised lines of iron, often disappearing into white rain and emerging much later on another ridge, where their signal lamps burn yellow through the permanent wet.

Every city produces a constant sound.

Not the chaotic noise of industry at work, with loading cycles and shift whistles and revving vehicles, but a continuous, monumental chord. Generators hum in low registers. Pumps strike an unwavering piston rhythm. Water travels through large pipes with a deep throat-like roar. At intervals—always the same interval, never early or late—pressure is released from an intake tower with a cry like metal being pulled slowly apart. The sound crosses the empty country for astonishing distances. It bounces from raw cliffs, moves across open water, enters drain tunnels, and returns in altered echoes.

In the gaps between settlements, that sound is the only proof that the world has inhabitants at all.

The inhabitants themselves are difficult to find.

There may be people in the lit rooms behind the long factory windows, moving among gauges and polished valves. There may be crews inside the rail cars that pass at unvarying speeds across the causeways. At certain hours, small figures can be seen crossing a floodlit gantry in heavy pale coats, but they are too far away to make out clearly, and when they disappear through a doorway the doorway does not open again. The landscape does not need people to maintain the impression of habitation. It has already internalized their systems. The machines breathe, circulate, illuminate, and complain with the disciplined permanence of organs.

The rules of the plateau are simple, though no one has written them down in a place visitors can find.

Nothing arrives except water.

Nothing leaves except heat.

No living thing with blood can survive the altitude for long, though plants, fungi, and lichen are permitted to root anywhere they can find warmth. Wind exists only as a pressure difference inside built structures: a draft through a tunnel, a leak around a sealed door, a ventilation current between towers. In the open country, the air is still. Rain falls because it has always fallen. Steam rises because it has always risen. The factories operate because stopping would be a change, and change is not one of the available conditions.

Even decay has been given another form. Steel does not rust away; it becomes furred in orange mineral bloom and remains structurally whole. Concrete does not crack progressively; it bears its existing cracks forever, each one dark with moss and water. The fresh mountains do not erode into gentler shapes. Their ravines have been carved to a particular depth by incessant runoff and will go no deeper. They are landscapes of arrested beginning: everything looks like it formed yesterday, and everything has looked that way for longer than memory.

At night, the normal gray of the sky deepens to a deep mineral blue, though the cloud shelves remain visible from below, lit by the cities. Sodium lamps turn rain gold around the loading yards. White arc-light spills from maintenance platforms. Along the railways, red signals proceed across the dark in lines too straight and regular to be stars. Far out on the plateau, isolated pump stations shine alone beside black water, their reflected lights trembling in pools whose surfaces never ripple beyond the same small, repeated pattern.

The cold is not biting. It is comprehensive.

It enters metal, stone, water, lungs. It keeps every scent clean and faint: wet iron, crushed lichen, sulfur in the vent-fields, warm oil around generators, mineral mud in the drainage basins. Breath appears briefly and then joins the rain. Exposed hands ache. Boots sink slightly into saturated moss or ring sharply against basalt. The only warmth comes from conduits, service tunnels, turbine-house doors, and the strange green margins around geothermal outflow, where vegetation grows shoulder-high in a country otherwise too high and too cold for such abundance.

Beyond the last station, there is always more plateau.

More fixed rain. More raw black ridges. More miles of empty rail. More silent wet gardens clinging to the edges of industrial heat. The continent extends under its permanent cloud deck until the distances themselves become abstract, until the hum of machinery seems not to come from any particular settlement but from the ground beneath everything.

It is a young world that has finished becoming itself.
