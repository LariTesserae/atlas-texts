The high country is not a country of stone.

From a distance, if distance can be trusted here, it seems to be a range of green-black mountains under a lid of pearl-colored cloud. The ridges rise thousands of meters above the low weather, their slopes folded in terraces and ravines, their peaks lost in dim vapor. But there is no exposed bedrock anywhere. There are no cliffs in the ordinary sense. Every escarpment is a wall of woven roots, every plateau a roof of compressed leaves, every gorge a split between immense living structures.

The whole range has been made.

Not recently, and not by hands capable of measuring the whole of what they made. Its makers had stone blades, fire-hardened digging sticks, cordage twisted from vine fiber, wedges of shell, baskets, wooden mallets. They cultivated plants not merely for fruit or shade but for behavior. They taught roots to braid. They selected trees whose trunks fused when planted close together. They guided reeds across open chasms until the reeds made bridges, then anchored the bridges with soil and moss and generations of additional growth. In time the builders died, their settlements vanished into their work, and the work continued to grow according to rules nobody now fully knows.

The mountains are gardens enlarged beyond human proportion.

Their foundations are packed loam, old timber, carbonized reed mats, woven rootstone, shells, bone ash, and innumerable layers of living growth. What was once a retaining wall has become the trunk of a forest. What was once a canal has widened into a fog-filled valley. The broadest uplands are not ground at all but the flattened crowns of organisms the size of provinces: slow vegetal masses whose outer bark has accumulated orchards, pools, villages, and forests of smaller plants.

There is space everywhere.

The vegetation is total, but it does not make a jungle. It makes architecture: immense arches of pale vine spanning empty air; miles of fern-paved plazas beneath canopies so high that their undersides form a second sky; hollow stalks wide enough to hold weather systems. A traveler can walk for days through a single root gallery without seeing another person. The emptiness is amplified by the dimness. Light enters as an old green memory, diluted through layers of leaf and vapor. Even at midday the far side of a valley may be invisible.

Warm rain comes often but seldom dramatically. It condenses on every surface. It gathers along the undersides of leaves and falls in slow, separate drops. Root walls sweat continuously. Water moves through the land in concealed systems: under woven floors, inside hollow beams, down channels lined with glossy liverwort, out through the mouths of carved wooden spouts that may have been made centuries ago and are still performing their task.

There are lakes, but they are strange to find. A lake may appear as a dark circle between giant buttresses, its surface level with the surrounding walkways. Or it may hang in the air, enclosed in a transparent membrane of living sap-skin, held between two trunks. Rain birds drink from such suspended waters without breaking them. Sometimes a membrane bursts in the night, and a whole hillside wakes to a silver waterfall, only for the roots beneath to absorb it by morning.

At this altitude the clouds come below the inhabited terraces. They drift through the lower apertures of the living mountains, white and heavy, carrying the smell of wet pollen, leaf rot, and the faint mineral odor of old clay. Above them the air is warmer, unexpectedly warm, held by the breathing mass of vegetation. The plants give off heat in their deep fermentation chambers. A person sleeping beside a root wall can feel a pulse of warmth passing through it at dawn.

The land has weather of its own.

The upper crowns gather mist from the passing sky. Their leaves close in the cool hours, forcing vapor through porous towers where it condenses. The towers weep into cisterns, canals, and moss basins. On some days, broad sections of the range exhale together. Fog pours outward from vents along the slopes, spreading over the void in long white rivers. These rivers do not always descend. Some drift uphill, flowing toward the heat of the high gardens, as if gravity has become a matter of temperature and attention.

There are rules here, though they are not written except in growth rings and old cord markings.

No one cuts a load-bearing root. No one takes seed from a bridge-vine without leaving three seeds in its cradle. Fire is kept only in clay-lined cavities, never directly upon the living floors. When a path begins to twist away from its intended direction, people do not straighten it. They recognize that the path has entered a growing season and must be allowed to choose its route. A doorway that has narrowed overnight is not forced open; it is fed mineral water and spoken to in the low, repeated phrases taught by gardeners.

The oldest rule is that no surface is dead.

A basket left on the ground may root. A wooden handle can sprout pale leaves after a season of rain. A discarded sandal, if made from the proper fiber, will slowly become an ankle-high tuft of grass. People build with this knowledge. Their houses are not assembled so much as persuaded: frameworks planted in circles, walls braided from living shoots, roofs coaxed from broad-leaved creepers. A house may lift itself several handspans during a decade, its floor flexing gently underfoot as the roots beneath redistribute weight.

There are few houses compared to the size of the land. Most are clustered around old water courts or sheltered inside trunk hollows. The range is too vast, and its inhabitable spaces too numerous, for it to feel crowded. A tiny community may be the only human presence across a thousand valleys. Their lamps are bowls of glow-moss or beetle cages with woven lids. At night the built landscape recedes almost entirely. There are only warm points of greenish light, hanging among black leaves.

Animals are abundant, but most have learned the discipline of quiet.

Pale climbing deer move along the vertical root faces with split hooves that grip bark. Their coats are patterned like lichen, their antlers hung with tiny epiphytes. Large, soft-winged moths shelter under eaves, folding their wings around whole sleeping families for warmth. Flocks of long-legged marsh birds wade through the shallow water that collects on terrace floors; their feet barely disturb the surface. They do not call. They communicate by opening and shutting the colored throat fans beneath their beaks.

In the deepest galleries live the bellows beasts, broad-bodied herbivores with mossy hides and nostrils like flower cups. Their breathing is nearly soundless, but the passage of air through their internal chambers causes nearby vines to sway. They graze by pressing their mouths to the walls and drawing out excess sap. Where they have fed, new buds emerge in dense, bright clusters.

Predators exist as absences in the foliage. There are vine-colored cats that never touch the ground, and long amphibians that wait in the dark mouths of irrigation channels. Yet even the hunt is subdued. A sudden shiver in the leaves, a brief patter of falling condensation, then stillness returning around an empty place.

The loudest sound is water, but even that is softened: drip, seep, the faint brushing of runoff through roots. Sometimes a giant leaf turns itself toward the light, and the movement produces a low leathery sigh that can travel for kilometers through the structure. Sometimes old wooden parts of the mountain shift under growth pressure, and a distant knocking passes through the earth like someone slowly building a door.

Erosion has not defeated this place. It has been recruited.

Rain gnaws at the outer slopes, opening gullies; roots spill into those gullies and make new ribs. Wind strips leaves from the exposed crowns; beneath them, the old leaf mold deepens and feeds fresh growth. Landslides happen, but they are slow collapses of braided material, more like a body changing posture than a mountain breaking apart. A fallen terrace becomes a hanging garden. A drowned root hall becomes a breeding marsh. The range is ancient precisely because it yields.

And there are breaks in it.

Not ruins, exactly. Places where the world’s agreement with itself appears thin.

A stair might descend for several hours through a trunk, only to emerge on a ledge above the same stair’s entrance. A canal can carry floating leaves past a village every afternoon, though villagers swear no source lies upstream. In some fog seasons, shadows detach from their plants and drift independently among the walkways. They are harmless unless followed.

Far above the occupied terraces are the Seed Horizons: broad, empty platforms beneath the highest dim sky. Nothing grows there except low mats of silver moss and occasional solitary poles made from the fused stems of extinct plants. The platforms were constructed for a purpose no living community remembers. Their edges extend for hundreds of kilometers, perfectly smooth, looking out over cloud and distant green ridges. Rain gathers there in shallow sheets. Great animals cross them silently—six-legged grazers with transparent flanks, through which one can see seeds turning slowly in their stomachs.

At the center of each horizon stands a basin.

The basins are made of wood, though the wood has the grain of bone and does not rot. They collect rainwater. Sometimes, in the dim noon, the water reflects not the clouds above but a different landscape: dry stone valleys, a bright white sun, black waves under stars. People do not drink from these basins. They place offerings nearby instead—seed cakes, carved spoons, bundles of fragrant reed—and leave before their own reflections appear.

Below, the living range continues to manufacture itself: bridge, roof, orchard, canal, nest, wall, ladder, mountain.

It is a wilderness only because it has outlived the scale of its makers.
