At the top of the mountain there is a door no larger than a freight elevator.

It is sunk into a seam of dark metamorphic stone, behind a loose curtain of lichen and wire-moss. Nothing marks it from a distance. The surrounding summit is all pale scree, wind-flattened snow that never melts, and black ridges exposed like old bones. The air outside is thin enough to make thought feel expensive.

Behind the door is the Green Engine Vault.

It occupies a single chamber in the mountain, though “chamber” is misleading. It is a contained ravine turned inward upon itself: a vertical pocket perhaps sixty meters deep and only twenty across, crowded so densely with stems, leaves, roots, pipes, catwalks, ducts, cables, and mineral formations that there is no unclaimed air in it. The walls rise straight up, close enough that every sound returns almost immediately. They are faced in old riveted iron, slate, and patches of glazed ceramic insulation. Beneath these coverings is the true mountain, cold and compressed.

There is no water here.

Not a drop collects in the seams. No condensation beads on the railings. The leaves have no wet shine. The air is dry enough to sting the sinuses, dry enough that unprotected wood has become pale and resonant. Yet the vault is lush.

Vegetation fills it with an almost aggressive softness. Broad, dark fronds issue from vents in the walls. Low mats of gray-green cushion growth spread over concrete plinths and abandoned switchboxes. Thin white trunks, jointed like instrument tubing, climb the rock face and branch into fans of velvet-black foliage. The plants do not drink. They grow through a different economy: mineral dust, carbon-rich air, trace heat, and the electromagnetic hum of the machinery. Their roots enter cracked cable sheaths and old mortar with equal ease. Some have worked into the insulation of transformer housings; others flower around warm bearings in rings of dull metallic blue.

The flowers are dry things. Their petals are thin mineral membranes, translucent at the edges, opening toward current rather than sun. When a generator load shifts, they turn all at once.

The light is inadequate but constant.

A handful of industrial lamps hang from the upper gantries, their housings furred with rust, their glass smoked from decades of operation. They emit a weak amber glow that seems to settle in layers instead of traveling. Farther down, the plants make their own muted illumination: pinpricks in moss beds, a blue seam along a leaf edge, the occasional soft green pulse from a root mass wrapped around an electrical conduit. Nothing casts a clean shadow. Every surface seems to retain a little of every other surface’s darkness.

The vault is loud.

It is not loud in the manner of weather or crowds. There are no gusts, voices, wings, paws, or flowing water to complicate it. Its noise is mechanical and continuous: the deep, rectified throb of engines somewhere beneath the floor; the steady strike of a piston whose motion has become too regular to count; fans turning behind grilles; belts traveling through hidden shafts; current whining in ceramic insulators. The iron walls take the sound and distribute it through the whole packed space. Railings tremble. Leaf tips quiver. Fine dust rises from old ledges and remains suspended, as though the vibration has taught it not to settle.

The machinery has outlived its purpose, if it ever had one that could be named.

There are scattered structures within the vegetation: a small control room on stilts, its windows opaque with the inside growth of silver ferns; a crane track disappearing into foliage halfway up the wall; a maintenance platform reached by a ladder whose lower rungs have been swallowed by root cloth. A row of tanks, each no taller than a person, stands near the chamber floor. Their labels have long since peeled away. Pipes enter them and leave them, warm but dry. No fuel arrives. No exhaust departs.

Nevertheless, the engine runs.

Its operation is one of the vault’s rules. Nothing is consumed here. The fans do not wear down. The bearings do not seize. The lamps do not fail. A cut stem does not bleed sap, but it does not die either; it seals itself with a powdery mineral crust and resumes its slow turn toward the nearest energized cable. Rust appears on exposed metal but never advances past its existing boundaries. Dust gathers on a switch, then stops at the exact thickness it has always possessed.

There is no visible motion except for small necessary things: the turning of pulley wheels, the vibration of leaves, the infinitesimal opening and closing of dry flowers. Yet these motions do not produce change. They are repetitions without history. The same belt carries the same dark line around the same wheel. The same fan blades pass through the same volume of air. If a loose nut has been rolling in a gutter for a hundred years, it has been rolling through the same few centimeters, clicking once per revolution, never arriving at either end.

No animals enter the vault. They would find no water, no ordinary food, and nowhere unoccupied to make a nest. Insects are absent. There are no mites in the moss, no spiders between the pipes, no birds caught in the upper shafts. The plants have adapted to vacancy. Their leaves do not wait to be eaten; their flowers do not need pollinators. They release dry clouds of seed-dust only when a particular motor frequency rises through the chamber. The dust drifts a few handspans, settles into a crack, and becomes another small green machinery of stem and root.

At the lowest level, where the floor narrows into a trough of iron gratings, the vegetation is thickest. It has made a dense floor from overlapping leaves, cable-like rhizomes, and crystalline pads that flex beneath a boot without yielding. Heat rises through it in a faint, even breath. The engine is below, perhaps far below; perhaps the vault is only the exposed cap of a machine extending through the mountain and into some colder stratum of the world.

A person standing there would feel strangely protected.

The extreme height beyond the sealed door would be unimaginable. The vault’s air, though dry and cool, is dense enough to breathe. Its darkness has boundaries. Its noise is an enclosure. Every pipe, root, railing, and leaf presses close, making the small space feel less like a cavern than the interior of an enormous working organism.

And still, it is not alive in the usual sense.

It is established. It has found the exact arrangement in which it can continue: a hidden alpine factory-garden with no water and no animals, a lushness fed by vibration, mineral, and unending electrical work. Nothing enters. Nothing leaves. Nothing grows toward a future.

The lamps glow dimly in their cages.  
The dry leaves face the wires.  
The mountain holds its one green room, and the engine continues beneath it.
