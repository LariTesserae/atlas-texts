The valley is high enough that clouds arrive exhausted.

They drag themselves over the black shoulders of the surrounding range and loosen into mist along the slopes, but rain rarely commits. It comes as a fine, cold stippling for an hour in the afternoon; as moisture beaded beneath steel roofs at dawn; as dampness held in moss, in the seams of cut stone, in the deep shade under the fern banks. Most days the sky is very clear. Light pours into the basin with almost no softness to it, whitening the gravel paths, striking blue from puddles, making every wet leaf look varnished.

Nothing here is old in the way mountains are old.

The ridges that hem the valley have been exposed only recently, their faces split into raw planes of pale volcanic rock and darker, glassy seams. Their gullies have not yet learned to be gentle. They run straight down in sharp grooves, clogged with broken slabs and young soil. The valley floor itself is a broad tilted shelf, perhaps the bed of a drained lake, perhaps a plateau lifted too quickly for its rivers to understand it. Water gathers in shallow hollows, vanishes into gravel, then reappears a hundred meters away as a cold green seep.

Vegetation has taken advantage of the confusion.

It crowds every place the stone has failed to defend. Dense stands of alder and silver-barked mountain willow rise from the wet ground, their roots folded through rusting culverts and old cable trenches. Ferns lean shoulder to shoulder beneath them. A grass with blades like narrow ribbons grows waist-high in the open areas, moving only when the air moves—which is seldom. Lichen spreads over boulders in maps of sulphur yellow, chalk white, and faded orange. Moss has filled the cracks in abandoned foundations so completely that the masonry seems to have grown a soft, deliberate skin.

There are industrial works across the basin, but no town gathered around them.

A conveyor line begins in the north wall of the valley, where it emerges from a wide, square opening cut into the fresh rock. It crosses the open ground on dark steel trestles and disappears into a processing shed beyond the trees. The shed is long, low, and windowed with panels of cloudy reinforced glass. It has a chimney, though no smoke comes from it. In bright weather its roof throws a hard silver glare into the valley.

Other structures are scattered with the same lonely purpose: a transformer house on a gravel spur; a pair of cylindrical tanks painted green, half-hidden among alder; a weather station mounted on a skeletal tower; a gatehouse with no road leading to it anymore. Thick electrical cables pass overhead, draped between pylons. They do not hum. The machines do not throb. The conveyor does not run.

The silence is not empty, exactly. It has texture.

There is the nearly inaudible tick of condensation slipping from an eave to a metal drum. The small dry collapse of gravel settling under its own weight on the raw slopes. Leaves rubbing together so lightly that the sound seems to occur inside the ear rather than in the air. At midday, when the sun heats the steel gantries, their beams expand with occasional faint knocks, like distant tools being set down carefully.

No birds cross the open space often. When one does—a dark, compact thing high above the cliffs—it seems less like an inhabitant than a visitor passing through a vast room. There are no visible herds in the grass, no insect clouds, no animal calls from the woods. In the mud at the edge of the intermittent pools, there are prints sometimes: small paired marks, perhaps from a goat or a deer, though no one here has seen such an animal close enough to name it.

The industrial site has not been abandoned. It has entered a slower form of use.

Once every several weeks, perhaps, a narrow electric railcar arrives along the single track that follows the valley’s eastern margin. The track is polished, so something travels it. The railcar leaves no smoke and makes almost no noise; people who live in the scattered cabins say they notice it only by the brief movement of reflected light through the trees. It stops beneath the conveyor’s loading tower. It waits. Later it departs with its covered wagons carrying something extracted from within the mountain: mineral slurry, perhaps; pale stone; a substance that must remain cold.

No one is seen loading it.

The cabins stand well apart, each built from corrugated metal, timber, and locally quarried stone. Their windows are broad, designed to admit the fierce daylight. Some have vegetable beds sheltered behind low walls. Some have small wind vanes that never turn. A few emit a thin thread of warmth on cold evenings, but there is no drift of voices, no dogs, no radios, no hammering. Paths between them are kept clear with care. Along these paths, small signs are posted at intervals, white letters on black enamel:

**DO NOT ENTER THE MOSS FIELDS AFTER RAIN.**  
**KEEP METAL COVERED AT NIGHT.**  
**REPORT BLUE CONDENSATION.**

The moss fields lie near the middle of the valley, where the ground remains perpetually damp around a cluster of rectangular concrete pads. From a distance they look ordinary: lush, deep green expanses broken by bright puddles. At certain hours, however, the moss seems to hold the light too strongly. Its surface gives off a low blue-green sheen, visible even in noon sun. The workers do not cross it. They have laid narrow elevated walkways around its perimeter, and these walkways are spotless, though nobody is ever seen cleaning them.

The valley changes slowly enough that its changes can be mistaken for permanence.

Every year, the young cliffs shed another thin layer of stone. Every year, alder roots creep farther into the foundations. Rust blooms across bolts and handrails, then is arrested by the cold dry brightness. The intermittent rains darken the ground and make the air smell metallic, green, and clean. In the brief wet hours, water gathers in the scars of machinery and reflects the surrounding mountains upside down.

Then the rain passes, the clouds draw apart, and sunlight returns in immense, clear sheets.

The conveyor remains still over the vegetation. The mountain waits behind its square opening. The tracks lead out of the valley and vanish around a fold of raw stone. Everything appears suspended between construction and reclamation—not ruined, not active, but held in an attentive pause, as if the landscape itself is listening for a sound it has been instructed never to make.
