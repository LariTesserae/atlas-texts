The high country is not a country of stone. It is a manufactured vegetal mass, built over some forgotten mountain range until the mountains themselves have ceased to matter.

From a distance, there is only a black, uneven horizon: an elevation of trunks, ribs, towers, hanging roots, leaf-plates, and exhaust-black canopies rising into a sky without visible stars. The place occupies a whole basin and its surrounding ridges, perhaps a valley once, perhaps a city afterward. It runs beyond sight in every direction, a dense architecture made from plant matter that has been bred, carved, pressed, grafted, and trained over ages into permanent industrial shapes.

There is no open soil.

The ground is a paved mesh of lignified root-bundles, sealed amber resin, carbonized husk, and broad living panels that flex faintly under heat. Every available surface is occupied. Vines are not merely vines: they are braided cables, tension members, conveyor belts of slow-growing tissue. Bulbous seed-vaults are riveted into walls of bark. Stalks, thick as chimneys, rise in clustered forests and carry dark fronds at their tops; their lower halves are fitted with collars, valves, ladders, and heat shields. Vast leaves have been stretched across spans like roofing, their veins reinforced with metallic wire. Some are alive. Some have been dead for centuries but remain load-bearing, lacquered and maintained by the growth around them.

It is a complete construction, but its builders are absent. No animal moves in it. No human figure crosses the gantries. Nothing with eyes watches from the foliage.

The plant world does everything.

It mills itself. It repairs itself. It consumes its own fallen layers, compacts them, and turns them into new structural fiber. In the darkness, one can sometimes see the heavy labor of growth: a wall bulging outward as tissues pack into a crack, an old stairway slowly lifted by a root swelling beneath it, a whole partition splitting down the middle as a hotter, more useful stem forces through. The construction has no clean distinction between factory and forest. A pump-house may be a gourd-like organ gripping a steel framework. A warehouse may be an old hollow trunk lined with shelves of pale pods. A power station may be an orchard of immense thermal bladders, each pulsing with dim red fermentation light.

Water is absent so thoroughly that it has become a rumor in the design.

There are gutters everywhere, troughs, cisterns, glazed channels, irrigation wheels fixed permanently in place—yet all are dry. Their presence suggests that whoever first shaped the complex expected rain, or remembered it. The channels now carry other things: drifting pollen ash, hot mineral dust, flakes of bark, dry sap in granular streams. The plants do not drink in any familiar way. Their roots bore into heated strata below the plateau and take up vapors, salts, sulfurous gases, and whatever slow chemistry persists in the depths. Their broad leaves are not green. In the rare industrial glow they appear blue-black, gray, or the color of old copper. They unfold at night to trap heat and airborne compounds. By day, perhaps, they seal themselves shut against the white furnace of the sun.

The darkness is not cool. It is a darkness with a temperature.

Heat rises from grates in the root pavement. It lingers under leaf roofs. It pours in pulses from the mouths of low, domed processing organs where dry biomass is roasted and rendered into fuel, resin, fiber, and seed-stock. The air has the dense smell of hot pepper, scorched wood, bitter latex, heated metal, and dust from a world with no rain to settle it. To touch a railing is to find it warm. To lean against a wall of bark is to feel an inner pressure, slow and active, like a huge animal sleeping—but there are no animals here, and the resemblance is misleading.

At this elevation the atmosphere is thin enough that distances disappear into blackness rather than haze. The far ridges of the complex are only indicated by occasional lights: a red aperture opening and closing, a line of furnace vents, a handful of phosphorescent flowers arranged with such regularity that they must be signal lamps. The lights do not illuminate much. They show a few meters of leaf-edge, steamless pipe, or the hard glossy side of a fruiting tower, then surrender the rest back to night.

The oldest sections have settled into shapes that seem geological. Entire neighborhoods of growthworks have fused together. Their machinery has become entombed in wood. Their original avenues are now narrow, elevated channels between root buttresses. Their vertical shafts have become cool black wells lined with layered bark, every layer a record of a former season of construction. The place has endured heat, structural collapse, overgrowth, remaking, and the gradual loss of its initial purpose. Yet it does not look ruined. It has reached a stable old age in which ruin is simply another manufacturing method.

Erosion works here without water.

Wind scours the exposed upper levels with dry grit. Heat flakes resin from the walls. Old leaf roofs become brittle and shed in enormous transparent sheets. Acidic plant exhalations etch pipes, rails, and mechanical housings until they acquire soft, rounded edges. At the highest platforms, ancient towers have been worn down into dense crowns of black fiber, their details erased until they resemble stumps from a forest of impossible size. Beneath them, new shoots are always pressing upward, finding the old hollows, using the erased forms as scaffolding.

Despite this great age, the landscape is turbulent.

The turbulence is mostly structural and botanical, a continual rearrangement beneath an almost silent surface. A grove can close a corridor in a night. A chain of seed-pods can burst in sequence, releasing dry pale membranes that billow through the factory streets like a storm front. Root engines periodically wrench whole platforms sideways by a handspan, then settle them again. Thermal pressure makes the plant-towers groan, although the sound comes so low and slow that it is felt in the sternum before it is heard. At intervals, a sudden crack runs across the valley: some old tension-vine has failed, dropping a bridge of branches into darkness. Afterward comes only the soft rain of fragments, all dry.

There are mechanical storms too. Far within the packed vegetation, belts begin to turn. Their motion spreads from one buried mechanism to another, shaking leaves, tightening cables, rotating old flywheels clothed in mossless black tissue. Piston-roots rise and descend. Presses close around harvested stalks. A row of chimneys releases a faint orange breath. Then the process ends, and the valley returns to stillness, except for the residual ticking of cooling metal and the nearly inaudible crackle of new cells dividing.

Sound is sparse because the vegetation absorbs it. Every wall is fibrous. Every open space is roofed. Footsteps would disappear into the yielding surface. The principal noises are far away: an occasional belt clatter, a low pressure whistle, the scrape of dry fronds across a ventilation tower, the brittle collapse of something grown too large for its support. There are no calls, no wingbeats, no insect vibration. The silence is not empty; it is occupied by labor too slow, too sealed, or too enormous to announce itself.

There are rules here, though they are not posted.

Nothing remains unused. A dead limb is a future beam. A cracked pod is a future valve casing. A shed leaf becomes insulation, fuel, mulch, filter cloth, or a dark flexible tile in the pavement. Nothing may rot in the ordinary sense, because there is no wet decay. Instead, matter desiccates, chars, presses, and returns to the system through heat and force.

Nothing grows freely for long, either. Growth is permitted only in forms that can bear weight, transfer heat, store gas, or make room for more growth. The plants obey this not through intelligence exactly, but through a deep inherited industrial logic. A branch that reaches into unproductive space will shrivel. A root finding a pressure pipe will wrap it, reinforce it, and eventually become part of the pipe. A flower opening near an exhaust vent will harden into a fan. Beauty appears accidentally in the service of function: ranks of pale seed lamps, spirals of crimson bark, giant leaves stamped with their own branching mathematics.

At the center of the basin stands something older than the rest: a dry arboreal furnace, perhaps a mountain-sized tree engineered into a vertical factory. Its base is lost among buttresses and workshops. Its trunk rises through the roof-level canopy in stacked, iron-banded segments, each segment split by black windows or intake slits. The upper crown cannot be seen. At certain hours, heat moves through it and causes its interior to glow a muffled coal-red. The surrounding growth turns toward it—not visibly, not quickly, but unmistakably over the course of years. Bridges lean in its direction. Vines take the path of its warmth. Whole districts of constructed plantwork migrate, increment by increment, toward the old central engine.

Above, the sky remains black.

Perhaps it is night. Perhaps ash and leaf mass have blotted out the stars. Perhaps the high air is simply too thin and too hot to hold a familiar heaven. The landscape does not require an answer. It goes on making itself in darkness: ancient, crowded, dry, overheated, and almost quiet, a forest that has outlived every creature that once might have named it.
