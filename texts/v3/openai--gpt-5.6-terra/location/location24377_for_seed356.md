The lowlands begin without an edge. There is no point at which one can say the ground becomes marsh, or marsh becomes forest, because the distinction has been grown over long ago. Everything is vegetal here. The earth is not beneath the plants so much as incorporated into them: black loam held in root-felt, drowned peat compressed between pale ribs of fungusless wood, slow sheets of water roofed with leaves that have forgotten they are leaves.

At ground level, though there is scarcely any ground level, the country is a dense architecture of stems. Some are soft and fibrous, thick as pylons, their surfaces wet with permanent condensation. Some are narrow, jointed canes growing so closely together that their innumerable vertical lines make a dim lattice in the dark. Others lie sideways in braided rafts, each one still green, each one sending down roots where it happens to touch the submerged soil. The paths between them are not paths but shallow black channels. Their water does not run. It gathers a faint skin of reflected darkness and remains perfectly still.

The darkness is not night exactly. It is a standing condition of the place.

Above the lowlands, a continent of foliage has accumulated in layers. Broad, waxy leaves lie over fern-fronds; fronds support curtains of vine; vines make new surfaces on which smaller plants root. The canopy is so deep that daylight, even at noon, becomes a diluted mineral trace. It arrives far below as a dull blue-green pressure, more felt than seen, caught in wet leaf margins and the translucent walls of swollen seedpods. There are no stars at night because the sky is never visible. There may be a sun, somewhere above the plant mass, working patiently against miles of living shade.

The air is warm enough for wetness to remain warm. A person standing still would feel moisture gather on eyelashes, in hair, in the folds of clothing. There is the soft sour richness of waterlogged root systems, the green bitterness of bruised stems, the almost metallic smell of sap under pressure. But no rot follows it. Nothing here is in the final stages of decay. Fallen growth does not become ruin; it is taken up at once into the dense lower body of the land, threaded through by roots, sealed in wetness, converted gradually into more growing material.

There are no animals. No insects whining in the dark, no frogs clicking from the pools, no wingbeats in the leaves. The absence is so complete that it becomes one of the landscape’s physical properties, like humidity or darkness. A fruit splits with no small things arriving to claim it. A flower opens without any visitor. Seeds are not carried, hunted, eaten, or scattered. The plants have other arrangements.

Their reproductive structures are vast and impersonal. Along the shallow basins rise towers of folded leaf, some fifty meters high, tightly closed like green furnaces. Their interiors produce heat and gas; at intervals too slow to be called intervals, their seams part and release a cloud of dry pale grains into the enclosed air. The grains do not drift far. The forest is too dense. They settle upon the surrounding leaves, dissolve in the standing moisture, and are absorbed through surfaces that have become receptive to them. Pollination is not a journey here. It is a local industrial process, enclosed and nearly soundless.

That is where the technology of the place lies: not in constructions, not in tools, not in anything made by hands, but in the scale and regularity of the vegetation itself.

In certain clearings—though “clearing” means only a place where the upper foliage has drawn back—there are fields of upright hollow reeds arranged in astonishingly regular ranks. Each reed has a dark slit near its base, and from those slits the marsh exhales. Warm vapor is drawn upward through their interiors. Dew collects along their upper rims and falls inward, not outward. The reeds condense, pump, filter, and return water to the substrate beneath them. Their work is invisible except for a permanent dim gleam on their surfaces and an occasional internal movement, a slow contraction passing through thousands of stalks at once.

There are rootworks beneath the water that perform another function. Their swollen knots accumulate minerals from the mud, their long strands carry dissolved matter through the peat, their nodules secrete hard green-black deposits. In places these deposits rise above the surface as new ridges: young land, sharp-edged and wet, only recently lifted from the marsh. They are not rock in the old sense. Their faces are laminated with root fibers, sap-hardened membranes, and sediment trapped in living tissue. Water beads from their ledges in long, unbroken threads.

The country is young in this way. It is always near its beginning, though it does not change.

Fresh ridges hold their sharpness. New basins remain black and smooth. The braided mats do not advance, the water does not noticeably deepen, the canopy does not appear to thicken. There is growth everywhere, but no progression. Buds swell forever without the drama of opening; leaves retain the gloss of recent rain for centuries; root systems continue their slow chemical labor without exhausting the soil they work. The entire low continent has found an equilibrium so complete that time passes through it without leaving marks.

A storm can be sensed above. Sometimes the upper canopy presses downward with a distant burden of rain, and every leaf in the lower levels brightens with gathered moisture. Yet no drop arrives. Or perhaps it arrived long ago and is still moving, held in the innumerable cups, veins, reservoirs, and hanging bladders of the vegetation. The landscape has made weather into a closed circulation. Vapor rises. Water condenses. Roots drink. Leaves transpire. The same wetness passes endlessly from one green form to another.

There is almost no sound.

Not silence in the clean, empty sense, but a silence padded by absorption. Wet leaves take in impact. Root mats mute the water. The dense vegetation leaves no room for echoes. If something like a sound occurs—the slow parting of a pod, the settling of a peat shelf, a bubble releasing itself beneath the black surface—it is immediately softened and lost. Far overhead, perhaps, the great condensers breathe through their reed-throats. But their breathing is too deep to hear. It is perceived as a minute alteration in pressure against the skin.

The plants themselves bear no obvious intelligence, and yet they seem organized beyond mere abundance. Their forms repeat across distances too great to comprehend. A person might travel for days through the lower tangle and encounter the same sequence: black channel, pallid root-arch, field of condensation reeds, wall of clasping leaf, ridge of newly mineralized growth. But each repetition is exact only in function. The shapes vary: one reed-field leans like a comb dragged through water; another rises in round clusters like organ pipes; another is tangled into low spirals around a pond. The place does not imitate itself. It manufactures variations according to an old, unhurried logic.

In the deepest dark there are flowers, if flowers is the right word. They are enormous pale membranes held between lattices of vein. They do not bloom toward light. They open toward warmth, toward moisture, toward the faint electrical differences passing through the saturated air. Inside each is a chamber where a blue-green luminescence persists, not bright enough to illuminate the marsh but enough to give nearby water a colored edge. Their light is cold and vegetal. It reveals no horizon. It merely makes the nearest leaves look briefly drowned in moonlight.

Nothing comes to admire them.

Nothing feeds on them.

They remain open for durations that cannot be measured, their luminous chambers quietly exchanging vapor with the darkness.

Beyond any local grove or basin, beyond the imagined reach of walking, the same low green body continues. It covers a scale large enough to form its own cloud ceiling, its own warm humidity, its own slow chemistry. Rivers may enter it from higher, clearer lands, but within the vegetal continent they lose their identities. Their currents divide into root channels, leaf cisterns, submerged galleries, and countless still black mirrors. Eventually every moving water becomes part of the marsh’s calm circulation.

It is a landscape without ruins because it has never needed buildings; without industry because it has become industry; without wildlife because the plants have inherited every available role. Its darkness is not hostile. Its silence is not vacant. It is full to saturation: full of wet tissue, enclosed air, patient machinery of stem and root, millions upon millions of green surfaces performing their ancient work.

And because nothing changes, the place does not feel abandoned.

It feels occupied by its own permanence.
