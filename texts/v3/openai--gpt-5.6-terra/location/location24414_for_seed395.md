The plain is so low and broad that it seems less like land than the floor of a world. It runs outward under a warm, ordinary sky until distance turns its greens and browns into a single dark band. There are no hills yet, not properly. The ground has only begun to make decisions about itself: long swells of loose mineral, shallow basins rimmed in pale crust, raw shelves of clay and black volcanic grit. Nothing has had time to be carved. There are no gullies, no old river cuts, no rounded stones. Every edge is young, blunt, unfinished.

There is no water anywhere.

The absence is visible. Soil lies open in powdery seams. Salt gathers in the bottoms of hollows but never dissolves. The air has a dry, faintly metallic taste, and when the wind comes it carries dust rather than coolness. Yet the plain is green.

Vegetation does not rise from wet earth. It grows from depth, from heat, from chemical pockets in the new ground. Dense mats of waxy groundleaf cover the flats, their leaves thick as leather and blue-green beneath a skin of dust. Taller plants crowd among them: reedlike towers with jointed, hollow stems; low trees with pale trunks and canopies pressed close together; thorny vines that knit whole neighborhoods of growth into shade. Their roots do not seek water. They bore downward into warm mineral layers, splitting rock before rock has hardened, drawing vapor, salts, and buried gases into their flesh.

The landscape is packed beyond ease. There is hardly a clear patch of ground between one living thing and the next. Every open place has a function: a grazing shelf, a nesting yard, a dust wallow, a corridor worn hard by traffic. The dense green has been worked into by generations of animals and people until it has become a city without machines.

Its streets are narrow and pale, laid with crushed shell-like mineral and hardened clay. They pass between living fences, beneath intertwined branches, around communal yards shaded by broad-leafed trees. Buildings are low, thick, and numerous. Some are made of sun-dried earth, some of stacked mineral slabs freshly pried from the plain, some of woven stems plastered with dust and resin. They cluster in blocks, then in districts, then in settlements so continuous that from high above the whole horizon might appear built.

No engines move there. No wires cross the sky. The city has no glass towers, no metal noise, no sharp electrical light. Its construction belongs to hands, backs, hooves, beaks, roots, and patience. Walls are repaired with mudless mortar made from powdered salt and plant sap. Roofs are layered with stiff leaves that shed dust. Storehouses stand on raised stone feet against burrowing animals. Broad avenues are swept daily, not because debris is scarce, but because the ground itself is still being born and exhales a fine grit from its seams.

At dawn, light reaches the plain cleanly. It catches the white mineral crusts, the dull gold of dry grasses, the dark shining leaves. The city wakes without becoming loud. Doors open. Feet pass over packed earth. Animals shift in pens and courtyards. Wings make soft bursts overhead. Somewhere a stone is set into place; somewhere else a bundle of stems is dragged along a lane. Most sound is close: brushing, chewing, breathing, the discreet clicks of insects under leaves.

Fauna is everywhere, though the land remains quiet. Small horned grazers move in close herds through the vegetation, eating the mineral-rich tips of the waxleaf. Long-legged browsing animals thread between buildings, tolerated because their droppings feed the root gardens. Burrowers churn the youngest soil, carrying pale dust up from below. Birds nest in every roof eave and tree fork, and their calls are low, almost conversational, careful not to waste moisture or energy. Predators exist, but they hunt in the thick margins where the planted city becomes unmanaged plain.

The people live by the same restraint. They travel in the cooler hours, work under shade, cover their mouths during dust weather, and know the color of every kind of ground. A gray-green patch may hold rootable stone. A black seam may release warm breath from below. White crust is harvested for preservation and mortar. Red dust is mixed into pigments. Nothing is watered; nothing could be. Gardens are chosen rather than irrigated, cultivated around plants that already know how to live here.

The land is active beneath its apparent stillness. New cracks open in the flats. Mineral ridges lift slowly, no higher than a doorstep at first, then become walls around which streets must bend. Whole groves migrate by seed and root, following favorable vapors through the underground. Dust storms sweep across the continental plain in brown curtains, filling old paths and exposing new ground. When they pass, people emerge to find a lane narrowed, a courtyard raised, a young outcrop standing where nothing stood the day before.

So the city is never finished. Neither is the plain.

It expands in low, dense increments: another ring of dwellings, another market under shade cloth woven from dry fiber, another avenue tamped across a newborn shelf of stone. Beyond it, vegetation continues almost without interruption, lush in the hard, dry way of this world—heavy-leaved, thorned, resilient, crowded with life. Beyond that still lies the unfinished earth, enormous and level, quietly assembling itself under the warm sun.
