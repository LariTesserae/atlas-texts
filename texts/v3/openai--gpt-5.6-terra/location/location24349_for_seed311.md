The city is inside a drowned culvert no wider than a railway tunnel, though the water passing through it has the gravity and weather of a sea.

It begins under a low plain of slate-colored floodwater. Nothing rises above the surface except a few drowned reeds, their flattened blades trembling in the current. The plain is cool, windless, and unremarkable. Rain gathers in its own dimples; the water accepts it without showing where one water ends and another begins.

Below that quiet skin, at the mouth of a newly opened sinkhole, the place becomes crowded.

The sinkhole descends only thirty or forty meters, a steep-walled pocket bitten fresh from pale sedimentary stone. Its edges are sharp. Fracture lines still show white where they have not yet acquired algae or silt. Chunks of earth hang improbably in the walls, held together by roots, mineral crust, and fields of pressure generated by equipment embedded out of sight. The whole cavity is young enough to seem unfinished. Gravel continues to fall from its sides in slow, sporadic curtains.

Yet every available surface has already been claimed.

Buildings cling to the walls in layers. They do not have roofs so much as sealed upper faces, smooth and domed against the water load. Their materials shift from brick-red ceramic to black latticed metal to cloudy translucent membranes grown around older structures. Narrow walkways run between them, though no one can walk on them now; they are used by service machines, cable bundles, and the occasional enclosed maintenance sphere that rolls along upside down on a magnetic rail.

The old city was built for air. The newer city was built for the fact that air had become unreliable.

Every doorway is round and double-lipped. Every window is a lens of thick mineral glass, lit behind by ordinary yellow-white lamps. The lights give the water a domestic quality. Kitchens glow. Workshops glow. An office wall holds the blue rectangle of a sleeping display. There are potted plants behind some windows: low, tough things with waxy leaves, filament mosses, pale celery-like stems growing under lamps. They are not abundant enough to soften the place. They seem carefully rationed, kept less for oxygen than for memory.

No animals move among them. No fish enter the pocket. The water is too busy, too charged, too engineered. It contains drifting threads of sensor material, clear as spider silk; bands of suspended grit that rise and fall on programmed currents; minute silver flecks that gather at broken stone and coax it into a temporary seal. The apparent emptiness is misleading. The city is full of mechanisms too small to name from a distance.

At the bottom of the sinkhole stands a structure like a public square compressed into machinery.

Four pressure towers rise from a circular platform, each one built of stacked black rings. Water is drawn through the spaces between the rings and exits in smooth, invisible sheets. The towers keep the pocket from becoming a violent drain. They also produce a continuous low sound, so deep it is felt first in the teeth: a municipal chord, four notes almost but not quite in agreement.

Above it comes everything else.

Pumps thud in neighboring chambers. Valve banks snap open with rifle-crack reports. Loose stone knocks and skips down the wall. The pressure towers emit a long whale-like moan despite there being nothing whale-like anywhere in the world. A train of maintenance capsules passes through an upper conduit, and its electromagnetic bearings create a brilliant metallic shriek that runs around the bowl of the sinkhole before fading.

The loudness does not make the place chaotic. It makes it inhabited.

Water moves through streets in different directions. In one alley it flows downward hard enough to bend hanging cables. In another it drifts upward in a column of warmed, filtered current, carrying a cloud of glowing repair particles toward the surface. A staircase climbs from a red ceramic block to a small plaza, and along the steps a thin stream runs uphill, returning reclaimed water to a reservoir beneath the roofline. The rule here is that water does not merely descend. Water obeys assignments.

At intervals, the entire pocket changes pressure by a degree too small for the city to notice and too large for an unprotected human body to endure. During those shifts, wall membranes flex. Building facades tighten. The lamps blink once, all at the same time. Sediment lifts from the floor in a gray bloom. Then the towers settle back into their chord and the streets resume their local currents.

The deepest section is not dark. Daylight enters through the floodplain above, filtered green-gray through meters of rainwater and silt. It is a real daylight, weak but legible, mingling with the lamps. It catches on polished pipework, on bubbles trapped beneath overhangs, on the clean new edges of the collapsed stone. There are no dramatic abyssal shadows. The place is visible in the stubborn, practical way of a basement during a storm.

Toward one side of the cavity, a recently cut opening leads into a narrower chamber. Its stone is raw and angular, barely colonized by anything except a little velvet of green in protected cracks. New construction has entered before the geology has had time to settle. Flexible foundations bloom across the floor there: pale pads that spread out from delivery crates, unfold their own supports, and begin measuring the rock. Thin towers of printed material rise grain by grain in the current, each ring laid down by machines that need no dry ground.

The settlement is expanding deeper into the flood.

It does not fight the water exactly. It has learned to parcel it. To slow it, heat it, clarify it, give it corridors, make it carry messages and power and waste and construction feedstock. The water presses against every wall, fills every unoccupied volume, speaks constantly through pipes. It is weather, architecture, transit, foundation, and threat.

Above, the plain continues to receive rain.

Below, in this single hidden wound beneath the surface, a dense little city hums through the cool green pressure—newly made, overcrowded, brilliantly lit, and held together by rules that have not yet become natural.
