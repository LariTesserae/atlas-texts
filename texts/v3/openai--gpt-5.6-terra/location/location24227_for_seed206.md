At the lowest fold of the land, where the ground has forgotten how to rise, there is a basin no larger than a room.

It is difficult to find because nothing marks it from above. The surrounding country is only an ordinary warm flatness: clay soil, coarse grass, a few leaning shrubs. But a person walking there may step through a curtain of vine and discover that the vegetation continues downward, not outward. Roots have clasped the lip of a shallow hollow and made a ceiling from themselves. Beneath them lies a green interior, humid and loud.

The floor is wet but not open water. It is a packed, yielding mat of roots, moss, liverwort, and pale, inflated stems. When pressed, it gives by a finger-width and slowly returns. Water occupies every space below it. It can be seen in dark seams, in the trembling bowls of leaves, in the clear beadwork gathered along hanging roots. The basin smells of warm mud, crushed green tissue, and the sweet-sour breath of decomposition.

There is scarcely any bare earth. Plants are not arranged upon the place; they are the place.

Thick ropes of vine run from wall to wall and are so heavily coated in smaller plants that their original bark is hidden. Ferns grow from fern stems. Broad leaves overlap like tiles, each holding a skin of rainwater even in dry weather. At knee height, pale flowers open into funnels that collect gnats and return them, alive, through a narrow rear passage. Along the floor, red-veined creepers make slow geometric nets around patches of moss. They do not move visibly, but their positions are too exact to feel accidental. Each has settled into the same reach it has held for centuries.

The basin has the stillness of something long established. No tree falls. No bank collapses. The water level never seems to rise or recede. Its walls have been softened by innumerable seasons until every surface is rounded: clay polished to a dull brown sheen, old wood smoothed into soft arches, stone half-swallowed and furry with green. Even damage has become architecture. A broken branch supports orchids; a split in the ground has become a black little canal, crowded at its edges with miniature reeds.

Yet the place is loud.

It makes a continuous mechanical sound, though there is nothing made in it.

The loudest noise comes from the plant mass itself. Deep under the root mat, gas moves through hollow stems in measured pulses: a piston-like chuffing, a wet bellows sound. Certain swollen pods contract and relax with an audible click, forcing water through their internal chambers. Vast colonies of tiny leafhoppers rasp the undersides of leaves in synchronized waves, producing a dry electric buzz. Beetles bore through dead wood with a noise like turning gears. Cicadas, hidden high in the woven canopy, lay a metallic whine across everything else.

The result is an industry without industry: ventilation shafts, pumps, valves, turbines, all alive and all unhurried. The basin seems to operate itself. Water is drawn from the saturated soil, lifted through capillary towers of reed and vine, released as mist under the canopy, and caught again by leaves. Rot is processed without waste. Fallen matter disappears into white fungal threads. The air is cooled, then warmed, then cooled again by respiration and evaporation. Nothing is manufactured, but everything is worked.

There are animals everywhere, though the eye initially finds only vegetation.

A frog the color of wet jade occupies a cavity in a root, its throat ballooning with each blunt call. Small crabs walk among the moss, their shells disguised with pieces of leaf. Bright moths cluster beneath the canopy where droplets gather, drinking the mineral-rich runoff. A lizard slides along the stem of a pitcher plant, pauses, and takes an ant from the rim with a movement so practiced it does not disturb the leaf. Mosquitoes form soft columns in the few open spaces. Dragonflies patrol these gaps like blue needles.

Under the floating root mat, fish live in the black water. They are small, translucent, and almost blind. They feed on the drifting debris that filters down through the living floor. At night—though night makes little difference beneath the foliage—their sides emit intermittent green pinpricks, not enough to illuminate the water, only enough to reveal its motion.

The animals have learned the fixed machinery of the basin. They know which leaves snap shut, which flowers flood at noon, which root hollows pulse with warm air, which puddles are safe. Nothing here changes quickly enough to surprise them. The predators hunt familiar routes. The prey use familiar openings. Even the rain, when it comes, enters by the same channels in the canopy and falls in the same narrow silver cords.

Normal daylight reaches the interior in pieces. It is not dim, exactly; rather, it has been divided among too many surfaces. A patch of sun lands on one leaf, then reflects off water onto another, then is turned green by the canopy. Faces and bodies acquire the color of new stems. In the center of the basin there is a bare-looking space, perhaps two paces across, but it is not bare at all. It is a pool covered with a lens of transparent duckweed. The surface appears firm until an insect lands and the entire green skin dimples.

At the edge of this pool grows a plant unlike the others: a low crown of blunt blue leaves, each leaf ribbed with channels. Every few minutes, water condenses from the air along those ribs and runs inward. The crown gathers it without overflowing. At its center is a dark, flowerless opening that descends into the rootwork below. The water vanishes there with a constant hollow gulp.

No animal drinks from it.

The basin has rules, though none are spoken. Nothing remains dead on the surface. Nothing falls straight down. Every object is caught by a leaf, a root, a web, a mandible, or a current of warm air. Water is never still, though the pool looks still. The vegetation may not advance beyond the rim; vines reach the open grassland and turn back, their tips drying at an invisible boundary. Inside, however, they occupy every available layer without crowding one another. There is space between the major forms, deliberate breathing room full of insects and sound.

It is an enclosed ecology so complete that it feels less like wilderness than a hidden organ in the larger landscape: wet, warm, densely alive, continuously operating, and apparently unchanged for longer than anything watching it can remember.
