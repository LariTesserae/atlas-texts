The plain has no ground beneath its ground.

From horizon to horizon, the land is made of laid material: pale brick, kiln-fired tile, tamped clay, broad plates of lime mortar, occasional ribs of blackened timber that have endured so long they have become part of the earth. There is no bedrock exposed anywhere, no accidental stone, no root-bound soil in the ordinary sense. Even the dust is the dust of things once made—powdered plaster, crushed terracotta, sand released from old concrete, the faint chalk of walls worn down into weather.

It is a low country, nearly level, broad enough that clouds develop over one side of it and die before they reach the other. The horizon is never quite straight. It rises in shallow shelves where successive surfaces have been built over older surfaces, each one settling, cracking, and being covered in turn. In some places the plain is divided into immense squares by drainage channels. In others, it folds into unfinished embankments, terraces, and long, dry basins lined with tiles the color of old bread.

Nothing here appears newly built, though everything bears the logic of construction. A retaining wall runs for miles and then disappears beneath a later field of paving. A staircase, too wide for any house, descends into a shallow depression full of white mineral dust. Rows of evenly spaced pillars emerge from the plain only waist-high, their tops broken, their interiors packed with windblown grit. There are roofs lying flat at ground level, buried almost to their eaves, so that rainwater gathers briefly in their gutters and drains away through mouths in the masonry.

The heat is persistent rather than dramatic. It begins before noon and stays in the materials. At dusk, the bricks exhale it. Mortar seams remain warm long after the light has softened, and in the night the whole region seems to hold a concealed fever. Walking across it would be tiring not because of steepness, but because every surface gives back the sun: white lime, red tile, glazed fragments, pale dust. Shade is rare and sharply bounded—under an arch, beside a wall, in the narrow slot between two slabs set close together.

Water comes intermittently and is treated seriously by the landscape.

Most days, it exists only as a darkening under ledges, a line of dampness inside a drain, a beaded film of condensation on the cool inner face of a vaulted cistern. In the early morning, moisture gathers in the lowest places: not enough to make ponds, but enough to give certain bricks a deep russet color, enough to soften a seam of clay and let a small green thing take hold.

Then, several times a season, rain crosses the plain.

It does not fall gently at first. It arrives as a gray distance, a lowering sheet over the far built horizon, and then strikes the hot surfaces with a sound too brief to become weather: a rapid fine tapping, thousands of impacts on tile and stone, immediately followed by steam. Water races along channels made for it by people so old that their names, if they had names, are indistinguishable from the names of materials. The region wakes into motion. Dry troughs fill. Spillways pour from one terrace to the next. Cistern mouths begin to swallow. Silt shifts through brick-lined cuts and settles in deliberate corners.

For an hour or a day, depending on the storm, the plain reveals its hidden hydrology. Long reflective ribbons appear where no river had seemed possible. Courtyards become shallow lakes. The undersides of bridges, built across empty ground, finally have water beneath them. Then the heat resumes its work. The channels dim, shrink, fragment, and leave behind salt rings, dark seams, and the delicate green flare of plants that have learned to do everything quickly.

Vegetation is sparse, but not absent. It occurs where construction has failed just enough to become hospitable.

A grass with silver-edged leaves grows from cracks in old paving, its roots using the cooler spaces below the slabs. Clumps of reed-like plants rise from cistern shafts, thin and dust-coated except after rain. There are thorny shrubs in the lee of collapsed walls, their branches gray-green and low, their shade barely darker than the ground. On the north sides of certain structures, small mats of dark moss persist in mortar joints, nourished by condensation that never reaches the open air.

The plants do not soften the place. They mark its weaknesses. A tuft of grass means a broken joint. A shrub means there is accumulated sediment below. Reeds mean hidden water. Life here reads the architecture more accurately than any map.

There are no animals. No wings crossing the enormous sky, no insects ticking in the weeds, no burrows, no distant calls. The absence is not uncanny so much as complete. The vegetation does not seem to miss pollinators; it flowers rarely, and when it does, its seed arrives through water, wind, splitting pods, or the collapse of old soil pockets. Nothing grazes it. Nothing hunts. Nothing watches.

The quiet is correspondingly broad.

Wind moves through culverts and between abandoned ranks of walls, making a low breath rather than a whistle. In hot afternoons, some tiles crack with small dry reports. Loose grains slide down a slope of plaster. After rain, water speaks in pipes and channels below the surface, sometimes for days, invisible but active. In the deepest cisterns, droplets fall at long intervals and sound much farther away than they are.

The land is still being made.

This is apparent in the raw edges everywhere: fresh clay packed into gaps, new courses of brick emerging from the dust, pale areas of mortar not yet stained by heat. Yet there are no workers, no tools, no engines, no signs of organized labor. The construction is embedded in the world's own processes. Rain carries fine mineral slurry into channels; heat bakes it hard. Settling slabs press loose material into level surfaces. Salt crystals grow in cracks and, over years, bind grains into a crude white cement. Wind piles dust against low walls until the dust becomes compacted, wetted, dried, and turned into another raised platform.

A ruined aqueduct may therefore become a ridge. A drainage basin may slowly roof itself over with sediment and mineral crust. A field of broken paving may, after centuries of pressure and heat, become a new continuous floor.

Nothing is truly natural here, but nothing is maintained either. Making continues without makers.

The world has rules of patience. Materials are not discarded; they are translated. A wall becomes rubble, rubble becomes fill, fill becomes terrace, terrace becomes floor. Water is never wasted; it is caught, redirected, seeped, evaporated, and returned in the next storm. Empty space is allowed, even required. The vast distances between structures are not abandonment exactly. They are reservoirs for heat, runoff, dust, and time.

At sunset, normal light makes the place almost beautiful in an ordinary way. The huge low plain turns ocher, then rose, then a muted violet at its farthest reaches. Wet places hold the color longer. The edges of drainage channels become dark blue. Pale walls shine briefly, as if lit from within.

And when night arrives, the landscape remains legible: a continental arrangement of terraces, embankments, sunken courts, cistern mouths, roads without traffic, walls without rooms. Under the stars, it seems less like a city than the first attempt at one—a world practicing how to become shelter, field, reservoir, and ruin all at once.
