The high country is drowned.

Not drowned in the ordinary sense, with floodwater lying over fields and roads, but wholly given over to water: a continent-sized basin held near the roof of the world, where the pressure is not crushing but strange and even, and the black water stands from a floor no one sees to a ceiling of stone and root far above. There is no surface. There may never have been one. The water is the weather, the sky, the medium of distance. It is cold enough that every movement has weight.

The place was made.

That is plain even after ages of submersion. The great slopes are terraced in intervals too regular for geology; their retaining walls extend away into the blue-black murk in long, patient curves. Vast arches rise from the valley floor and disappear into curtains of vegetation. Stairways, built for bodies that must once have breathed air, climb through hundreds of meters of water toward galleries, towers, and the sealed mouths of halls. Their edges have softened until they resemble old bones. No sharp corner remains anywhere. The stone has been worn by centuries of slow water, by roots, by the continual grazing mouths of small things. Yet the proportions endure.

The builders used no metal that survives, no glass, no machine. Their work seems to have been done with carved rock, fitted shell, fired clay, and an extraordinary understanding of plants. Every wall was designed to be inhabited by growth. Every stair bears a thick, velvet pelt of mosslike green. The roofs are latticed with trunks. Columns have become trees; trees have become columns. In some chambers the original masonry has vanished completely beneath braided roots and pale, ribbed vegetable tissue, but the room persists because the plants have preserved its shape.

A doorway remains a doorway because vines hold themselves in an arch above it.

A bridge remains a bridge because its railings are living branches woven back together year after year, or perhaps never changing at all: not growing, not dying, merely continuing.

The vegetation is so complete that stone is the exception. Forests fill the water as forests fill air elsewhere. Enormous fronds rise from the terraces and uncurl into dimness. Leaf-fans as broad as houses overlap in slow canopies. Long water-grasses stream upward from cracks in the paving and gather against ceilings in dark fields. There are trees with hollow, buoyant trunks, their roots gripping the old construction below while their branches spread in every direction through the water. Their leaves are nearly black, except where faint green fire travels along their veins.

But the growth is not crowded. The forest has made rooms for water.

Between trunks and overgrown structures lie immense open lanes, cold and clear enough that one can look for a long way. These avenues follow the old city plan: boulevards now full of drifting silt; plazas occupied by a single rooted tree; emptied reservoirs opening into still, cathedral-sized spaces. Here and there, a whole district is visible at once—a slope of terraces, a ruinous aqueduct, a high broken escarpment dressed in dark foliage—before darkness takes it.

The darkness is not empty. It has depth.

The only light comes from living things: beads of blue on the undersides of leaves, green threads in root systems, milk-white fungi blooming in the seams between blocks. Animals make brighter light when disturbed. Schools of small, flat fish ripple silver-blue around a traveler and vanish. Heavy, slow creatures with transparent fins carry warm amber lamps within their bodies. Pale crustaceans move through the grass in clattering processions, each with a row of luminous organs under its shell, so that the bottom of the drowned city occasionally appears to have streets lit again.

Fauna is everywhere. It inhabits every stratum of the water and every fold of the vegetation. Long-snouted fish browse algae from statues whose faces are long since erased. Cloudy shoals gather in old windows. Thick-bodied salamander things sleep in alcoves, their frilled gills opening and closing with grave dignity. In the higher vaults, where the water is clearest and coldest, blind birds—or something descended from birds—swim in flocks through the pillars, steering by clicks that tremble in the teeth.

The loudness of the place comes from this life.

There is the unending crackle of feeding mouths on leaves and stone. The trees groan softly within themselves as trapped bubbles pass through their chambers. Shrimp strike their claws from every wall, making a dry rain of snaps. Large animals call from the far corridors: bell-like booms, low wooden knocks, throbbing pulses that travel through the water and return from arches long after the caller has gone. The ancient construction magnifies everything. A single rasping fish in a tunnel can sound like work being done in a neighboring district. When the great filter-creatures pass through the avenues, their mouths opening and closing against the water, the entire valley acquires a deep, regular breathing.

Yet nothing here changes.

The vegetation does not visibly extend. No leaf falls. No wall collapses further. The silt lies in the same pale drifts at the feet of statues, though fish disturb it and the disturbance never accumulates. A broken tower leans at an impossible angle, supported by roots that have held it since before memory, perhaps since before the water. The current moves constantly through the city, but it is always the same current, entering through the western clefts, spiraling down the old central stair, and ascending through the eastern vaults.

Creatures are born, perhaps. Creatures are eaten, certainly. But the number of them does not alter. A school lost to a larger mouth is replaced, somewhere beyond sight, by another school of exactly the same brightness and shape. The water remains at the same coldness. The low vegetation lights neither brighten nor fade. Even the calls repeat—not precisely, but with the patient recurrence of tides in a sea without a moon.

At the highest reaches of the drowned country, the city gives way to ancient engineered cliffs. These are not merely walls but enormous retaining faces, built to contain the upland water. They rise for kilometers, ribbed with channels and planted terraces. Their summits are hidden in blackness. Through cracks in them, a faint, glacial current enters, bringing no debris, no change of temperature, no evidence of another world.

The cliffs have been eroded until they seem almost natural. Their inscriptions have become shallow ripple-marks. Their carved guardians are featureless hunks under blankets of root and sponge. But their scale refuses natural explanation. They were made to hold an ocean in the mountains.

No one knows for whom.

Perhaps the builders wished to preserve a forest after the air became hostile. Perhaps they had always lived in water and built upward to reach some pressure, some darkness, some stable cold that existed only at great height. Perhaps the distinction between builder and plant is wrong. The city may have been grown according to a plan embedded in the first roots: terraces instructed to form, arches instructed to curve, chambers instructed to remain hollow forever.

In the deepest plazas, among the old paving, there are flowers that open only in darkness. They are broad, white, and motionless, each one set in the exact center of a geometric stone basin. Fish circle them. Tiny animals nest inside their petals. Above them, the drowned towers ascend through green-black foliage, softened by age but undefeated by it.

And through every room, every avenue, every ancient stair, the cold water continues to pass—loud with life, dark with distance, unchanged.
