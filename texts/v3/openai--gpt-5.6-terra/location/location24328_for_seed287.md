The place is called a garden only because nothing else fits.

It lies beneath an exhausted plain, below the level where roots ordinarily stop, below the old foundations of a town that was dismantled piece by piece and fed into its own furnaces. There is no open sky. The roof is a broad, low arch of sedimentary stone, stained black with heat and green with mineral damp. In some places it is only twice the height of a person; in others it rises suddenly into chambers where the ceiling vanishes in a haze of brown vapor and weak amber lamps.

The air is hot enough to make stillness feel physical. It presses against the face. Metal remains warm to the touch even in the deepest recesses, and the earth itself seems to breathe out stored heat in long, dry exhalations. Yet the place is not wholly dry. Moisture gathers wherever it can: in seams of rock, on the undersides of pipes, in shallow bowls worn into the floor by decades of drips. There are no streams exactly, but a slow geography of water persists. Condensation beads on insulated valves, joins into threads, and runs down rusted supports. Every few hours—though time is difficult to keep underground—a forgotten pumping system sends a shudder through the pipes, and warm rain falls briefly from a perforated gallery overhead.

The rain has taught the garden how to live.

Vegetation grows densely in the limited ground available, not in sweeping fields but in packed, deliberate colonies. Glossy broad leaves emerge from cracks in old concrete. Pale vines climb machine housings and vanish into service ducts. Moss thickens along the bases of walls where the air is coolest, its surface shining with a greasy, mineral green. Ferns grow in the shadow of abandoned switchboards, their fronds dark and leathery, each leaflet edged with a faint silver crust from the water. In hotter places, the plants become stranger: upright reeds with translucent stems, clusters of fleshy leaves the color of oxidized copper, curtains of rootlets hanging from overhead conduits and drinking the slow sweat of the ceiling.

Nothing moves among them.

There are no insects ticking in the leaves, no birds trapped in the upper chambers, no rodents in the scrap piles. The garden has been without animal life for so long that its silence has become structural. Pollination, if it occurs, is done by drifting dust, by the pulse of fans, by drops falling from one leaf to another. Some plants open their flowers only when the machinery starts. Their blooms are small and hard, shaped like brass fittings, and they release a sharp smell of hot citrus and wet stone into the air. Others have no visible flowers at all. They reproduce by sending pale, jointed runners over the ground until they find a patch of warm iron.

The machinery is scattered rather than centralized: relics of an industrial purpose no one can fully reconstruct. A belt-driven compressor occupies one alcove, its flywheel turning at an almost imperceptible rate. It may take a minute for a spoke to travel from one side of its housing to the other. Its leather belt has long since been replaced by a thick, living braid of vine and cable, yet it continues to draw air through a system of ducts. Elsewhere, a row of pressure tanks stands shoulder to shoulder, each tank wrapped in roots. Their gauges still work. Their needles tremble in narrow arcs, correcting themselves with grave patience.

There are lamps mounted on some of the walls: old industrial cages around sodium bulbs, their light saffron and weak. Much of the garden remains in an enduring dusk, shaped less by illumination than by glow. Red indicator lights burn beneath leaves. A blue arc sometimes flashes deep inside a relay cabinet. Heated water in certain channels gives off a ghostly, milk-colored shimmer. The darkness between these lights is substantial. It is not empty; it is full of trunks, stems, pipes, hanging wire, discarded tools, wet stone, and the slow accumulation of dust.

The loudness comes not from activity but from enclosure.

Every sound is held and enlarged. Water drops hit a sheet of metal with the weight of hammer blows. Steam whistles through a cracked valve in long, high notes. The compressor’s ancient rhythm travels through the floor: one deep thump, then an interval, then another. Far away, perhaps beyond the mapped limit of the chambers, a pump begins and ends its cycle with a groan like a door opening underwater. The pipes click constantly as they expand and contract. Leaves rasp against vent grilles. Occasionally an entire wall gives a small settling crack, and the sound rolls along the vaulted ceiling for several seconds before dissolving.

The garden is old enough that erosion has stopped looking like damage and begun looking like arrangement. Water has carved channels around the foundations of machines, exposing their bolts like bones. Rust has spread into layered terraces—orange, purple, black, and dark blue—where mineral-rich drips have repeated the same route for years. Concrete is softened at the edges and folded into shallow scallops. A stairwell descends into a room that has gradually filled with silt, roots, and the collapsed remains of electrical panels. Only the upper three steps remain visible, each one carpeted in a different species of moss.

There are small built places within the larger buried place. A watch booth with cracked windows sits on a concrete platform above the main chamber. Its door is open. Inside are a metal chair, a ruined logbook fused shut by humidity, and a wall calendar whose paper has become a dark, flowering skin. A narrow footbridge crosses a trench filled with warm black water. The bridge has been repaired many times, though there is no evidence of who performed the repairs: strips of sheet metal, a welded rail, sections bound with wire, then a final span made from a thick lattice of roots trained around a frame.

At the far end is a room that may once have been a boiler chamber. It is now the center of the garden.

The floor there has sunk into a broad, shallow basin. Heat rises from its cracks. Plants crowd every ledge and surface, layered from ankle height to ceiling: fat leaves, thin leaves, climbing stems, pale fungal shelves, long ropes of root. The old boiler stands in the middle, split open from top to bottom. Its interior has become a vertical pool of darkness. Warm vapor pours from the rupture in slow folds, and the nearest plants lean toward it as though it were daylight.

Every so often the boiler makes a sound from deep within itself: not mechanical exactly, not natural either. A low, resonant note, like air moving through a vast bottle. In response, condensation trembles on the leaves. A few blossoms open. The gauges in the outer corridors twitch. Somewhere beyond the basin, a pump answers with one delayed metallic knock.

Then the place settles again into its long maintenance of itself: heat retained, water gathered, iron dissolved, roots advancing by fractions of an inch. It is not abandoned in the simple sense. It has merely outlived its operators and continued following the rules they left behind, while quietly making new rules in the dark.
