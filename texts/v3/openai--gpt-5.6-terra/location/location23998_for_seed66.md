The city occupies a high basin where a mountain range has worn itself down into shelves, gullies, and broad mineral terraces. It is not a green place. Nothing roots here. There are no trees along the roads, no grasses in cracks, no soft dark borders around the water. The ground is pale shale, brick-red siltstone, salt-streaked concrete, black retaining walls wet with condensation. On warm mornings the whole basin appears to sweat.

Water is never far away, though it is rarely seen in a clean, open body. It gathers in gutters cut centuries ago through stone; it lies beneath iron grates; it threads through culverts, locomotive yards, market undercrofts, and the low foundations of apartment blocks. Rain comes often, not dramatically, but in long gray installments. Clouds snag on the upper ridges and unravel themselves over the city. By noon, roofs shine. Walls carry black watermarks. The narrow ravines between districts breathe mist upward.

The place is vast enough that it has weather districts. The eastern platforms can be dry and sunstruck while the old central wards are still dripping under a ceiling of low cloud. On the western escarpment, where the factories stand, rain mixes with coal dust and mineral vapor and falls in a thin brown sheen. The high air is cool at night, but the city keeps itself warm: steam mains below the avenues, furnace halls along the rail lines, bakeries and tanneries and metalworks, thousands of kitchens venting into enclosed courtyards. The result is a warm, damp atmosphere with a metallic aftertaste.

Everywhere, there are animals.

They are the city’s true crowd. Flocks of broad-winged cliff pigeons fill old cisterns and occupy bridge ribs, ledges, chimneys, abandoned signal towers. Their wings make a continuous soft thunder in the rain. White-backed goats, descended from quarry stock, move through the upper streets in great patient bands, nosing at mineral runoff and licking salt from masonry. They sleep beneath loading cranes and along the sheltered faces of warehouses. Their hooves have polished the centuries-old stairways to a dull gray shine.

There are dogs in every neighborhood: heavy-coated freight-yard dogs, narrow drainage dogs, pale courtyard dogs that know the schedules of every butcher and noodle stall. Cats gather by the steam vents, warm as coals and nearly as numerous. In the undercity, where water runs through brick channels beneath the old capital, vast colonies of bats live in the vaulted drainage galleries. At dusk they emerge in sheets, not eerie so much as practical, sweeping the streets clear of midges and flies.

The largest creatures belong to the perimeter marshes, where the basin floor has never entirely been tamed. These are not leafy marshes. They are expanses of wet clay, shallow standing water, reedless sediment, old pilings, and rusted flood machinery. Herds of long-legged water buffalo move there, black backs rising through mist. Their handlers live in low concrete stations on the drier embankments, connected by narrow-gauge freight lines. The buffalo haul barges through the shallow canals and pull stone carts where trucks sink. They are slow, enormous, and familiar. Children paint their horns during holidays. Factory whistles pause when a herd crosses the main industrial road.

The absence of plant life gives every animal unusual prominence. A bird on a cable is a shape against open sky. A goat standing beside a door has nowhere to disappear. The city’s ecology has had to inhabit built surfaces: roof gutters, exhaust vents, culvert mouths, warm transformer rooms, abandoned mines, the dark spaces beneath raised roads. Food arrives by rail from lower valleys and coastal plains—grain, vegetables, fruit, hay, fish packed in ice—and is portioned, traded, stolen, fermented, dried, cooked, and carried everywhere. Animal feed is a major civic concern. So is manure. So is the recovery of bone, hide, grease, feathers, wool, and dung fuel. The city wastes very little, partly from thrift and partly because there is nowhere for waste to go except down the basin, into someone else’s water.

Its architecture has grown by accretion. The oldest structures are cut directly into the weathered slopes: warehouses with stone fronts and rooms extending far back into the mountain, public baths built around natural warm seepage, administrative halls whose walls are the exposed faces of ancient quarry pits. Later generations laid brick over the stone, then reinforced concrete over the brick, then steel walkways and pipe racks across everything. Whole neighborhoods hang on retaining walls in stacked rows. Rainwater runs from one roof into another’s collection channel, then through a filter box, then into a shared courtyard tank.

Railways are the city’s skeleton. They enter through mountain cuts and tunnel mouths, descend in long spirals, cross the basin on elevated viaducts, vanish under markets, and emerge again beside mills. Freight moves slowly: tank cars of fuel, wagons of feed, ore from the high mines, crates of machinery, trains carrying people from district to district. The gradients are difficult, so cable engines assist the climbs. Their thick black cables hum over the city, and at intervals a carriage rises through rain and steam, windows glowing faintly.

Industry is old enough to have settled into the terrain. Nothing appears newly imposed. The smelters have their own drainage channels and their own weathered hills of slag. The power stations are built into cliffside caverns, using falling water from reservoirs higher in the range. The chemical works occupy a former lakebed, ringed with flood walls, their pipes painted in layers of faded warning colors. Repairs are constant but unhurried. Workers replace a valve; rust returns. A wall is repointed; rain darkens it again. The city does not transform quickly. It metabolizes.

Sound accumulates rather than erupts. There is the low persistent movement of water under grates. The rattle of trains crossing steel spans. Hooves on paving stones. Goat bells, truck gears, market calls. Fans turn inside boiler buildings. Steam periodically releases with a long exhausted sigh. In the wet districts, pigeons murmur from every cornice. In the high districts, wind runs through overhead cables and makes them sing in different notes according to their tension and load.

Yet there are long intervals when the city seems almost still. Rain hangs in the air. Smoke rises straight from the chimneys. Buffalo stand knee-deep in clay without moving. A tram pauses at a switch while hundreds of birds wheel around the clock tower. Water gathers at the edge of a stone gutter, swells, and drops.

The mountains enclosing the basin are visibly ancient. Their faces are rounded where they have endured exposure, sharpened only where quarrying or collapse has cut into them. Bands of different rock show through: gray limestone, dark volcanic layers, copper-stained seams, pale rock flaking into dust. Every slope bears the evidence of long accommodation—terraces, avalanche barriers, drainage steps, abandoned rail spurs, patched roads tracing routes that older roads had already chosen.

Beyond the city, the land continues in inhabited fragments: pumping stations, buffalo yards, weather observatories, worker colonies near mines, reservoir villages perched above the flood lines. At night, these places are marked by small fields of yellow electric light. The basin itself glows faintly beneath cloud, a low industrial constellation in the mountains.

It is a place built without the expectation of beauty, and made beautiful anyway by use: wet stone under ordinary daylight, animals crowding warm doorways, cables disappearing into mist, the patience of infrastructure, the enormous old basin holding rain, smoke, industry, and life in its worn hands.
