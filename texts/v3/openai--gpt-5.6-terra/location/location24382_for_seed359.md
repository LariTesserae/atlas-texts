The place is called a garden only because there is no better word for a place where so many green things have been persuaded to live without soil, rain, or sky.

It occupies a shallow basin no larger than a village square, though the boundaries cannot be approached directly. From above—if above exists here—it might resemble a thumbprint pressed into a warm sheet of fired clay. Its enclosing walls are built of stacked pale blocks, each block roughly hand-sized, fitted without mortar in careful, interlocking spirals. The walls are not old enough to have softened. Their edges remain freshly cut, their corners sharp. Pale dust gathers in their seams, but no crack has yet had time to become a ruin.

Everything is made.

The ground is a dense pavement of little plates: ceramic scales, beaten copper leaves, bits of black glass, charred bone, compacted ash, woven reed mats hardened with resin. It rises in low terraces and bowls, never quite forming a natural slope. Footsteps make no single sound. One step may click on shell, whisper across dried fiber, knock against hollow clay, and settle into a softness of braided moss that is not moss at all, but a living mat fed by warmth stored in the construction beneath it.

There is no water anywhere. No wetness, no damp smell, no hidden seep in the deepest shade. The air is dry enough to pull the breath cleanly from the mouth. Yet vegetation crowds every available ledge and join. It grows from sockets built into the walls, from urns set at waist height, from narrow slits between floor plates. The plants have thick, folded leaves like green leather; silver-haired stems; round pads coated in wax; clusters of translucent bladders that hold not water but a slow, pale vapor. Some drink heat. Some feed on mineral dust. Some spread their roots into copper channels and draw a faint current from the warm understructure.

Their lushness is not soft. It is packed, deliberate, almost architectural. Every plant seems to have been trained by hands: tied to cane frames, looped around stone rings, leaned into lattices, grafted through holes in fired-clay screens. Vines cross the basin overhead in a low canopy, but their leaves do not make a roof. They make a patterned hesitation in the light. Normal daylight enters from somewhere beyond the walls, warm and cream-colored, and falls in moving squares through the vinework. The sun itself is rarely visible. Looking toward it produces the impression that it is on the other side of a ceiling, shining through many layers of thin material.

Among the plants live animals in great number.

They are small enough that the garden remains intimate, but there are always several within sight: velvet-backed lizards slipping beneath trays, pale insects assembling themselves along the edges of leaves, long-legged birds with clay-colored beaks walking solemnly through the terraces. Their nests are built into the construction—not found, but made from it. A bird may line a socket in the wall with fiber stripped from old rope. A beetle may occupy the hollow of a carved wooden handle. Thin mammals, warm as hearthstones, sleep in woven baskets hung under the vine canopy and emerge only when the light has become less direct.

Many creatures have the patient quality of objects. They sit for so long that it is easy to mistake them for ornaments, knots, discarded gloves. Then an eye opens. A tail shifts. A cluster of insects detaches from a wall and walks away in the shape of a hand.

The basin is mostly quiet.

There is no running water, no wind strong enough to worry the leaves, no machinery. Sound arrives as isolated details: the dry crack of a seedpod opening; the scrape of claws on tile; a bird’s single low note, repeated only after long thought; the tiny collapse of dust from one seam in the wall to another. In the middle of the garden stands a structure like a well, though it has never held water. It is made of dark wood, rope, and polished stone, with a crank worn smooth by generations of palms. Sometimes, without anyone touching it, the rope turns one finger-width around the spindle. Then all the animals fall still.

The inhabitants say the garden is growing downward.

This is why it was built in layers. Beneath the visible paving lie chambers of kiln brick and packed salt, then beds of charcoal, then galleries lined with old tools: stone blades, copper hooks, pestles, awls, digging sticks, cups of fired mud. Below those are roots—not plant roots, but the roots of the place itself: braided cables of hair, reed, wire, tendon, and green stem descending through the foundation. No one digs to find where they go. The garden’s rules are clear in the way rules are clear around a fire or an edge: things may be added, mended, pruned, fed, tied, carved, and carried. Nothing may be removed from below the first layer of paving.

Occasionally a new object appears at ground level.

A small door in a terrace. A stone bowl still warm from firing. A miniature ladder leading nowhere until, weeks later, a niche opens above it. Once, a family of white lizards was found sleeping inside a copper helmet that had not been there the previous evening. The helmet bore marks of use: finger grease, scratches, a dent at the crown. It was warm, and its inside smelled faintly of smoke and crushed leaves. No one claimed it. It remains there now, half-hidden in the foliage, and the lizards have made it their house.

The world beyond the basin is not absent, exactly. It presses close behind the walls. In places the pale blocks show scenes on their flat faces: a line of trees advancing through a desert; the interior of a sleeping animal; an ocean made of white powder; a procession of people carrying lamps under a black noon. These scenes are not carvings. They are shallow openings, no thicker than a fingernail, and if watched for too long they continue to change.

At sunset, the garden does not become dark in the usual fashion. The daylight weakens, but the leaves begin to reveal dull internal colors: amber veins, blue fuzz, red margins. The mineral plates in the ground hold their heat. Animals emerge from every constructed hollow. The basin feels fuller at dusk, as though its density increases when no one is trying to count what is inside it.

Then the walls begin their slow work.

A block may shift by the width of a grain of sand. A vine support leans into a new angle. A clay socket opens a little, accepting the root that has been waiting against it for months. Nothing is sudden. Nothing announces itself. But by morning, a passage that led between two beds may have narrowed; a perch may have become a stair; a plant may be flowering from an impossible place in the underside of a stone.

The garden is young, though it contains tools older than its builders and animals that seem to remember forms they have never possessed. It is still deciding what kind of world it is. For now, it is warm, dry, crowded, quiet, and intensely tended: a small constructed wilderness without water, endlessly making room for more life.
