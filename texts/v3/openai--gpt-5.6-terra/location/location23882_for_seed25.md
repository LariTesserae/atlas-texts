The land lies below the horizon rather than upon it.

It is a low country of basins, long shallow troughs, and flattened ridges whose surfaces have been worn down not by wind alone but by continual passage. The ground is dark mineral: salt-black mudstone, slick slabs of shale, porous shelves of old limestone. In places it breaks underfoot into plates as thin as fingernails; elsewhere it rises in polished folds, broad enough to resemble the backs of enormous buried animals. Nothing grows from it. There are no grasses threading the cracks, no shrubs taking hold in sheltered hollows, no trees against the sky. The stone is too frequently disturbed, too wet in the wrong seasons and too dry in the others, too thoroughly inhabited.

The darkness is not absolute. A weak grey luminance gathers high in the clouds, usually hidden by them, and on rare occasions a white, cold moon appears as a blurred stain. Most light comes nearer the ground: from the pale undersides of moving bodies, from mucus shining on rock, from colonies of bead-sized organisms embedded in the damp walls of ravines. These do not illuminate so much as reveal motion. A passing herd will flicker into existence in segments—a thousand wet flanks, jointed legs, blind heads turned all in one direction—and then close again into the dark.

Water is present everywhere without ever becoming a river.

It condenses along the undersides of overhangs and drips in slow, regular lines. It collects in basins among the stone, in ankle-deep sheets and black pools with hard mineral rims. Rain comes intermittently, fine and cold, falling for hours without rising to a storm. The terrain drinks none of it. It runs along old channels polished smooth by generations of feet, mouths, tails, shells, and bellies. At the lowest points, where the water stands, the fauna gathers so densely that the pools appear to boil. Their surfaces pulse with drinking, spawning, feeding, drowning, and the simple physical pressure of too many lives trying to occupy the same wet darkness.

The sound begins before the shape of the place becomes clear.

There is the continuous churning scrape of innumerable limbs over stone. There are clicks like hard rain on bone, the clatter of shells striking one another, the thick suction of soft-bodied things hauling themselves through mud. Beneath these is a low communal note: not a call exactly, but a vibration produced by whole masses of creatures rubbing, breathing, drumming their bodies against the ground. It passes through the soles of the feet. At intervals it swells into something deafening.

Then the calls begin.

Some are high, metallic shrieks that travel through the gullies in swarms. Some are deep enough to be felt more than heard, booming from unseen throats beneath limestone shelves. There are choruses of dry rattles, wet bellows, snapping jaws, whistles blown through horned nasal chambers, and abrupt silences that are worse than the noise because they mean something large is moving nearby. Nothing sings pleasantly. Every sound is territorial, reproductive, alarmed, hungry, or merely compulsory: the noise of bodies needing to be recognized in a world where sight is unreliable.

The animals are the country’s vegetation.

They cover surfaces, occupy layers, create shelter and weather for one another. Low grazers—or what resemble grazers—move across the stone in mats, rasping at films of mineral-feeding microbes invisible in the darkness. They have broad grinding mouths and pale armored backs, and travel in such tight ranks that individual bodies cannot be separated from the flow. Smaller creatures live among their plates, feeding on shed skin, parasites, spoor, and the warm condensed moisture trapped between them.

There are burrowers beneath the flats, though their tunnels are never stable. The walls slough inward; larger animals crush them; rain fills them. Still, the ground is threaded with temporary passages and breathing holes. From these come pale blind things with too many limbs, emerging at the sound of distant impacts. They feed on carrion, on eggs, on the mineral crust accumulating around old bones. Some are delicate, translucent, and nearly silent. Others are dense as rocks and move with a grinding force capable of shifting loose slabs aside.

Along the ridges travel the hunters. They are not elegant. They have evolved for contact, not pursuit: blunt sensory crowns, wide bodies, hooked forelimbs, rows of teeth angled inward like fish traps. In the dark, they find prey by pressure waves through the earth. A herd may be moving quietly for minutes, then every creature in it turns at once, a single pale ripple passing through hundreds of backs. The herd compresses. The outer animals lock their plates. The smaller bodies vanish beneath them. Then, from somewhere beyond the last visible shimmer, comes a blow like a fallen boulder.

Afterward, the feeding lasts a long time.

There is no empty distance here, even where the terrain opens. A plain may extend for miles, low and treeless under its lid of cloud, but it is full: ridged with migratory lanes, speckled with resting colonies, crossed by lines of crawling forms. The horizon itself seems to move because it is made of motion. Great seasonal gatherings travel between the condensation fields and the deeper pools. Their paths have cut the land into a readable geography: troughs of compacted powder, polished corridors, banks of accumulated shell and bone. Some routes are older than any living animal. The stone has been lowered by them, made smoother, darker, and more densely grooved.

Death does not leave much behind. Soft remains are consumed almost immediately. Hard remains become habitat. Ribs form arches under which clutching things breed. Skulls collect rain. Shells stack in drifts, then fuse with mineral deposits into low white reefs. In the oldest hollows, the ground has a layered composition of stone, salt, shell, compacted droppings, and the fine dark dust of things reduced by time and traffic.

The rules of this place are simple and merciless.

Anything still enough becomes a surface for something else to inhabit.  
Anything warm becomes briefly valuable.  
Anything loud may be calling a mate, marking a path, or announcing food; the distinction is often discovered too late.  
Water belongs to whoever can hold its edge.  
Darkness is not concealment but crowding: every unlit space already contains a body.

And yet there are quiet moments, though never empty ones.

In the hour after rain, condensation thickens on every mineral face. The largest calls subside. The moving masses loosen and spread across the flats, drinking from thin reflective seams in the rock. The clouds sag low enough that their underside seems to touch the ridges. A cold mist slides through the gullies, carrying the smells of wet shell, iron-rich mud, ammonia, opened flesh, and the faint stony sweetness of freshly exposed ground.

In that muted interval, the country seems almost ancient and patient. Its vast animal population does not make it feel young or chaotic, but established—an old ecology with no green foundation, sustained by damp stone, microbial films, mineral seepage, and the endless conversion of one creature into another creature’s shelter, food, signal, or path.

Then, far away, something begins to knock its body against the earth.

The sound passes from basin to basin. Hundreds answer. Thousands. The ground trembles under the accumulated declaration of life, and the dark low world becomes deafening again.
