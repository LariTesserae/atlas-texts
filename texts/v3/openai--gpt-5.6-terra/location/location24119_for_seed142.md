The city occupies a high basin on a world that has forgotten weather.

It is not a basin in the gentle terrestrial sense. No river ever cut it, no green slopes lean down toward it. It is a vast depression in old black stone, ringed by escarpments so worn that they seem less like cliffs than the remaining edges of some colossal, removed thing. The rock has been exposed for ages beyond counting. Its surfaces are softened not by rain but by minute expansions, mineral shedding, the patient settling of crystals under warm darkness. Every ridge has the blunt, exhausted shape of permanence.

Above the city there is no visible sky.

There is space, certainly. A vaulted blackness rises beyond the chimneys and transmission towers, but it gives no stars, no moon, no reflected glow. The basin’s walls disappear into it. The darkness is not foggy; it is perfectly clean. Things are visible only where the city permits them to be visible.

Long sodium-colored lamps burn along the roadways, one every hundred or two hundred paces. Their housings are mounted on steel poles that have been painted so often they have become thick and rounded, their old layers cracked into scales. The light does not reach far. It gathers close to the ground, catches the rims of gravel, the squared edges of foundations, the pale scars of old tire tracks, then gives up. Between lamps lie intervals of absolute obscurity.

The city is sparse in the way an industrial installation is sparse: not empty, but purposefully separated.

There are blocky residences clustered around heat conduits; narrow rows of identical doors built into the lower slopes; repair sheds with corrugated roofs; processing towers spaced far apart across the flats; substations fenced with heavy mesh; rail lines crossing the basin in straight, improbable diagonals. The roads are broad and overbuilt, their pavement made from dark vitrified aggregate. Most of them lead to places that do not look inhabited—pump houses, sealed silos, loading platforms, exhausted quarries, compressor stations standing alone in a field of mineral dust.

Nothing rusts quickly here. There is too little water in the air, too little chemical invitation. Metal ages by dulling. Bolts lose their sharpness. Rails become dark and satin-smooth. Painted signs bleach not under sunlight but under years of lamp heat and airborne grit. Letters remain readable long after they have ceased to matter:

**SECTOR 9 TRANSFER**  
**FURNACE ACCESS**  
**DO NOT OPEN DURING PRESSURE BALANCE**  
**PUBLIC CONDUIT / KEEP CLEAR**

The final sign is repeated at the margins of the city, where enormous pipes emerge from the ground and travel away into the black.

Whatever moves through them is not water.

The pipes are warm to the touch. They tick once every few minutes as they adjust to an unchanging pressure. Their insulation is wrapped in pale ceramic cloth, repaired over centuries with plates, wires, tar-like black sealant, and strips of metal cut from obsolete machinery. At their joints, a faint mineral vapor sometimes escapes and hangs near the ground, visible in lamplight as a silver distortion. It has no smell until one stands close, and then it smells of hot stone struck with iron.

The basin itself is dry enough that even dust seems reluctant to lift. The ground is covered in a granular crust of ash-gray minerals, compacted into shallow plates. Under the lamps it appears like snow, but it is warm. Footsteps leave precise marks that remain for years unless crossed by a vehicle or obscured by the slow fall of powder from the factories.

There are no plants. Not even the tough, low forms that usually occupy cracks and bad soil. Crevices in concrete remain bare. Drainage ditches contain no moss, no insects, no black film of microbial life. A discarded glove can lie against a wall until its fabric becomes mineral-stiff, but nothing eats it. A wooden crate survives not by rotting but by drying into a gray, brittle geometry.

The city has learned to make peace with this absence. It does not beautify itself with gardens. Instead, its communal squares contain arrangements of stone: circles of polished basalt, vertical slabs of pale salt-rock, heaps of colored slag set in carefully chosen gradients. In front of the administrative hall stands a dry fountain whose basin is filled with black glass beads. Once a day, perhaps, hidden machinery turns them over. The sound is like distant surf, though no resident has ever heard surf.

The warmth comes from below.

Deep beneath the ancient basin, old industrial works tap a layer of geothermal pressure or something more peculiar: a mineral engine in the planet’s crust, a slow reaction that has been stable for longer than the city. Heat rises through wells and shafts, feeding turbines, furnaces, residential ducts, and the broad covered walkways that connect the inhabited districts. The city is warm even under the black vault. Windows glow faintly amber. Vents breathe dry air across the streets.

But the heat is disciplined. It does not vary with day or season because there is no day and no season. It is maintained at the same level with religious precision: warm enough for bare hands, warm enough for machinery, never warm enough to invite softness or growth.

The factories do not roar.

Their age has made them quiet. Great wheels turn behind concrete walls with a low, almost subsonic thrum. Conveyors move beneath covers. Lifts rise in shafts with muted chains. Every so often a pressure valve releases a brief sigh, and the sound travels astonishingly far across the open flats before being absorbed by the darkness.

There are people here, though one may walk for an hour and see none.

They live according to shifts that no longer correspond to any celestial cycle. A worker may appear at the end of a lamp-lit road: hooded coat, pale dust on their boots, face briefly illuminated, then gone again behind an equipment wall. Their footsteps are startlingly loud. Conversation is kept low, partly from custom and partly because every sound feels recorded by the landscape.

The inhabitants have a habit of pausing at boundaries: doorways, rail crossings, the edge of light. They look outward into the basin as if expecting something to arrive.

Rare creatures do.

They are not animals anyone has fully classified. Small, pale shapes are sometimes found in the warm cavities beneath the power stations: six-limbed mineral feeders with transparent hides and hard black mouths, living off trace chemical deposits around the vents. They move very slowly. Their bodies are cool despite the heat around them. No one knows how they reproduce. No one has ever seen two together.

Farther out, on the upper slopes, there are larger things: long-legged silhouettes that cross between the lamp fields once in several years. They make no sound and leave tracks only where the crust is especially fine. Their joints reflect light with a dull, nacreous gleam. The city does not hunt them. The official rule is old and unchallenged:

**If one is seen beyond the outer conduit, all transit pauses until it has passed.**

No one remembers why.

Beyond the built districts, the basin extends into a region of old works and older geology. There are abandoned extraction pits, their terraced walls worn down into smooth amphitheaters. There are ranks of pylons with no wires between them. There are sealed tunnels marked with obsolete corporate symbols, their entrances half-buried in dry scree. A railway line runs toward the northern escarpment and enters a tunnel whose mouth is perfectly round, too regular to have been cut by ordinary machines. No trains use it. Yet the rails leading into it remain polished.

The city’s darkness is not simply the lack of light. It has weight in the mind. It makes each lamp seem deliberate, each lit window a statement of continued occupation. People do not waste illumination. They carry hooded lamps rather than bright ones. Building interiors are dim, lit from below by heat channels or by narrow strips of industrial phosphor. Faces are known by outline, gait, voice, and the small familiar sounds of tools.

The oldest residents say the basin has always been like this: high, warm, dry, dark, and still. They say the city was not founded so much as uncovered. The first builders found foundations already set into the stone, pipes already descending into the earth, stations waiting for engines. They added to it carefully, following plans whose originals were never found.

Whether this is true is difficult to establish. Records endure, but they contradict one another. Dates are meaningless in a place without dawn. The same maintenance work may be listed as having occurred forty years ago, four hundred years ago, or “before the first settling.”

And still the turbines turn.

Still the conduits pulse faintly beneath the streets.

Still the black slopes hold their ancient shape around the lit structures.

Nothing in the basin is truly dead, but neither does anything seem eager to become alive. The city continues in an equilibrium so old that its inhabitants mistake it for safety. Its machines are repaired, its roads swept, its lamps replaced one by one. Its people sleep in warmed rooms and wake to the same darkness. Far out beyond the final streetlight, the dry mineral plain waits without changing, and the immense unseen sky gives nothing back.
