At the very top of a young mountain there is a basin no larger than a town square, though it feels enclosed on all sides by distances that cannot be crossed. Its walls are black, newly broken stone: basalt ribs, fractured only recently from the larger ridge. Their edges are still too sharp to have names. Nothing has had time to soften them. In places the rock has split into narrow, vertical slots, and from those slots comes a constant vapor, pale in the dark.

The place is almost entirely without sky.

A low ceiling of cloud presses into the basin and stays there, caught among the high walls. At night—perhaps it is always night here—the cloud absorbs every scrap of light. One sees only by nearness: the polished wet shoulder of a stone, a run of pale lichen, the occasional sheen of water moving over a root. There are no stars. There is no distant glow from villages below. The darkness is thick enough that the greenery seems to be recognized first by smell: crushed fern, cold moss, leaf mold, the iron scent of rain on fresh rock.

Vegetation has filled every pause left by the mountain’s violence.

Small trees lean together in the shallow soil, their trunks narrow and crowded, their roots exposed where the earth has not yet settled. They have the constrained, urgent look of things growing in a place they may lose at any season. Ferns spill from cracks in dense shelves. Moss lies over boulders in a deep green pelt, and water-loving weeds make curtains over the mouths of fissures. Broad leaves gather condensation from the cloud ceiling, then release it one drop at a time. Their dripping is nearly continuous, but never gathers into a stream for long. Water appears in pockets: a clear trembling pool in a stone cup, a dark runnel under roots, a wet black plane on a ledge. Then it vanishes down into the mountain.

The basin is damp rather than wet. Rain comes in hard, brief passages, slanting through the trees and stopping abruptly. Afterward, vapor rises from the stone as though the earth itself were breathing out.

The loudness comes from below.

Under the vegetation and the thin soil is a system of pressures: hot mineral water descending, cooling, contracting; cold water entering fractures; trapped air forced through constrictions. The mountain has made, without intention, a kind of engine room. Every cleft has its own voice. One emits a steady deep roar, too low to locate. Another whistles with a piercing regularity. A third gives off sudden metallic knocks as a slab shifts against its neighbor. Water runs through hidden stone channels in hard, rhythmic bursts. The sounds overlap until they resemble machinery at work behind a wall: pumps taking suction, pistons cycling, steam escaping, belts turning under load.

But there is no machinery.

No pipe, no bolt, no cut surface, no human footprint. The industrial character belongs to the mountain alone—to its repeated operations, its pressure and release, its ceaseless sorting of water, heat, gravel, and air. The forest has adapted to the racket. Leaves quiver without wind when a subterranean pulse passes. Fine roots seem to grip tighter around the warm cracks. In some places the moss is stained orange and white by mineral breath.

Nothing living moves through the undergrowth except the plants themselves.

There are no birds in the cloud. No insects tick in the wet bark. No mammals have found the narrow approaches, or perhaps the noise and sudden ground-shudders have taught them to avoid it. This absence makes the place feel even more densely occupied. The ferns, roots, vapor, stone, and water take up all available attention. A person standing there would not feel alone so much as outnumbered by processes.

The ground is unreliable. A shelf that was solid last month may have collapsed into a newly opened throat. A pool may drain overnight, leaving a polished bowl full of white mineral crust. A fissure may begin steaming where yesterday there was only moss. The basin is young enough that it is still deciding what shape it wants to be. Its changes are not grand from a distance—the mountain remains a mountain—but within this small enclosed space, every day rearranges routes, sounds, and borders.

There are rules, though none are posted.

Do not trust dry stone: it may only be drying on the surface.

Do not follow the loudest noise: loudness often means depth, and depth here is hidden.

Where the moss is brightest, the ground may be warm.

Where no plant grows, something is still happening.

And if the vapor abruptly stops, wait. The mountain is usually only gathering itself for the next release.
