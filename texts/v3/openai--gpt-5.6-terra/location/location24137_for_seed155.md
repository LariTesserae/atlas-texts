The city has grown into the mountains rather than being built upon them.

At first glance it appears abandoned, because there is so little stone exposed and so few straight manufactured surfaces left visible. The high valley is broad and old, worn down to a patient shape by ice that has long since retreated. Its ridges are not sharp; they are the shoulders of an ancient world, green-black under a cool, rinsed sky. The slopes descend in long shelves and terraces toward a basin where the settlement lies. No forest surrounds it. There are no separate trees in the ordinary sense. Vegetation is the ground, the walls, the roofing, the pipes, the retaining works, the streets.

Everything is plant.

The roads are pale bands of compressed root-fiber, ribbed with harder woody seams. They run in broad, nearly empty avenues between structures that have been trained, grafted, and repeatedly cut back for centuries. Some rise as cylindrical towers of layered bark, each floor marked by a dark horizontal mouth of window-leaves. Others are low factories grown around old steel frameworks: vast vegetative sheds with roofs of overlapping waxy fronds, their walls swollen around girder bones. In a few places the original industrial masonry remains, but only as a dark mineral core beneath a living overcoat. Brick chimneys have become trunks. Rail embankments have become cordons of rootwood. Former cranes stand in the yards as skeletal trellises, all their cables replaced by tendrils that tighten and loosen in the wind.

The city is sparse in the way a mountain town is sparse. It occupies much ground but leaves much ground unoccupied. There are plazas of mossy turf, service yards full of wet gravel and giant leaf-litter, vacant switchbacks climbing the hillsides. Between districts, the land resumes itself: a field of waist-high blue-green reeds, a slope of lichenous shrubs, an open basin carpeted in dark low vegetation that ripples like fur when weather crosses it. The buildings do not make a hard edge against this growth. They thin into it. A warehouse becomes a hedge, a hedge becomes a slope, a slope folds into the valley.

Moisture is present without ever becoming abundance. It hangs in the place.

Rain arrives in narrow gray curtains, often only on one side of the valley. It gathers at the undersides of leaves, in cable-vines, in the undersized gutters that still run along certain old service buildings. By afternoon, long clear drops fall from roof fronds one at a time. Water travels through the city in hidden channels: hollow roots under streets, vertical cambium pipes inside tower walls, furrows worn into the planted pavement. The few open pools are black and shallow, held in old loading basins and the pits of collapsed foundations. They steam faintly in cold sunlight, not from heat but from some slow metabolic exchange beneath their surfaces.

The air smells of wet wood, iron, bruised stems, mineral dust, and the clean sourness of high elevation.

Sound makes the place feel populated even when it is not.

There is the constant deep operation of the industrial vegetation. Pumps throb inside root-cellars. Sap presses through trunk-sized conduits with a low, irregular knocking. Vent towers exhale through reeds of metal and hollow cane, making foghorn notes that wander in pitch. Along the old rail corridor, a chain of electric traction motors still works under moss and leaf-wrap, hauling sealed freight pods from one district to another. The pods move slowly, almost ceremonially, their wheels shrieking on damp rails. They pass through tunnels whose entrances have been narrowed by growth, then emerge coated in pollen and torn leaves.

At intervals, something like a factory siren sounds across the valley. It begins as a groan from within the western works, rises through several incompatible tones, and ends in a chorus of clicking shutters and snapping seedpods. No one seems to answer it. A minute later, vents open on the hills. White vapor pours out in disciplined lines. The living roofs turn their leaves over, exposing their pale undersides. Somewhere below, turbines begin to spin.

The industrial system has not been replaced by nature. It has been metabolized by it.

In the central district, old processing halls cultivate structural timber, resin, fibers, and chemical sap. Their interiors are warmish, damp, lit by ordinary lamps suspended beneath vaults of leaf and branch. Machines prune, scarify, graft, and harvest. Their articulated arms are rusty but precise. Conveyors carry bundles of bark, husks, pressed leaf-sheet, and translucent blocks of hardened resin. On the walls, pale fungi-like maintenance diagrams have grown around faded painted instructions. A sign might still read CAUTION: HOT SURFACE, though the surface beneath has become soft green cambium and cool to the touch.

The rules of the place are partly biological and partly infrastructural, and neither system fully understands the other.

Doors open only when the wall recognizes pressure, warmth, or a familiar electrical rhythm. Staircases may lengthen overnight as their root supports seek damp ground. A district that was accessible yesterday may be closed by a curtain of woven stems, not as a defense but because the city has decided to produce a new membrane there. Streetlights flower at dusk: each one unfolds a cluster of white, lantern-shaped bracts around an old filament bulb. In the morning the bracts curl shut and drip.

Maps are unreliable. Not because the city moves quickly, but because it changes with the quiet persistence of growth. A canal may become a root trench. A station platform may lift several inches over a season. Entire building facades thicken, buckle, and acquire new entrances. The older neighborhoods, particularly those higher on the northern slope, have become so internally overgrown that their original geometry is only guessed at through the ridges of roofs and the square shadows of buried windows.

Reality becomes uncertain in smaller ways.

At the basin’s center stands a water tower wrapped in red vine. Its reflection appears in pools that are nowhere near it. Certain alleys seem much longer in mist than they are in sun. The valley’s echo sometimes returns before a sound is made: a freight horn comes back from the eastern cliffs, then the actual horn sounds from behind you. Rain falls vertically except around the high chimneys, where droplets pause in midair, gather into beads, and crawl upward along the soot-darkened brick.

No one calls these miracles. The city has had too long to get used to itself.

Animal life is scarce. There are insects in the flowering ducts and beetles beneath the warm machinery, but even they are heard more often than seen. A few pale mountain birds nest in the upper ventilation stacks. Their calls are thin and metallic, almost lost under the plantworks. Occasionally, in the open reed fields outside the settlement, something large moves with deliberate silence beneath the vegetation, making a lane of bent stalks. By the time one reaches it, there is only dampness, a disturbed film of pollen, and perhaps one clean hoofprint pressed into the living ground.

The people, if they are there, live mostly inside the grown structures. Their presence is suggested by illuminated windows, by the clatter of tools in green-walled workshops, by laundry hung in the protected courtyards where vines are trained into latticework. At shift changes, figures appear on the broad root roads in insulated coats, carrying lamps or baskets of cut material. They move between the towers and factories with the resigned familiarity of people who know which walls will breathe steam, which bridges will flex, which sections of pavement may decide to bloom.

Beyond the city, the high country continues in calm, eroded expanses. The mountains have survived every industry and will likely survive this one. Their slopes are covered not by wilderness in the old sense, but by the outer tissues of the same vast botanical system: sparse groves of pillar-plants, long cables of vine crossing ravines, wind-combed fields of dark leaves. In the distance, the old ridge lines dissolve into cloud.

At night, the settlement gives off a muted green-gray glow. Its factories pulse amber from within. Condensation shines on every surface. The loudness softens but does not cease: pumps, rails, distant vents, the settling crack of enormous roots beneath old streets.

It is a city still working after the end of the distinction between city and forest.
