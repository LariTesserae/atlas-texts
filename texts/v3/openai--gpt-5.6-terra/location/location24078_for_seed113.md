The city is built in a cavity that has not finished becoming a cave.

It lies only a little beneath the surface—close enough that rain remembers it, close enough that roots find cracks in the ceiling and descend in pale ropes—but the place is wholly underground. The roof is a young skin of fractured stone, thin in some places, fat with clay in others. It has been cut through by recent water, and water still holds the authority here. Every wall shines. Every seam drips. In the warm air, moisture hangs in a low silver mist and gathers on handrails, paving, cables, and the undersides of leaves.

The settlement occupies a single basin, perhaps no larger than a district of an old city, though it feels broader because it is mostly empty.

There are streets, certainly. Their surfaces are black mineral composite, laid in precise hexagonal bands that do not quite touch; between them, narrow channels receive the continual runoff. There are platforms suspended above shallow pools, retaining walls grown around exposed rock, stairways following the natural folds of the hollow. But there are no crowds. No vehicles. No windows lit by inhabitants. The buildings stand far apart in the basin, each one isolated amid wet courtyards and low gardens, their pale facades rising from the damp like tools left in a workshop after everyone has gone.

Some structures are only two or three storeys high. Others are narrow towers that reach almost to the cracked ceiling, not in defiance of it but in support: their upper portions spread into delicate branching braces, holding stone slabs in place without visibly touching them. The braces hum at a pitch below hearing. When the hum deepens, dust rises from the ceiling in soft brown veils, and a new fissure seals itself with a glaze of dark glass.

The city has not been constructed so much as persuaded to assemble.

Its materials are responsive. Walls slowly rearrange their pores to guide dampness away from rooms. Bridges thicken beneath imagined weight and thin again when left unused. A collapsed culvert may wake overnight and knit itself back together, weaving mineral fibers across its own break. Nothing is hurried. Nothing appears alive in the animal sense. Yet all of it is busy.

At the basin’s center lies the waterworks: a round, open reservoir sunk into the floor, perhaps twenty meters across. Its water is almost black, but it catches the dim light from above and scatters it in green reflections across the ceiling. From the reservoir rise a number of thin silver columns—not pipes, exactly, but coherent streams held upright by fields whose workings are invisible. They ascend, bend elegantly through the air, and vanish into intake mouths high along the cavern walls. At irregular intervals one column fails, falling at once into the pool with a crash that fills the entire hollow. Then another forms beside it.

This is the principal sound of the place: water striking water, amplified by stone.

There are others. Pumps pulse somewhere beneath the paving. Deep in the walls, valves exhale. An old transit tunnel at the basin’s eastern edge produces a regular metallic knocking, as though a train passes far away every few minutes, though no train ever emerges. The ceiling crackles when rain moves across the ground overhead. Sometimes a whole section of the city sings briefly: not music, but a rising chord of stressed materials correcting themselves.

The loudness makes the emptiness more pronounced. Every sound has room to travel.

Vegetation has entered through all the routes that water can take. Ferns crowd the bases of retaining walls. Broad-leaved plants stand in the channels between pavement segments, their leaves waxy, dark, and perpetually beaded with droplets. In places, root mats have spread across the ceiling, drinking seepage from above; their hanging tendrils are thick as ropes and carry small lantern-like growths, translucent pods full of faint green light. Moss grows in architectural patterns on the city’s surfaces, not because anyone planted it that way, but because the walls regulate warmth and mineral content with exceptional consistency.

There are gardens designed around no human scale. A courtyard may hold one immense leaf, raised on a stalk taller than a person, with nothing else around it but wet stone and a bench. Another contains a grove of thin white trunks emerging from ankle-deep water. Their crowns press against the ceiling and flatten there, making a woven canopy that trembles when the ventilation shifts.

No insects gather around the lantern-pods. No birds nest in the braces. Nothing stirs in the pools except water itself.

The absence of animals is so complete that even the plants seem to have adapted to it. Their flowers open without fragrance. Their fruits, where they appear, soften and dissolve on the branch, returning quietly to the damp soil. There is no evidence of teeth, tracks, nests, feathers, shells, or scavenging. The city’s organic life is all persistence: leaf, root, mold, algae, vine.

At the northern margin of the basin, a row of doors stands beneath a low overhang. They are very old by the standards of the city and very new by the standards of the rock around them. Each door has no hinge, no handle, no visible seam. They resemble slabs of cloudy amber set upright in the wall. Moisture runs down their faces and disappears before reaching the threshold.

Sometimes, a door becomes transparent.

Behind it is not a room but another condition of the city: a bright dry avenue, perhaps, or a sky-colored chamber full of white structural frames. It lasts for a few seconds. Then the amber clouds again. The city does not explain these apertures. It does not seem concerned whether anyone witnesses them.

The rules here are practical, though not familiar. Water is never allowed to stagnate for long; the streets alter their slope by fractions to prevent it. Nothing falls freely if it can be supported. Cracks are catalogued by the structures around them, and their growth is answered with tension, heat, or mineral deposition. Light is conserved. It comes from the occasional fissure overhead, from biological glow, from the green reflections of the reservoir, and from faint bands embedded in the paving that illuminate only when one is close enough to need them.

The warm air is heavy with stone smell: wet limestone, iron-rich clay, leaf rot, ozone from the field-generators. Breathing it feels almost like drinking.

Above, the world has rain and weather and perhaps people. Below, in this shallow wound beneath it, the city continues its maintenance without citizens. It gathers water. It braces the ceiling. It cultivates the roots that bind the soil. It listens to the ground settling around it.

It is not abandoned exactly.

It is simply operating at a scale of waiting that makes habitation seem temporary.
