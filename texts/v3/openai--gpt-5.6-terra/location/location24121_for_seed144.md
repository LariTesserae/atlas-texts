At this altitude the heat has nowhere to go.

It lies in the basin all day, flattened under a hard white sky, caught between escarpments of red and black stone. The valley is high enough that the horizon seems unnaturally near and brilliantly cut: ridges of oxidized shale, terraces of pale salt, old volcanic necks rising like blunt towers. Yet the sun is fierce. The air is thin but not clean. It carries alkali dust, animal musk, hot metal, and, in certain pockets, the green mineral smell of standing water.

Nothing in the valley is empty.

From a distance it appears barren: sparsely tufted with wire grass, thorn cushions, a few low trees bent into shapes of permanent suspicion. But closer in, every layer is occupied. Cracks in the rock hold dark beetles. Dry streambeds are scripted with hoofprints, pawprints, the drag marks of tails, the stitched trails of insects. Lichens laminate the shaded stone. Small birds crowd the cliff ledges, moving in sudden sheets from one fissure to the next. Herds of long-legged, dun-colored grazers travel the basin floor in loose files, raising only a small dust wake. They know the terrain better than roads do.

The valley’s water comes unpredictably.

For weeks the channels may be dry, their beds hard as fired brick. Then afternoon clouds gather over the far escarpment, bruised-purple and low, and rain falls in narrow curtains. It may never touch the settlement at all. It may strike one ridge so violently that, an hour later, brown water comes hissing through the gullies, carrying sticks, stones, dung, feathers, and scraps of old plastic. The floods do not last long. They have been doing this for centuries and have found their proper paths: shallow ravines, polished chutes, reed-choked sinks, a chain of mineral pools below the cliffs.

At dawn, condensation forms beneath overhangs and on the undersides of machinery. It gathers in beadlike rows along pipes and rails, drips into carefully placed troughs, and disappears before noon.

The industrial works are scattered rather than centralized, as though they grew up around necessities and never agreed to become a town. A pumping house stands beside one of the deeper pools, its corrugated roof bleached nearly silver. Farther away, on a shelf of black basalt, a processing shed hums behind a windbreak of stacked slag blocks. A cableway crosses the valley in three long spans, carrying ore buckets—or sometimes empty buckets, clicking patiently through the heat. There are solar panels on roofs, a weather mast, transmission poles marching a little way into the open country before stopping abruptly. A small rail spur enters from the west and ends at a loading platform covered in dust.

Most of the structures are too small to dominate the land. Their doors hang open. Their windows are shaded with mesh. Water tanks stand on stilts. Fuel drums have been painted white. Between buildings lie yards full of useful things: belts, pipes, cracked crates, coils of cable, a stripped engine block turned into a nesting place for birds. Industry has not displaced the animals; it has given them new ledges, shade, warm surfaces, and leaks to investigate.

Goats sleep beneath the conveyor. Pale foxes patrol the perimeter of the waste heaps. Large carrion birds use the cableway towers as lookout points, their wings opening and closing in the uplift. At night, when the works are quieter, thick-bodied moths batter against the few exterior lamps, and a striped scavenger comes down from the upper slopes to drink from a valve that never seals completely.

The dominant sound is not machinery, though machinery is present. It is silence interrupted by specific things: a loose sheet of metal ticking in the heat; the distant cough of a diesel engine starting reluctantly; a bucket striking another bucket on the overhead line; flies; wings; the brief frantic alarm of some animal that has seen a predator. Wind moves through the valley only in the late afternoon. Before that, sound travels strangely. A shouted instruction at the pump house can be heard half a kilometer away. So can the clatter of hooves on a dry riverbed.

The stone has settled into its own old arrangements. Cliffs have weathered into shelves and windows but do not seem actively crumbling. The flood channels are worn smooth, their edges softened by recurring water. Even the industrial scars have begun to acquire a local character: rust blooms the same orange as the hills; spilled ore becomes part of the ground; tire tracks fill with silt and then with beetles. There is no sense that the place is being conquered. It has accepted the machines provisionally, on the condition that they endure the heat, the dust, the sudden water, and the animals’ indifference.

In the middle of the basin is a shallow, salt-edged wetland, no larger than a field. Reed stems rise from it in sparse ranks. The surface is usually still, reflecting the enormous sky with a clarity that makes the clouds seem submerged. This is where the valley gathers itself.

Grazers arrive in the morning. Birds arrive all day. Workers come with tanks and filters. Predators wait on the margins. The water is not plentiful enough to make the place lush, but it is sufficient to make every life visible. Nothing wastes motion here. Nothing comes by accident, though everything seems temporary: the rain, the herds, the engine noise, the thin mirror of the pool.

By evening, the heat loosens. The high cliffs turn violet, then almost blue. Dust hangs low over the tracks. The cableway continues its slow journey across the darkening basin, each bucket a small black shape against the last light. Somewhere beyond the sheds, a generator runs for an hour, then stops.

Afterward the valley becomes quiet enough to hear water dripping from a pipe into a metal pan, one measured drop at a time.
