The town occupies a shallow basin of pale stone, barely above the level of a grey inland sea that cannot quite be seen. The horizon is blocked by a ring of low embankments, freight walls, and old retaining terraces, all softened not by moss or grass but by long exposure to rain, soot, salt, and the rubbing bodies of animals. Nothing grows here. No weed lifts through a crack. No lichen takes hold on brick. The gutters are clean of roots. Even the damp places have only mineral stains: greenish copper bloom, white salt feathers, black oil iridescence.

It is called a town because there are streets, addresses, public clocks, a station, workshops, and rows of narrow dwellings with pipes entering their walls. But it is not large. From the raised service bridge at its center, one can see almost all of it: the square reservoirs to the east, the chain of engine sheds to the south, the old market arcade, the pumping house, the animal gates, the blank apartment fronts, and the basin wall encircling everything like the rim of a drained vessel.

The streets are too wide for the buildings. Broad lanes of dark, damp paving run between sparse constructions: a boiler works here, an empty loading yard there, a row of six houses separated from the next row by a field of gravel. The spaces between are deliberate. They are not vacant exactly. They are given over.

The fauna uses them.

Every open stretch is occupied by creatures moving in orderly, tireless abundance. There are herds of low, many-legged animals whose shells look made of worn ceramic drainpipe. They emerge from culverts in broad, steaming files, their jointed undersides ticking against the stone. There are white-necked birds with no wings, tall as lamp posts, which stand in clusters along the reservoir edges and strike their beaks against the metal railings. There are slick black things, round as barrels, that roll in slow groups through the streets, stopping at certain iron grates to press their warm bodies over them.

Above the roofs, a permanent shifting canopy of smaller animals circles: not birds, not insects, but angular, membrane-finned creatures with reflective bellies. Their collective motion makes the sky seem to turn. They pass through the exhaust from the mills, through the rain, through the plumes of the cooling stacks, and never appear troubled by it.

The town has been built around their routes.

Doorways are raised a meter above the pavement. Pedestrian bridges connect second floors. Rail lines run in covered trenches, because the larger herds will not cross an exposed rail. Every district has a horn tower, and each tower gives a different warning: a three-note brass bellow for the procession of shell-backed crawlers, a rolling mechanical siren for the barrel-creatures, a clattering burst of chimes when the tall white-necked animals gather in enough numbers to block a road.

There is never panic. The animals are too numerous for panic to be useful, and their patterns are too old for surprise.

At dawn—though dawn is more a greying of cloud than a change in the town—the first shift begins at the pumping house. Pistons wake under the basin floor. Steam rises from vents in steady white sheets. Conveyor belts begin carrying crushed black mineral from the quarry tunnels to the east furnaces. The factories are not grand; they are compact, brick-bodied, patched with corrugated iron and riveted steel. Yet their noise fills the basin completely. Hammers pulse. Fans throb. Turbines keen inside concrete housings. A long belt of buckets rattles overhead from the processing shed to the rail yard.

The animals answer it.

Their calls weave through the machinery as if the machinery were another species. The white-necked ones produce a wet, resonant knocking from deep in their throats. The shell-backed herds click and scrape. Something beneath the reservoir grates gives off a low sustained note, like an organ pipe being played underwater. The airborne creatures make a dry crackle whenever they bank together.

The sound is loud enough that people communicate with lamps, flags, hand signals, and the coded opening and shutting of shutters. Children learn the signals before they learn written numbers. The language of the town includes a gesture for “the north culvert has opened,” another for “keep above street level,” another for “do not start the machine; they are sleeping in the intake.”

Water comes intermittently, but it comes everywhere when it does. Rain moves over the basin in narrow, regular belts, as though released from valves in the clouds. One street can be wet and shining while the next remains dust-grey. Condensation gathers continuously on the undersides of bridges, on pipe insulation, under the flanges of storage tanks. The air smells of cold iron, wet concrete, animal heat, and a faint sourness from the reservoirs.

There are no rivers in the open. Water is kept in channels, cisterns, boiler loops, drainage galleries, and square basins guarded by heavy mesh. It is shared with the fauna according to arrangements no one remembers making. At certain hours, the municipal pumps slow. Gates rise from the reservoir margins. The animals enter by species: first the rolling black bodies, then the pipe-shelled crawlers, then the tall pale things, delicately stepping down into water that comes only to their knees. Nobody works during this interval. The mills idle, the rails wait, and the whole town listens to drinking: splashing, suction, grating beaks, thousands of small hard feet moving at once.

Then the gates close. The pumps resume. Work continues.

The strangest part is not that this has always been the arrangement. It is that nothing seems capable of altering it.

The brickwork is old but does not collapse. Rust forms on exposed metal, then stops at the same dark-orange thickness. Rain falls in its appointed bands. The animal migrations return along the same streets, at the same pace, around the same corners. A cracked window above the eastern bakery has remained cracked for generations; its loose triangular shard trembles whenever the south turbine reaches full load but never falls. The reservoir level rises and lowers by a handspan each day, no more. The clocks are synchronized not to any distant capital but to the breathing cycle of the great unseen animals beneath the basin.

Even repairs seem preordained. A worker will notice a seam leaking steam, fetch a wrench, climb a ladder, and find the bolts already loosened to exactly the degree required. A pavement stone displaced by a herd will sit beside its empty bed until the night shift returns it. The repair is not an intervention. It is part of the recurrence.

No plants soften the edges of this life. The town is all hard surfaces and inhabited gaps: brick, slag, glass, wet steel, porcelain shell, bone-white beak, black water, ash-coloured sky. Yet it does not feel dead. It feels overcrowded with a kind of life that has no interest in greenness.

At the basin’s western edge stands a small public platform, built from old rail girders. Visitors, if any come, are brought there and told not to descend. From that height, the place appears almost peaceful. The industrial roofs shine under ordinary cloud-filtered daylight. Steam drifts in low ribbons. The sparse buildings hold their positions. Herds trace dark lines through the streets. The air is full of motion, sound, and damp breath.

And beneath all of it, beneath foundations and pipes and the measured pounding of engines, the ground occasionally makes a slow answering sound—one deep knock, felt in the teeth more than heard.

No one in town looks up when it happens.

No one looks down, either.
