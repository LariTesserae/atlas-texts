The hollow lies only a little below the ground, but it has the feeling of being much farther removed from the surface than depth alone should allow. A person could reach it by following a crack between sun-whitened stones, lowering themselves through a sloped throat of earth, and emerging into a chamber no larger than a village square. There is no architecture in it. Nothing has been made here. Yet the place possesses boundaries, proportions, and a settled hush so complete that it seems arranged by some older, slower intention.

Its ceiling is low in places and opens high in others, not lofty enough to become grand, but large enough that the bright air can collect under it. The rock is pale, chalky limestone streaked with ocher and dark mineral veins. Long ages of intermittent water have smoothed it into shallow bowls, fluted walls, and shelves that look almost handled. Rain from above enters through hairline fissures. It does not fall as a proper drip except after storms. More often it appears as dampness: beads gathering along the ceiling, a darkening in the porous floor, a brief coldness in the air before a drop slips down somewhere out of sight.

The chamber is bright.

This is its first strangeness. Through a broad seam in the roof, daylight enters in an unwavering white blade. The seam is not wide enough to show much sky, only a hard strip of blue or cloud, but the stone around it receives the light and distributes it. Fine mineral crystals embedded in the walls catch the sun and return it softly. The ground glimmers in patches, as if dusted with glass. At noon, the entire hollow becomes nearly shadowless. Even its recesses are not black; they remain a dim green-gray, full of reflected light.

The warmth belowground is gentle and held. The chamber does not bake like the land above, nor does it cool much at night. Its air smells of dry clay, warmed stone, old rain, and the faint sour-green scent of the few plants able to persist here.

Vegetation gathers only where water has taught it to gather. A fringe of fernlike growth surrounds one dark seep in the rear wall, each frond small and leathery, more gray than green. Thin grasses emerge from cracks in the floor and lean toward the shaft of light. There are clumps of pale moss under the dripping places, dry and crisp around the edges, plush only at their centers. A few rootlets hang down from the ceiling in loose, dusty threads, having found their way from some scrub tree above. They do not reach the floor. They merely tremble when the air shifts.

The rest is emptiness: open stone, bare floor, scattered flakes of fallen mineral crust. The hollow is not crowded with life despite the life it supports. Each presence has room around it. A beetle crossing the illuminated ground seems exposed, singular, almost ceremonial. A lizard on a warm shelf is the only lizard in sight for long minutes. The chamber makes distance out of smallness. Nothing interrupts the eye between one wall and another.

But the animals are numerous if one waits.

Small swifts enter through the upper slit in sudden bursts, circling once through the light, then vanishing into narrow holes in the walls. They nest in deeper passages where the stone stays warm. Their calls are brief and needle-fine, easily swallowed by the chamber. Bats sleep in folds of the ceiling, not in dense black curtains but singly or in loose little groups, each tucked into its own crease. They stir late in the day, unfolding with patient irritation.

There are pale crickets beneath stones, glossy ants carrying fragments of seed hull, and squat cave snails that travel astonishing distances over the wet margins after rain. The snails have translucent shells marked with brown spirals, and by daylight they seem almost empty. In the shallow pools, when they exist, tiny blind fish flicker like scraps of moonlight. The pools are never dependable. One week they can fill the little basins carved into the floor; another week they shrink to a film and a ring of mineral lace.

The most noticeable inhabitants are the fox-sized burrowers that come in from tunnels along the chamber’s lower edge. They have long forelimbs, blunt faces, and dark eyes protected by clear inner lids. Their fur is sandy along the back and nearly white underneath, powdered with the dust they dig through. They are not tame, but neither are they alarmed by all movement. They live by patient excavation, widening old cracks and turning over thin patches of soil in search of grubs, roots, and the pale crickets.

Their tools are simple, though no one would immediately call them tools. At the entrances to their burrows lie selected stones: flat wedges, fist-sized pounders, sharp chips of chert. The animals carry these in their mouths and brace them against stubborn clay, pressing or striking with their forepaws. The stones are used until they split or wear smooth. There are no piles, no workshops, no deliberate constructions. The burrowers do not shape the chamber; they merely take advantage of what the world has already weakened for them. Still, among the natural rubble, one sometimes notices a familiar pattern: a line of three chosen stones near a hole, a shell used as a scoop, a strip of bark dragged underground and abandoned after serving some brief purpose.

Nothing in the hollow changes quickly. The water keeps working, but at the pace of a breath repeated for centuries. A mineral drop swells, releases, and leaves behind the faintest rim of calcite. Dust drifts in from above and settles in the same shallow pockets. Roots descend another finger’s length in a season. The ceiling’s cracks widen imperceptibly, while the floor becomes smoother under the occasional wash of rain.

There are signs that the chamber has been in its present state for a very long time: walls worn to rounded forms, old animal paths polished into the dust, pale crusts laid down and broken and laid down again. Even the disturbances belong here. A fresh scrape in the soil, a feather caught in a damp seam, the collapse of a small edge of clay—these do not alter the hollow so much as join its established grammar.

It is mostly quiet. Water may tick once from a high ledge. A swift may cry and answer itself from the upper dark. The burrowers make a soft scraping sound when they work, like a hand moving across coarse paper. Sometimes a dry grain of stone falls from the roof and makes an unexpectedly clear click on the floor.

At certain hours, when the sun aligns exactly with the ceiling seam, the bright beam reaches a shallow depression in the center of the chamber. There, moisture persists longer than elsewhere. The light finds the wet stone, and the wet stone throws it upward in ripples across the surrounding walls. For a while the empty hollow seems to be full of moving water, though there is scarcely enough water to wet a palm.

Then the angle passes. The ripples disappear. The chamber returns to its pale, patient brightness, holding its few plants, its hidden animals, its old tools of stone, and its slow collection of rain.
