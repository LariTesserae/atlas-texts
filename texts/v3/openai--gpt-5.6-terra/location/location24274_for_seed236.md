At the height of the escarpment there is a town no larger than a cathedral precinct, gathered inside the broken crown of a young volcanic ridge. It is not a city that can be seen from far away. From the surrounding uplands it appears only as a dark seam in the stone, a concentration of green-black growth caught among fresh, angular cliffs. The place has boundaries: a horseshoe wall of raw basalt on three sides and a descending field of pale mineral dust on the fourth. Beyond that dust there is almost nothing—high, warm emptiness, folded ridges, and a sky bleached white-blue by altitude.

No water runs here. No rain falls in the familiar sense. The gullies are dry and have always been dry, their sharpness preserved because nothing has had time, or enough liquid, to soften them. Rock has split under heat, under slow internal pressure, under the rooting of things, but it has not been worn down. Every ledge has a clean edge. Every fracture looks recently made.

Yet the hollow is lush.

Its greenery is not soft in the way of lowland leaves. It rises in dense, crowded masses from cracks in the basalt: tall ribbon-plants with dark waxy blades; clustered fans of silver-green fronds; knuckled shrubs whose skins are glassy and translucent at the tips. They draw what they need from the air and stone. Night brings a thin mineral exhalation from the cooling ridge, a heaviness barely visible in the moonlight, and the plants drink it through millions of fine pores. In the morning their surfaces are powdered with salts—yellow, pearl-grey, rust-red—which they gradually fold inward and digest.

Some of the larger growths resemble trees only by function. Their trunks are bundles of rigid hollow reeds, fused together with resinous mineral bark. Their crowns are lattices of broad, overlapping plates. There is almost no shade beneath them, but there is shelter: a warm pocketed stillness, dense with root-smell, dust, and the dry sweetness of flowering organs.

Flowers open from the walls rather than from stems. They are deep cups of orange membrane, black at the throat, and they do not offer nectar. Inside each is a trembling velvet growth that feeds on vapors and minute grains of airborne metal. Their pollen is heavy. It does not drift far. It gathers on insects and on the noses of the small browsing animals that push through the vegetation.

Fauna is everywhere, though little of it moves openly.

Pale, six-legged grazers stand shoulder to shoulder in the narrow planted lanes. Their bodies are low and broad, armored in overlapping scales the color of kiln-fired clay. They chew the tougher ribbon-plants with a patient side-to-side motion, then settle into the warmed stone at dusk, becoming nearly indistinguishable from the walls. Above them cling flocks of membrane-winged creatures, each no larger than a hand. Their wings are patterned like stained stone: cream, umber, green. They make no audible flutter. They open and close in slow, deliberate pulses, crossing the gaps between roofs and trunks like scraps of shade.

There are burrowing things too—small, blind, social animals that build the town as much as its inhabitants do. They tunnel into the softer ashstone beneath the basalt shelves, carry polished fragments in their jaws, and line their chambers with dry plant fiber. Their mounds have been incorporated into walls, stoops, ovens, animal pens, and shrine-like recesses. In this place construction is never entirely separate from nesting.

The built portions of the settlement grow from the hollow’s natural geometry. Basalt blocks have been stacked without mortar in close, heavy courses. Some were clearly placed by hands; others seem merely to have settled into positions that made them useful. Narrow dwellings press their shoulders together beneath overhanging cliff faces. Their roofs are made from slabs, woven reed trunks, and broad mineral-leaves cut from the larger plants after they harden. The lanes between them are so tight that a person could touch both sides with outstretched arms.

There are terraces, though nothing is irrigated. On each terrace, stone basins hold powdered ore, ash, seed-cysts, and cultivated mats of dark green growth. Low walls protect them from the wind. The residents tend these gardens with brushes, baskets, digging bones, and smooth volcanic scrapers. Their work is precise but unhurried. There are no engines, no lamps, no running mechanisms, no visible metalwork beyond what the ground itself supplies in flakes and nodules.

The town is dense because the hollow is habitable and the surrounding country is not. Every crevice has a use. Walls serve as heat stores. Rooftops support crawling vines. Stairs double as drying racks for fibrous fruit. Animal enclosures sit beneath sleeping rooms, lending them warmth during the cold hours before dawn. Small courtyards contain communal stones polished pale by countless hands and bodies resting against them.

Despite all these presences, the place is quiet.

There is no running water, no wind strong enough to roar through the passages, no machinery, no loud birds. Sounds arrive individually and disappear: the dry clicking of a grazer’s mouth; a foot scuffing powder from a stair; the soft collapse of a seed pod; a faint rasp of leaves rubbing their mineral skins together. Voices, when used, are low. They do not carry far in the dense lanes, and perhaps the people have adapted to that fact. Much is communicated by touch, by the placement of objects, by gestures made at close range.

At midday the light is ordinary and strong. It enters the hollow almost vertically, turning the basalt blue-black and the pale dust painfully bright. The town’s green growth catches it in waxy sheets. The whole place seems dry enough to crack, yet it has an undeniable abundance: bodies in doorways, creatures on walls, roots lifting paving stones, blossoms pushing from shaded seams.

By evening the high air turns gold, then violet. Heat leaves the upper rock slowly. The plants open their night pores. A faint, metallic scent settles through the streets, like warm stone after a storm in a world where storms never arrive.

Nothing changes quickly here. The ridge is still young, still lifting by fractions beneath the settlement. A wall may develop a new hairline crack over a generation. A terrace may rise a finger’s width as the ground swells. A plant colony may take decades to cross a roof. The people rebuild in response, not because of disaster but because the land continuously makes small requests of them.

In the center of the hollow stands an unroofed square, hardly larger than a village green. Its ground is paved in dark polygonal slabs. At its heart is a fissure, narrow and perfectly dry, from which a warm breath occasionally rises.

No one has bridged it.

The square bends around it. Gardens stop at its edge. Children are taught not to throw stones into it, not because anything answers, but because the stones are needed aboveground. At night, the fissure reflects no light. It is simply a line of blackness among the warm, inhabited stone: the reminder that this fertile, crowded little city is perched on a new piece of world, and that the world beneath it is still becoming.
