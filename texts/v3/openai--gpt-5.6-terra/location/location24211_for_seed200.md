The land lies low and wide beneath a ceiling of weather that never quite becomes rain.

It is a basin, though not a basin made by age. Nothing here has been carved down, worn smooth, or decisively shaped. The ground seems to have risen only recently from some softer state: sheets of dark mineral mud, hummocks of red clay, bulbous shelves of stone still bearing the rounded, unfinished look of cooling wax. Ridges do not yet know whether they will be hills. Channels have not committed to being rivers. Everywhere, water waits in shallow folds of the earth, held just below the surface by an invisible skin.

The heat is constant. It does not blaze from a sun—there is rarely a visible sun—but comes up through the soil and hangs in the air, damp and bodily. Every breath has weight. The atmosphere smells of green sap, warm peat, sweet rot, and the faint metallic scent of freshly exposed ground. Condensation gathers on every leaf, on every stem, along the smooth backs of stones. It does not fall so much as accumulate, bead by bead, until the whole landscape shines in the dimness.

Above, there is a pale, unbroken cloud vault: gray-green at noon, violet-brown at its lower edges, lit from somewhere beyond itself. The light is weak but pervasive. There are no clean shadows. Things close at hand are visible in detail—the ribbing on a leaf, the translucent eyelid of an animal, the tiny roots pressing against the mud—but distance becomes a wet haze. Across the basin, one can make out masses of vegetation and the darker lift of newly forming ground, but their borders blur into vapor.

Vegetation has taken advantage of the unfinished earth with astonishing speed.

Roots go everywhere. They spread through soft mineral beds, clasping stones before the stones have fully settled, knitting floating mats across pools. Low forests of broad-leaved trees crowd the firmer ground, their trunks flaring at the base like buttresses made of wet clay. Vines descend in ropes, then root again where they touch the ground. Ferns rise shoulder-high in the open places. Reeds fill the shallows so densely that a pool may look solid until a flock of small animals enters it and the surface breaks into rings.

Some plants appear too eager for light that scarcely exists. Their leaves are enormous, dark, and lacquered, turned upward to catch brightness from the clouds. Others have pale undersides, almost luminous in the dimness, and when a warm wind passes through the basin they turn together, revealing brief tides of silver-green. Flowers are abundant but not delicate: thick cups, waxy throats, dangling clusters swollen with nectar. Their colors are deep rather than bright—purples like bruises, red-black maroons, yellows visible only at close range.

There is no empty ground.

Even the bare mud is crowded with life: tiny red runners, transparent worms, black beetles with wetly reflective shells, pale jointed things that fold shut when touched by a drop of water. The edges of every puddle are stitched with eggs. The undersides of leaves carry clustered larvae, folded wings, sleeping bodies. A fallen fruit may be covered within minutes—not because anything has arrived, but because the creatures were already there, hidden in the grass, beneath the bark, inside the soil’s breathing pores.

The fauna is the basin’s true weather.

Birds—or creatures close enough to birds—move through the canopy in dense, noisy bands. Some are glossy and long-tailed, their calls like struck clay pots. Some are small, blunt-winged, and innumerable, chirring continuously from the reeds. Large amphibious animals ease through the pools with only their eyes and nostrils visible above the green scum; their throats inflate with resonant, low notes that can be felt in the sternum. In the mud flats, herds of broad-backed grazers feed on reeds and succulent shoots, their hides patterned with algae and trailing small white insects. They move slowly, almost lazily, but the world around them seethes.

Everything is accompanied.

A lizard-sized hunter follows each grazer’s footstep, taking flushed insects. Little fish collect in the hoofprints before the water has settled. Long-legged waders hunt the fish. In the branches above, snakes hunt the waders’ chicks. Something striped and catlike watches the snakes, not with urgency but with the patient certainty of a creature that has never known scarcity. Even decay is inhabited: mushrooms rising from damp wood, mites grazing the mushrooms, tiny frogs hiding within their caps.

The noise has no beginning and no end. It is not the noise of a place waking or settling; this land does neither. It maintains itself at a tremendous volume. Buzzing, clicking, throat-calls, wingbeats, wet splashes, the soft suction of mud releasing roots, the constant ticking of water gathering and slipping from leaf tips. Beneath these sharper sounds is a permanent bass hum, produced by insects in the soil and frogs in the water and perhaps by the warm ground itself.

Yet despite all this activity, nothing truly changes.

The clouds remain at their same dim height. Pools do not drain. The hot air does not cool after dark, and darkness never wholly arrives. Plants flower, fruit, rot, and grow again in overlapping layers so that the green abundance of the basin is always exactly itself. Predators feed, prey breed, carrion disappears, eggs fill every niche left by the eaten. No season advances. No dry spell opens the ground. No flood remakes the channels. The unfinished hills remain unfinished, their soft folds held in place by roots and moisture and some deeper agreement.

It is a young world that has stopped at youth.

In the center of the basin, where the ground is least decided, stands a field of black, rounded stone knobs rising waist-high from the mud. They are warm to the touch and slick with moisture. Between them grow white-stemmed plants whose leaves close slowly around insects but never seem to digest them; the trapped insects emerge hours later coated in pollen and fly away. Around these stones, the animal sounds alter. They become softer, more synchronized. Hundreds of throats pulse at once in the reeds. The water makes no ripples unless touched.

One might expect the place to feel wild in the sense of danger, but its danger is not sudden. It is density. There is nowhere to stand without entering someone’s territory, feeding path, nest, hiding place, nursery, or body. The ground gives slightly underfoot. The air rests on the skin. A vine may be alive in a way one did not first understand. A still pool may contain a thousand watching eyes. To be alone here would be impossible; even silence would be crowded.

And still, the basin has a kind of peace.

It has no ruins, no roads, no cut lines through the green. Nothing has been cleared to make room for anything else. The land has not been arranged. It simply persists in a saturated equilibrium: hot, wet, dim, unfinished, and full beyond measure—an immense breathing cradle of mud and leaf and animal sound, holding its first shape forever.
