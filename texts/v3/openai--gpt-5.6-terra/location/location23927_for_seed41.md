The place lies below the ordinary ground, though not very far below it. A person could stand in the dry thorn-country above and never suspect that, beneath a crust of pale limestone and hot dust, there is a hollow full of green breathing things.

It is called a sink, perhaps, though it is more like a buried courtyard made by water over a very long time. The ceiling has fallen in at one end, leaving a slanted opening through which daylight enters reluctantly. From above, the opening is only a ragged crease between roots and stones. Down here it becomes a high, dim wound in the earth. Sunlight arrives as a thin gold dilution, caught in hanging vines and broad leaves before it can reach the floor.

The air is hot. Not the fierce, clean heat of open country, but a held heat: wet at the lips, mineral on the tongue, smelling of leaf mold, warm clay, old rain, and the faint sourness of animals sleeping close together. The stone walls sweat constantly. Moisture gathers in black seams, runs a little way, stops, gathers again. During the short rains, water pours through the fissures above in silver threads, filling shallow basins in the floor and waking every root in the hollow. Then the rain passes, and the basins shrink back into mud, leaving rings of white mineral and the tiny tracks of things that came to drink.

There is no river here. Water has no grand course. It lives in pockets: in cupped leaves, in hollows of rotten wood, beneath flat stones, in a green-black pool at the lowest point of the chamber. The pool is scarcely wider than a table, opaque with fallen leaves and the reflected ceiling. It is always still at first glance. But if one watches, bubbles rise from somewhere below, slow and regular, and a blind fish sometimes disturbs the surface with a pale flash of its belly.

The hollow is not large. One could cross it in a few dozen careful steps. Yet it is crowded enough to feel endless. Roots descend from the broken ceiling in dense ropes, some thin as hair, some thick as wrists, all searching downward. Ferns grow from damp ledges. Glossy-leaved shrubs occupy the pockets of soil between fallen stones. A fig tree, rooted somewhere above, has sent its roots through the ceiling and made a second trunk in the chamber itself: a pale, twisting column that divides into buttresses before it reaches the floor. Around it, moss climbs in a dark felt. Fungus spreads in shelves along old wood, ochre and smoke-blue in the weak light.

Nothing here is still, but almost nothing is loud.

Leaves shift against one another with the faintest leathery whisper. Drops release from the ceiling at long intervals. Beetles move under bark with a dry granular ticking so small it can be mistaken for one's own blood in the ears. Somewhere in the wall, a creature gnaws softly at limestone, or perhaps merely taps it with its mouth. The quiet is not emptiness. It is density without announcement.

Animals occupy every layer.

Tiny frogs, green-brown and nearly invisible among the wet leaves, sit in the folds of roots and watch with round bronze eyes. Geckos cling to the walls, their bodies the color of chalk, their toes spread like petals. On the fig roots live colonies of ants that cultivate pale fungus in hidden galleries; they emerge in narrow processions after rain, carrying fragments of leaf larger than themselves. Moths rest in the dimness under ledges, wing patterns resembling mold, bark, or the face of some tired little god. A warm cloud of midges hovers above the central pool, always in the same shaft of diluted light.

There are snakes too, though they are rarely seen whole. A curve under the leaf litter. A scale-pattern briefly resolving on a branch. A dry eye reflecting nothing. They are small, patient animals, well-fed in this crowded place. The hollow supports lizards, frogs, nestlings fallen from the world above, and the occasional rat that descends through a root tunnel and finds it cannot resist staying.

At dusk—which is difficult to recognize here, because daylight is dim even at noon—the bats wake in cracks overhead. They do not explode from the ceiling; they loosen themselves one by one. A membrane unfolds. A body rotates. A single dark shape slips out through the broken roof. Then another. Their departure is nearly silent, a soft disturbance in the air. By full night the chamber belongs to the things that do not leave: frogs beginning their restrained glassy calls, insects rubbing legs beneath leaves, the blind fish rising to take something fallen into the pool.

There are signs that people once came here often enough to alter it, but never enough to own it.

Near the entrance, three low stone steps descend from the sloping rubble. They have been worn smooth, then softened by moss. At the foot of the steps stands a small platform made of fitted rock, perhaps waist-high, perhaps once part of a shrine or a storage ledge. Its purpose is gone. A clay bowl lies broken in the roots nearby, its pieces held apart by the steady growth of a small fern. On one wall someone long ago marked a line of red pigment—hands, or suns, or simple circles. Damp has carried the paint downward in thin rust-colored tears.

No tool remains. No door, no rope, no lantern hook. Whatever people did in this hollow, they did it with bodies, stone, clay, fire, and repetition. Perhaps they came for water in the dry months. Perhaps they left offerings at the pool because it bubbled without a visible spring. Perhaps they sheltered here from the heat above. The place has kept their marks in the way a tree keeps a scar: incorporated, no longer explanatory.

The rules of the hollow are modest and absolute.

Nothing dries completely. Even bone, even old wood, even the dust in the most sheltered crack retains a little softness.

Nothing falls quickly. Leaves descend through branches and roots, catch, rot in midair, become soil on ledges. Rain travels by hesitation. Animals move as if the walls are listening.

The brightest place is not necessarily the safest. The shaft of light under the broken roof is where hawks sometimes look down, where falling stones strike after storms, where the larger lizards hunt. The darkest places are warm and full of eyes.

And no space remains unused for long. A vacant cup in a stone becomes a nursery for mosquito larvae. A dead beetle becomes a meal, then a tunnel, then soil. A crack widens, receives a root. A root dies, receives fungus. Fungus receives insects. Insects receive frogs. The whole chamber continually rearranges itself without expanding.

After a hard rain, the sink changes character. Water curtains down the walls. The central pool rises and joins other hollows, making a temporary black chain across the floor. Frogs appear from impossible places. Snails climb upward by the hundreds, seeking the walls. The roots drink so fiercely that some seem to pulse. For a few hours the hollow is almost bright, lit by the reflected grayness of stormwater, and the smell becomes green enough to taste.

Then the water withdraws. The walls resume their sweating. The insects return to their concealed labor. The old steps gleam darkly beneath moss. Above, the dry country heats beneath a hard white sky.

Below, in the small buried chamber, life remains close-packed and patient, keeping its quiet.
