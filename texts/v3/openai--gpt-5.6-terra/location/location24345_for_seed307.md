At the summit there is a basin no wider than a room.

It is hidden in the fold of a new mountain, where two slabs of black stone have sheared apart so recently that their broken edges still look wet. The greater landscape is all altitude: a white, hot sky; distant ridges blurring in heat; raw faces of basalt climbing out of cloud. But the basin cannot be seen from any ordinary path. It is tucked beneath an overhang of volcanic glass, behind a curtain of mineral droplets that falls steadily from the rock without seeming to diminish the rock at all.

Inside, the air is hot enough to make breathing deliberate.

Water is everywhere, though there is no pool. It beads along the black walls, gathers in shallow hollows, runs in bright threads through fissures, and returns as steam from the warm floor. The stone sweats. The ceiling sweats. Even the air seems to condense briefly on the skin before vanishing. Beneath the thin films of water, the rock is crowded with colors: iron red, sulfur yellow, greenish copper stains, milk-pale salts blooming in seams. Nothing soft grows here. No moss reaches for the dampness. No grass takes root in the cracks. The basin is too young, too hot, too chemically restless. Its only gardens are crusts, scales, and translucent skins that may be mineral, may be microbial, or may simply be the mountain practicing life.

The floor is alive with animals.

At first they look like stones shifted by drips. Then one turns over, showing a folded underside of pale jointed legs. Another opens like a fist and clambers up the wall. There are hundreds in the basin, perhaps thousands, packed into every crevice and depression: blind, wet-bodied things with shells of volcanic glass; long ribbon animals that knot themselves around warm rivulets; small squat creatures plated in white mineral scales. Some carry pebbles on their backs. Some hold sharp flakes between hooked limbs and scrape the salt from the walls. Their tools are crude, but unquestionably chosen: needles of obsidian used to prise open mineral blisters, little hammers of dense rock used against shell, splinters carried from one wet crevice to another.

They make almost no sound.

A shell clicks once. A stone slips half an inch. Water taps in a patient, irregular rhythm. The greatest noise is the faint thermal ticking of the basin itself as the mountain expands in sunlight.

The creatures do not seem to fear one another in the way animals elsewhere might. Their bodies overlap. A ribbon passes through a cluster of plated crawlers, and neither reacts. Two larger shell-beasts face each other beside a steaming crack, each holding a shard of black glass. They tap the shards together—not in conflict, not quite in exchange. The glass softens at the edge from the heat below, and they press it against their shells, repairing chips with a careful, ancestral concentration.

At the center of the basin lies a wet stone no larger than a bowl.

It is not a stone in the usual sense. It has a pulse: a slow swelling of its surface, a dim shifting beneath its mineral skin. Every few minutes, a line opens in it and releases a thread of clear water. The thread runs outward through the crowded floor, and the animals lean toward it. Not all at once. Nothing here moves with obvious urgency. But every body adjusts by degrees, arranging itself along the new flow. The basin’s entire population quietly reorients around this momentary source.

Then the water does something wrong.

It runs uphill.

A bead released from the central stone crosses the floor, reaches the wall, and climbs it in a shining vertical line. Other droplets follow. They gather under the overhang, hanging there in a clear, trembling lens. Within the lens, the basin appears inverted: the creatures pacing across a ceiling of stone, the steam descending, the sky far below. The lens grows heavy but does not fall. Eventually it thins into rain, but the rain rises, vanishing into a crack high above.

The animals ignore this. Or perhaps they are the only things here that understand it.

Beyond the overhang, the high mountain burns under the afternoon sun. Its slopes are bare, newly raised, and sharp enough to cut the clouds that strike them. Water threads through its raw seams. Steam rises and gathers. In time, wind and frost may blunt these ridges; soil may settle in sheltered places; something green may attempt the ascent.

But not yet.

For now, in this concealed hot hollow near the roof of the world, there is only stone, water, heat, silence, and an impossible density of small lives attending to the mountain’s unfinished thoughts.
