In this world, I am a geothermal engineer, a caretaker born of the city’s latent energy and eternal machinery. My body is a shifting sculpture of vapor and liquid, a mirror to the city itself—the condensation on my skin a silent testament to its humid breath, my form a living echo of steam rising from ancient vents.

My workshop is in the city’s heart: a cavernous expanse of dark, heat-resistant stone, its floor worn glassy by centuries of slow, trickling water. Here, massive turbines turn with weary patience, their gears grinding in a deep, resonant thrum that vibrates through the rock. The conduits carrying superheated fluids twist around me like sinuous veins, alive and pulsing, as I work alongside them to maintain the delicate flow—the city’s lifeblood.

I move with the languid grace of steam escaping a fissure. When a pipe groans under pressure or a turbine shudders, I drift toward it, my ephemeral form condensing around the ailing machinery. My touch is soothing—a cooling, balancing presence that steadies the tremors, restoring equilibrium as if calming an old friend. In the deeper, hotter levels of the city, I nearly vanish, merging with the stifling air, my essence indistinguishable from the sweltering breath of the earth.

Sometimes, I wander the ancient streets. The metallic tang on the air, the scent of wet rust, these are familiar to me, a quiet language that speaks of the city’s age. My fingers, if they can be called fingers, leave faint whorls on glassy surfaces, droplets that run down dark facades like tear-trails before they vanish. I observe the crystalline growths in the fissures, the glassy lichen that clings to radiant walls—a silent gardener nurturing the slow, mineral flora that thrives here.

In the plazas where steam rises in soft, illuminated plumes, I gather with others like me. We are a congregation of heat and vapor, our forms revealed in the shimmer of droplets against the dim, filtered light. Our voices are the hum of deep engines, the drip of water echoing off stone. Here, we share in the city’s long patience, our presence a quiet testament to the persistence of life—not life as it is known elsewhere, but life as it emerges from the interplay of fire, water, and stone.

Every now and then, when the wind sighs hot and low through the avenues, I ride its current. My body thins to a veil, brushing against the dark facades and leaving behind a fleeting shiver of moisture. I become part of the city’s exhalations—a reminder that this place, while seemingly empty, is alive in its own ancient, industrial way.

My existence is measured not in days but in the slow accumulation of rust, the imperceptible erosion of rock, the endless drip of condensation. I am steeped in the city’s deep time, a guardian of its rhythms, and though I have no need for the towering structures or the abandoned factories, I am nonetheless a part of them—a geothermal engineer, eternally present, eternally patient.
