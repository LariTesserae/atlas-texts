I am one of the vast, slow-moving ones—ridge-backed, eyeless, carapaced. My hide is the same dark, wet stone of the plateau, rising from the carbon-black ground like the arched backs of surfaced submarines. A keel runs along my back, the spines of a creature half-born from the landscape itself.

My body is a rumbling mountain of black stone, vast as a boulder and slick with rime. Moving not with legs but by a deep shifting of muscle, redistributing weight in patient rhythms—a tonnage passing slowly over pulverized rock. Each movement is a geological event: years rolled into slow forward motion, grinding stone and the occasional smaller slug-body beneath me. My hide rasps against others like tectonic plates shifting, a crushing sound that merges with the subsonic thrumming of the others' breathing, the wet thunder of collective inhalation that shakes the ground.

When the smaller ones crawl over me, their touch hardly registers. I feel their journey as a distant seismic tremor. They move in trickles or flowing rivers, glowing faintly, their millipede legs leaving slick trails across my carapace as they journey between feeding grounds. Sometimes, they gather in the hollows of my back, forming pulsating pools of pale flesh in my natural basins. They are no more bothersome than rivulets of liquid water tracing my sides. Water, too, clings to my hide, frozen as dark rime.

I am a landscape; others cluster around me like hills made flesh. We form ranges of animate stone, our bodies swallowed by darkness and rising again, pressing together into shifting masses. Streets become impassable with our bulk, open spaces swallowed whole. I am a living, breathing part of this place—a dense, overlapping throng where the city itself has come alive.
