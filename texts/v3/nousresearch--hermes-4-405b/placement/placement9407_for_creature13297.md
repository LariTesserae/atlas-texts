In this world, I would be of the Stone-Kin, an elder of the "Echo-Layers," those who delve deep within the ancient strata of the great city-mountains, our senses keen to the subtle resonances of the stone. My life is bound to the earth's deep time, and my work is to listen for the slow, geological songs that echo through the mountains and the ice. My fingers, broad and calloused from a lifetime of tracing these resonant vibrations along ice-bound tunnels and through colossal, echoing halls, read a language written in the interplay of solid stone and restless ice-veins, understanding their long, evolving dialogue.

I wear the traditional garb of my folk, thick layers of cured hide and dense furs, but mine is etched with faded inks and pigments ground from rare minerals, each marking denoting generations of observation, a shared history passed down through my lineage. My eyes, though still heavy with the weight of accumulated centuries, see beyond the visible spectrum, attuned to the subtle heat-signatures of geothermal vents or the faint, tell-tale shimmer of latent energy held within certain ice formations. These are the signs only another Echo-Layer would recognize, secrets of the deep earth that guide our excavations and constructions.

Though my body has grown heavy with time, each movement deliberate and ponderous, I retain the art common among us: an ability to find the resonance within the rock and within ourselves, a harmonious frequency that allows the sheer weight of a massive stone to yield and move to our coordinated, rhythmic efforts. In the slow, rhythmic swaying of my clansmen, in the precise placement of fulcrums, in the collective heave that breathes new space into existence—a slow, tectonic breath embodied—I am the keeper of these patterns, the living memory of echoes. My work is not simply architecture but a form of deep communion with the enduring substance of our world, and in the slow unfolding of this labor, I, too, find my place among the Stone-Kin.
