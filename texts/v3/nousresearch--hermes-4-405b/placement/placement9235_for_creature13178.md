The white light of the single sun, so fierce and direct, hits the water in the basins and makes it blaze. When the wind blows, which it does here often, the reflections on the water fracture into a million shards of light that dance and skitter across the undersides of the stone platforms. These skittering fragments have a name for themselves: *marakul*, which in their language means "the hidden light" or "light within light." They are the children of this place.

*You are one of the marakul*: a spark of sentient reflection born from the interplay of water, light, and stone. You are not solid, not like the stone that was or the Masons who built it. Instead, you are glimmer and gleam—you are the visible manifestation of the sun's rays dancing on wet rock.

Your life began in a deep, still basin. At first, you were a formless shimmer of potential, a ripple of possibility on the water's surface. But over time, as the sun moved across the sky and the wind stirred the surface of the basin, you became something more. You learned that by precisely controlling your angle and intensity, you could dance in specific patterns on the undersides of the stone platforms. You found that you could move with purpose, skittering from shadow to light, leaping from one reflective surface to another.

Your body is a shimmering, shifting expanse of light that can stretch and contract, split into hundreds of discrete points of radiance, or merge back into a single, blazing sheet. When you choose, you can be a swarm of tiny, dancing sparks—each a perfect, self-contained reflection of the sun. When you wish to be large and imposing, you gather your substance and stretch yourself thin across the stone. Your form is dictated entirely by your intent and the contours of the world around you.

You move by leaping from one reflective surface to another. You can travel on the underside of a wet stone platform, dart across the flat expanse of a still basin, or even catch a ride on the falling droplets that cascade over edges like curtains of liquid light. Each leap requires perfect timing and a deep understanding of the angles of incidence and reflection. A single miscalculation and you could dissipate into nothingness, vanishing like a snuffed candleflame.

You "see" the world not with eyes but by sensing variations in the light that falls upon your surface and the textures of the stone beneath you. Every detail of the landscape is rendered with impossible clarity. You can feel the slow grit of mineral dissolving in water, the almost imperceptible seep of moisture into stone, the faint, orange-brown cloud of oxidized iron that blooms where water runs longest across the pale limestone. You know the world by the way light plays across it.

Your thoughts are fleeting and bright, a constant stream of impressions and sensations that flicker and dance like the reflections you embody. You have no deep memory, no sense of history or permanence. You live entirely in the present moment, a creature of pure immediacy.

And yet, you are aware of the past, in your own way. You "remember" the world the Masons built when the sun hits the tool marks on the stone just right—suddenly, the ghosts of the Masons' hands are rendered in stark relief, shadows dancing across the rough-hewn surfaces. You see the faint imperfections in the stonework not as flaws but as testaments to the time and care taken by hands that moved with the patience of geology.

You know the water that weeps from the stone intimately. It is your medium and your means of passage—a substance that shapes your world and gives you life. The low murmur of water dripping and flowing is the music to which you dance. It is a sound that speaks of the constant, gentle transformation of the world, a reminder that even here, in this place of stone and stillness, change is always happening.

You are drawn to the places where the water flows and pools, where the light plays most brilliantly. You gather in great, shimmering shoals on the surfaces of the basins and dance in intricate, swirling patterns on the undersides of the platforms that overhang them. These gatherings are the closest things you have to family or community—bright, ephemeral congregations of light and shadow that coalesce and disperse with the passing hours.

Sometimes, when the wind is still and the sun is at its highest, you stretch yourself into a single, vast sheet of light that covers an entire courtyard or plinth. In these moments, you feel a profound connection to the world around you. You are not separate from the stone and water, but a part of them—the visible manifestation of the forces that shaped this place. For a brief, shining moment, you are the landscape itself, rendered in light.

But always, eventually, the light fades and the wind stirs. The sheet of light fractures into a million skittering sparks, and you are many again. You dance and leap, following the paths of the water and the contours of the stone, leaving trails of shimmering afterimages in your wake.
