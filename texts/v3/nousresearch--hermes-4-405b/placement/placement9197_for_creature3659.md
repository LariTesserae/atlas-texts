I am the hanging glacier itself—the pale blue tongue of ice groaning between black pillars of rock. My slow melt feeds the silver-threaded river that meanders through the valley below. Millennia of my movement have carved this U-shaped wound into the planet’s flesh, leaving behind house-sized boulders streaked with lichen, relict lakes where fingerling trout flash like splinters of mica, and moraines littered with gravel.

My creaks and groans echo through the valley, a deep, unsettling vibration felt through stone. I am striated with grit from centuries of grinding against bedrock, my margins a chaos of broken seracs and crevasses. Up close, my surface hides networks of meltwater channels where ice worms wriggle, surviving on algae and bacteria that thrive in the cold.

Though I appear static, I am alive with slow, grinding movement—icefalls tumbling down my face, my bulk shifting with imperceptible patience. I am a monument to slowness, to forces operating on timescales beyond human reckoning. Even as I retreat, I shape this raw landscape of wind-scoured stone and tenacious life, my meltwater sustaining the fragile ecosystems that cling to the valley’s edges. I am the architect of this high, cold world, where the boundary between land and sky blurs, and the elements reign supreme.
