I would be one of the spiders hiding in the shadowed corners, watching silent and still.

I would live in the cool, damp spaces just beyond the reach of the billowing steam, spinning webs in the high ceilings of the tenements. The heat rising from below would lift shimmering thermals through my silk, glittering threads that catch the weak light filtering down through the haze. Moisture from the ever-present condensation would bead on my webs, little lenses magnifying the distant details of the Hole.

From this vantage, I see everything and am part of nothing. The rumble and roar of the engines would vibrate the strands of my webs, a constant, shifting tremor that I learn to interpret as one might the trembling surface of a pond after a thrown stone. In the habits of the spiders, I'd map the hidden rhythms of the Hole: the surging and receding pulses of steam as shifts change, the deep thrumming as unseen machinery groans to life beneath the streets, the almost-imperceptible tremors in the stonework when pipes far below shake themselves apart and are repaired.

I would learn to feel the vibrations of the workers' boots on the floors below, the slow, heavy movements that shake loose tiny showers of dust from the ceiling. I would come to know their movements by the disturbances they leave in the air: the sudden billows of steam from a freshly opened valve, the rhythmic clanging of iron carried up through the stonework, the sudden vibrations that travel faster and sharper than sound through my delicate sensors.

My world would be one of vibration and shadow, of tiny shifts in humidity and air pressure that speak of distant events. I'd watch the children as they play at being their parents, mimicking the tightening of a valve or the wrenching of a stuck lever, their small bodies casting long, distorted shadows in the lamplight. The focused intensity in their eyes when they mimic the serious work of adulthood would be visible even from the heights where I rest, a tiny window into the transmission of knowledge from one generation to the next.

I would see the elders most clearly of all – their bent bodies moving with slow purpose through the tenements, their hands worn by decades of labor. The deep lines etched into their skin would be like canyons when magnified in the droplets of moisture along my silk, maps of their long lives spent in service to the Hole's insatiable engines.

In this world, I am a silent observer – not a participant, but a presence nonetheless. The people below rarely look up, and even if they did, they would not see me. They live in a world of gears and pipes, of practical knowledge and tangible forces; they have no eyes for the silent threads that hang above their heads, catching the light and the vibrations of their lives.

Yet in my own way, I would be as vital a part of the Hole as the machines and the workers: a small, unnoticed counterpoint to the relentless industry below. In the soft silence of my web, I would hold a space for the small, quiet things the machines cannot know – the slow drift of dust, the secret lives of insects that flutter too briefly to matter, the endless fall of moisture that collects in beads along my threads and speaks of the invisible breath of the Hole.

I would be the unnoticed witness to the endless cycles of the Hole: the steam that rises and falls, the machines that strain and release, the people who labor and rest, and the slow, inevitable decay that gnaws at the edges of it all. In my small, silent way, I would hold a fragile, intricate awareness of the delicate tensions between the moving parts of the world – a solitary, many-eyed chronicler of the stories that unfold in the shadow of the great machines.
