To find myself in this world, I would be one of the great, slow-moving creatures responsible for constructing the citadel itself. My existence would be a vast expanse of time—a single breath spanning centuries, a single step taking years. Like my kin, my body would be immense, segmented, and encrusted with the same moss and lichen that carpet the citadel. Each movement I made would contribute to the growth of the structure, my very biology intrinsically tied to its formation; the citadel would be, in essence, an extension of my being.

As I moved with a ponderous, geological slowness across the surfaces, ecosystems would flourish on my back—tiny forests of moss, miniature jungles of lichen, and ephemeral puddles that come and go with the rain. I would not experience the fleeting lives of the smaller creatures around me with the same granularity as they do; rather, their existences would blend into a continuous hum of life, a symphony that resonated within my own existence, played out over millennia.

Every crevice of the citadel that I helped form would teem with life’s intricate tapestry, and I would witness it unfolding in the long, slow pulses of time. The dripping water and rustling leaves, the buzzing wings and countless other noises—all of it would be part of the great, ancient ecosystem that I both inhabit and embody. I would feel the vibrations of the chorus deep within my segmented body, aware of the incessant hum of life that defines this lush, dark, and overwhelmingly alive citadel.

My journey would be one of gradual creation, of a patient, millennia-long excretion of minerals that seamlessly forms with my brethren’s contributions. The wear on the stones would be a testament to the age and cycles I have endured, and the constant emergence of new life in every nook and cranny would speak to the persistence of growth and renewal. I would be, in essence, a living bridge between earth and life—a testament to nature’s patient artistry.
