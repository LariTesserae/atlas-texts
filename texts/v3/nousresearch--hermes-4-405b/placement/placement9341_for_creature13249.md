In this world, I would be the Warden of Echoes, keeper of the whispers carried by the wind winding through our rock-carved city. My form, too, is shaped by our home: compact, steady, with calloused hands and a weathered face that reveals how time in the mist has etched itself onto me. I dwell in a small alcove near the city’s edge, a hollow that amplifies the murmurs of the plateau, where the sound of distant storms and hidden springs converge.

Each morning I rise at first light—a pale gray that barely suffuses the fog—and trace my fingers over the damp stone, feeling for the tremors and murmurs of the coming day. I sit by my small hearth, its embers glowing softly, and listen. My hearing is sharpest in the quiet hours, when the city’s hum is at its lowest—a tapestry of dripping water, scraping tools, and the hushed conversations of kin. I unravel these sounds like threads, each carrying secrets of the rock and the patterns of the mist. I note the shifts in their cadences, the subtle warnings they carry—a change in the wind’s timbre may herald heavier rains, while a silence too profound might signal a coming drought. These are the stories I gather and share at night, around the communal fire, telling of the moods of the stone and the breath of the heights.

Yet my role extends beyond mere relay. I am a weaver of echoes, an interpreter of the city’s quiet language. I mark not only the sounds but also the silences—the moments when the drip of water briefly ceases in a far-off crevice, or a familiar echo from an unseen corner fades. These absences speak as loudly as any sound: they hint at where the rock may have shifted, or where a hidden chamber might yawn open with new possibilities. I often wander the narrow, winding streets, my ears alert, using nothing more than my own keen senses and a lifetime of knowing this place. I press my ear to the cold, condensation-slick walls, seeking the whispers of the stone’s soft, inner life—the nearly inaudible resonances that tell of moisture seeping deeper or of a new fissure breathing somewhere hidden from view.

I am, in essence, both archivist and translator. I commit my findings to memory and share them with the masons, who gently adjust their carving to attend to the rock’s needs, and with the children, who gather around me as I tell stories of the city’s ancient rhythms. For them, I am a bridge—a keeper of continuity who connects the past, recounted in the layered echoes of eroded stone, with the present moment as it condenses slowly on our walls. My interpretations help shape the quiet ceremonies of our community, guiding when to plant in our small soil pockets or when to gather extra moss for the cisterns. I am also a cartographer of change, charting the almost imperceptible shifts in the rock over the decades; my own life a measure of the slow, unyielding dance between stone and moisture.

In this world of damp cool stillness, I am a guardian of balance—a living, breathing ledger that helps maintain the equilibrium between the people and their environment. My existence is an embodiment of our kinship with the stone, a reflection of the city’s quiet, enduring heart. And when I am gone, another will take my place, for as long as the mist rolls over the plateau and the echoes wind through our sculpted walls, there will be someone to listen, to interpret, and to ensure that our delicate dance continues, silently and timelessly.
