The landscape here is a cathedral of ice and stone, a single confined grove of pillars and frost. This is a place of sharp peaks and sudden drops, recently emerged from some colossal fracture or upheaval – the rock faces are sheer, still shedding dust and the occasional rattle of small stones as the frost heaves them loose. Elevation here is extreme, pressed close on all sides by cliffs of grey stone laced with quartz that catches the brilliant, almost painful light. Sun glares off every surface: ice, water, polished rock, the sheen of frost-rimed mosses.

This is a damp place, perpetually cold enough to keep the air crisp but not so cold as to forbid moisture entirely. Here, in the shelter of the peaks, meltwater and condensation gather in cracks and hollows, carving shallow basins where water stands frozen at the edges, rippling faintly at the center like liquid glass. The air itself carries a chill that bites at the skin but holds a scent of wet rock and something greener, more alive – the lush, dense tangle of mosses, lichens, and surprisingly hardy ferns that coat every sheltered surface. Here, life blooms in the damp and the cold, a miniature jungle of brilliant emerald greens, deep forest mosses, and the startling white of frost-laced fronds. That vegetation forms thick carpets in sunlit areas, climbs the pillars of rock, and hangs in shaggy beards from any overhang it can find.

The grove itself is a hollow bounded by these sharp young cliffs, a space that feels enclosed, defined, like a vast natural hall or nave. Within it, all is bright, almost blindingly so, with the sun striking down from a sky that feels very high and distant beyond the jagged rim of rock above. Sound is muffled, absorbed by the moss and the frost-rimed stone; only the drip of meltwater, the faint sigh of wind in the high places, and the occasional skitter of a falling pebble break the profound quiet. Yet despite the stillness, this place is alive with unseen movement.

Fauna thrives here – abundant, diverse, a secret ecosystem pulsing in the cold. Insects with frosted carapaces like living jewels flit between frost-blooms, their wings catching the light. Small, thick-furred rodents with paws like icepicks dig burrows in the moss, vanishing at the first hint of shadow. Bird nests, woven from lichen and frost-grass, perch on high ledges, their occupants watchful and silent. Larger shapes move with deliberate care in the deeper shadows, hunters weaving between the stone pillars, leaving tracks that fill with meltwater and freeze again. This is an ecosystem in motion, a delicate balance of hunter and hunted, growth and decay, all playing out against the stark geometry of the stone.

Human presence here is faint, scattered, almost erased by time and weather. Crude carvings, worn almost smooth, mark a few of the larger stones – spirals, lines, symbols whose meaning is lost. A single low stone shelter, its roof long since collapsed and covered in moss, leans against one cliff face. Inside, hidden beneath a drift of frost, lie a few hand-forged metal tools and the remains of a fire pit filled with ash and bone fragments. These remains speak of early tool use, of a people who came, stayed briefly, and then vanished or moved on. They add to the sense of the place being ancient, knowing, holding secrets in the grain of its stone and the patterns of its frost.

Weirdness touches the place too, a subtle dissonance in its beauty. The light here has a quality that seems to shift and intensify without reason, casting deep blue shadows beneath the ferns even at noon, or making the frost-grass shimmer with unexpected colors. The water in the basins sometimes moves against the wind, forming patterns on its surface that suggest something beneath, something stirring. The very air seems to hum with a barely perceptible energy, the cold carries strange scents, and the mosses sometimes pulse with a faint inner luminescence, visible only at twilight. This place feels alive in ways beyond the merely biological, as if the stone itself dreams.
