The cavern is not ancient. It's new-made, and vast: a sloping basin beneath some more-solid substrate, extending past sight. The air is cold—not the deep chill of undisturbed stone, but a restless, damp-edged cool, stirred by movement. Everywhere, condensation beads on surfaces like sweat, drips from sharp corners, pools in crusted basins. The sound of water percolating through the cavern's superstructure is a constant subterranean rush, frequently lost beneath the groan of engines.

Because everything here is built. The ground is not ground but a mosaic of riveted plates, gridded walkways black with water, freshly graded slopes of crushed stone. Square stacks, venting vapor that joins the dripping air, mark out a cityscape without permanence. Walled pits drop into artificial ponds, pipes run vertically up rock, industrial platforms jut into the gloom. No surface is natural; even the cavern walls, rising into darkness, are carved in recent fractures, their stone exposed and jagged, too sharp-edged to have eroded. High artificial cliffs loom over lesser structures, their vertical faces weeping black water from runoff channels.

The scale is immense yet underfilled. Construction camps occupy one district of the basin floor—bare shelters between stacked components. Further out, skeletal factory frameworks are abandoned in mid-assembly, girders already slimy with condensation and pale fungal creep. Faint shafts of light from above pick out other structures on higher levels: maintenance catwalks clinging to the cavern's eastern slope, the lower galleries of elevator shafts disappearing into the ceiling rock. There's so much space between presences, the structures standing in their own held terrain, the atmosphere thick with the sense of things underway—sounds of metal, excavation, heavy machinery at work in the lower levels. This is not decay; it is existence as perpetual effort. Production is starting everywhere, never completed. The air thrums with that constant action.
