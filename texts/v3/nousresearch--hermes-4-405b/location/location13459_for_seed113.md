The descent begins as a narrow maintenance shaft, lined with the smooth white biopolymer that covers the city above. The air grows noticeably colder within a few meters, enough to prickle the skin with gooseflesh. The constant background thrum of the city—endless voices, footsteps, distant machinery—fades rapidly here to be replaced by a low, subterranean thunder.

An opening looms, then expands violently: the shaft emerges onto a narrow ledge hanging in dizzying space. This is the heart of the Cistern—if it can be said to have one. The space is vast, cathedral-scale, easily large enough to swallow a hamlet whole. Below and above, the walls plunge and soar into dimness; diffuse light filters from unseen panels or perhaps a bioluminescent ceiling kilometers overhead. The light that reaches this depth is weak, diluted, Philippian—it must be artificial, but it mimics daylight well enough to leave the exact time impossible to guess.

The space is not empty. From every surface hangs or sprouts a profusion of dangling, weeping structures, like the limbs of enormous stone chandeliers. Each formation oozes water from its tip at a steady rhythm; the droplets fall in curtains, catching the weak light like strings of pearls, each bead briefly glowing with refracted luminescence before vanishing into the shadows below.

These globules form a constant, city-scale rainfall—and as they fall, they are caught and channeled by a second, equally dense network of structures rising from below. Some of these catchment basins are simple, cupped baffles that shatter droplets into clouds of finer mist. Others feed into ducts and tubes that carry their harvest away into side channels, stairwells, and catacombs riddling the walls. It’s an infrastructure as elaborate and delicate as veins beneath city pavement, every surface dripping and trickling and flowing somewhere.

The air is cool, damp, perpetually misted by the shattered rain. The low thrumming sound has detail now: a deep foundation of flowing water from the largest collection pipes, a constant hiss from spray nozzles designed to scatter droplets evenly, and a high, reedy chorus as the falling water forces air through narrow gaps in the traps.

The structures of this place are varied but share an architectural language. The older, deeper elements are raw geological forms—ribs of exposed bedrock, thrust upward by tectonic collisions so violent that they sheared and bent and folded, leaving sharp, angular facets and deep, shadowed crevasses. Into these have been carved, extruded, or grown a polyceramic scaffolding to support the ducts and conduits. This scaffolding is young where it meets the bedrock—jagged, unfinished edges and rough, mineral-veined surfaces showing where biomineral secretions were recently deposited. Further from the rock, the scaffolding is older and exhibits layered ossification, forming smooth, hard shells around the pipes.

The dominant materials are all cool to the touch despite the city's ambient warmth above: wet stone, glinting polyceramics, and the ever-present moisture that beads on every surface.

No animal life makes its home here. The environment seems actively hostile to it—probing sensors detect faint ultrasonic pulses designed to disrupt navigation, and the sheer volume of falling water acts as a constant physical deterrent. The only motion comes from water falling, mist swirling, and the occasional shudder of pipes as pressure builds within them.

This space is urban not in its human occupancy but in its layered organization. It is clearly engineered: the weeping rock and dripping pipes are not natural phenomena but a sophisticated piece of hydraulic infrastructure. One can look down into shafts and see smaller, functionally similar chambers below, stacked like cells in a hive, all contending for the same falling water. This is not a wilderness, but an enormous machine whose purpose—water distribution? Flood control? Atmospheric maintenance?—is hidden in the complexities of its construction and operation.

What little brightness there is comes from ingrown bioluminescent strains. Embedded in the polyceramic shells of the pipes are veins of what appears to be fungal tissue, carefully cultured to glow with a weak blue-green radiance that backlights the falling droplets like liquid emeralds. These veins show evidence of pruning or grafting, as if the light itself is being cultivated and harvested in the same meticulous way as the water.

The humidity and constant moisture have encouraged lush growth—mosses bearding every outcrop, algae coating still pools and slow-flowing channels. Ferns cluster wherever spray from the pipes creates perpetual mist, their fronds hovering like delicate green halos above unseen water sources. They are pale things here without sunlight, growing in loose tangles of palmate leaves that only hint at the vibrant life found on the surface above.

This place is active—intensely so—but in a way that creates its own peculiar stillness. The system is in constant motion, but it is balanced: water falls, is caught, is redirected, and falls again. Channels fill and empty like tides. The mist swirls. The only stillness is geological.

It would be easy to call it empty, but that is not quite true: it is full of water—countless tons of it held in suspension by the intricate plumbing, each droplet tracing its own path through a vast, engineered cycle—and full of sound, the rushing and dripping and hissing of a machine diligently performing its hidden work.
