The landscape is a subterranean metropolis known as the Grotto Sprawl, a city of low, densely packed stone structures that seem to press in from all sides. The air is cool and perpetually damp, the ceiling high enough to allow a half-light from unseen fissures above, water occasionally seeping through and collecting in grottoes among the stonework. The city is not ancient, but rather embryonic, its stone blocks freshly hewn and marked with rough chisel blows; the pathways through the city remain uneven and narrow, the walls unplastered. There are few plants—a rare cluster of fungi in a forgotten corner, the odd lichen clinging to rock—but little else. The few residents—laborers and chiselers—move with purpose, their tools primitive but their work unceasing: a clink of stone on stone, a scraping, a low creaking as another block is levered into place. The city is strangely quiet, though, the mute stone absorbing most sounds. The rules of this world are inscribed in the ongoing construction, each new block, each new space carved out adding to the Grotto Sprawl's sense of self, its life a dynamic collaboration between water, stone, and the slow, careful labor of its inhabitants.
