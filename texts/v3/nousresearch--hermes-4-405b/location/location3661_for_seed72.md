The first impression is of descending — the entrance is a vertical shaft, narrow and dripping, the elevator cage big enough for a half-dozen grown humans but often carrying machinery or stacked pipes of supplies. It’s an old freight elevator retrofitted, its mechanisms scraping and clanging during the twenty-meter descent.

Below ground, it’s as hot and damp as a sauna. The air is thick with mineral smells — iron and copper, the scent of steam — and the constant roar of machinery and rushing or dripping water. Valves hiss and pipes ping as they expand. The walls are rough-hewn rock, wetly shining, and the space within is honeycombed with tunnels wide enough to drive small vehicles down, the floors frequently covered in shallow puddles or sheets of flowing water directed into drains.

The settlement is small, laid out across an area not much larger than a large house with outlying sheds, but layered construction has filled every available space. Structures are built organically, squeezed between rock and pipes, walkways clinging to walls and ceilings, ladders leading to secondary and tertiary levels. It’s a maze, and it’s dense. Steel girders and concrete patchwork vie for space with exposed conduits and bundles of thickly insulated wires. Everything is damp. With every footstep, with every groan of the infrastructure, as if weeping rocks themselves will make this new space. It’s all floodlit by hanging fixtures like factory lamps, casting deep and shifting shadows.

Facilities are simple. Dormitories built into alcoves, a mess hall with long tables, lockers lining the wet corridors. Equipment locker areas are packed with tools for mining and construction, industrial wet-suit variants hang in steaming drying rooms. A constant low thrumming comes from the air scrubbers and water pumps that keep the caves habitable. Everything is utilitarian, scraped raw where it rubs against rock.

The heat is oppressive, a constant physical pressure only alleviated by the nearness of water. This is not the realm of plants. Occasional patches of moss near the weeping walls are the only green living things that grow here, thin and pale like scars on the stone.

In stark contrast to the scarcity of flora, this subterranean environment teems with life. The stone seems to crawl, coated with an ever-shifting carpet of creatures. Pale, eyeless fish dart through the channels of water. Crabs with long, probing legs skitter and click across the wet concrete. Slugs, fat and glistening, leave silvery trails. Swarms of tiny insects hum in clouds around the lamps, coil like living rope along the pipes. The heat and the moisture foster this staggering, primitive abundance in every puddle and every crevice.

This is a place of slow emergence. The existing space is subdued, its boundaries defined. But beyond the settlement's infrastructure, the edges are embryonic. Mined caverns are raw and unfinished, their edges not yet smoothed by time or the hands of inhabitants. Tunnels end abruptly in piles of rubble and freshly broken rock, or open into great dark voids where the distant drip of water echoes. Weeping wall surfaces are a slow bleed. The space feels alive, and unfinished, as though the settlement itself is a temporary intrusion in the rock’s slow formation.
