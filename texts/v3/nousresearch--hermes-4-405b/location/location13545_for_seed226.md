The air here is cool and thick with the scent of wet stone and chlorophyll. You stand at the edge of a nameless settlement, its rough-hewn wooden structures—dark with dampness—evenly spaced across the shallow slope of a high mountain valley. Mosses in a dozen shades of green and chartreuse fatten every surface, swelling over the edges of hand-ressed logs and creeping between the flat stones of footpaths. The town feels simultaneously young and ancient; the wood is raw, unweathered, the marks of the adze still sharp on its surface, yet the stones that pave its central clearings appear to be emerging from the earth itself, half-buried and surrounded by mud that never quite dries. Everywhere the sound of water asserts itself: the chuckle of rivulets finding their paths between freshly-laid foundation stones, the steady plink-plink-plink of droplets falling from the lips of stone basins artlessly hollowed into the mountainside, the pervasive whisper of condensation gathering on cool surfaces.

Vegetation flourishes in the constant moisture; ferns unfurl fiddleheads as broad as your hand, vines with leaves like polished jade climb the support posts of covered walkways, epiphytes crown the rain-slicked branches of the few slow-growing trees permitted to remain within the settlement’s loose boundaries. Even the air seems to hold the potential for growth, heavy with the promise of fog condensing out of the cool, bright light of a sun that never quite burns through the highland haze.

Despite its lushness, the scale here is intimate. The entire settlement would fit neatly within the walls of a single large building elsewhere: a cluster of a dozen small structures, a larger communal hall with a roof that sags under its own mossy weight, the whole precinct defined by a low stone wall still being pieced together from frost-shattered rocks gathered from the surrounding slopes. Though the buildings are dense for their size—compressed together against the damp chill—the overall impression is one of emptiness. Vast stretches of open space yawn between the structures, filled only with the sound of water and the slow growth of mosses on bare soil. Movement is rare: now and then a figure swathed in layers of undyed wool emerges from a dwelling, moves with deliberate care along the slick paths, disappears. No animals call from the surrounding slopes—only the wind and the water break the quiet. The dominant sensation is of a place being slowly dreamed into existence, a settlement emerging with such glacial patience that even its human inhabitants seem to move at the speed of the stones themselves.
