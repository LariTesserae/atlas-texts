The world here exists suspended in a liquid atmosphere, a planet drowned deep in an ocean of its own breath. Even at this altitude — for the ground is high, a scatter of mountain peaks just barely jutting from the endless water into the thin slosh of interface between liquid-air and liquid-sky — the cold bites with the sharpness of the abyss. There is no dry land, only gradients of immersion. The mountains themselves are ancient, their peaks worn soft by centuries of aquatic erosion, with long deep valleys between them filled with darker, colder, quieter liquid.

The light is perpetual twilight, filtered through fathoms of gray-green water-sky. It is dim, dim enough that bioluminescence would seem garish — except there is no visible fauna, no animal life at all. The only sound is a low, pervasive drone, a continuous loud exhalation that seems to issue from the water itself. This world is all liquid, yet there is wind — the water-air ripples and shudders, carving slow currents, stirring the submerged valleys. It's not an animal sound. It has no breath, no pause, no life, only movement, immense and relentless.

Despite the cold, a lushness thrives here — thick forests of kelp-like plants root in the submerged slopes, their wide green-black fronds undulating in the slow currents, reaching up through hundreds of feet toward the slightly less crushing pressure of the surface layers. Closer to the mountaintops, in what would be alpine meadows were this world not submerged, are fields of a different sort of plant: low and wide, almost like inverted bowls, spongy and pale, with leaves like ribbons that drift and tangle in the aquatic breeze. These fields are sparingly scattered across the slopes where the peaks shoulder up closest to the edge of the aerial sea.

Amid these plants, set into the rock and perched on outcrops, are small primitive structures. They are low domes, crudely fitted from pale stone, without doors or windows. It's as if they are not built to be entered but simply to exist, to mark a presence. There is no sign of tools more complex than stone. These structures are faint, but distinct, hinting at some process of construction — not natural formations. Yet there are no builders. No signs of inhabitants at all.

The water itself has an odd, silvery quality — it isn't just clear or briny, but seems to hold light within it, a pearlescent sheen that makes distances deceptive and shimmers faintly even in the gloom. And sometimes in the very lowest parts of the valleys, where shadows gather in the folds of the mountains, the water itself seems to grow darker, more viscous, as if it is not just water but something else, a denser fluid, a different phase of matter. There the undisturbed sediment settles in slow, glittering drifts, as if catching and holding whatever faint light penetrates so deep — like snow made of ground glass. It's a place where the world still moves, still changes, still grows — a dynamic, active environment — yet it is eerily empty of any visible animal life. The loud, constant sound is the only sign of persistent energy, the only vibration stirring the cold aquatic air.
