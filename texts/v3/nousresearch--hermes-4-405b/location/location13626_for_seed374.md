The sound reaches you first—an all-encompassing, roaring drone that saturates the very air, inescapable and omnipresent. Even before the place itself reveals its nature, the noise presses in, set at a level that doesn't vary, hour upon hour. Pitches seem to swirl and morph within it—grinding, rhythmic hammering, a pervasive wet sizzle—but the overall pressure is absolutely stable, a permanent condition. It fills every space, a constant assault on the ears, wearying but unchanging.

Gradually, your eyes adjust to the dim light that barely illuminates the world. A low haze hangs in the air, diffusing what little light there is into a murky twilight that seems perpetual. The landscape is both vast and constructed—an immense, sprawling settlement, its structures built low to the ground, covering land that extends far beyond the limits of sight in every direction. The air is thick with moisture. Puddles of water gather in every hollow, in the angles between structures, in the ruts of pathways. The ground is muddy and thick, with no soil to speak of, only mineral sludge. It's as if the land is perpetually recovering from heavy rain, perpetually on the verge of becoming a marsh. The air is very warm.

The structures here are simple—crude walls built of wet, freshly-dug stone, timber frames made from rough-cut logs still oozing sap, rammed earth, piled rubble. They are packed close together, almost touching, forming narrow alleyways and small courtyards. There are no open spaces, no parks, no gardens. The building is intense, ongoing, and yet primitive. Everything feels newly constructed yet already decaying in the damp air. There is no plant life here, and that absence feels active. No moss, no grass, no weeds, not even the suggestion of fungal growth. It is a world of mineral and worked material alone.

The ground is raw geology. It is plain to see where the earth has been recently carved away, piled up to make foundations. But no sediment has had time to build up, no layer of soil has had time to form. The frequent water reworks everything; it guides the constant, primitive construction but prevents any kind of stable ground or erosion from taking hold. The land is in flux but the state of flux is unchanging. Structures rise out of the mud, extensions are built, old walls collapse and are quickly replaced, yet the work is always at the same primitive level. Materials are dug, carried, laid, but never advanced. Generation after generation, the same crude methods are used, the same simple structures are made.

Here and there, human figures move through the dim, noisy world. Their clothing is simple, often mud-stained. They work, they carry things, they shout to each other over the roar of the sound—all they do is build crude structures, move stone, fell timber, haul mud. The air is heavy with the scent of their sweat, of wet earth, of smoky fires. They are not alone in this world. Fauna is abundant here—rats dart in and out of the rubble piles, feral dogs prowl the alleys, tied-down livestock wait near dwellings, scavenger birds circle constantly overhead. They make their homes in the gaps in the dense construction, their lives part of the constant activity. They, too, add their sounds to the overwhelming drone—barking, creaking, the squawk of birds, the small noises of lives lived close together.

It is an environment in constant motion, and yet nothing ever changes. The construction never advances. It is perpetually on the edge of dissolution, perpetually in the process of being built again, but never finished in any larger sense. The rules of the world are simple: build, create shelter, find food, endure the noise, exist. There is no progress, only a relentless, dynamic equilibrium—the city being constantly rebuilt as fast as it decays.
