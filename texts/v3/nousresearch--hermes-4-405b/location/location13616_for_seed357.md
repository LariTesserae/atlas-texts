The land is a city, but the city is alive.

Imagine a place of cooked, sawtooth mountains rising straight and startling from a basin floor—a basin that was once, long ago, a shallow sea of the youngest, deepest blue. Though the true seas have withdrawn, the memory of them lingers, not only in the great, sharp crystals—like shattered glass mountains—encrusting the valley walls, but in the very breath of this place. At a high elevation, where the air should be thin and sharp, it instead hangs heavy, thick, weeping. It sweats great, slow tears that run down the faces of building-tall structures in rivulets, pooling at their bases and evaporating once more into the humid atmosphere.

Everything here is built—not with hands or tools, for there are none—but grown, secreted, extruded from some deep biological imperative. And what has been grown? A sprawling, turbulent cityscape, its structures hewn from what appears to be solid, living plant matter. Not treesnot vines—not in any terrestrial sense. It is as if the very air has given birth to a thousand colossal, architecturally inclined fungi, or some vast, unevolved mold has run riot across the basin, throwing up structures in humming, chaotic fervor. These plant-buildings are not the friendly trees of a childhood tale. They are humped, angular, bulbous, some rising like termite mounds cast in deep jade or striated amber, others like the buttressed roots of unimaginable banyans, but grown in reverse—thrusting upward. Some have facets like the faces of smoky quartz, others glisten with a slick of extruded, wet-looking resin.

There are no roads in this metropolis, no discernible streets; instead, alleys and vast, irregular plazas form where the living architecture does not. Some "structures" are no more than vast, laminated platforms, split and fissured with deep, shadowed crevices, where water steadily drips. Others are dense, pseudo-corals, porous and scored with myriad holes. Tall, wavering towers twist impossibly skyward, their forms hinting at constant, subtle spasms of growth, while beneath them lie the ruins of previous growths—low, crumbled mounds that are quickly being reabsorbed into the landscape, feeding the next chaotic surge. The smell is thick, chlorophyllic, and faintly fungal, underscored by the tang of ozone and something vaguely, worryingly metallic from the moist air.

It is hot here, an oven's breath held captive by the high valley walls and the moisture that cannot escape. And yet, everywhere, the wetness reigns. Weeping rocks dot the periphery of this living architecture, their mineral faces slick with condensation. Dampness beads on every surface. The air itself seems to condense upon your skin. The ground is not soil—there is no earth—but a carpet of thick, fibrous mulch, a testament to the constant destruction and renewal. In this basin, the plant-structures press up against each other, cacophonous and loud with the sounds of their own making: the groans of vast, resinous structures stretching and settling, the sudden creaks and cracks of new growth splitting old, the constant patter of heavy drips from a thousand overhead edges, and, most of all, a low, vibrant hum that seems to emanate from the very substance of this plant-built world. This is the sound of rapid, turbulent creation, of a city that is never still, but always pushing itself skyward in frantic competition, even as parts of it collapse back into the primal soup from which it endlessly rises.

There are no animals. Only the plants, and this fierce, hot, wet, dynamic city of theirs, restless and strange, shouting its existence into the heavy, dripping air. It is a place of immense, chaotic vitality.
