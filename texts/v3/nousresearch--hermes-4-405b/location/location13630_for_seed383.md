The Trundlevast stretches before you, an underground city carved into an ancient, water-sculpted cavern. It is a realm of cool, damp air and perpetual soft light emanating from vast, bioluminescent fungal forests festooning the cavern walls. But the dominant feature is the city itself: not a place of living residents, but of perpetually rebuilding, self-organizing structures. The Trundlevast is built not of stone or metal, but of a slow-moving, semi-liquid mineral slurry.

Think of antlion traps – but on the scale of an entire cityscape. Toroidal rings of digger-machines, resembling colossal millipedes, are constantly carving downward into the stone, expanding the cavern. These rings, each dozens of stories tall, are in constant motion, creating ever-deeper circular excavation pits. Their movement generates a deafening, low-frequency hum, nearly subsonic, punctuated by the constant crumbling of bedrock and the grinding of wheels. This is the dominant sound of the Trundlevast, a purposeful cacophony that limits spoken conversation to shouts.

In the center of these pits, smoothly sculpted arterial channels direct the pulverized stone and minerals excavated by the rings. Mixed with water from deep aquifers and sculpted by fields of quick-growing, feathery crystalline structures, this slurry becomes the primary construction material of the city.

It is extruded by a second class of machines: the architects. Crab-like in form and reminiscent of breaching whales erupting from the ocean surface, these machines rise frequently from the largest slurry channels, extruding molten mineral paste that freezes instantly into new, temporary dwellings and infrastructure. These extruded buildings are remarkably abundant, allocated to the Trundlevast's constantly wandering inhabitants: a diverse menagerie of creatures that seem to have their origins in a dozen different eras and ecosystems, from burrowing feather-rats to six-legged reptiles to bellowing, shaggy humanoids. They are also tiresomely abundant, demanding the frequent construction of new spaces.

Buildings here are strictly utilitarian and not made to last. Each narrow, cell-like dwelling is clearly made to match one creature, one life. In most settlements, you have a bedroom, a kitchen, a street to walk down – but not here. The concept of owned, private space is secondary to survival. And why? Because the slurry slowly loses cohesion as its energy fades. Its consistency is only maintained by a weak energy field, replenished by natural radiation in the rock, but that field is finite. It simply doesn't last. The mineral paste eventually dries, cracks, and crumbles, sagging back into the channels to be reprocessed. A hundred years, maybe two – and the dwelling collapses, ensuring a relentless churn of construction and destruction.

There are unknown depths both above and below – tunnels leading up to ancient, collapsed mine shafts; fissures opening into deeper, wetter caverns. Spores and mycelial threads from the fungal forests catch on the moist cavern breeze, sometimes lighting on walls or broken stones, seeding bright new constellations of slowly shifting, organic constellations. Water drips everywhere, slowly carving the cavern larger still, coalescing into pools and rivulets that tell stories of even older excursions into dryness. This endless building and unbuilding creates a landscape of shifting, temporary architecture, perpetually sculpted by gigantic, tireless machines.
