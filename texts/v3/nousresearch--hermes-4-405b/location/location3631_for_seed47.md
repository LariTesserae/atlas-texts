The Sky Reefs hang at the edge of a planetary system, a vast and ethereal archipelago adrift in the void. Each "reef" is a colossal shard of impossibly light, glass-like rock — a mineral forged in the cooled breath of a dead star — kilometers tall and wide, with eroded faces of near-flattened plains that stretch into the distance, scarred by time into patterns like fossilized ripples. They catch the light of a distant sun and scatter it through their crystalline structures, creating faint, ever-shifting auroras in the profound silence between them. The only sound is a deep, almost subsonic hum — a cosmic string vibrating, or the hum of the reefs' own cold cores.

Here, water is absent, replaced by strange superfluids that seep from subsurface chambers and pool in shielded crevasses. These pools resemble quicksilver or liquid light, impossible for terrestrial life but harboring ecosystems beyond modern understanding. On the highest, wind-exposed surfaces, the only "vegetation" is cracked lichen or hardy, crystalline moss that clings to the vertical cliffs, barely visible from afar as a faint discoloration.

A divine wind tears through the highest elevations with relentless force — the solar wind given physical substance. It weaves between the shards, sculpting them with the patience of eons. "Weather" here is not rain or snow but the sudden X-ray flares and charged particle storms that sweep through the system, bathing the reefs in harmless, ghostly light. During these brief, silent storms, the strange vegetation pulses with bioluminescence, casting faint, eerie radiance over the plains.

The few fauna are solitary, elusive, and adapted for the emptiness. Enormous, near-invisible, whale-like filter feeders drift through the void between reefs, feeding on dust and ionized gas. Occasionally, smaller creatures that resemble living kites or crystalline bats are glimpsed riding the currents of the divine wind.

Scattered across the Sky Reefs are relics older than any star: smooth, curve-edged artifacts half-buried in the crystalline soil. No one remembers who built them. Were they machinery? Tombs? Temples? Their materials defy corrosion. Their shapes are meaningless, yet full of mystery — a message in a language unspoken for a thousand millennia.

The tech that built the relics — and the reefs — is beyond modern physics. It bends the logic of the universe. The reefs' orbits are too complex, their substance too light, their age too vast. They are a paradox suspended between being and non-being. It is whispered that the relics can open doors in space-time, and that the divine wind is a sentient solar flare sent to guard them from misuse. The truth is beyond knowing.

This world is ancient, silent, and impossibly vast. It is an empty monument to the dawn of time. To stand here is to hear the birth-cry of the cosmos and the long, slow sigh of its eventual end.
