The landscape is an enigma of damp stone under faint light, of roaring silence, of vibrations in stillness. It’s entirely artificial, constructed by unknown hands—a toadstool ring at a forgotten world scale. Everything is here; nothing is not made. No basalt, no sandstone—cut rocks as damp as the idea of rain, walls seeping slow water like a slow secret told. But the builders left no writing. They left no plants bigger than moss. They left only the cathedral of rock that feels like a room, a space no bigger than a broad, empty barn—and a teeming, sparse soundscape.

This is a hollow grove of gargantuan standing stones too ancient to be natural but less ruined than the hills. Their forms, weathered-down dolmens and menhirs, are smoothed by ages in this cool, damp air. They lean against the wind and each other as if to shut out the sky—ceilinged by their own vast capitals in a small, contained clearing with boundaries marked by stone.

Water gleams everywhere, dripping or sliding over the constructed surfaces. It runs in miniature channels carved by centuries of erosion, assembling islands of dry stone. Water near the surface is not a threat of flooding; it is part of the settled form of the place—weeping rocks and dew forming on high, dark walls. The air is cool—barely chilly, condensing in small places—a damp chill that gets in your bones and warns of fever.

The ground is level, constructed at some low elevation—a hollow in the land—and only here, in this small area, is anything built to this degree. Inside this bounded space, the hand of the builder is absolute; outside, beyond the ring, nothing but a gray-blind wall blocks the view. There are no blankets of grass or broad-leafed ferns, but sparse, dark moss grows in crevices, a light fuzz along the lower edges of the megaliths. Above, in the dimly lit and shadowed parts, small, isolated plants with bitter, tough leaves emerge from pocket soils in the stone walls.

Light comes from an unseen source—if it comes at all—a gray-shadowed, bruise-toned dimness that gives little hint of true color. It gives the rocks a sense of depth, of worn softness, of dark gray and black water stains.

There is no sight of sun or stars through the giant stone ceiling, but the wet ground reflects every echo. A constant dripping from above falls like a rain that takes minutes to drop—no net gain, no net loss—water held by the slow suck of air, of moss, of time. The sound is a loud, insistent, wet hum that grows. Sometimes a new trickle starts. Sometimes a rock cracks and shifts—the noise is louder than the drip, a noise from the earth, from water, from no animal—but the sound is there, ever-present, wet, cool, dim, and, by itself, alive.

Inhabitants are not hidden or shy. They writhe in the damp air, in shadows—a shifting fog of life, plankton-sized and soft-bodied, no single one alive for long. Their life is a frenzied pulse, a flush of tiny bodies that rain from above in wet falls to die in startling numbers among the rocks or scuttle along dripping walls, feeding on what little organic matter the sparse plants and mosses produce, and feeding each other. The fauna is abundant, ephemeral, a skittering, living mist in the hollow stone room.

The made environment leaves no wild room, no natural ground. Every drop of water, every patch of moss, appears part of the same slow cycle, the same primitive machinery. The vegetation and the sparse soil pockets are part of the builder’s vanished plan. The rules of the place are not written; they are felt in the cool, seeping air. The grove lets out no secret.
