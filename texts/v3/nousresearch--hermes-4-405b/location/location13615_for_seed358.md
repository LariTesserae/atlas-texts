The descent is a slow, dripping affair. One is always going down here, down into a subterranean that is more alive than imaginable. The air, perpetually warm and close, condenses the moment it touches the exposed geology; moisture beads on stone, trickles and drips into unseen channels. The ground weeps, wet and slick with the constant seeping of ground water. The light, where it can find purchase, is dim — splitting through narrow faults in the rock above or the occasional phosphorescence of certain stone formations. The stone itself is raw, emerging, so new to the business of being that it seams to breathe and shift.

This cave system is vast, stretching beyond the immediate reach of sight. The main cavern, a monstrous hollow carved by uncounted eons of patient water, is densely packed. Every surface is alive. Thick colonies of bioluminescent fungi cling to the walls, their pale, dappled light feebly illuminating the scene. Strange vegetation, sparse but tenacious, clings to any niche or outcrop. Condensation drips from mosses and ferns, water trickling down every crevice.

This is an ecosystem both raw and incredibly abundant. The fauna is dense, almost overwhelming. The air is thunderous. It’s a landscape saturated with life and the sounds of engines and industry sometimes invading the soundscape. Insects hum in the thousands: chirps, buzzes, whines, a rainforest orchestra confined and magnified by the stone. Every now and again, the sounds of mechanisms and machinery can be heard, faint echoes of the world above. The sound here is a constant companion, a palpable pressure against the eardrums.

The few artifacts left behind by the builders are tiny and scattered — a rusted pipe, half-swallowed in stone; a crumbling wall in the distance. The sound here is a constant, deafening symphony, the ceaseless hum and roar of the world above bleeding in through narrow fissures and unseen passages. The world is in constant flux, but down here change is slow. You are here, and you must adapt.
