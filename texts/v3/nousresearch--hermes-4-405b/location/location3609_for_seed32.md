The landscape is a pocket-world contained within the hollow core of an ancient, elephant-sized geode—a natural void in a world otherwise lacking water and significant life. This chamber, no larger than a theater's stage, exists in perpetual, cool equilibrium.

Every surface is constructed, the interior fully paved in interlocking plates of smooth, slate-gray and soft violet minerals that fit together like jigsaw puzzle pieces. Veins of faintly luminous crystal run through these plates, and across every inch of wall, floor, and arched ceiling clings a dense coverage of hand-sized, glowing fungal discs. They are packed so densely that no substrate remains visible—all is fungal skin, a continuous mosaic of discs. The discs resemble thick, porous stones, pallid beige or deep black, emitting a soft, cold, slightly greenish-white light that fills the chamber with a cool, calcium-bright luminance.

In the low, cool air, a constant rushing roar emanates from hidden crevices, a sound like wind tearing through deep passages. Air sighs and wheezes from fissures between the mineral plates, continuously circulating. The atmosphere is dry, carrying a scent of old dust and wet cave walls, spiced with an electric, graphite-like sharpness.

The only sign of life here are a few bioluminescent, fern-like growths clustered near the roaring vents, their sparse fronds shedding green light. Their simple forms suggest nature undisturbed by tools or technology.

This hidden world exists in a state of established erosion and balance, unchanging for eons, a dry and desolate grotto inside a stone.
