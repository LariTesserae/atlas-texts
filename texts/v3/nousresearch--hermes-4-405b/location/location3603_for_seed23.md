The Highpool Crags are a paradox of motion and stillness, sound and silence. They rise into a brittle, sun-glared atmosphere—cold enough to sting the skin even in the perpetual daylight, yet saturated with moisture. An ever-present mist writhes through the peaks, condensing on ancient, ice-rimed stones and weeping down their faces in a thousand glittering rivulets. It's a high, weathered place, old enough to have settled into its own forms: great monoliths like the bones of the world, their surfaces pitted and grooved by countless ages.

Despite its elevation, the crags are wet. High-altitude bogs and moss-choked pools edge the rock faces. Ice forms constantly but doesn't stay long; it creeps in lacy fingers across stone, only to melt in the glare or shatter under the crags' strange, turbulent dance. Water is everywhere—dripping, pooling, slicking the rock—but it's all sharp with cold. Its noise, however, is strangely muted. The whole place is a symphony of near-silence: dripping water, the hollow clink of stone against stone, the far-off cries of something like wind tearing through distant passes. Yet there's an invisible hum, like the stones themselves are vibrating at a pitch just below hearing.

What little vegetation exists here is sparse and tenacious. Lichens etch the stones in patterns like old scars or faded script. In crevices sheltered from the worst light, pale mosses cling. And everywhere, the ice-vines—strange, semi-sentient growths that visibly pulse with the crags' inner energy. They twist up the rock faces, their translucent surfaces threaded with channels of rapidly flowing water. They seem almost to breathe, thickening and thinning in a slow, even rhythm.

But the most remarkable feature is the city. Not a city as one might expect—it's built into the living rock, structures carved from the stone itself. Interlocking balconies, spiraling staircases, arched bridges and hollowed-out chambers. All of it bears the marks of primitive tools—rough-hewn edges, uneven surfaces—but also a sort of organic complexity that speaks of deep instinct or age-old tradition. This "urban" density is confined to a single sprawling structure, a settlement scaled like a cathedral or a vast hive. Within its boundaries, however, it's dense. Passages wind through solid rock, chambers stack and interconnect, the whole creation layered like the strata of the mountain.

No beings are visible. Yet their presence is palpable in the intricate carvings that adorn every surface—strange sigils, figures with too many limbs or none at all, leering faces that might be masks or might be the rock dreaming of life. The air hums with their absence. And the whole edifice is in constant, chaotic motion. Stone grinds against stone as sections of wall bulge outward or sink inward; bridges twist like sinews, realigning their connections; stairways ripple and flex like muscles. It's a slow, continuous reshaping, the architecture in a state of perpetual, turbulent becoming. Cracks spread and seal themselves; entire chambers dissolve while new ones crystallize from dripping stone.

The fauna, too, is abundant—but entirely mineral. Stone frogs with jeweled eyes cling to the weeping walls, moving with ponderous, almost imperceptible sloth. Crystalline spiders drift on the icy winds, spinning webs between the shifting structures that flash like glass in the perpetual sun. And deeper in the crevices, great slow-worms of living rock undulate, their bodies made of the same stuff as the crags.

The rules here are strange. Gravity seems fluid; staircases climb at impossible angles, water flows upward as often as down. The light, though bright, is not quite solar. It has a prismatic quality, refracting off the ice and wet stone to drench the crags in permanent, shifting rainbows. There is no decay—only constant transformation, the city and the mountain becoming one another in an endless, silent dance.
