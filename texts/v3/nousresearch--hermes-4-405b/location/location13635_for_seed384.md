The cold bites instantly, even through the suit. It's a deep, penetrating chill that I feel in the back of my teeth when I draw a slow breath through the insulated mask. But the real sensation isn't the cold – it's the *pressure*. It presses in from all sides, a constant, leaden embrace that never lets up. This is Deepwater Falls, a sunken place.

We’re suspended in near-total darkness, the beams of our headlamps catching only needles of silt drifting like slow-motion stars in the inky void. Occasionally, the light glints off the edge of a colossal, shadowy structure – the outer bastions of Old Deepwater. The city wasn't always here, wasn't always *this*. Legends persisted of a thriving metropolis before the Great Submergence, a place of industry and light that hubristically clung to a precarious coast. Then the waters came, not with a catastrophic rush, but with a slow, inevitable, centuries-long creep. Deepwater didn't drown so much as it was subsumed, its foundations yielding to the ocean’s patient claim.

Now, it’s a ghost city, submerged under nearly two thousand feet of crushing, black water, a monument to erosion’s dogged persistence over human ambition. The water is everywhere, thick and viscous with the cold. You can almost feel its weight in your lungs, taste its ancient, metallic tang even through the life support.

The air – what little there might be trapped in the few still-somewhat-sealed structures – is an afterthought. This is the realm of liquid. Puddles don't form; everything is perpetually, utterly soaked. Every surface weeps moisture. The dominant atmospheric presence is the subtle drift of fine sediment, stirred by our passage or by the ponderous, almost imperceptible currents that shift through these drowned canyons.

Scattered about, defiant in their sparseness, are the Deepwater Ghostlights – or perhaps, ‘Deepwater Graves’. These are sparse, globular flora, each about the size of a clenched fist, their translucent bodies glowing with a faint, internal, bioluminescent blue or green. They’re the only vegetation that survives down here, living grave markers in the endless night, clinging desperately to the skeletal remains of iron balconies or the sides of crumbling concrete facades.

The cityscape itself is a shadow-play of industrial design gone feral. The architecture is definitively human – or, it was. You can still make out the gridded streets if you follow the lines of less-dense rubble. Corroded rebar claws skyward like the ribs of some gargantuan beast, and crumbling concrete walls lean at precarious angles. But nature, or whatever passes for it at these depths, has been busy. Strange, dark mineral concretions cling to every surface, and in some areas, the metallic structures are so densely furred with them they look almost like geological formations in their own right – the city in the long process of becoming part of the ocean floor.

These concretions are dense, layered upon each other, creating a tight, intricate tapestry that obscures what lies beneath. They jut out in fantastical, almost organic shapes – spires, shelves, bulbous protrusions. Predominantly a deep, bruised purple, they are shot through with veins of black and a startling, eerie turquoise that seems to drink in the faint light of our lamps. Despite their strangely beautiful appearance, their surfaces are rough, almost like pumice, and scrape against the suit if you brush too close.

Deepwater Falls hums with a quiet, contained *process*. It’s not the vibrant hum of a living ecosystem, but the slow, grinding sound of decay and cold, deliberate repurposing. The dominant feeling is of immense, indifferent activity. Water constantly seeps through cracks in the ancient structures, depositing layers of mineral and silt. Some of the larger buildings are visibly shedding their outer layers, flakes of rusted metal and powdered concrete drifting downwards like a constant, slow-motion snow. There's the ever-present, almost inaudible creaks and groans of the decaying superstructures settling, shifting under the relentless pressure of the water column above. The city isn't dead; it's being digested, particle by particle.

The silence here is profound, broken only by the rhythmic sigh of our rebreathers, the occasional static crackle in the comms, and those deep, unsettling groans of the city. Sound doesn't carry far in this medium. Every noise is instantly swallowed, dampened, leaving only a vast, echoing quiet that presses in as insistently as the water. It's less a place of peace and more one of eerie, stifling isolation. You can hear your own pulse, thudding in your ears.

There are inhabitants, after a fashion. The Scuttlers are elusive, rarely seen. We catch glimpses: spindly, multi-limbed shadows, perhaps the size of a large house cat, adorned with plates of chitin that reflect our lights with a dull, wet gleam before they vanish into crevices or wreaths of dense mineral growth. They skitter across vertical surfaces with an unnerving agility, always at the edge of our vision. Their intelligence is impossible to gauge, their forms too alien, their behavior too secretive. But they are undeniably *there*.

Then there are the largest presences: slow, ponderous shapes that drift with the deep currents in the wider avenues of the city.
These are not animals as we know them, nor are they simple geological oddities. They are colossal, roughly ovoid structures, easily the size of small blimps, composed of countless layers of those purple and turquoise mineral deposits, accreting around some unseen core. Internally, they appear to be almost hollow, crisscrossed with large chambers and intricate tunnels, almost like natural sponges or honeycombs, but these internal spaces aren't empty. They are filled with a matrix of fine, silvery filaments, so densely packed they almost seem like a solid mass, which shimmer faintly with a cold, internal light.

They drift, bobbing gently, guided by unseen currents or some internal mechanism. Occasionally, one of these giants will discharge a dense cloud of minute particles from several orifices along its mineralized hide, like a slow, ponderous exhalation of what looks like fine, dark sand or spores. What these particles are, what purpose they serve, remains a mystery. Are they spores, eggs, waste? Are these creatures growing, dying, or simply *processing* their environment? They drift past shattered windows and through broken archways, silent, enigmatic spectators to the city’s decay. They seem more like geological processes made mobile than living beings, a kind of slow, cold, alien life – or unlife – that belongs utterly to this submerged world.

Deepwater Falls is a cathedral, a mausoleum, a foundry, and an ecosystem all at once, small in its confined volume but immense in its implications. It’s not a place you simply enter; it *absorbs* you. The overwhelming sense is of an environment where human presence is an anomaly, a brief intrusion into a place that has found its own terrible, alien equilibrium, far from the sun, far from the world of air and light. It is a place where the rules of the world above have been entirely rewritten by pressure, darkness, and cold.
