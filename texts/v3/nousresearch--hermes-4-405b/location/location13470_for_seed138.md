The canyon floor sprawled beneath us, a vast expanse of shattered black rock under a perpetually overcast sky. Rain fell constantly in thin, wind-slanted veils, slicking the angular stones until they shone feebly in the meager light. Here at the bottom of the world, the air remained iron-cold, the kind of chill that seeped through layers of wool and into the marrow. Steam rose in places where fumaroles vented from hidden chambers below, blending with the drifting mists to create ephemeral curtains across the desolate panorama. Little grew in this stony labyrinth—only occasional patches of frost-resistant lichen clung to sheltered crevices, their pale greens almost lost to the gloom.

A single structure broke the bleak expanse: a city of black basalt buildings clustered around a series of geothermal vents. The architecture leaned toward brutal efficiency—blocky towers pierced by narrow slits, interconnected by networks of ridged stone pipes that carried steam from the depths. High stone walls surrounded the settlement, their angular shapes repeated in the jagged peaks that loomed above. At the city's heart squatted a massive central edifice whose purpose could only be guessed at from its thick walls and the deep thrumming that resonated through the rock underfoot.

Moderate winds sighed through the canyon, carrying the constant hiss of rain against stone and the low, rhythmic thumping of industrial machinery from within the town's walls. Inside those walls awaited all the expected signs of paleo-industrial society—narrow streets slick with runoff, impassive citizens gathered in sparse groups, the ever-present tang of wet stone and escaping steam on every chilly breath. Through the haze one could make out smaller structures near the city, long low sheds housing generators powered by harnessed geothermal energy.

The surrounding peaks pressed close, their sharp summits lost in the clouds above. Somewhere beyond them the canyon must have found its way to warmer lands, but here in this enclosed world, winter held eternal sway. Tube worms flourished at the warm edges of the vents, their casings glowing with faint bioluminescence in the perpetual twilight. The only other life appeared in the form of small, pale birds that nested in the crags above, their lonely cries echoing down through the thin air to mix with the sounds of the city below.

Low stone walls marked the boundaries of the town proper, the area within them rigidly ordered into blocks of four-story buildings connected by networks of pipes. Industry appeared limited to the vent fields outside the walls—a series of pipelines and steam-driven generators clustered near the largest openings. The canyon walls themselves bore signs of long-ago fracture and collapse, suggesting some past cataclysm that had exposed the geothermal labyrinth below. And through it all, day and night and every hour in between, the rain never ceased its gentle, cold descent.
