The glacier hums.

You arrive above a cloud-buried spine of peaks. Sunlight claws through the thinning air, sharp and raw. The valley below is fjord-cut and fjord-deep: a slow-motion wound in the planet’s flesh, U-shaped and rubbed raw by millennia of ice. That glacier still clings to the valley’s headwall, a hanging tongue of pale blue ice groaning between black pillars of rock. The scale is modest by the standards of high places, the valley no longer than a morning’s trek from end to end, but surrounded by biting peaks that disappear into clouds. You could almost mistake it for untouched tundra at first glance—scattered stones, coarse gravel, a few patches of moss and low, wind-whipped cushion plants huddled between boulders. But the emptiness has a weight here. The valley feels scooped out, hollowed, each surface smoothed by time’s slow grind. Footsteps echo strangely in the thin air, as if transmitted by rock itself.

Dark green stains creep along the walls where seeps glisten. In places, the valley widens into shallow basins where relict lakes pool, fed by trickles from the walls above. Reeds grow at their margins, and fingerling trout flash like splinters of mica through the ice-cold water. Jagged moraines cut across the valley, their crests littered with boulders dropped by the glacier in retreat. Some are enormous, house-sized blocks of granite streaked with lichen—black, orange, and pale gray—and furry green moss along their undersides where dampness clings.

The air bites with cold, a constant chill that sinks into your bones even when the sun beats down, unhindered in its brilliance. The sky overhead is huge and depthless, a blue so pale it seems almost white. When storms roll in, clouds drag across the peaks and the valley dissolves into mist and thunder. Rain lashes the rock, condensation beads on every surface, and temporary waterfalls cascade down the cliffs, roaring until they vanish as abruptly as they appeared.

The dominant sound, though, is the wind. It screams through the peaks with deafening force. It keensthe—the—the—the—the—the—it screams through the peaks with such ferocity that the world seems to tremble. The frequency of the howl rises and falls, a mournful, unrelenting song that grinds against the eardrums. Scree shifts in endless restless cascades down slopes. The river chuckles and gurgles, its voice sometimes drowned by the wind, sometimes rising in its own roar when heavy melt surges through.

Up here, the world is both empty and densely alive. Rock rabbits freeze among the scree, gray fur blending into the stones. Golden eagles ride the thermals, their shadows rippling over the gravelflats. Marmots whistle alarms from sun-warmed boulders; their voices carry across the emptiness, sharp and clear between the wind’s onslaughts. A few mountain sheep pick their way along the higher slopes, phantom shapes drifting between outcrops. In the deeper pools of the river, otters leave wet-slick trails over the rocks and slide beneath the surface in search of fish.

The only constant is stone. Gray and black and veined with quartz, sculpted by weather into stark ridges, smooth faces, and jumbled falls of scree. There are no trees—only low, wind-tortured shrubs along the banks of the largest streams, their branches twisted into knots and gnarled by the relentless gales. Nothing much taller than your ankle grows here. Even the hardiest lichens cling desperately to crevices, surviving on whatever moisture the mists and occasional rains provide.

At the valley’s head, the glacier looms. It creaks and groans, sending occasional rumbles through the valley—a deep, unsettling vibration that rises through the soles of your feet. Icefalls tumble down its face in slow, grinding avalanches. The glacier has not changed in living memory. Its margins are a chaos of broken seracs and crevasses, the ice striated with gravel and grit from decades—centuries—of grinding against bedrock. It is a monument to slowness, to forces that operate on timescales beyond human reckoning.

This is a place of raw stone and wind, of fleeting water and tenacious life. It is bright and cold and ageless, a high basin scoured by ice and etched with the passage of eons. There are no roads here, no sign of human intrusion beyond the most tentative—a scattering of carefully piled cairns left by wandering hunters, following paths worn by generations of hoofed mammals through perpetual wind and scraping sky. The rules here are simple and unforgiving: endure the wind, find the hidden water, trust the stone beneath your feet. Everything else is weather.
