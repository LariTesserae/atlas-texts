The air here tastes of damp stone and chlorophyll. We stand on an elevated plateau, ringed by distant mountains whose peaks are lost in perpetual mist. This is a high, lush place—a world constructed layer upon layer, hourglasses of slow time accumulating in every surface.

A vast citadel sprawls across the open expanse, or rather, it *is* the expanse. Buildings extend in all directions, a dense, interlocking puzzle of ancient architecture. It is a city that never knew tool or mortar, for every stone, every archway, every spiraling turret and vaulted corridor is grown. Countless generations of great, slow‐moving creatures have secreted these minerals, their biology patient as continents—a calm, gradual excretion accumulating over millennia into arched bridges, hollow halls, and buttressed terraces. There are no seams, no joins that did not emerge from life itself. The stones are massive, yet each fits perfectly against its neighbors, surfaces worn smooth by rain and time.

Foliage explodes from every crevice. Dense mosses coat the lower walls in thick, velvety sheets. Vines as thick as human torsos climb the towers, their leaves broad and waxy, dripping with condensation. Orchids and ferns spill from windows and ledges. In the dim light—washing everything in deep greens and umbers and dusky grays—every surface breathes moisture. Tiny droplets gather on the tips of leaves, on the limbs of epiphytes, on the cold, dark stone. Air roots hang in veils, drinking from the humid air. Everywhere, water beads and trickles; small, clear pools gather in stone depressions, fed by constant seepage from above. The sky—where visible between the uppermost towers and the canopy—is often a low, bruised ceiling of cloud, promising rain. Mists rise from the pockets of deeper shade.

But there is no quiet. A deafening chorus resonates through the citadel, a vibration felt in the bones. It is not the roar of wind or storm, but the overwhelming sound of life. Millions of creatures rustle in the vegetation, hum in the undergrowth, buzz in the air. Insects chirp and click. The air thrums with wings—the heavy pulse of beetles, the whine of smaller fliers, the tread of unseen feet on stone and vine. Frogs croak from wet crevices; their calls echo down labyrinthine passageways. Birds with bright plumage flit through the canopy, adding sharp cries to the din. Countless things—chitinous, feathered, scaled, furred—shift and crawl. The air is thick with the scent of green life and wet stone, the musk of small bodies. This is a city built for life, by life, and it teems with its own overwhelming fauna. The darkness itself seems alive with their chorus.

Time here moves in long, slow pulses. Water drips, stone weathers, beings live and die across durations that dwarf a human lifespan. The citadel is a single, ancient, breathing entity, lush and dark and overwhelmingly alive.
