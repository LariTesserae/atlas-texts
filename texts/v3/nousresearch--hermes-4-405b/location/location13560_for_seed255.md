The City of Condensation hums and scrapes in the damp dark. Around you rises a metropolis built from raw, wet stone—everywhere the marks of crude tools, the chiseled gloom of a place never touched by true daylight. Streets ribbon between structures of cold, uneven rock, worn smooth in low places by perpetual runnels of water that weep from fissures above or gather in shallow pools where the pavement dips. There's no soil, no plants, only the mineral tang of wet basalt and granite, the fungal reek of slow, pervasive damp.

Rain falls intermittently, a soaking mist that beads on every surface before trickling down to join the endless flow—a city perched on the cusp of becoming, its foundations emerging from some primal, half-formed geology. The air moves in slow, chilly currents, carrying the clang and clatter of construction from distant quarters—a low, trembling din beneath your feet, as though the whole settlement were grinding itself deeper into the bedrock. Water sloshes underfoot, drips echo in cavernous alleyways, and everywhere the sound of metal scraping stone, of hammers striking, reverberates through the low-slung architecture.

Life is sparse here, and strange. Small, pale creatures skitter between the wet stones—basic, almost prototypical forms moving with hesitant purpose, as though unsure of their own existence. They avoid the clumping, dense structures where the city's builders live and work, their lights feeble and infrequent in the gloom. It is a place entirely made, yet raw and unsettled—a testament to primitive labor in a world still shaking off its embryonic chill, where the darkness is thick and the sound of creation never stops.
