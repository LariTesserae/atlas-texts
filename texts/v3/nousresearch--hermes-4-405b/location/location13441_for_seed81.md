The city exists in a contradiction: a vast, dim, high-altitude basin where the air feels thick with latent heat and unshed water. It’s perpetually near rain, yet also fever-hot, as if the ground bakes the moisture into a clinging vapor that condenses on every surface. The air leaves a metallic, mineral tang on the tongue. This place is settled, weathered by eons of geological patience and the relentless drip of water, yet it feels imposing, almost hostile in its grandeur. Here, almost nothing grows; the biology here is something else entirely.

You stand on the edge of an open region—a valley cradling something between an ancient settlement and an industrial necropolis. At first glance, the city appears empty. Streets stretch into the gloom, paved with dark, igneous stone worn glassy by time and water. Immense structures rise like geological features: factories with towering smokestacks that cough only occasional, tired breaths of steam; residential blocks, their windows dark and hollow; bridges that span chasms filled not with rivers but with slow-moving, oily condensation. The sound here is a deep and resonant quiet, broken only by the groan of wind through fissures in the rock, the distant thrum of machinery buried below, and the ever-present drip of water echoing off stone. A low, hot wind sighs through the avenues, carrying the scent of wet machine oil and warm rust.

The city is built atop a massive geothermal engine. Vents and fissures in the rock release blasts of heat and moisture that contribute to the perpetual dampness. The stone here is warm to the touch, even when the air is cool. It’s a place built to harness steam and fire, yet the dominant technology is harnessed geothermal energy: pipes and conduits snake across the ground, beneath streets, and up the sides of buildings, channeling steam and hot water. The tech is industrial, but it’s old—weathered, patched, and repurposed over millennia. Gears and pistons are visible, turning slowly behind reinforced glass or open to the humid air; engines grind with weary patience; everything is made of thick, heat-resistant materials.

The absence of plant life is absolute. What little flora exists is mineral: crystalline growths in deep fissures where mineral-rich water seeps, or patches of strange, glassy lichen that feed on the radiant heat. There are no trees, no grasses, no familiar shapes. Animal life, too, is sparse and strange: pale, blind insects scuttle in the wet shadows, and the occasional clatter of something armored and multi-legged echoes through the empty buildings. Perhaps more highly adapted creatures move through the deeper, even hotter levels below, but on the surface, the fauna is rare and fleeting.

The weirdness of this place is deep, in the stone itself. The buildings are constructed with a scale that feels slightly wrong—doors too tall, steps too shallow—as if built for beings subtly different from humans. In places, the city seems to merge with the natural rock: towers rise seamlessly from cliffs, and lower walls are carved directly from the basin’s stone. The sound of dripping water is everywhere, accumulating in channels carved into the streets, pooling in vast underground cisterns, and trickling down the faces of buildings. Sometimes, the water collects in basins where it shimmers with unnatural heat, releasing plumes of steam that catch the dim light.

The light here is dim, filtered as if through layers of volcanic glass or thick, perpetual cloud. It casts everything in shades of grey and blue, with occasional muted glows from inside the buildings—perhaps bioluminescent fungi, or the soft light from the city’s eternal machinery. Shadows are deep and long, and what little wind stirs the humid air carries the scent of hot stone and the faint tang of machine oil. The world feels in constant, slow motion: steam seeps from vents, pipes hiss with the movement of superheated water, and all around you, the slow processes of erosion and accumulation continue their patient work. This is a place of powerful forces in near-equilibrium, a landscape that hums with deep, ancient energy.

Somewhere beyond the immediate, the basin extends toward hazy horizons where the city’s structures give way to raw cliff faces, steaming geysers, and the raw, unworked stone of the high-altitude world. Despite the open scale, the space between landmarks feels immense, empty, and haunting. It’s a place built and abandoned, repurposed by geothermal forces, watery decay, and silence.
