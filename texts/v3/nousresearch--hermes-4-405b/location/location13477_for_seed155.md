The place is a city — but not a city of stone or steel. It's a city grown rather than built, a metropolis of living, woody tissue, stretching over a vast plateau at high elevation. It's cool here, perhaps because of that height, perhaps because of the damp air that drifts between the colossal structures. It rains sometimes, just enough to keep everything alive, just enough to leave droplets condensing on leaves and branches, but there's no great river, no sea. Just damp.

The city's buildings are mostly ancient — immense, slow-growing trees of some unknown, primordial species, millennia old and deeply eroded. Their forms have been smoothed and shaped and hollowed out by processes that must have taken centuries. Tunnels bore through colossal trunks, windows open in bark grown thin and translucent. These trees form the foundational structures, the great towers and courts. Their roots twist down into the mountain, gripping stone that must be just as ancient. They're sparsely distributed, huge dark shapes looming over vast, flat stretches of emptiness.

But this is not a dead city, or even a purely natural one. It's a weird place, full of reality breakdowns — little glitches in the edges of your vision, spaces that seem to fold into themselves or tear open into nothing. The plants that make up the city are not just plants; they're the only thing here. They form a total living mass, a pure-plant ecosystem that has become architecture. Some of them pulse and hum, blocking out other sounds with their own strange, loud song.

The gaps between the great trees are filled with other, smaller plants, a thousand species growing with sparse, balanced density, vines and mosses, leaves and bulbs, channeled and shaped by the inhabitants into walls and roofs, platforms, streets, bridges — a whole infrastructure laid over the primordial trees. This is not wild growth; it's cultivation, slow, careful, ancient urban design. Engine noises sometimes echo down the plant-lined streets, the voices of factories where machines fueled by the city's own living substance work at some scaled production. You can hear the thump of pumps, the clang of pressurized systems, occasional bursts of electrified power arcing and snapping. It’s a place of active processes, weather cycles and biological pace still in motion, even if the animal life is now rare.

It's hard to say if the city's builders are still here. Perhaps they've moved on, or perhaps this is their idea of perfection — an ecosystem in motion, a living machine perpetually reshaping itself. The place is in the process of becoming itself as you write: getting weirder, the plants more and more like buildings, the buildings more and more like plants, until even that distinction starts to break down.
