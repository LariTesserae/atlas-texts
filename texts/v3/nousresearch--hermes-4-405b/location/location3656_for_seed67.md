The sky here isn't sky—it's the Giant Ceiling, a shifting mosaic of tarnished aluminum tiles, some the size of small countries, held aloft by unseen engineering. They clank and groan but never shift enough to truly change the view. The Ceiling weeps constantly; a thin, rusty rain condenses day and night on its metal panels, draining down in innumerable rivulets and streams that crisscross every vertical surface.

The world itself is a constructed basin, impossibly vast—perhaps a continental shelf of artificial terraces and gullies, their surfaces covered in young, sharp-angled durasteel and sheet iron. Despite the air being warm, hot even, the landscape is dominated by a profound industrial stillness. Nothing grows. The only "vegetation" is a nonbiological accretion: pale, salt-like crystals that form delicate fractal blooms—like radiation patterns caught in slow crystal growth—where the condensation pools and evaporates. These mineral efflorescences frosting the edges of rivulets are the only breaks in the world's overwhelming metallic rigidity.

Water is everywhere near the surface, but never deep. It constantly trickles, drips from overhangs, pools in shallow basins of riveted metal that stretch for miles. The atmosphere is humid, warm, filled with the smell of wet iron and ozone. Puddles steam gently in the heat. The whole landscape glows under the Ceiling with a flat, even light, not quite cheerful—it only heightens the sense of overexposure, of being perpetually damp under stage lights.

This place is saturated with machines. Engines—great crane-like arms, valve-covered pistons sunk in the foundations, conveyor belts dripping condensation like bunting—are packed in near-touching, layered over and under one another. They dominate every view, a dense forest of riveted steel and greasy iron. But they rarely move. When they do—every few hours, a hiss, a puff of sour steam, a groaning pivot—it's a seismic event in the quietude. The air is alive with the hum of their idling currents, the occasional whine of straining metal, the creaks of thermal expansion.

Fauna, however, boils across every surface. The dominant creatures resemble massive, rust-colored planarian flatworms, some as large as anacondas, others the size of fingernails, or even microscopic glints in the puddles. These megaworms glide and crawl over the weeping metal landscapes, slowly sucking up condensation or gnawing at the crystal blooms. Their passage is silent. Smaller insectile things, like chrome drill-bits with crimson eyes, skitter across the metal plains in vast, coordinated herds. And above, slow, bulbous zeppelin-floaters drift like living blimps between the Ceiling and the ground, trailing long gelatinous feeding tubes that dangle into drainages and sump pools. Their numbers are overwhelming, an ever-moving tide of nonvertebrate life that shifts in endless, teeming patterns without disturbing the equilibrium of the machines. These creatures never die or age visibly. They simply are, their population maintaining a constant, dynamic churn against the static backdrop of the built world.

The whole place is both immensely strange and unyieldingly static. The rules of this world are frozen in place. The condensation falls at a constant, calculable rate. The machines hum at a fixed frequency. The flatworms glide, the floaters drift, in patterns that never deviate, never evolve. The crystal blooms appear exactly where the microclimate dictates, following laws of physics that here are absolute, unbreakable. Water flows, life teems, but no change occurs. It is a warm, wet, mechanical purgatory, a continental engine idling in perpetuity.
