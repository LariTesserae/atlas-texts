The landscape here is defined by dampness and stillness, balanced precariously within a small, self-contained hollow. This space — no larger than a cathedral's nave — exists only to harbor moisture, catching perpetual drips and weeps from its enclosing stone walls. Age has worn the rock smooth, leaving shallow basins and depressions that cradle clear water in a series of interconnected pools. These miniature reservoirs, scattered throughout the hollow in sparse, irregular patterns, form a hidden network of condensation. Between them, the ground remains bare and dry, speckled only by occasional patches of pale, water-stressed lichen that cling to life in the dim, constant light. The air itself is cool and heavy, thickened by a quiet chill that mingles with the scent of wet rock and the faint, mineral tang of ancient water. Nothing grows beyond that lichen — no vegetation breaks the monotony of stone, and no animal stirs among the stillness. Instead, this is a realm of pure, unadorned erosion; a single, low-slung basin meticulously carved by time into a place where water lingers. Although the world outside is warm, here the temperature is perpetually cool, and while distant, unseen streams murmur softly, within the hollow itself only the intermittent plink of droplets against stone punctuates an otherwise unbroken quiet. Scattered, almost imperceptible marks — perhaps the faint traces of small, long-abandoned shelters or hollowed-out niches in the rock — hint at structures once present, but now worn nearly smooth by time. In this deliberate isolation, where no winds reach and no living thing alters the balance, the landscape exists in eternal equilibrium: a timeless, self-contained sanctuary of water and stone.
