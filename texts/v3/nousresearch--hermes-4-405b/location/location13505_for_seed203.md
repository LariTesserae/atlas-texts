The landscape is a single, planet-sized cave — so vast its ceiling curves out of sight in the far distance, lost in atmospheric haze and distance. Light comes from everywhere and nowhere: crystalline veins running through the cavern walls emit a soft, pervasive glow, while enormous bioluminescent fungi cling to the stone and hang like chandeliers. The air is filled with drifting organic mist, catching the light and scattering it into soft beams that illuminate the depths. It is cold here, the air damp and still. You feel the chill in your bones — the kind of deep, penetrating cold that seeps into you and never fully leaves.

Over tens of thousands of years, this cavern formed from a slow-motion catastrophe: the collapse of a far larger cave system, a sequence of collapses each on the scale of mountain ranges. The remains are sharp and freshly exposed, cracked but not yet smoothed by erosion — crystalline structures project at jagged angles, frost-ferns of mineral deposits coat every surface, freshly broken rock glints wetly in the light. The cave is mostly empty, hollow — space within space, vast and open. In places, there seems to be no floor at all — just endless drops into darkness, swallowing light whole.

It is a plant-dominated ecosystem. Thick, rope-like vines the size of rivers hang from the cavern ceiling, plunging kilometers into the depths. They glow with an internal light, the colder the environment the brighter their bioluminescence. In places, the vines fuse together into living islands, biomes adrift in the endless space of the cavern. They support dense jungles of pale, cold-weather vegetation: slow-growing mosses, lichens, fungi, and enormous fronds. "Total plant-as-substrate" — the plants fill the cavern space, create much of its structure, and form the architecture of the place. They are the cavern, in many ways.

Rivers of meltwater cascade from cracks in the ceiling, plunging into the darkness and throwing up clouds of spray that flash to ice in mid-air. Deep in the cave's far reaches — weeks of journey away — the cavern floor is a chaotic landscape of shattered rock, enormous blocks the size of mountains tumbled against one another. They are beginning to be reclaimed by the plant life, which grows over and through the cracks. This cave is occupied — but sparsely. Scattered, small primitive villages cling to the living islands. They are simple but elegant structures assembled from carved stone and shaped plant matter, woven into intricate forms. Walkways connect islands and disturbed vegetation show where the inhabitants gather food. These are the only signs of intelligent life. Rare, shy creatures live here too — six-legged, faintly luminous things no larger than your hand, primitive insect-analogues that live among the moss and fungi. You hear the wet drip of condensation, the distant splash of water, and the rustle of wind through the hanging vines. Rarely, a cave-in or avalanche will shake the entire cavern, distant and muffled. The place seems both eternal and impermanent — poised on the brink of sudden, chaotic change.
