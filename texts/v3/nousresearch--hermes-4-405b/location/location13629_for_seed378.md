High on the shoulders of a mountain range, where the air burns thin and bright, the Glass Metropolis perches. Built from a transparent, obsidian-like material, the place glistens wetly—as if just polished by a recent downpour, or still weeping minerals from the warm rocks below. But this city has never known rain; instead, vast condensers high on the spires and hanging in great lacework sheets between structures bleed the moisture from the atmosphere itself, channeling it into intricate runnels and crystal reservoirs. It is a place of engineered damp, always shimmering with water near the surface: condensing on the spires as the hot sun beats down, evaporating in the heat, only to condense again.

No green things grow here. The terrain is a treacherous expanse of volcanic glass, long cooled into sharp, glittering peaks and treacherous ravines. It is a place of knives, of fractured sunlight. The city's materials reflect this harshness. Its towers rise like shards of black ice, edges sharp enough to draw blood. Stairs and platforms are carved directly from the mountain's glass, worn smooth over time but also perpetually slick with the dew that clings to everything. The surfaces are polished to a mirror shine, reflecting the brilliant sun until the air itself seems to hum with heat and light.

Despite the oppressive brightness, there are few sounds. The condensers hum, a low thrumming that vibrates in the bones rather than the ears. The steam vents hiss intermittently, emitting geysers of scalding mist that catch the light and obscure the upper reaches of the spires. Occasionally, a cracking or groaning can be heard as the mountains shift—a deep, resonant sound that speaks of immense pressure and slow movement beneath the urban shell.

Though plant life is absent, the fauna is abundant. Great iridescent beetles scuttle across the glass plains, their carapaces shimmering like oil slicks. Their legs make no sound on the smooth surfaces, and they move with an eerie fluidity, as if swimming through the thin air. Above the city, vast flocks of creatures reminiscent of moths drift on thermal currents, wingspans easily spanning several meters. Their bodies are crystalline, refracting the sun into kaleidoscopic patterns across their surroundings. They are silent, adding to the unnerving quiet.

The city itself was clearly built for function rather than comfort. Buildings are utilitarian, with few concessions to aesthetics. There are no signs of wear, no weathering—the structures exist in a state of equilibrium with their environment, maintained by some unseen mechanism. Pipes snake along the ground and up the sides of edifices, steaming faintly as they transport hot water. Spires topped with condensers rise above everything, casting long, skeletal shadows that move with the sun. The architecture feels alien, as if designed by minds that found beauty in mathematical precision rather than organic curves.

This is a world where the rules are strange. The horizon shimmers with a perpetual mirage, the light refracted by the hot, wet air and the glassy surfaces. Water beads on every edge and plane, condensing from the atmosphere only to evaporate again in moments, caught in an endless cycle of phase changes. Time seems to flow differently here; the slow creep of condensation and evaporation, the languid drift of the crystalline fliers, the ceaseless thrum of machinery—all create a sense of suspended animation, of a place caught between moments.

There are no people in sight. Perhaps this metropolis was built by its current inhabitants—the fauna that thrives within its crystalline walls. Or perhaps it is a relic, a testament to a civilization long gone, now maintained by its own systems and inhabited by creatures that have adapted to its unique conditions. The isolation is palpable, the quiet profound. This is a place of stark beauty and unsettling stillness, a high glass city where water and stone and silence reign supreme.
