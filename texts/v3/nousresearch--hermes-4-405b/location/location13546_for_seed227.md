The landscape reveals itself as a kind of pocket within an immense, dark, and largely empty space. We are low, at the bottom of something vast — a cavern system, perhaps, or a crater deep enough that the sky is little more than a memory. The air here is consistently cold, a damp chill that clings and condenses. It's not wet enough for standing water to accumulate throughout, but the moisture is ever-present. Dripstones have formed over unthinkable spans of time; they are the sculptors here, evidence of slow, patient change. Water gathers in pools where it can, small hidden lagoons of cold clarity.

The emptiness of this place is profound. Vast stretches reveal nothing but the damp rock and darkness. Yet, within this void, a strange lushness persists. It’s not jungle-thick; it’s an explosion of life within the cracks and crevices. Mosses in startling greens and deep violets carpet the hollows. Liverworts. A stunted, hardy underbrush flourishes where water seeps and the rock face softens into something like soil. Pale fungi, luminous and ethereal, cling to the damp walls and fallen stones. They are the second, subtle light source, beyond a few faint constructions.

This hidden world is not entirely untouched by civilization, or something resembling it. Scattered through the caverns are simple, almost crude structures. They are not grand. Think of low sheds built from the plentiful fallen rock, draped with hides or woven fibers to keep out the worst of the damp. A few pitons, painstakingly hammered into the cave walls, bear the marks of simple metal tools. Everything is handmade, primitive. The materials are local: stone, wet-hewn wood from somewhere above or dragged in, bones. Fire pits, carefully ringed with stone to contain the embers, are dark and cold. The absence of life in the structures suggests they are long abandoned, or used only fleetingly.

There are creatures here, though they are rare. A species of pale, slow-moving lizards, their skin almost translucent, clings to the cave walls. Their movements are languid, almost imperceptible in the dim light. Their metabolism, perhaps, is tuned to this frigid environment. Occasionally, a species of bird-like creature, perhaps from far above, might be seen winging through the caverns, startled by any movement, and their occasional cry is quickly swallowed by the other sounds.

And what sounds! For this is not a silent realm. It is deafening. The roar of wind, funneled and amplified by the caverns, is a constant, a low, thundering drone that seems to vibrate in your bones. The drip and trickle of water, echoing and re-echoing off the stone, becomes a complex, layered chorus. There are groans. Booms as rocks shift, massively, in the slow erosion of the cavern system. A deep, resonant thrum from some source further in, perhaps the beating heart of the mountain itself. Any sound you make — a footstep, a breath — is instantly lost.

The ecology of this place is one of extreme specialization. Roots, from stunted shrubs, burrow deep into the thin layers of decaying moss and fungus. The light is so dim, so minimal, that everything here exists on the very edge of survival. The cold is a constant companion, making every movement stiff, every breath visible. This is a slow, patient world. Everything happens gradually: the formation of dripstone, the erosion of rock faces, the growth of the pale fungi that are the only bright sparks in the overwhelming gloom.

The rules of this world are simple. Light is precious. Sound is overwhelming. The damp chill gets into everything. Stone endures; it is the fundamental material, the canvas upon which all else is but a faint, beautiful smudge. Time feels immense. You feel contained, held within the stone.
