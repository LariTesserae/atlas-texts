The city squats on the high plain, a sparse constellation of structures — some towering, some sprawling — beneath a perpetually dark sky. There are no stars, no moons; only a faint, sourceless luminescence that emerges from the ground itself, casting long and dimmer shadows amid the buildings. The light is not bright enough to banish the darkness that reigns overhead; the city exists in a perpetual twilight, an eternal gloaming.

The ground beneath is ancient, time-flattened into a nearly featureless expanse of greyish-black rock, gritty and unforgiving. It slopes away in all directions, giving the impression of great elevation and exposure, as though the city is perched precariously at the summit of some vast, unclimbable plateau. Scattered thin layers of dust, like grey ash, drift gently across expanses of bare rock, never truly settling.

The city itself is surprisingly well-preserved, appearing almost new in its industrial grandeur, as if it were built yesterday and then abandoned in the same moment. Buildings of dark stone, steel, and glass rise at wide intervals, their surfaces smooth from the erosion of eons yet bearing no trace of the elements that would decay them; dry air, steady temperatures, and the absence of weather have gracefully aged them without destruction. The aesthetic is utilitarian and robust, hinting at functionality and endurance — observed only in the absence of function. Dark windows, some intact and others shattered (though the glass shards are oddly absent, as if they too have been incorporated into this static equilibrium), suggest vacant interiors, hollow spaces filled with silence and dust. Walkways, rail systems, and broad avenues lie empty, the lines of mechanization frozen mid-task.

There are no plants, of course; the nutritional landscape is hollow, with no water to drive cycles of life or decay. Everything is mineral, everything is dry. What vegetation might once have been present is only a memory etched into the bitter, inert soil.

The rare, infrequent fauna — dark, scuttling things with pale underbellies and complex acoustical organs — mostly avoid the city grounds, appearing and then vanishing into the vast open spaces between clusters of buildings. What creatures may once have dominated have long gone extinct or retreated, leaving only these quiet survivors.

Sound is a treasured rarity: the grating of wind sometimes carries through the gaping mouths of empty windows and doors; the faint echoes of distant rockslides on the edges of perception; occasional, softly clanking noises in factories where no gears turn, no engines burn.

The city is abandoned, yet somehow it seems aware. Its lines are precise, its pathways clear, and its inert structures exude a sense of expectation — as if it were sleeping, waiting for something to call it back into operation or sentience. But no breath of wind stirs the dust into true action, no hand restores the broken glass, and the industrial silence remains unbroken, a monument to itself.
