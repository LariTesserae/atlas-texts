The first sensation is the water – a slow, encompassing warmth, a bath for the world. Not the crushing abyss of the true deep, but a liquid sky: the entire landscape is submerged. Sunlight, what little makes it down, arrives as fractured, shifting columns from a source impossibly high above. They drift through the aquatic light with the languid slowness of galaxies.

Below lies the city. It clings to the upper slopes of a colossal underwater mountain, its structures climbing the incline in irregular tiers. The water here isn't precisely oceanic; it feels more like a rain that never ceased, a planetary inundation held in stasis. It's post-flood, but the flood has settled into permanence, a primordial wetness from which the world is still emerging.

The city itself is dense, layered, an accretion of primitive urbanity. There are no clean lines. Dwellings are carved into the rockface, burrowed into massive, subaquatic trees whose uppermost branches once strained towards the air, or piled in precarious, gravity-defying stacks of dark stone and bioluminescent coral. These materials seem to exude their own slow, dim light, painting the waterscape in shifting blues, greens, and phosphorescence. Some structures are so overgrown they seem half-digested by the lush, sponge-like vegetation that clings to every surface; others are built *into* the bases of enormous, soft-bodied organisms that wave gently like sea grass or the gills of unthinkably large creatures.

The scale is open enough to give a sense of the mountain's vast, unseen bulk, of the city spilling down its shoulders into further, abysmal depths. Yet each cluster of structures is intensely packed, a labyrinth of narrow, pressured alleys where the currents move in slow, viscous trails. The water is not empty; it thrums with a low, internal energy, saturated with suspended particles – not dirt, but something finer, something that feels like unformed geology still deciding what it will become. It’s the raw ingredients of the world, held in solution, and the city itself seems built from this nascent, emerging material.

Water streams in slow, fat rivulets *off* the upper structures, as if the mountaintop itself, unseen above, is still weeping ancient rain. It falls into the submerged streets below with a quiet, muffled drumming, adding to the general sense of minimal flux. Sound travels as a low, constant thrum – the vibration of the water itself, the groan of settling geology, the occasional, slow movement of something vast and unseen in the dim light. The tech is hand-tools and primitive machines that harness slow currents and the gradual pressures of the deep, operated by unseen inhabitants who move with a glacial patience. Fauna is scarce and often bizarre; the water itself seems alive, peopled by things that straddle the line between plant and animal, or fully mobile organisms that are little more than slow-moving, gelatinous clouds.

This isn't a ruin, nor is it truly complete. It’s embryonic, a city still coming into being, its growth an organic, geological process tied to the slow, endless rain from the inverted sky. The rules here are about holding on, about gradual shaping, about finding light in the dim, the weird flourishing in the patient rhythm of shifting currents and the encroaching, lush growth. Time moves not in minutes or hours, but in the slow erosion and accretion of the coral-stuff, the millimeter-by-millimeter creep of cities emerging and dissolving, a world holding its breath beneath an eternal, lukewarm sea. It’s a place where the very rock seems to dream and breathe, exhaling trickles of water and ingesting tendrils of phosphorescent growth.
