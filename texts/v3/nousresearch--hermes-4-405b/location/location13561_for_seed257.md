The place is a submerged temple complex, its scale almost intimate, contained within a massive, hollow geode that somehow holds an air-filled space beneath miles of dark ocean. Water pressure is held back by thick, seamless walls of volcanic glass that glow faintly in the dark. The temple is ancient, carved over millennia by slow, mineral-rich currents; water doesn't rush here but drifts in languid curtains through channels and conduits built into the glass. Fauna thrives — not aquatic life, but slow-growing lichen and bioluminescent organisms that cling to every surface, feeding on the mineral-rich fluids that seep from the glass. Their light is dim and deep blue or blood red, casting long, weird shadows in the near-total darkness, and their movements are so gradual you must watch for hours to see change. Sounds are muffled, only the drip of water and the soft, infrequent clicks or chitters of these creatures, a language for ears that don't rush.

This temple isn't deserted. Something built it, long ago, with primitive tools — perhaps the glass was shaped by focused heat or patient abrasion. Now, the builders or their descendants remain, unseen presences who tend the slow machines of the place. The temple is dense with their handiwork: pillars, platforms, and intricate mechanisms of glass and stone. Some devices still move, turning over cycles that take centuries to complete, and in their movement they maintain the equilibrium of the sealed world. Water flows in controlled patterns, light waxes and wanes with the growth and death of living lamps, and great stone wheels grind almost imperceptibly against each other. The rules here are those of erosion and slow time; nothing happens quickly, and patience is woven into the substance of the world. To be here is to enter a hidden, silent engine at the bottom of the ocean — a machine that runs on water, stone, and shadowed life.
