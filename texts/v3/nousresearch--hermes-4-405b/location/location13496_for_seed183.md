The air is cool and thick with the weight of recent rain, an air that you could wring water from, clinging to your skin and inviting a constant, background clamor of droplets impacting rock. This is a place of extremes, pressed up against the sky on a mountain of ancient, black basalt, and yet knit through with the persistent presence of water, seeping and carving and pooling in every crevice. No blade of grass, no root or any trace of green mars this stone world; if biology exists here, it wears a mask no eye can recognize. It is not a dead place, though. Far from silent, a low hum emanates from deep within the mountain, a groan-a-fracture-a-drift of something immense shifting. The light is harsh, as if the air itself clarifies and focuses the sunlight, glittering off every wet surface and casting long, stark shadows that writhe like living things.

The scale of this scenery is imposing — the mountain drops sharply away in one direction, while behind you the land ripples and folds, a labyrinthine valley of broken stone, scattered with boulders larger than houses. Everywhere you look, the rock is layered and dense, piled upon itself in jagged heaps as if the mountain itself had shattered but refused to collapse. Spikes and arches of broken basalt rise from the valley floor, sculpted by ice and rain into impossible shapes, looking like the teeth of a buried god. Between them, shallow pools of gathered rainwater gleam like shards of fallen sky, deceptively calm for all the roaring of water tumbling over cliffs in the distance.

Against this backdrop of raw geology, subtle signs of an ancient presence emerge. Simple, primitive structures rise from the bedrock, half-swallowed by stone and time. A series of low walls, carved from the native rock, wind across the valley near the cliff's edge. They are no more than waist-high, worn nearly smooth by centuries of wind and water, yet their construction speaks of exacting care. The stones fit together with meticulous precision, each block chosen and placed to endure. Scattered among these ruins are taller spires, sharp and narrow as shards of broken glass, jutting like splinters from the mountain's face. More refined, in a way, than the rough-hewn walls below, they bear similar weathering but possess a note of impossible tallness and narrowness.

No birds sing here. The only forces at work are the persistent drip of water, the low stone noise of the mountain, and the endless caress of the wind. But the strangeness is more than that. It is in the way the light bends at the edges of things, in the way shadows refuse to lie flat, thickening between rocks as if they held physical weight. It is in the slow transmutation of the stones around you — a slow flicker of their surfaces, as if they are subtly turning over, muted patterns of light shifting in a slow, crawling dance as you watch, reconfiguring without ever moving. The silence echoes here, but within it waits a deeper quiet — the stillness inside the stone, the damp whisper of the air, and the hum of something deeper, something vast, that lies beneath it all.
