The landscape here is defined by what fills it: an ancient, interconnected mass of vegetation that has become architecture and substrate, a living city beneath the surface. You descend into it — contentment glows here, everything glowing with its own muted light. In this subterranean hamlet, water seeps constantly through the walls and drips from above, a network carved for millennia by slow erosion.

Imagine a low, arched root-corridor, water beading on its walls. Condensation hangs in the air. All surfaces — floor, walls, ceiling — are woven from thick, mature roots, fronds, and wood, plaited and tangled into forms and structures that support whatever industrial function once thrived here. The scale is intimate, like a room or a grove, but it is densely packed with woven plant material and the rusted, electrified machinery of a compact, cathedral–scale underground factory system. Pipes and gears nestle into hollows shelled by fungal blooms; a great brass engine rests within a trunk, vines threading its gears. The air thrums with their low roar, the hiss of steam, the rhythmic clatter. Though warm and humid, the space feels still. It's motionless but loud, saturated with presence, in a deep equilibrium. You see this place now and understand what it offered: all life here was plant, the strange comfort of a pure-plant ecosystem made home.
