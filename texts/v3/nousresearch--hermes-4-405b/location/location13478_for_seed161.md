The landscape is a plateau—a single, colossal, hand-shaped platform floating unsupported in a soft twilight sky. Its surface, smoothed by ages into the texture of polished bone or worn soapstone, stretches perhaps a hundred paces from palm to fingertips. Here, in this small, cradled space, life has taken root with fierce intimacy.

Water, absent as a flowing element, manifests as thick, glassy veins of clear sap trapped within the plateau's stone. The air carries a warm, resinous scent like heated amber. This warmth is gentle, held constant by the stone itself, which feels like sunbaked wood although no sun penetrates the gloom beyond the platform's edge.

And what grows here is lush: dense carpets of silver-grey moss clump between cracked mineral ridges, while low shrubs with waxy, copper-colored leaves cluster thickly against the plateau's rising knuckles. Crusts of pale lichen trace spiraling patterns across stone worn smooth as river-polished slate. In these plants, the only moisture is held within thick cuticles or deep rhizomes—a closed cycle of survival.

This place is inhabited. A village crouches within the plateau's palm—dozens of low, dome-shaped dwellings built from stacked, sun-bleached stones mortared with cracked grey clay. These little houses press close together, their walls nearly touching, the paths between them worn to bowl-shaped depressions by centuries of bare feet. Overhead, doorskin hides strung on sinew ropes flap in a sluggish breeze. Smoke threads lazily upward from clay chimneys, bluing the already-dim air.

The villagers are here too—a community of small, stooped figures with skin like sun-baked clay and eyes like wet river stones. Their clothing is rough-woven from dried moss fiber, their tools of carved wood and knapped pebbles: all made by hand, primitive but perfectly fitted to their needs. They move with a slow, deliberate economy, maintaining rope bridges slung between leaning spires of rock, harvesting sap from stone fissures, tending to the plants that grow within.

Sound layers this space: the constant drone of wind over the plateau's edge, the percussive scrape of stone tools, low chatter in a language like clicking pebbles, the drone of insects drawn to the plantlife's stored moisture. Life thrums densely here—flightless birds groom iridescent beetles from the moss, while six-legged reptiles sun themselves on warm stones. This place feels complete, settled into its final form—a small, ancient, floating world where every element has been worn by time into patient equilibrium.
