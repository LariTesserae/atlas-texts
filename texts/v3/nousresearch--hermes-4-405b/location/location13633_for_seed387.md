The universe of Absalom Prime is a pocket-scale, near-empty world contained within the broken canister of an ancient terraforming probe, lost and flooded somewhere in the depths of an alien ocean. What remains is a submerged, mineral-structured urban landscape, its architecture characterized by settled-form corrosion and dilapidation, like the carcass of a mechanical leviathan.

Despite its ruin, Absalom Prime is far from silent. The ceaseless pulse of enormous turbines, coupled with the whir of aged integrated machinery, creates a deafening cacophony that reverberates through the deserted streets and hollowed-out structures. Illumination comes in the form of dim bioluminescent strips that intermittently flicker across the inner surfaces, casting shifting patterns that hint at the activity within.

Absence defines as much as presence in this peculiar world. Flora is utterly unknown—where one might expect the sprawl of a post-flood ecosystem, none of the usual hydrophytic adaptations exist. Indeed, the cityscape shows no signs of conventional plant life at all, its spaces instead filled with the ever-changing flux of hydrodynamic processes. Microscopic life suspended in the ocean's currents drifts around the structures, a constant yet unremarkable presence that only adds to the near-void quality of the place.

Even stranger are the remnants of pre-life forms: energy patterns that emerge without explanation, altering the mechanical composition of buildings, and then vanishing as quickly as they appeared. Non-recognized biology might be at play here or the remnant energy signatures of dissolved, exotic materials; no one can say for sure. The only certainty is that nothing is fixed, nothing is as it was before.

Industrial technology still perseveres. There are factories and production lines hidden within the complex, their machineries scaled to the urban context, yet their purposes have long been forgotten. These constructs now operate without command, acting according to some long-abandoned algorithm, their mechanical imperatives as regular—and as irregular—as the cosmic pulses that might have once powered them.

Absalom Prime's temperature is warm, a side effect of its still-active engines and the ocean’s embrace. Its overall atmosphere is one of eerie calm, yet within that serenity lies a turbulent heart. An undercurrent of chaotic change exists in every corner of the cityscape, from the shuddering of machinery that has no defined purpose to the erratic bursts of piped energy that surge through corroded circuits. A hint of the Solaris-esque creation and destruction might even be at work here, as if the flooded urbanity were attempting to redefine its own history, moment by fragmentary moment.

This is Absalom Prime, at once dead and wildly, deafeningly alive—a dissonant symphony of dilapidated industry, an underwater domain both hidden and ceaselessly evolving.
