The way down is warm, cinched deep in the earth, the air thickening with each step into a syrupy humidity. Light filters weakly through fleshy, bioluminescent leaves that form a near-solid canopy, layers upon layers of foliage so dense that the boundaries between individual plants dissolve into one undulating green mass. Vines as thick as your arm snake through the upper canopy, pulsing with an almost vascular rhythm, and strange root-like structures descend into the depths, glowing softly as they burrow into the still-forming earth.

This is not a forest floor; it is a cavern, ancient and unfinished, its raw geology still weeping into existence. Stone flows like wax, translucent and glowing with inner light, forming contorted shapes that look like they might rearrage themselves when you turn your head. The air shimmers with the heat, and the distant echoes of dripping water and shifting rock create an constant, hissing backdrop.

The chamber is vast—perhaps the size of a town, its limits lost in the endless green gloom. The ground is a patchwork of mosses and spongy fungal growths, interspersed with the raw, weeping rock still hardening into existence. In places, the ground has been scraped bare, revealing the embryonic stone beneath—a slick, wet surface that shimmers with iridescent color in the dim light.

Here and there stand scattered primitive structures, low and crumbling, made of rough-hewn stone and timbers that seem to have grown into place rather than been cut. They are small and half-swallowed by the foliage, their edges blurring into the green, as if growing back into it even as it grows over them. At their feet lie occasional artifacts—simple tools, rough carvings, or strange pots that seem more like natural formations than anything made by hand. The air is filled with a deafening roar that never quite resolves into a single sound, a cacophony that seems to come from every direction at once, the accumulated buzzing and shrieking and thrumming of a million separate things:

Life throngs the cavern in an overwhelming profusion. Countless species of insects, from tiny, jeweled flakes that glint and disappear as they move, to creatures the size of your thumb that buzz through the air like tiny helicopters. Strange, multi-legged crawlers scuttle over every surface, some no bigger than a thumbnail, others as large as your hand, their shells glinting like dropped gems. Hairy, segmented worms writhe through the mosses, their bodies studded with light-emitting organs that make them look like strings of glowing beads. And everywhere, there are larger creatures—winged reptiles that flit through the leaves like darting shadows, their scales iridescent in the low light; thick-bodied, toad-like amphibians that grunt and croak from the shadows; outsized millipedes and centipedes that ripple over the ground, their segments clicking like tiny beads.

None of these creatures seem to mind each other, or you. They live in a state of constant, chaotic motion, a turbulent bazaar in which every organism pushes and thrums and grows into every other with frantic energy. To walk through them is to be brushed and nudged and crawled on from every side, the air alive with their countless limbs and wings. It feels like the entire cavern is alive, pulsing with a fierce, wild energy that thrums in your bones like the deep thrum of the rock itself—a place where life has swallowed the earth, and the earth hasn't finished being born.
