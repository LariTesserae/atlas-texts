The Surveyor was right—this place didn't have a name yet. Just a string of parameters and a question mark blinking on the manifests. Water 2.0, they said. Wet enough to be considered "frequent," and we're feeling that now. It splashes under the rover treads, beads constantly on the canopy, condenses on the inside too if you crank the heat too high without the dehumidifier.

We don't know where the water comes from. A mile back the terrain was drier, rocky, sparse. But here the ground isn't just damp—it’s a saturated mat of dark vegetation and spongy moss that rolls out in every direction. That vegetation score, 1.8—lush. It’s true. Thick, rubbery leaves the size of tables drip with moisture, reflecting the rover's headlights in a thousand shifting, distorted points. Curtains of wet moss hang from every ledge of the high, eroded rock formations that rise like broken teeth from the plant life. That elevation score, 1.7, feels like an understatement when you’re down in the low ground with fog rolling down off the higher peaks.

These landscapes don't happen overnight. The erosion parameter, 2.3, paints a picture of a place that's been worked over by time. The rain, the wind, the slow, constant chewing of dampness at stone—everything’s been ground down to its essentials. Even the rocks here look like bones left out in the weather. It's all settled into its own state, in balance, like it's been this way forever.

And the scale—2.8, which means vast, the kind of vast where weather doesn’t just pass through, it gets created here. Clouds form kilometers above us, in perpetual slow-motion explosions, blotting out any sky beyond. The darkness parameter is a solid 0.0. No sun, no stars, no moon. Only the beams from the rover cut the black, catching the fog, the leaves, the eyes that gleam from the undergrowth.

Density: 2.1, meaning it’s packed. Where there's space, something grows. Even the air feels thick, heavy with the smell of wet earth, ozone, decay. And built structures? Scattered, mostly—little more than occasional clearings hacked out of the undergrowth, the metal frames of old machines overgrown. But occasionally the headlights catch something more deliberate: a low-slung, windowless building of dark metal half-swallowed by vegetation, the hum of generators audible even through the sealed cabin.

Tech level: 2.5. Industrial. The sound of engines, sometimes distant, sometimes shockingly close. Machinelike booms and groans in the distance, the whine of turbines. Last night I saw the sky glow orange on the horizon—probably some old automated factory still thrumming away in the dark, spewing vapor into the already thick atmosphere.

Fauna: abundant. 2.0. The night is full of them. We hear them calling to each other—deep, guttural sounds that echo off the rock formations. The crunch of something massive moving through the brush, the splashes just beyond the limit of our lights. They don't seem to care about us. Probably never seen anything like the rover before.

It's weird here—2.0. Strange. More than once, we’ve seen things that don’t make sense. Shapes in the fog, things moving in the vegetation that seem too big, too fluid. Even the plants seem to follow their own rules.

Sound: loud. 2.1. The constant drip of condensation from a million leaves, the rush of unseen watercourses, fragments of industrial noise carried on the wind. And the animals—howls, clicks, chattering that sounds almost like speech, but isn't.

Dynamic: 2.1. This place never rests. The wildlife never stops moving, the machines grind on, and even the terrain shifts—the rover keeps finding new rivulets of water cutting through the ground, new rockfalls from the eroded heights above. Every hour brings some new noise, some new shape in the dark.

We push on. The headlights carve a path through the black, and the wet world presses in, alive and strange. We're mapping it, inch by inch.
