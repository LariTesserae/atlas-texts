The place is a high, cool plateau, and it holds a city carved into and from a single, vast, eroded rock formation. The scale is intimate, a hamlet-sized settlement within the rock’s embrace, yet the stonework suggests urban density—a warren of dwellings, workshops, and low, winding streets that burrow into and cling to the formation’s faces. The air hangs damp and cool, carrying the scent of wet stone, and moisture gathers in runnels and shallow depressions, left by intermittent rain that falls as a cold mist, collecting also in pockets where the rock has been hollowed to form cisterns and small, still pools. The vegetation is sparse, limited to a few hardy, colorless lichens that cling to the shaded crevices, and twisted, stunted shrubs—thin remnants—that grow only where the earth has gathered in the hollows. Light filters through the ever-present mist, casting the place in a subdued, grayish atmosphere; it is never bright here, though on rare clear nights the stars pierce through. The rock itself is dark, deeply weathered, its faces rounded and sculpted by the patient work of wind and water into shapes that seem to shift and deepen with the angle of light. The city is quiet, the sounds muted by the damp air and the stone—a place of few human voices, distant and echoing, the scrape of a tool here and there, the drip of condensation, and the sigh of wind through the stone channels. The technology is simple, primitive; the people work stone and wood with hand tools, shaping their environment but not dominating it, and the city itself seems grown from the rock, not imposed upon it. The rules of this world are of endurance, of dwelling in balance with a damp, cool stillness—where change is almost imperceptible, and the stone and the cycles of moisture shape the days.
