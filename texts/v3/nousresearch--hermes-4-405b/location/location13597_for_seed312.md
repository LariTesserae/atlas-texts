The landscape unfolds as a vast, high-eloped region, a continent-scale plateau where the horizon is a jagged, saw-toothed line of sharp peaks. This immense expanse is sculpted by slow, patient forces, a young erosion etching its features in broad, deliberate sweeps across geological time. The atmosphere is deeply cold, a pervasive chill that clings to stone and sky alike, and light falls in what would be a normal spectrum for a planetary surface, though here it seems sharpened, almost brittle, casting deep shadows among the towering architecture.

For this is not a landscape of natural wilderness, but a dense, scale-defying urban expanse. Vast cities, built from stone and ice and the bones of the mountains themselves, stretch across the plateau in layered, near-touching settlements. These are cities of primitive tech – a world without advanced metals or engines or fuels, where the materials are stone, ice, rough-hewn timber from hardy mountain vegetation, and perhaps the cured hides and sinews of the abundant fauna. The technology is one of levers and counterweights, of ramps and immense stairs, of ice-screws and stone axes, a primordial megalithic civilization.

The cities are dense, structures piled upon structures, a verticality mirroring the mountain peaks they are built upon and amidst. Yet movement here is slow. Figures, humanoid but perhaps adapted to the perpetual cold – heavier of build, broader of hand and foot, clad in thick hides and furs – move with a ponderous, deliberate slowness through the massive stone avenues and across the wind-scoured plazas. The scale of the construction is planetary, implying either an incredibly numerous population or architectural methods that defy easy comprehension, perhaps passed down through countless generations, each adding incrementally to the colossal stoneworks. Sound is mostly quiet: the sigh of the thin, high-altitude wind through the canyons of stone and ice; a distant, rhythmic hammering like the slow beat of a geological heart; the groan of stressed ice; the occasional low call of some hardy creature; the percussive clunk of stone on stone.

Water exists here not as a ubiquitous presence but as pockets of damp, as intermittent moisture. It seeps from crevices in the rock faces, condenses on the cold inner surfaces of deep canyons and sheltered courtyards, forming glistening sheets. It collects in ice-melt pools that slowly freeze and thaw with the slow, deep cycle of temperature changes. Thin, meandering rivers, looking like veins of quicksilver in the grey stone, carve their paths through the urban expanse, fed by the melt from high glaciers and the infrequent, cold rain that falls on this high world. Flora is sparse, emerging only where these moisture pockets sustain them: dark, cold-resistant mosses and lichens painting the stone in subtle patterns, clusters of tough, wiry grasses where scree slopes meet sheltered verges. Despite this, fauna is surprisingly abundant, creatures adapted to the cold and the stone: large, shaggy herbivores, their bodies dense and slow-moving, grazing on the resilient vegetation; sleek predators with sharp minds and patient hunting strategies, their fur thick and often the color of stone or shadow; flocks of wide-winged birds riding the high thermals, their cries sharp and echoing in the vastness. The world feels normal in its fundamental physics, yet its sheer scale of primitive construction and the slow dynamism of its existence lend it an air of ancient, enduring mystery.
