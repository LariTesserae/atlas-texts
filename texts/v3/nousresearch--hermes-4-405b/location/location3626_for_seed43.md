The underworld beneath this kingdom, in a chamber no larger than the grand hall above, is a realm crafted entirely from fired clay. Not rough or hastily made, but dense and deliberate, as if shaped and stacked by the hands of generations. The air is warm, thick with humidity that constantly rises now that the great hidden aqueducts have shifted, and droplets of water run down every contoured surface.

Everywhere you turn, the walls, floor, and ceiling are covered in arched cells and recesses, stacked and filled like honeycombs in an immense hive. Some are small chambers large enough for only a tiny insect or small creature, others grand cavities that stretch to more than twice the height of a human. It’s like standing within the bones of a long-dead, earth-encased leviathan, its ribs visible in the raked light that filters down from some unseen vent in the ceiling high above.

Every surface is eroded, smoothed over long eons of dripping water, leaving behind pockmarked surfaces and strange mineral deposits glistening in the light. The entire space feels ancient, worn down to its essence, but remains solid and permanent.

This is an intensely active place. The air thrums with sound—a chorus of clicks, chirps, and the rustle of countless small bodies. Fauna teems in every crevice of this cavern: cave crickets with long, sensitive antennae, fringed tailless whip scorpions, harvestingmen with legs splayed wide, pale beetles with nocturnal eyeshines, plump white grubs with tiny asymmetrical spots. They skitter in and out of the many cells, clinging to walls, weaving among stalactites thick as stalagmites, and flowing like living water around your feet—if you stray too close. They are not hostile, but abundant, each finding its place in the dense, layered ecosystem thriving in the damp, warm, and safe enclosure.

The space is lit by a strange set of angled shafts carved from above, casting a web of shadows and bright lines across the wet floors. In the glow of this light, the colors of the clay become striking—deep reds, subtle browns, and pale greys emerging from centuries of minerals leaching from above to leave strange sedimentation lines along the damp walls.

Three stone wells drop into small aquifers, and water drips down the cavern’s many walls and columns, feeding the constant wetness. The sound of the droplets landing—each distinct yet blending into a constant shhh around you—mingles with the skittering of tiny legs, the constant, hushed chittering of unseen insects, and the deeper whirring of large beetles tunneling into the clay. It is loud, but not deafening—a dense chorus, a world alive with sound.

Here, everything has been constructed, laid out by design; there is no untouched stone in this realm. The steps leading deeper into the cavern are worn smooth and cupped in the center, as if trod by ancient pilgrims over centuries. Every alcove and ledge has been worked by hand, the marks of primitive tools visible along the floors and walls of each cell. Roughhewn pillars rise toward the ceiling, as if shaped by the first people to carve out this sealed refuge. The place is a masterpiece of primitive engineering—no metal used, only carefully stacked bricks and chiseled sediment—yet it is entirely made, man’s answer to the endless dark beneath the earth.

It was once connected to the world above, perhaps the realm where kings first met their gods, now cut off and repurposed by the quiet denizens of the deep. Every sound you make—every scrape or whisper—echoes back at you and is absorbed into the chorus of life.

In the middle of the chamber stands an immense slab of clay, its surface carved with symbols whose meaning is lost to time. Whatever the original builders intended, this cavern has become a world unto itself, a sealed ecosystem, rich and strange. You stand in a cathedral to a thousand tiny lives, a buried hive, a hidden wellspring filled with life, pulsing with the rhythm of water and secret histories.
