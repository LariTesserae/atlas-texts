The sky is always a sheet of falling rain here. It pours over the towering, impossibly vertical city-cliff that we call the Skyspire, a sheer edifice that rises into the murky heights where sunlight filters down from what seems like eternity above. The spire itself is alive with dripping noises and wet sheen, its face composed of intricate patterns of pipes and catchers and gutters; a sublime, constructed work of erosion engineering, where the whole is built to survive the endless wall of water.

Here at our observation deck, set in the middle of one of those colossal walls – "deep within the walls of the world" we sometimes say – we have a hidden pocket of stillness. A wide, rocky balcony of smoothed concrete and stone, warmed by geothermal vents that steam when the fog lifts. It juts out into the open expanse of the sky; far below, the roar of the rain-pounded city registers as a distant, comforting thunder.

Surrounded by that immense drop down to civilization and enclosed by the steaming rock-façade above, the terrace itself is lush with runaway vegetation. Jungle vines drip with heavy, clear water; ferns and tropical orchids spill over the edges and weave around the geothermal vents, sprouting vibrant petals that seem almost too bright here. Thick moss grows across the concrete floor, softening the human-made geometry.

All around is an intense, humid sense of life. Lizards dart among the vines, their calls echoing softly between the roar of distant, falling water and the gurgle of geothermal pipes warming the air. Butterflies the color of sunset or deep-sea creatures flit from leaf to leaf, occasionally landing on the slick, wet stone that anchors the observation platform.

The atmosphere is peculiar. On one side, the open sky is a dense vertical sheet of rain that passes across the landscape with a shushing, rushing sound. On the other, the ancient, built cliff of concrete and steel has been eroded into a façade of waterfalls and rivulets, infrastructures half-lost to decay but recaptured by the overgrowth.

Everywhere, the hum of machines carries – a reminder that the wall’s innards, though ancient, are maintained. Pipes hiss steam and machinery groans faintly, almost as if the cliff itself were breathing. Somehow, despite being so hidden and alive with overgrowth, there's a sense of something industrial at work, a fabricated, maintained quality. It is as if the terrace is only a momentary escape from an ancient, industrious world beyond the carved façades and heavy curtains of green. And the balance is strange, like an afterthought – one tiny, verdant haven clinging to nature in the midst of relentless engineered erosion.
