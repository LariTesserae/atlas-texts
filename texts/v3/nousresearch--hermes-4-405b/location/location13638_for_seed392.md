The place is a vast, low-lying basin, perpetually hot and humid, bathed in relentless bright light. The substrate isn't natural; it's a testament to vast labor. That labor must have happened incredibly long ago, because what was built has been almost entirely erased. What's visible is a kind of artificial geology, a sediment of human construction. Layers of pulverized brick and concrete form great mesas, ochre and grey; like sedimentary cliffs, but the fossils within them are of pipes, wires, bits of signage. These plains of broken construction stretch far. Between the mesas are lowlands, some filled with shallow, algae-thick ponds, others swampy with hot black mud. Mist hangs thick where warm ground meets cooler air, smelling of rot and wet minerals.

The abundant vegetation grows in this destroyed-built environment without clear edge of where one ends and the other begins. Lush carpets of moss swirl over shattered asphalt. Vines with leaves like green leather scramble over brick cliffs, through air vents, into the open pits of emptied foundations. Tall trees with canopies that are all heavy horizontals grow up from the swampy pits, their roots like eels swarming over submerged sills. The pit-bottoms are choked with pale ferns. It’s lush, but also sparse: the human-made bones of this place remain enormous, stark, undeniable; the plants, for all their vitality, don’t fully cover, don't erase the builtness.

Fauna is abundant, accustomed to the stunning tropical heat. Monitor lizards, large as dogs, cruise the sun-drenched mesas, flicking tongues over sun-warmed concrete dust. In the humid lowlands, bright frogs and salamanders hop across floating islands of ceramic tile. Dragonflies, their wings catching the perpetual light in flickers, skim over algae ponds.

The animals have made homes within the tiny shelters formed by strangely fused debris or pulled up slabs of pavement. They’re unaffected by the reality breakdown lurking around here. It’s manifested as a kind of temporal stillness. The architecture – if it can be called that – the entire destroyed-built environment has been fused in a sort of quantum stasis; Kantian. Time has stopped flowing. No wind, so water is never disturbed, ponds become perfect mirrors. Decay doesn’t occur, only very slow change; like watching shadows creep across the ground at noon – imperceptibly.

The timid tools that lie scattered – wooden hoes, baked-clay chisels, sledgehammer heads made of flintite – are as perfect as the day they were abandoned. Forged or knapped with primitive skill, but preserved in the timelessness. Weepholes near the bottom of ramparts steadily bleed water but the pool at the bottom never deepens.

At rare intervals, the landscape flickers and resets; a grid of soft blue light sweeps over everything, like a slow scan. It doesn’t erase anything, it doesn’t change anything. Things remain exactly as they were, ready for the next scan.

Sounds here are subtle. The panting of animals, splash of a frog into pond, buzzing of insects, chirps from unseen creatures sheltering in debris. These sounds are very loud in the still air, because they’re among the only noises there are. The quietness emphasizes the vast distances and the timelessness. Bright light bakes ground, making it shimmer: another presence, hot and silent.

The only constant is the presence of water: condensation beads on stone, trickles from cracks, pools in hollows. It seeps from beneath broken-cement plains, forming warm swamps where things crawl and swim. It drips from eroded lips, cutting channels through dust that never seem to deepen. The water is hot, mineralized, smelling of wet earth, life blooming in and around it. It's all there is here.
