The place is a high mountain valley, so new and sharp it seems the glaciers only just withdrew. It's bordered by cliffs so raw their stone hasn't even darkened with lichen yet, and the air between them is perpetually filled with a cold, clinging mist — water condensing from the thin atmosphere, weeping down every rock face, making the ground a marsh of icy meltwater. The cold is biting, deep, making the very rock feel brittle and the thin air carry a sting even in the stillness.

But within this realm of water and ice and mist, something thrives — *life*. Where the valley floor isn't submerged in the dim, reflective pools of shallow water, every inch of ground is claimed by colossal plant-structures. They aren't trees or anything so individual, but a continuous, sprawling mass of layered, thick, rubbery leaves: deep greens and cold, bruise-like purples and ghostly grays, covered in a thick fuzz of ice or beaded with condensation. Some leaves lie flat and heavy as paving stones, their surfaces already rough with the grit of erosion and colonized by cup-like growths that hold little pools of frigid rainwater. Others stand upright, their edges razor-sharp, young and untouched by decay. They form arches and vaults, creating natural tunnels and groves.

This dense, unbroken mat of vegetation — so total it chokes every inch of stone, leaving no space for soil — forms the foundations of the valley's architecture. Scattered throughout are dozens of small structures, visibly and unmistakably cultivated: low domes and skeletal towers woven from dried, hollow reeds; mounds built from packed, fossilized leaf-matter bonded with mineral-rich slime; platforms of woven fibers stretched over arches of living, hardened root. It's undeniably a settlement, a village — but one built without tools. The paths between structures are worn indentations in the endless leaf-layer, trails maintained by the passage of living things. Visible on the structures themselves are intricate patterns: some grown deliberately into the living plant material as ridges and bumps, Others are etched into the dead reeds by hand, filled with a dark, pollen-like dust that glimmers faintly in the perpetual twilight.

The atmosphere is dim, the light filtered through the dense plant-growth and the valley's mist, the sun never strong enough to warm anything. The only sounds are the slow drip of condensation from the rigid leaves, the squelch of water underfoot, and the low groans of the wind moving through the upper canopy and the cracks in the cliffs. Occasionally, a sharp retort echoes between the valley walls — a pod of gas or a cluster of spores erupting from some overripe structure on a plant. Rare, quick shapes — small, heat-conserving creatures — dart through the undergrowth, feeding on the cold fruits, the bitter leaves, and the slow-growing fungi that emerge from the plant-mass. The air smells of wet greenery, freezing water, and the faint, sweet-sour decay of fallen, pulpy leaves.
