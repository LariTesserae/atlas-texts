The cavern air clung, thick and stale, smelling of hot metal and damp stone. This was no natural cave; the cavern was a ruin — a hollowed excavation beneath some forgotten metropolis, its dimensions cramped like a buried cathedral. The space bristled with the remnants of industry: skeletal pipes snaking along walls, iron catwalks clinging rusted to the rock face, the fractured husks of dead generators. Flickering faintly in the dim light, exposed wiring snaked like veins over every surface, as if the very stone had been electrified. Water beaded constantly on cold pipes and seeped from crevices in the rough-hewn walls, running in rivulets down to the uneven ground, where it pooled in low depressions among the rubble of collapsed masonry and abandoned gear. Somehow, life thrived here: damp-smelling mosses clung to seeping walls; hardy, thick-rooted ferns unfurled huge leaves from cracks in the stone and around rusted machinery; vines cascaded down from unseen heights. The air hummed with a low, resonant frequency — the groan of stressed metal, perhaps, or the distant thrum of failing power. It felt as if the very rock were breathing, slowly — the cavern alive with a strange, decaying vitality, its air heavy with damp heat, its surfaces slick and teeming, its buried structures honeycombed with verdant life where all should have been barren.
