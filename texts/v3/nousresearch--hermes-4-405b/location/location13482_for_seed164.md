The sky is the first and most persistent thing. On the edge of space, it's an immense, imperious presence — thin, cold, and piercing blue even at noon. Its light falls with intense clarity, making every edge sharp, every surface vivid. This is not a sky that yields to weather; it is the weather, a vast, stable, unliving ocean above the peaks.

For this is a realm of impossible mountains: towering, fantastical peaks that claw at the upper atmosphere, their forms angular and young. Freshly erupted from some primordial, geo-industrial process, they haven't had time to soften. Gargantuan crystals of ice and strange, cold minerals glint like shattered mirrors on surfaces still sharp from their violent birth. The valleys between them, deep and shadowed, are not soft folds but angular clefts, vast ravines where the light barely penetrates.

Yet water is everywhere, clinging to the cold stone. Weeping rocks glisten, their faces beaded with condensation that never quite freezes, constantly fed by springs that seep up through cracks in the mountain flanks. High, water-laden clouds often blanket the middle slopes, pouring ephemeral rivers down into the abyssal valleys before vanishing as mist in the thin, frigid air. The air itself is saturated, near freezing, carrying a perpetual damp chill that bites even through heavy insulation.

And in this vast, cold, wet highland, life of a sort has taken hold — but not organic life. The builders have come here, creating a city that sprawls across impossible distances. Their constructions are immense, layered, and utterly strange: factories burrow into the living rock, their cores glowing with unnatural light from deep within the mountains. Enormous pipes and conduits, webbed with frost, wind through valleys and scale cliff faces, carrying unknown fluids. The architecture is harsh, geometrically severe, a fusion of brutalist necessity and the logic of machines pushed to their absolute limits. Yet even in this hostility, something resembling lushness clings: vibrant, alien molds and crystalline lichens encrust exposed surfaces, thriving in the damp, cold air and feeding off the waste heat and exotic emissions of the machines. These strange growths are luridly colored, pulsating with slow internal rhythms, their intricate, fractal patterns spreading across rock and metal alike.

The constant noise is immense: a deep, thrumming symphony of industry vibrating through the very bones of the mountains. The roar of titanic engines, the hiss of escaping steam from miles of piping, the rhythmic clanging of automated processes, and the ultrasonic whine of advanced machinery create a relentless, inescapable soundscape. The rock itself seems to sing with industry. Yet beneath this thunderous chorus, there is something else: an eerie, almost melodic resonance, like wind singing through crystal chasms or the infrasonic hum of some buried, world-scale reactor. It's as if the mountains themselves are a part of the mechanism, their resonant frequencies harnessed and amplified.

Nothing stirs that is not machine or rooted growth. No animal life disturbs the unnatural equilibrium: no birds challenge the thin air, no insects scuttle among the strange flora. This world is vast, overwhelmingly empty between its points of intense, furious activity. The city feels less like a thriving settlement and more like an immense, intricate engine whose purpose remains obscure. It is a place of perpetual motion and deafening sound, yet also of profound isolation. Nothing changes, yet everything is pushed to its limit, forever straining against the cold and altitude. The sheer scale defies understanding; weather systems could be born and die within the bounds of a single industrial district. This is not wilderness, nor ruin, but a vast, frigid industrial heartland at the very top of the world, perpetually working, perpetually alone, weirdly alive in its own alien way.
