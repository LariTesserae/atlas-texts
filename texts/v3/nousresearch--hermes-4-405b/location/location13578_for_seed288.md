The first impression is sound. Even before the cavern’s vast scale registers, a profound roar fills the air – not harsh, but so pervasive it seems embedded in the rock. Within this immense, cool, below-surface space, the atmosphere is dim but far from dark, bathed in soft bioluminescence.

An ancient city sprawls here, its structures built into the cavern walls and rising from the floor in a scattered, sparse arrangement. The erosion here is profound, primordial: nothing sharp stays, every surface smoothed and settled into its final form over epochs. This is a city built for permanence, yet it feels organic, as if the stone has agreed to hold shape.

Despite the absence of water – the air is dry, carrying no scent of moisture – the spaces teem with life. Thick, lush growths cascade down every vertical surface. They’re not plants as understood on the surface, but dense networks of fungal matter and symbiotic colonies, their structures holding moisture like succulents. The air hums with abundant fauna: chitinous flyers whose wingbeats contribute to the omnipresent sound, their bodies glowing softly like mobile constellations. Smaller, multi-legged things skitter across the cool stone, tending the living walls. These creatures are the city's true inhabitants, their collective activities creating a deafening yet strangely harmonious chorus. This metropolis was shaped long ago, but it has never been abandoned by life.

The sound itself, upon acclimation, reveals layers: the buzz of flight, the deeper vibration of subterranean processes, the subtle hum of the fungal networks. Nothing here changes quickly. Time accumulates rather than passes, the only technology the intricate biology of the cavern. It is an urban wilderness beneath the earth, deafening but calm, vast but alive, a place of immense age setting the only pace that matters.
