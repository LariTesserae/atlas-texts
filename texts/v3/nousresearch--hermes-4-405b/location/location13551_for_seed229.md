The strangeness unfolds in a highland grove, nestled on a cliffside aire like an amphitheater. At first glance, it might be mistaken for an alpine meadow transported to an arid world, bathed in a sunset garden light—until you breathe, and instead of cold, clear air, you inhale dry warmth, the scent of a thousand unknown blossoms, and something deeper: the scent of dry minerals, geology made intimate. Water is absent—not just gone, but replaced. Instead, the flora drinks light.

This grove is small, yet teeming within its defined arena: a natural bowl worn by ancient erosion into the mountain’s face, sides steep and offering a sense of enclosure, though no roof but a twilight sky with no obvious sun. Here, warm wind runs like liquid silk through plants whose shapes defy terrestrial logic. Spinach-green leaves unfold in spirals like nautilus shells; stalks resembling translucent jade support blooms like glass coral. They rise in sparse, deliberate clusters, each plant an island with ample space to thrive—like an orchard grown by unseen hands, its design both organic and deliberate. This isn’t the wildness of a jungle; it’s curated, intentional, as if each species has agreed upon its place.

What appears as lush vegetation isn’t fueled by water. Each plant quivers slightly, thrumming as if connected to a power source underground—or in the air itself. Light is the currency here, but not sunlight in any familiar form. A soft, dim glow suffuses the grove, radiating from the stones underfoot, the plant stems themselves, and perhaps the very air, which shimmers where it meets the dense, pulsating foliage. This light feels warm, almost tactile. It casts long, doubled shadows that suggest more light sources than meet the eye.

The soundscape is immense for such a contained space. Wind moves in great sighs and exhalations through hollow stalks, creating a choir of bass tones, constant yet shifting—a deep drone that might be the mountain’s own breath. Above this, a chorus of clicks, whistles, and chitters erupts: the fauna. Insects with wings like panes of stained glass bump against leaves; bark-skinned reptiles with faceted eyes cling to stalks, their tongues flicking out like tiny darts to taste the dry, electric air. They are almost caricatures of familiar beasts—too angular, too boldly colored, too agile. Abundant and boisterous, these creatures navigate the glowing, wind-rustled stems, their movements quick and jagged.

The built environment here is present but faint: a few stones stacked to form a low windbreak or a primitive shrine, tucked between larger plants. In one corner, a shallow cave mouth is blocked by a screen woven from stripped stalks, its pattern eerily similar to the spiral formations of the leaves themselves. These structures are not ruins, but functional, integrative. Ancient tools lie where they were last used—a chipped stone blade resting near a pile of hollow reeds, as if someone might return at any moment to their craft.

Turmoil is the norm—growth and decay accelerate in this place, visible to the naked eye if you watch a single plant long enough. Colors flash across a leaf’s surface; a bloom unfurls with an audible *pop* and begins to wither even as its brightest color peaks. The ground shifts underfoot—a dry, granular soil that rearranges itself in slow ripples, as if alive—and even gravity seems to relax its grip in pockets, letting spores drift like dust motes. This grove is hyperactive, pulsing, its strangeness elemental, rooted deep in the mountain and the light. It is life thrumming on a frequency slightly out of sync with the rest of existence—a high, arid garden fiercely, weirdly, wonderfully alive.
