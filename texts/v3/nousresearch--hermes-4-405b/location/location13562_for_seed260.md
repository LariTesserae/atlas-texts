The place is a high jagged plateau of obsidian-riddled granite, impossibly tall columns like fingers of a buried god, still growing by some agonizingly slow process. A dim, ozone-smelling twilight hangs thick in the air, lit from no discernible source, casting long purple shadows between the monoliths. Thick, rubbery vines the color of malachite snake around and through the rock faces; fat, slick ferns unfurl glowing chartreuse leaves in shadowed recesses where the light never quite penetrates.

There are no animals here. No buzzing insects, no skittering lizards, no wheeling birds. But a sound fills the air—not quite a roar, not quite static—a constant, deafening hum that vibrates deep in the bones. It is a noise like the groaning of a planet. Occasionally, the hum shifts into a pattern, a deep, resonant grinding, as if titanic gears are turning mountains into dust. The pressure it creates presses against the eardrums like angry hands, promising to shatter them if one listens too long.

Ruins dot the landscape, but not in any organized fashion. A crumbling archway of rough, hand-hewn stone stands in the center of a field of shards—giant, half-melted fragments of the surrounding columns. Jagged bits of metal, remnants of some prehistoric smelting process, litter the ground. In a fissure, a crude stone hut with no door or windows holds only dust and a few tools of iron, rusted beyond repair.

Here, reality itself is thin, the divide between the material and the immaterial worn paper-thin. It is a place where the laws of physics bleed into madness, where the ground is not always solid, and the air shimmers with unseen energy. A place where gravity ebbs and flows. A place that is not quite finished being born. A place that does not know that it is dying.
