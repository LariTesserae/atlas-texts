The inhabitants of the Highpool Crags are mineral beings, irrevocably intertwined with the living rock they inhabit. They are carvers and shapers, architects who exist through the mountain rather than control it, their existence a co-creation with the stones they live within and the strange, pulsing energy of the ice-vines. These beings leave no direct traces of their corporeal forms, only the evidence of their ceaseless work: the ever-changing architecture of their hive-like city, the intricate carvings that adorn every surface, and the living mineral fauna that wanders through it all.

Their existence is inseparable from the mountain's paradox of motion and stillness, sound and silence. They move slowly, in unison with the mountain's respiration – the drip of water, the crack and reseal of stone, the endless, slow dance of stone grating against stone. Their industry is that of adaptation and participation, their lives dedicated to accentuating and harmonizing with the natural pulse and rhythm of the mountain.

The carvings they leave behind are not merely decorative; they are sigils, diagrams of intent, a written language of becoming that channels the mountain’s energy and guides its continual transformation. The leering faces and bizarre figures etched into the stone might be masks or might be the mountain dreaming of life, but they are also maps, blueprints for the city’s perpetual reshaping, part instruction and part invocation.

Their emotional lives, too, would be carved from stone and ice – a slow, mineral sorrow or joy, tectonic shifts of feeling that unfold over eons. They coexist with the crystal spiders, stone frogs, and the great slow-worms of living rock, perhaps regarding them as kin or as further expressions of the mountain’s vitality. These beings are not separate from the Crags but are the Crags, their senses attuned to the vibrations of the mountain, the light refracting through ice, the constant, almost inaudible hum of stone alive with energy.

The rules that govern the Crags – fluid gravity, upside-down waterfalls, perpetual, shifting rainbows – are the medium within which these beings thrive, the very conditions that have shaped their unique form of existence. The constant transformation of their city and the landscape is not a threat but a fundamental part of their lives, a reminder that they are part of a system where decay is replaced by renewal and change is the only constant. The architecture of the place, with stairs that ripple and bridges that flex, suggests that these beings experience movement and change not as something to be resisted, but as a natural part of being, as essential and life-giving as the drip of icy water or the pulse of the ice-vines.
