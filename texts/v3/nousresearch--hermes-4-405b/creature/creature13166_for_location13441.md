The inhabitants of this place are beings of vapor, heat, and condensation, birthed from the city’s ancient geothermal heart. They move slowly through the damp, metallic air, their massive, amorphous forms shifting like living steam. In the city’s perpetual near-rain, their bodies coalesce into translucent, liquid shapes—dense clouds of mist that hover and drift, leaving ephemeral trails of shimmering droplets that vanish almost as soon as they touch the warm stone.

Their faces, if they can be called faces, are suggestions of structure: hollows in the vapor that resemble deep-set eyes, and subtle depressions or bulges that hint at mouths. Their gazes are long and patient, as if they have witnessed the city’s slow transformation over millennia. Their voices are soft, low thrums, like the sound of water percolating through deep rock or the pulsing of ancient, buried engines. When they move, their shapes ripple and flow, merging seamlessly with the steam rising from vents and the haze that hangs in the humid air.

These beings have no need for the towering, empty structures or the stone streets; their world exists in the interplay of heat, moisture, and the latent energy held in the basin. They inhabit the spaces between drops of water, the flow of steam through conduits, and the slow, resonant beat of the city’s geothermal heart. Their presence is felt in the way the warm wind sighs through cavernous halls or in the sudden condensation that forms on cold surfaces, as if they are breathing against the stone.

Though they seem ethereal, their connection to the physical realm is profound. Their bodies leave faint patterns on the glassy surfaces of buildings—whorls and eddies that look like wet fingerprints or the traces of fingertips dragged through condensation. These markings deepen over time, becoming part of the city’s mineral tapestry, a record of their slow, persistent touch.

In the lower, hotter levels of the city, where steam and fire reign, the beings are nearly invisible. They merge with the stifling air, the air itself thick with unshed water and latent heat. But on the surface, where cool mists drift and water droplets collect in basins of unnatural heat, they are more visible. There, they seem to gather—a silent, shifting congregation around the simmering pools, their forms illuminated by plumes of illuminated steam.

These beings are the city’s caretakers, its silent guardians. They have no need for the structures left behind by whatever civilization built the stone streets and towering factories, yet they maintain the delicate balance of its existence. They live in symbiosis with the strange, mineral flora, their movements encouraging the growth of crystalline forms that emerge in deep fissures and the glassy lichen that clings to radiant surfaces. When the city’s ancient machinery groans or shudders, the beings flow toward it, their vapor-like forms condensing around the machines, lending a calming, balancing pressure—as if soothing an old wound in the earth.

Their time is measured not in days or seasons but in the slow accumulation of rust on forgotten engines, the gradual erosion of stone, and the persistent drip of water. They are eternal, patient, and deeply interwoven with the geological processes that shape this place. They do not age or change in the way organic life does; instead, they shift with the city’s moods, adapting to each new burst of steam from a vent or the sudden cooling of a geothermal pool.

When the low, hot wind sweeps through the avenues, carrying scents of wet metal and heated stone, the beings become part of it. They ride the currents, their forms thinning into veils of vapor that brush against the buildings, leaving behind droplets that run like tears down the dark facades. In these moments, the city feels alive, animated by their ephemeral touch. They do not build or alter the landscape intentionally, but their presence leaves its mark—a quiet reminder that life here is not absent but has merely taken on a form that mirrors the ancient, industrial, and deeply geological soul of the city.
