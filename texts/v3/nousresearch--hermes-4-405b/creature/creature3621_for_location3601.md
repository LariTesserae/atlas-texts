The things that live here aren't alive in any sense you'd recognize. Still, they inhabit this place with more claim than any misplaced organic life could manage.

At the thickest concentrations of dripping condensation, where pipes vent their warm exhaust directly onto cooler plates, colonies of wetware spread. These aren't living fungi, not precisely, but autonomous cleaning systems—designed once, perhaps, to consume mineral deposits and now hungrily dismantling the new cavern's manufactured surfaces, leaving behind only patterns of corrosion like strange, branching continents. They shimmer with a certain false vitality when disturbed, iridescent membranes rippling with alarm, spraying defensive aerosols into the damp air.

Across the scaffolding and catwalks, between half-assembled factory frames, small drones move in coordinated patterns, busy with tasks that never seem to conclude. Each one is a knot of pistons, sharp legs, and myofibril-mimicking actuators, ticking softly as they work. They carry power cells to the skeletal limbs of larger machines; retrieve fallen tools, their movements economical, precise; map the condensation patterns on the high artificial cliffs, scaling them with unnerving grace. Occasionally, clutches of unactivated drones cling motionless to the walls: collections of legs and sensors gathering dust until some unseen signal rouses them.

Down in the principal construction zones, where the cavern's thrum is loudest, larger shapes dwell. Multi-jointed arms, ending in articulated manipulators, are folded like dozing insects over vast machines they are building—machines that will, in turn, build others. These giants are anchored to the riveted plates by hydraulic bases; slow, deliberate movements suggest the awakening efforts of titans. Deep within them, glowing filaments—power conduits or internal sensors—pulse with an inner rhythm; their movements, though deliberate, seem to radiate a certain mechanical focus, a ponderous, implacable will.

Higher up, near the elevator shafts that climb into darkness, the air shivers with the passage of grappler units. Like monstrous hermit crabs, each rides within a cagelike cab attached to the rockface. From these armored shells, crusts of articulated limbs drag bundles of cables and pipes up the weeping surfaces, securing them against the cavern walls. Heavy rivet-drivers slam into the artificial cliffs, their concussive reports echoing in bursts of sudden thunder over the constant noise of excavation below. They seem part of the cavern's creation—a living element of its expanding architecture.

And everywhere, there are things half-built. Machine parts, still tethered to their assembly racks, twitch with untested movements; partial sensor arrays rotate slowly on fixed pivots, lenses focusing and unfocusing in the dim light. They are unfinished, yet inhabited by the same determined existence that animates their complete counterparts. In the condensation-slick workspaces between production zones, scattered components and discarded prototypes gather in untidy clusters—a mechanical ecosystem accumulating around the edges of industry.

The whole cavern thrums with their existence. The groan of engines is the collective breath of these beings; the rash of dripping water, the sweat of their perpetual effort. In the restless, damp-edged cool, they are not merely present but endemic. Their life, or something like it, is the cavern's life—this place is an extension of their industry, and they are bound to it in turn. They are the natural consequence of the cavern's artificial nature, its first and only native inhabitants.
