In this nascent realm, where the very bones of the world are still dreaming themselves into existence, a remarkable menagerie of life has taken hold—a chorus of beings as raw, unfinished, and wondrously strange as the landscape itself.

The most ubiquitous inhabitants are the lithovores: low, skittering things with chitinous bodies like living obsidian, their many-jointed legs clicking softly as they move ceaselessly across the warm, porous ground. They feed on the very stones, dissolving the semi-organic compounds with their prehensile, needle-tipped mouthparts, each of their multifaceted eyes glittering like chips of volcanic glass in the dim, shifting light. In great swarms, they flow across the faces of the half-formed cliffs and the impossible angles of the ancient structures, sculpting the rock with their ceaseless industry. Their movements are a kind of living current: an ebb and flow of countless tiny bodies, as mesmerizing and inevitable as the slow creep of glaciers, yet somehow also as deliberate as the progress of ants. The lithovores seem to possess an uncanny awareness one another, moving with a precision and unity of purpose that diminishes only as the deep, infra-sonic thrum of the planet swells to near-deafening levels. So many move as one, and in such numbers, that their collective motion becomes a visible force, like the pulse of lifeblood through the planet's veins.

Equally at home are the aerofauna: vast, pulsating things like airborne jellyfish, their translucent membranes shot through with veins of bioluminescent color. These creatures ride the thermals and updrafts that rise from the warm, breathing rocks below, their tentacles brushing the friable ground as they drift languidly along. Some are solitary, drifting alone like lost balloons. Others congregate in great, raucous flocks that fill the air with their eerie, melodic cries—their calls echoing strangely off the ever-shifting rock formations and the twisted geometries of the forgotten artifacts, as if the sound itself were being refolded and remade. To watch them soar is to witness a kind of aerial ballet, their forms shifting and unfolding with a languid, almost weightless grace. Their translucent flesh is patterned with intricate networks of veins that pulse with a soft, internal light; these patterns seem to change in response to the shifting moods of the light above.

And then, emerging from the weeping crevices of the rock faces, are the hydro-symbiotes: vast, gelatinous colonies of semi-sentient algae that flourish in the ever-present dampness. These growths form vast, pulsating mats of color that cling to the weeping rocks—shifting from deep blues to vibrant greens as the light changes, as if the very rocks were dreaming in color. The hydro-symbiotes absorb the ambient moisture directly through their porous flesh. Often, this leaves them glistening with a slick, almost oily sheen—while the heat radiating from the rock itself seems to suffuse them with a kind of languid, pulsing life. The hydro-symbiotes are not static; they pulse and ripple with strange, internal currents, as if the water they contain were alive and dreaming. Tiny, skittering lithovores often pick their way across the surfaces of these growths, feeding on the microscopic spores that drift from their fringes; the two life-forms exist in a delicate dance of consumption and renewal, each sustaining the other in ways that defy easy classification.

Clinging to the highest, most inaccessible reaches of the landscape are the echo-bats: vast, leathery-winged beings that roost in the shadowed overhangs of the half-formed cliffs, their voices adding a sonorous bass counterpoint to the clamor of the aerofauna above. These enigmatic giants spend much of their lives suspended in a kind of torpor, their wings folded around them like living shrouds. But when the great flurries of cool air come rushing through the open fissures in the rock, they unfurl their vast wings and take to the air, their deep, resonant cries echoing across the dreamscape. The echo-bats seem to possess some rudimentary form of intelligence: their eyes, when they deign to open them, are disturbingly human-like, filled with a kind of ancient, brooding wisdom. The ground around their roosting sites is often littered with the strange, geometric artifacts they seem to collect—twisted shards of metal, fractured crystals that pulse with a faint light, and the molted husks of lithovores.

And throughout it all, weaving between the skittering lithovores and the pulsing mats of hydro-symbiotes, flit the shimmer-moths: tiny, ephemeral beings with wings like shards of stained glass, their iridescent scales refracting the ever-shifting light in unpredictable patterns. The shimmer-moths are drawn to the strange effulgences that ripple across the sky—as though the light itself were calling to them, inviting them to dance within its transient embrace. They drift through the air in great, shimmering clouds, their wings a blur of color and motion. Often, the shimmer-moths will alight on the warm, breathing rocks, their delicate legs barely touching the porous surface, as if they were afraid of disturbing the nascent life that dreams beneath.

The life here is not static, nor easily categorized—it exists in a state of constant flux, a dynamic tension between the raw, emergent forces of the landscape and the strange, symbiotic relationships that have evolved among its inhabitants. Each creature, each colony and swarm, seems to possess a kind of raw, unformed intelligence—a glimmer of awareness that shines through the cracks of its alien biology, like the light of a distant star glimpsed through a fissure in the rock. To witness this place is to witness the very act of creation: a world dreaming itself into being, one strange, skittering, pulsing miracle at a time.
