In the Hole, the inhabitants are the Machinesmiths and the Tenders of the Steam. Generations have lived by the rhythm and roar of the great engines buried deep in the earth here. Their ancestors were drawn to the hot springs and the rich minerals they carried, and they began to build the engines that would extract these resources, turning the Hole into a self-contained machine-city that feeds and heats itself on steam and ground-heat.

The men and women who live here are short and stocky, with broad shoulders and thick forearms from years of turning valves, wrestling wrenches against jammed pipework, and hammering iron into shape. Their skins are leathery, weathered by the ever-present damp and the steam, shadowed by the perpetual twilight that filters through the haze. Their eyes are hard and sharp; they can diagnose a misfiring boiler with the barest flicker in a pressure gauge and can spot a failing gear in a room full of thundering machinery. They live in the tenements around the edges of the Hole, in apartments that are little more than shelves suspended above the steam vents or burrowed into the packed, damp stone.

The Machinesmiths are the builders and maintainers of the Hole's engines, the artists who make sense of the hissing and clattering. They work with weathered hands and keen minds, their coveralls permanently soot-stained. Their children follow them down into the depths to learn the mysteries of the gears and pipes that their strange city depends on. The Tenders of the Steam, meanwhile, preside over the giant valves and regulators that control the flow of vapor between the hot springs and the machines or heating systems. They wear thick gloves and heavy aprons as they make their rounds, listening to the pulse of their charges and adjusting flows with the practiced, almost religious attention of deep-temple priests.

Children grow up playing in the shadow of the giant machinery, their games mirroring the work of their elders: makeshift valves and imaginary steam leaks are tended to with serious faces and careful mimicry. Education is practical, consisting of climbing through pipes and learning by touch in the dim machine rooms. Language is efficient, functional; there is little need for poetry in a life measured in exact bolt sizes and inlet pressures. The oldest among them, their backs bent from decades of stooping over valves and machinery, are venerated as living libraries of knowledge about the Hole's intricate systems.

There are no animals in the Hole beyond the few spiders that lurk in the high, weeping corners of the tenement ceilings, spinning webs in the ever-present steam and feeding on the unseen insects that thrive in the wet heat. No birds circle above the rim of the arena; no cats stalk through the alleys. The only wildlife is the moss that clings to everything, feeding on the condensation, and the occasional bright fungus that blooms like a fever dream on a patch of neglected stone.

The Hole is a place of constant, grinding vitality, where the pulse of pistons and the hiss of steam are the breaths of the city itself. The people there live shortened lives, their vitality poured into the endless task of keeping their isolated, self-contained district functioning. They are as much a part of the machinery as the pipes and gears that surround them, bound to the lives of their engines as the engines are bound to them. And yet they persist, swelling with a collective pride in their mastery of the Hole's depths, secure in the certainty that come tremor or shift, pipe burst or gear crack, they will be there, hands on valves, to silence the alarms and keep everything running. In the twilight world of the Hole, they are both the creators and the servants of the relentless machine. They are the keepers of the steam and the engines, and they would not have it any other way.
