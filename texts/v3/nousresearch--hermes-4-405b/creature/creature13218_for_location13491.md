The spectral ecology of this weathered plateau sustains entities as mineral and transient as the stones they haunt. They are the lichen-herders and stone-whisperers, a fading lineage of lithic beings who once shaped this highland shelf with deliberate hands. You might not see them at first, for their forms are slow and subtle, emerging from the patterns of growth and erosion. They are the ones who tended the glowing lichens, guiding their spread across the dark stone in patterns that shift with the light, their cool violets and warm golds a language of mineral communion. Their touch encouraged the pale stalks to rise, each tipped with a delicate chalice that gathers the sky's condensing breath.

These beings move with the patience of growing crystals, their bodies more conglomerate than flesh—a slow assemblage of the very materials they inhabit. They are the reason for the stone's sinuous, rounded forms, its edges worn into curves that recall a crude rose or the fall of exhausted land. Their hands, rough as the chert flakes scattered nearby, once worked the glacial erratics into their current configurations—tall, light gray teeth piercing the plateau's surface, each positioned like keystones in an archway meant to frame the passage of clouds and time.

The shallow pools gathered in low basins are their sanctuaries, still as groundwater forced upward rather than collected from above. It is here, in the quiet intervals between the slow drip of condensation and the occasional whisper of wet stone shifting in the earth, that these lithic beings gather. They harvest the droplets that bead on the chalice-rims of the stalks, not for sustenance as we understand it, but as a means to commune with the fugitive essence of water—the animating force in this mineral ecology.

The primitive artifacts—chert flakes and hammerstones—are remnants of a time when their presence was more active, when they shaped this shelf with deliberate intent. But now they are faded, their purposes abandoned or transformed. The encroaching curve of the highland behind and the sheer drop at the edge bound their world, a contained ecological cell where the only sound is the slow weathering of stone. In this deep silence, an ancestor of quiet, they persist as spectral stewards of the plateau's slow becoming, their forms and actions so intertwined with the landscape that they are less inhabitants and more emanations of the place itself.
