In this oppressive, glowing dark, life takes forms as strange as the geology that birthed it. The most ubiquitous inhabitants are the mineral-eaters: dense populations of fist-sized, chitinous creatures that move in ceaseless rivers across the cavern floor and walls. Their countless legs whisper against the rock like dry leaves. They feed on the glowing dust itself – specialized mouthparts constantly scraping at the volcanic glass, their internal chemistry converting geothermal radiation and minerals into energy. They are blind, navigating through IR-sensing pits along their flanks, shapes replicating into the hundreds of thousands. These are the primary consumers, processing the very rock and breathing out faintly metallic vapors.

The cavern walls themselves ripple with movement, draped in colonies of what might be sessile invertebrates: clusters of iridescent, segmented tubes that slowly wave in unseen air currents, filtering the thick, dusty atmosphere for organic particles released by the eaters' waste. Their surfaces are covered in gently pulsing cilia, flickering with their own soft luminescence. They occasionally discharge shimmering clouds of microscopic larvae upward toward the few air currents that drift past.

Scuttling through the debris on the cavern floor and along the edges of the fungal growths are small, sleek predators. Vaguely reptilian or insectoid in form, fast and flattened, they hunt with clusters of stalked eyes capable of detecting the faintest heat signatures or the almost-imperceptible tremors of prey. Many species exhibit remarkable camouflage, their armored plates shifting colors and textures to mimic their surroundings. Some possess short-range bioluminescence themselves, luring the smaller creatures close with false fungal lights before striking with extensible, spiked appendages.

Higher up, attached to the cavern ceilings and upper walls, thick carpets of bioluminescent fungi and lichen pulsate with an eerie life. These organisms are the primary light sources in the cavern, their rhythmic glow synchronized through chemical connections. Types of dense lichen with vein-like patterns cover every inch of the volcanic glass ruins, providing both a food source and shelter for a variety of small, skittering scavengers.

Hunting in the upper reaches of the cavern are large, though still lightweight, predators that glide silently through the air. Fragile, leather-winged creatures whose bones must be thin and hollow. They have vast, cupped ears for detecting the faintest rustle of prey far below, and enormous, multifaceted eyes that gather and amplify every stray photon of the dim light. Their slow wingbeats are coordinated, barely stirring the air as they drift in the dark, hunting by the fungal light.

Vertebrate life is present in small numbers, slithering, legless hunters with heavy, heat-sensing pits lining their snouts. They move with surprising speed for their bulk when ambushing prey, always remaining pressed low to the ground, absorbing heat from the volcanic floor. Once a year, they gather near geothermally heated pools to lay leathery eggs into the warm rock.

The veins of singing crystal are themselves covered with strange growths — perhaps symbiotic organisms that harvest the subtle vibrations, their pulsating surfaces emitting faint, subsonic hums. This suggests a deeper level to the ecosystem, where the distinction between biology and mineral is blurred.

Nothing is wasted. Even the dried, hollow exoskeletons of the mineral-eaters serve as microhabitats for tiny, wormlike things that weave shimmering threads of iridescent silk inside to build intricate, hidden structures. The air is heavy with the constant, quiet industry of life, a self-contained world sustained by the planet's inner heat, thriving in absolute isolation since volcanic activity sealed it away.
