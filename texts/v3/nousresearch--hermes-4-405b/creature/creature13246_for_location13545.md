The inhabitants of this place move with a ponderous, deliberate grace, as if their bodies, too, are molded from the wet stone and fertile soil. They wear thick layers of undyed wool, the fabric heavy and damp, clinging to their forms, each layer saturated with the same scent of moss and rain and woodsmoke that permeates the air. The youngest among them have skin like smooth river stones, unblemished and cool to the touch, while the eldest are as gnarled and weathered as ancient junipers, their flesh mapped with crevices, their hands telling stories of a life spent shaping stone and wood.

Their bones are dense, heavy, as if crafted by the slow accumulation of minerals, as if their frames gradually condensed from the mountain mists. Each step is deliberate, each gesture unhurried—as if they measure time in the growing of ferns and the weathering of granite. Their eyes hold the deep, impenetrable stillness of forest pools. When they speak, it is in tones as soft and measured as the drip of water from stone to stone, their words seeming to emerge from a place of profound contemplation.

They meet the world with a kind of distant attention, their presence quiet and constant, like the mosses that grow over every surface. Their lives are marked by rituals tied to the turning of the seasons and the endless cycle of water, rituals carried out with a solemn tenderness. The few children among them are slow to speak, their minds as yet untouched by the hurrying thoughts of the world below. They appear to absorb the world wholly, with a completeness of presence that seems to know no haste.

Though their bodies appear sturdy, even formidable in their solidity, there is a delicacy in their movements—a reluctance to disturb the quiet balance of their world. They seem as much a part of this place as the stones that emerge from the earth, as the water that sings in perpetual chorus, as the mosses that spread like a second skin over every living surface.

Their days are spent tending to the slow work of living. They coax tiny gardens from the thin soil, the plants lush and green despite the chill. Inside the rough wooden structures, fires burn low, tended with careful devotion. These are not hearths for wild celebration but quiet hearts, centers around which life gathers and holds.

At night, when the highland mists roll in, their world contracts into small pockets of warmth and lamplight. The communal hall fills with the soft murmur of voices and the gentle clatter of tools. In the low light, faces glow, illuminated by the embers of the fire and the slow, deliberate pace of a life lived in deep communion with stone and water and the quiet, insistent growth of green things.
