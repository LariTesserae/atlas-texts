The beings who have claimed this place as their own are the Hewn, massive, living figures that emerge from the heart of the mountain itself. They have no set form, the shape of their bodies dictated by the rock from which they break free. Initially, they appear almost indistinguishable from the stone around them—immense, amalgamate silhouettes embedded within the cavern walls, as raw and unfinished as the grotto itself. Segmented joints of basalt take the loose suggestion of arms, legs, a torso. Raw planes of stone shift and scrape past one another with a roar that briefly joins the cacophony of the water, their movements ponderous, deliberate.

When the Hewn begin to detach, things of the earth are still theirs alone. Each emerging limb moves with tectonic weight, an avalanche in reverse. Thick digits of solid stone, glowing with residual geothermal heat, reach out to grip the edges of the cavity left behind. Slowly, a rounded ridge of rock—a brow—hinges upward from the main mass to reveal a face: two deep-set cores of amber light smolder beneath, their gaze sweeping the cavern, taking in the submerged world around them.

They breathe in, and it is a sound like wind drawn through deep caves. It seems to pull at the very air—a hollow, rushing inhalation that tugs at your ears. Then comes the exhalation, carrying on it the scent of hot stone and ancient, undisturbed places deep within the earth.

When they move through the water, their forms become living islands—stone skins shedding rivulets, leaving trails of cooler water swirling in their wake. Schools of the native silver-slick fish, curious and unafraid, dart around these mobile fragments of terrain, finding momentary shelter in the eddies. The Hewn pay them no mind, focused instead on their own strange communion with the mountain from which they were born.

The Hewn communicate not with words, but with intention and weight. Their rumbling vocalizations are incorporated into the mountain's own voice—a seismic murmur that speaks of tectonic shifts, of the slow pulse of magma, of minerals crystallizing under pressure, ancient and deliberate beyond human comprehension. The cavern is their cradle, their cocoon, and the water that churns here is their source, a wild and thrashing womb.

Some of the Hewn appear to be in states of perpetual emergence: half-formed giants forever in the act of tearing themselves from the living rock, their bodies caught in a timeless struggle against their own birth. Their lower halves remain fused to the cavern wall, a straining sculpture. Yet even these static figures pulse with slow life, the subtle flex and shift of stone muscles hinting at the potential energy locked within.

It is difficult to say if they are truly alive in any sense we would recognize, or if they are something else entirely—a geological process made manifest. Are they carved, or do they carve themselves? Perhaps they are the mountain's thoughts, its dreams.

They seem to share a collective consciousness, a deep, resonant awareness of the stone and the water, a single mind distributed across multiple stone bodies. Their glowing eyes, when they catch the cavern's diffuse light, convey a profound, alien intelligence—patience beyond measure, a will that operates on a timescale of epochs.

Rarely, a Hewn might detach completely from the cavern wall to become fully mobile. Their movements remain slow, each gesture unhurried, deliberate. When they do venture out, their heavy steps send palpable vibrations through the water and into the surrounding stone, announcing their presence. They traverse the submerged cavern floor or wade through the turbulent water, their forms imposing and elemental, seemingly impervious to the chaos of their environment.

Most of their number remain partially embedded, colossal figures rising from the walls of the grotto, their lower halves indistinguishable from the mountain's flesh. A constant, low scrape of stone against stone fills the air as these anchored Hewn slowly shift and turn, freed parts swiveling atop the fused, the sound a counterpoint to the roaring water.

These near-static figures serve as guardians, perhaps, or simply as part of the mountain's great, slow breath. Some, facing inward toward the cavern's heart, seem to be in silent communion with one another, their inner fires pulsing in unison. Others stand sentinel, facing out toward the churning water or the breathing room at the chamber's apex.

When the Hewn move fully, they often seem to do so with a specific, if inscrutable, purpose. One might wade into the deeper water and begin to gather the largest boulders, prying them up from the cavern floor with hands like quarrying tools. Some of these stones they bring back to add to the growing cairns that emerge from the water or cling to the cavern walls. Other stones are carried away and dropped into the roaring turbulence, where they vanish. Perhaps this is a form of worship, a ritual offering of the mountain's substance back to the water that shapes it.

Another of the Hewn might climb, heavy and implacable, to the jagged, unfinished edges of the cavern wall. They pull themselves up, massive hands finding purchase on raw rock, until they reach the cracks that weep mineral-rich water. They remain there for long stretches, seemingly unperturbed by the cascade that pours around and over them, as if drawing sustenance from the fluid or the fissure itself.

The wooden artifacts—the splintered platforms and that single, waterlogged wedge of wood—seem to be largely beneath their notice. They are remnants of a softer, smaller, more transient kind of life, now waterlogged and decaying. These objects are alien to the stone and the slow time of the Hewn. Occasionally, one of the moving figures might pause by one of the wooden clusters, a massive hand descending to prod at it, as if curious about its fragility, its difference. But such attention is fleeting, and they always turn away, leaving the wood to its slow dissolution in the dark water.

Among the Hewn, the smallest are perhaps the size of a full-grown oak, while the largest emerge as titans, their visible portions alone rivaling the great statues of antiquity. There is a sense that the Hewn are still growing, still emerging, that the cavern is not their home but a womb from which they are perpetually being born. The water that churns here is their cradle, its violent embrace a catalyst for their slow genesis.

At times, the gaze of the Hewn seems to turn inward, toward the very stone of the cavern, as if in contemplation. Their inner fires might dim and then pulse brighter in response to some silent, shared call.

The Hewn are not beings to be understood by fleeting minds. They belong to this place as much as the rock and the water. They are the life that stone dreams of when it dreams of life, emerging in this raw grotto at the top of the world.
