The beings woven into this living landscape are elemental things, their existence a continuous negotiation with the infant land:

Here, life-forms are born from steam and stone, ephemeral and ancient simultaneously. They might be perceived as obsidian-scaled lizards, their hides not merely black, but containing swirling, trapped patterns, as if tiny galaxies had been captured within cooling volcanic glass. These lizards are not creatures that simply reside; their bodies form integral parts of the rock, their slick scales indistinguishable from the condensation-slick basalt. The largest may be as small as a human hand—grafted onto the sides of boulders like dark, breathing nodes—or broad as tabletops, plates of fused obsidian shifting slowly like breathing, articulating armor over limbs that blend seamlessly into crevices and shelves of cooling rock. They are arranged in tessellated patterns across some of the steeper, steam-wreathed faces, so perfectly integrated that only the occasional slow blink of an eye—a wet, dark slit opening and closing—or a subtle shift in the surface of their crystalline bodies reveals them. Their movement is the repositioning of a limb that is also a natural shelf, a slow exhalation released through fissures that might be mistaken for venting steam. They process minerals directly from the rock they are fused with and from the thick, warm moisture in the air, filtering it into their crystalline structures, a slow, continuous feeding. These lizards are not repelled by open geothermal vents, but instead make up a significant portion of the structures immediately surrounding some of the steaming pools and fissures, for this is the most direct source of both heat and dissolved minerals. The steam condenses and trickles down the glassy black rock before being wicked up and absorbed into their bodies.

Clouds of six-legged insects, thick enough to cast their own localized shadows on the uneven stone, contain individuals that are not fully independent. These insectile forms appear to function as transient subunits of a larger, hive-like superorganism embedded throughout the basin. The superorganism, perhaps a vast, distributed fungal mesh or a gelatinous, fibrous network spread through the warm soil and interlinked via root-like strands and pulsing veins under the moss and soil, experiences the basin through these tiny, semi-autonomous eyes, tentacles, and neural extensions. The insects aren't born; they are grown from the superorganism’s substance, exuded from countless tiny orifices in the rock face, the soil, or the bases of the larger plants and fungi. These unformed globules quickly coalesce, differentiate, and harden into an insect form once separated, their carapaces still soft and damp when they first break away. Their lives are extremely brief, expiring within hours or even minutes. Some seem to return to the superorganism and dissolve back into its mass as they die, sinking into soft earth or being reabsorbed at the points from which they emerged. They never stop moving, a constant, restless, chittering chorus that is not merely sound, but a form of environmental data transmission back to the hive-mind. When a particularly dense cloud settles on a patch of vegetation or a steaming rock, they sometimes merge, their individual carapaces dissolving at the points of contact, temporarily rejoining into a shapeless, quivering mass of sticky, black fluid that seems to process or digest the substrate before breaking apart again into hundreds of new, identical insect forms.

High above, amidst the giant unfurling fronds, bird-like creatures, adorned with iridescent plumage, shriek—not from beaks, but from vibrating membranes stretched across the leading edges of their broad wings. These are often a shimmering, almost metallic turquoise or deep violet that reflects even the dim light filtering down from above the basin. Their voices sound like discordant, multiphonic horns, a layering of multiple notes that create harsher, dissonant chords. These creatures, too, are part of something vaster. They are the vibrant, vital, and mobile extensions or blooms of a different organizing principle: the immense plants and fungi are far more than they appear. The largest tree-ferns and obsidian-scaled fungoid growths may possess a distributed intelligence, a slow vegetative consciousness rooted deep beneath the basin, and these iridescent flyers are their ephemeral reproductive or sensory organs—cast out to carry spore or seed equivalents across the basin, shrieking their presence and hopefully finding purchase. Frequently, these winged forms intentionally crash-dive into the tangle of vegetation below. They do not fly away again. Instead, they become enmeshed as their bodies dissolve, their substance and energy reabsorbed by the parent plant or fungus. This process births new fronds, leaves, or fruiting bodies, incorporating the experiences, the coded genetic information of the “bird” as it is assimilated. The sounds they make can sometimes be seen to issue rhythmically as visible pulses of denser air, rippling outwards.

Among the animals, there are soft-bodied, warm-blooded things that resemble skinned, reddish-pink, or bruise-purple mammals—large, segmented worms or colossal, hairless rodents, depending on the angle, their hides glistening wetly. They form dense, chittering colonies packed tightly into the deeper crevices and burrows within the cooling rock, their naked, segmented bodies pressed together. They huddle not only for protection, but because their collective metabolism generates intense heat, which may prevent the surrounding rock from fully cooling and hardening too quickly. They labor tirelessly, their strong, clawed forelimbs—which sometimes seem oddly shaped, like crude hands, lacking distinct fingers but broader, spade- or paddle-shaped—quarrying stone and tunneling new caves, expanding their networks. These creatures might serve as one of the primary forces breaking down the larger boulders and ensuring the finer fragmentation of rock into the soil. The soil they help produce soon becomes prospective ground for the encroaching plant life or the strange root networks of the fungoid organisms that dominate this place. These rock-burrowers may be eaten by the larger, shadowy forms that move through the dense undergrowth, but they live in wretched abundance, their numbers ensuring that their work of preparing the land continues.

Larger shapes, the true leviathans, move in the dimness at the periphery of clear sight, their forms suggested more by the violent rustle and snap of vegetation than by distinct outlines. One repeated impression is of enormous, reptilian heads, blunt and heavy-browed like those of giant lizards or crocodiles, but lacking visible eyes—their skulls are instead dominated by massive, intricate, nasal and olfactory cavities, vast pits ringed with complex bony or cartilaginous structures, constantly tasting the air. They possess exceedingly long, powerful necks that allow these sensory organs to sweep through the dense foliage with considerable reach, extending from twenty to nearly fifty feet. Their immense, barrel-shaped torsos, suspended low over the ground or partially submerged in some of the larger pools, are covered in thick, armored hides, barnacle-like and encrusted with mineral deposits, mud, and the shells of opportunistic parasites and hitchhiking organisms. On land, they move with a surprising grace, supported by thick, pillar-like legs ending in splayed, almost hand-like feet with widespread digits that distribute their enormous weight across the soft, uneven terrain. Some drag wide, flattened tails that leave temporary furrows in areas of wet soil, these marks quickly filled by the constant seep of groundwater. These massive beings may lack complex cognition as we understand it, but they are intelligent in reading the basin's subtle chemical gradients, its thermal differentials, and the pressures exerted by circulating water. They know where the rock is weakest, where the geothermal energy is strongest, where the minerals are most abundant, and it is they who carve new channels for the runoff. They are natural dredgers, using their sheer bulk to widen channels and alter the landscape, their patient, powerful movements shaping the basin, opening new areas for colonization, and ensuring that new pockets of condensation or heated pools form. Their youngest or most vulnerable state might involve a purely aquatic phase, sheltered within heated pools that serve as immense, communal nursery areas. The young are eel-like and nearly blind, recognizable by the early development of their prominent olfactory structures.

The dense vegetation itself, the huge tree ferns unfurling fronds taller than a person, the strange succulents, the vines knitting the cracks into emerald veils, are not merely passive backdrop. Some manifest as dense patches of enormous pitcher plants, their bizarrely complex, often iridescently colored traps shaped like multi-chambered organs or grasping claws, specifically designed to capture and digest not only insects, but also small vertebrates, including the abundant lizard-things and perhaps even juvenile forms of the rock-burrowers. The viscous fluid pooled within these traps isn't merely acidic; depending on the plant and its proximity to geothermal activity, it can be scalding hot, super-saturated with specific minerals scavenged from the basin’s water sources (copper greens, iron reds, sulfur yellows), or even faintly caustic and corrosive. The plants’ structures reveal how they condense atmospheric vapor with obscene efficiency, their surfaces often eerily cold to the touch despite the ambient warmth. They might channel this collected water, along with absorbed rainwater, along specialized grooves and channels directly into their digestive pools, which become filled with captured prey and the resulting nutrient broth. Other forms of life here derive nourishment not just by killing and consuming in the familiar sense. Some ferns or succulents achieve gigantism by directly incorporating other, smaller organisms into their own structure as they grow—engulfing slow-moving insects, tiny vertebrates, or even encrusting the shells of the six-legged insects as mineral inclusions or sources of complex organic molecules.

This churning, hyperactive, almost frantic ecosystem has a cyclical or rhythmic thrum to it, driven by vibrations emanating from deep below the basin's floor. A slow, powerful pulse, like a subterranean heartbeat, seems to originate from far beneath the surface, causing the water to ripple periodically with thrumming waves and directing the steam plumes to synchronize into taller, more defined columnar shapes with each beat. At these moments of peak intensity, the inhabitants react: the calls of the winged bird-like creatures might swell into louder, more intense, almost painfully dissonant choruses then abruptly fall silent; the rock-burrowing creatures might pause their constant digging, raise their eyeless faces and glistening bodies from their burrows, turning them towards the source of the vibrations before resuming their toil with renewed energy upon the pulse's fade. Even the steam's pitch and intensity, or the subtle shifts in color of the bioluminescent fungi, could be tied to these vibrations. The impression is that more ephemeral entities coalesce or become visible during these cycles—the six-legged insects may frequently gather in vastly denser numbers, forming shapes that could be schematic representations of symbols, maps, or leviathan organs in the few patches of open ground before dissolving again as the pulse fades. The entire basin itself seems to be inching forward, spreading at its undefined edges as new volcanic rock cools and is rapidly colonized, as tunnels are methodically expanded by the rock-burrowers, and as the patient movement of the massive, low-slung crawlers alters the landscape with every colossal step.
