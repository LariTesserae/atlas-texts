You stand at the edge, hesitant, breath frosting instantaneously in the thin, gelid air. The first thing to strike you—once the mind-bending *scale* of the plateau recedes from immediate shock—is the cold. Not just a temperature but a sensation—a pervasive, penetrating *wetness* that sinks past layers and into bone. The second thing is the sheer incomprehensible immensity. It stretches out level and horizonless, a flat dark plane that merges with the oppressive twilight sky at some indefinable point in the distance. Even with some form of dim and sourceless light bleeding from fixtures or the built environment itself, the plateau is shrouded in near-total darkness. The light only serves to throw the structures into silhouette, deepening the shadows.

You step forward slowly. Your boots crunch against the dark ground—solid, unyielding stone. The air is unnervingly empty of familiar scents; there's no dirt or rain, only the cold sterile tang of wet rock and *something else*, something slightly metallic and musky. It doesn't smell like anywhere you've been.

Gradually, you begin to hear the plateau. Low frequencies shake the ground beneath you—a near-subsonic thrumming that vibrates through stone. There are the wet sounds of shifting weight, of stone scraping against stone, of bodies moving with ponderous slowness. A heavy, continuous rustling and snapping, like thick reeds in an impossibly slow wind or stone grinding against stone under immense pressure. This is not an empty place. You feel the thrum transmitted through the stone—a suggestion of movement you cannot yet see.

It's a sound felt as much as heard, a deep vibration carried by the air and conducted through the stone beneath your feet. This is not a sound of emptiness. It is the sound of something immense *shifting*. A sound so low you feel it in your bones, a thrumming in your skull. A sound that suggests something patient, something ancient.

The scale becomes more vivid with each step. The horizon remains a flat, barely perceptible line. Above you the sky is a uniform leaden haze devoid of stars. You are small here—very small. The horizon rearranges itself around you as you advance. The feeling of vulnerability is intense.

When you do begin to see them, at first you are sure they must be geological formations—rocks to massive to register as living things. Great mounds of dark, wet stone litter what appears to be a city street. More accurate: whole city blocks made of stacked boulders, leaning against the sides of cyclopean buildings that rise toward the dark sky like the ribs of petrified giants. Huge, slow mounds of something heavy and dark that swallow the feeble light.

It is only when one such hump, indistinguishable from the others, ripples with a slow inner movement that you freeze. You stand rooted, realizing the landscape is not still. Not rock, not piled debris—all around you the ground is alive.

It is a jarring moment of recalibration. What you took for hills or stacked rubble is flesh—a seamless transition between geology and biology. At first, it is impossible to place these entities in a familiar framework of life; they seem like shapes formed from the same black stone that constitutes the plateau itself. But then, you see a slow flex, a change in a silhouette too smooth for stone—and gradually, they resolve into figures.

They are *large*, more like landscapes than animals—vast, rounded bodies set low to the ground, their surface a rough, wet-looking hide, dark as the surrounding rock. Smooth, eyeless faces shielded beneath heavy carapaces emerging from the body like barnacles on a ship's hull or keels on an upturned boat. They move not with leg-based locomotion but by some undulating wave of muscle beneath their thick hides, redistributing weight in slow, patient rhythms. Each undulation weighs tons: years rolled into slow forward motion. Stone grinds beneath them and you realize, with a jolt of slowly dawning awe, that this is the source of at least some of the world's deep vibration.

They are not solitary creatures. In every open space, they cluster together, swallowing courtyards in their bulk. Open spaces are almost entirely blocked by mounded creatures, bodies pressed wetly together. They gather, their bodies pressed tightly into clusters that seem to form their own topography—hills of flesh rising from the ground. Streets become impassable, choked with bodies where the creatures press together into long, shifting masses lying side-to-side. As their bodies shift, rock is pulverized beneath them.

As you watch, you feel no sense of being observed. These creatures possess no visible eyes, and their movements continue uninterrupted, driven by some internal rhythm or external pressure you can't perceive.

This is not a place in which you would walk easily. It is *crowded* with their overlapping presences. The air vibrates with the sounds—a low, wet thunder of inhalations and exhalations, the crunch of stones under their vast weight, and the rasp of their hides sliding heavily against each other. Each movement seems to take minutes; perhaps hours. There is no hurry here. The pace is glacial, slow, the rhythm of a world whose year might be centuries long. Some of these creatures are in constant motion, others lie still for long minutes, maybe hours, before shifting with a sound like continents grinding.

As your eyes adjust to the dim light, you see smaller things moving among the great bodies. Pale, sluglike creatures streaked with dark, moving with eerie sureness around and *over* the larger animals. They leave trails of faint phosphorescence on the cold stone. Their bodies ripple in slow waves—gills opening and closing beneath thin, wet-looking skin to reveal glimpses of some complex internal anatomy, like gears meshing or molars grinding in slow motion. They are forming long, weaving chains—hundreds strong—flowing like rivers between the great bodies. They pool in depressions in the landscape, gathering in circular masses like pale, pulsating jellyfish. Occasionally, one will crawl slowly over the hide of one of the larger beasts, and both creatures seem entirely untroubled by the contact.

These smaller creatures are not slugs or worms, but something stranger—soft-bodied things with segmented bodies and strange, millipede-like legs that end in flat, webbed feet, constantly testing the surfaces. They vastly outnumber the larger species. They ooze over rails and spill down gutters, dropping with wet splats from overhangs constantly.

In some places, entire walls are coated with them, forming living curtains of flesh that obscure the built structures entirely. They seem to feed off the city itself, eating stone or minerals or something too deeply ingrained to wash away.

You watch one such building slowly disappear, its surface transformed by the writhing movement. It's impossible to say if the creatures are destroying it or becoming part of it. Boundaries here are permeable.

Water has gathered here—not as pools or rivers, but dewlike, condensed upon surfaces, frozen in black sheens. It beads across the built landscape, a rime of captured cold. The creatures' bodies are often covered in a layer of it. Here, water seems to emerge from deep within the landscape. It gathers in hollows and depressions, at the foot of walls, and along the seams of broken paving stones. It never quite escapes the stone, never flows out freely—it is trapped, frozen, a wetness that binds landscapes together. A universe of captured moisture.

The city itself seems to participate in this cycle. Some walls are thick with frozen liquid water, bloated like waterlogged corpses. Others look to have dissolved, collapsed into piles of smooth, wet stones.

The light hasn't changed. It is the same uniform near-absence, the same cold and sourceless illumination that throws everything into silhouette. Occasional fixtures bleed pale light into the air—or seep from the built environment itself. The world remains painted in cold phosphorescence, but somehow the creatures seem less hampered by it than you are. Their lives, defined by different rhythms, operate on different scales.

The structures themselves seem to be changing as if in response to pressures still at work below the landscape. The landscape and its inhabitants *belong* to each other, shaping and reshaping over timescales beyond comprehension. As if this place were not a single moment in time, but many moments, many eras, existing simultaneously. A very slow-forming world, and in its own way, beautiful.

It's impossible to imagine them anywhere else—these immense creatures belong to the high plateau, to the cold, to the mineral darkness. They are its embodiment, its animating force.
