The beings that inhabit this living city are not separate from it; they *are* the city, or rather, an emergent expression of its continuous, feverish creation. They are essentially highly mobile, temporary organs of the vast, collective plant-organism that constitutes the metropolis.

Imagine them as pulsating, ambulatory nodes of biological activity, varying in size. They are not possessors of a fixed form in the way animals are; their shapes are fluid, dictated by immediate need and the flow of vital fluids within. Many might resemble enormous, sessile seed pods or galls that, when the city's internal pressure demands, break free from their moorings on the larger structures. They might unfurl cilia-like, root-feet or extend pseudopods of solidified sap or resin to pull themselves along, their movements a slow, deliberate ooze or a surprisingly sudden, elastic snap if they need to traverse a gap or ascend a sheer wall. Their "bodies" would be composed of the same dense, fibrous plant matter, resinous secretions, and crystalline encrustations as the city itself. When they move, they leave trails of the same thick, fibrous mulch that carpets the ground, or perhaps a smear of sticky, rapidly solidifying fluid that might itself become a new, tiny growth.

They lack faces or discernible heads; their "perception" is perhaps osmotic, a direct exchange of chemical signals with their immediate environment, or they might "see" via light patterns refracted through their crystalline components. Their interaction with the city is one of constant exchange. Some of these beings might act as localized processing units, drawing in the condensation and air to synthesize new materials. Others could be "constructors" or "masons," not building consciously, but secreting layers of resinous material onto existing structures, guided by the city's inherent growth patterns, their bodies visibly thinning and deflating as they lay down new building material. If one were to touch a stagnant pool of water, it might visibly absorb it, its tissues becoming turgid and perhaps causing a new, temporary limb to sprout for locomotion. Conversely, if a section of a mobile entity dries out, it might simply shrivel, break off, and fall to the ground, becoming mulch to be reabsorbed elsewhere.

There's no central nervous system or individual consciousness. Instead, each mobile entity is a node in a vast, decentralized network. Their "communication" would be a continuous flow of biochemical signals, perhaps through the very air, laden with moisture and spores, or through direct fluidic exchange when they touch. The low, vibrant hum of the city might be the sound of this collective, a symphony of biological processes resonating through theplant-structures and the mobile entities alike, coordinating growth, decay, and the movement of these strange, temporary inhabitants.

When they are "spent," perhaps having distributed their biological payloads or fulfilled a circulatory function within the city, they simply settle. A mobn might slow its oozing movement, its cilia retracting, and it would begin to put down more permanent, root-like anchors, its form solidifying, becoming part of the structure it rests against. Its individual, pulsating character fades as it integrates, its energies dispersing back into the collective, its former body now just another nodule, another facet in the ever-expanding, ever-collapsing architecture of this living metropolis.

Thus, the city's inhabitants are not citizens in the human sense, but rather a dynamic, fluid populace of temporary, semi-autonomous growths, entirely dedicated to the continued, chaotic, and magnificent self-construction of their world. They are the hands, the breath, and the very pulse of this living, steaming, weeping city.
