This city is not barren, despite the thirst of its air: it is densely inhabited, every crevice and ledge teeming with organisms uniquely adapted to the rhythms of its dry, sun-baked stone. The inhabitants, however, are as strange and ambiguous as the city itself, their forms hovering in an unsettling limbo between fauna and flora, between arid survival and surreal profusion.

The most ubiquitous dwellers are the small, lizard-like creatures clambering over the walls and leaves. At first glance, they appear to be nothing more than unassuming reptiles, armored in thick, grey plates that blend seamlessly with the thirsty beige of the city. They move in eerie quiet—a slow, vertical drift that speaks more of growth than locomotion. Yet, these are no ordinary lizards: they exhibit a startling disregard for gravity, clinging effortlessly to sheer surfaces. Their skin possesses an almost fungal resilience, resembling chitinous armor but flexing with a slow vegetable suppleness. The creatures neither hunt nor scavenge in any conventional sense. They do not move in pursuit of food; they seem rather to *gather*, accumulating in dense clusters on the broad, flat leaves of the city’s pervasive foliage, their tiny, patient forms becoming more indistinguishable from the plant-matter day by day. Perhaps they feed on sunlight or the minerals absorbed by the leaves. Perhaps, over slow decades, the plants absorb *them*, drawing sustenance from their armored husks.

These small beings share the city with far larger, stranger forms. Throughout the dense vegetation and shadowed corners of the architecture, the partial remains of immense beings protrude—ambiguously shaped limbs like petrified wood or gnarled roots, fossilized carapaces, and broad, ridged plates that might be bone, or bark, or something altogether different. These remains are so ancient, so calcified by the dry air and slow time of the city, that it is impossible to discern whether they are long dead or simply dormant, growing at a rate beyond perception. Do these larger beings continue to live, sustained by subterranean sources of moisture or by the patient, mineral-rich sap that must run within the city's bones? Or are they the last, petrified remnants of a vanished generation, their substance slowly being reclaimed by the relentless advance of the stone and the thirsty roots that snake through every fissure?

Their forms are disturbingly ambiguous, possessing an aesthetic quality that suggests both life and stillness in equal measure, blending seamlessly with the city's structure. Many appear to be more plant than animal: broad, rough-barked appendages like tree-limbs jut from the ground or dangle from overhangs, some bearing the scars of broken-off branches. Some have shells or carapaces that are clearly hollow, the weathered apertures revealing only deeper darkness or the dim shapes of structures growing within. The walls of many buildings form shelves and protrusions that seem to have coalesced around these petrified creatures, the city’s growth having incorporated their remains as though they were natural features of the landscape. In courtyards and sheltered alleyways, immense, bracket-fungus-like shelves—taller than doorways—crawl with the smaller lizard-things, their motionless bulk suggesting they are either the shelter, or perhaps the sustenance, of their smaller neighbors.

Even the foliage of the city, which blankets every surface, seems to defy easy categorization. The plants’ leaves are huge and thick, storing precious moisture against lean times. Their trunks and stems score the angles with unexpected curves, much like the city’s architecture. But the pale, fleshy growths that fold like hands or bracket fungus among the plants—do these belong to the plants, or are they something else? Do they parasitize the plants, or are they a reproductive phase of some hybrid organism, released into the air only when the dry winds blow?

For there are days, rare days, when a thin, papery whisper drifts on those same still, parched breezes. A fine, almost invisible dust blows through the city, catching the sunlight in a slow, glittering dance. This evanescent spore-storm carries the promise of new life—or perhaps merely the seeds of endless replication, spreading the city's strange, dry vitality to new terrains.

No wings beat at these heights, no chittering or buzzing disrupts the drone of silence. The smaller lizard-things communicate—if such a term applies—only through the subtlest shifts in their clustering, through slow redistributions of weight on leaf or stone. Their silence, too, feels like held breath, punctuated only by the dry rustle of their passing and the rare sigh of the spore-wind. They move in patterns that seem less like foraging and more like a slow, ritualistic migration, or perhaps the gradual reshuffling of stars in a firmament.

They require water, these beings, as all life must—but the water they crave is locked away inside the plants and the stone, and they have grown cunning at extracting it. They live in a constant, patient negotiation with thirst. And with each other: the lizard-things browse the thick moss that grows on the petrified carapaces of their larger kindred, picking at it with deliberate care, as though tending a garden. The plants, in turn, may draw some nourishment from the armored hides of the creatures that rest upon them. The city itself, alive or dead, probably feeds on them all, in its slow, stone way.

These inhabitants show no awareness of an outside world beyond the city's sun-baked stones. Their rhythms are circadian, measured by the slow crawl of shadows across the city’s curves, by the gradual warming and cooling of the thirsty stone. They do not build, do not gather, beyond the slow accumulation of their own bodies in the clusters that litter every surface. They are content to simply *be*, to exist as an integral part of the city’s fabric, as unremarkable and essential as the stones and the dust. In this place, life itself has been subdued to the patient tempo of geology, of slow-grown stone and petrified wood, of silent lichen creeping imperceptibly across still greater silences. They are as much a part of the city as its stones and its strange, thirsty foliage—a living, breathing ecosystem thriving impossibly in the high, dry air, forever caught between life and stone.
