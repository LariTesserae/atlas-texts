The inhabitants of the City of Frozen Moss are as much a part of its landscape as the emerald-green thickets and the dripping condensation—a people inseparable from the environment they inhabit, their lives and customs woven into the very fabric of their hidden world.

Their physiology reflects the constraints and the climate of their city. They are a diminutive people, their small stature an evolved adaptation to a place where narrow passages and close-built, low dwellings dominate. Most adults stand little more than four feet tall, obliging them to move with a practiced efficiency through the arm's-width walkways. This slightness does not denote fragility, however; their frames, though small, are sturdy, having developed beneath layers of warm hide and fur they wear as a second skin.

Pale as the crystal-filtered light that cascades from the cavern ceiling above, their skin has seen neither harsh weather nor harsher sun. The cool, damp air imparts a translucent quality to their features—a network of delicate veins sometimes visible beneath the surface. Many of the adults exhibit a similar pattern of weathering, faces marked by the need to navigate countless years in close quarters: fine, whisper-thin scars caught by low doorframes or the rough edges of stone walls, noses subtly flattened from incautious turns in the tight, narrow passages. 

Their hands, calloused and worn, tell the silent story of a people intimately familiar in the bones of their city. Fingers, thick-knuckled and strong, handle even the most slippery stones with confidence. Every adult in the city knows these passageways by feel, their eyes often downcast, their movements economical and purposeful, as if conserving energy in a world that demands quiet and care. When they speak, their voices are soft, the syllables formed with a measured cadence that seems almost to blend with the mossy silence around them. Confrontation and loud outbursts are nonexistent. Emotions, when expressed, are muted.

Every aspect of life here is shaped by the city's unique rhythms. Birth is hushed; death is met with the same stoic acceptance. Even the rare celebrations are subdued, the quiet thud of a wooden bowl set down, the soft clink of hand-carved bone dice being passed from one small, practiced hand to another. Their eyes seem too large for their faces, pupils wide to capture the strange, bright light that filters down from the crystal above. It gives them a permanently startled look, as if they are seeing the world for the first time, gazing into mysteries that are whispered in the language of the moss.

In their quiet, unchanging routines, they maintain the city’s equilibrium. They harvest moss, cultivating it where it grows thickest, letting it wither where it becomes unruly—a slow, meditative act that fits the pace of their lives. The ever-present vegetation feeds them, with wild ferns and small, hardy shrubs providing sustenance. They dig the dark soil made fertile by blind, pale earthworms, coaxing forth enough to keep starvation at bay in a place perpetually cold and damp.

Their clothing—layers of cured hides and dense fur from the tough mountain sheep whose ancestors wandered into this subterranean refuge centuries ago—sheds water and holds warmth, a necessary adaptation to the cool, moist air. The garments, like everything else, speak of lives adapted to quiet endurance. There are no vibrant dyes; the leather is worked with the same patient caution used in navigating the narrow passageways, stained only by the moss that clings to every surface.

Their tools—crafted from locally gathered bone and polished stone—reflect a technology both primitive and perfectly attuned to their needs. They have no metal, no fire forged in the hot world outside. Instead, bone scrapers, chisels, and hammers shape the timber they use to expand or repair their dwellings. Wooden bowls and utensils serve their meals, each piece hand-carved over months of nearly soundless work. Rope, twisted from the sinews of those same mountain sheep, binds together the timber beams that shore up their homes.

Even the fauna exists in harmony with these quiet inhabitants. Dark, moss-backed beetles share the damp walls with the inhabitants; pale, near-translucent spiders build webs in undisturbed corners of dwellings. Small birds, their muted plumage blending seamlessly with the moss, flit through the hanging vines, their high, thin calls broken only by the slow drip of condensation. The sight and sounds do not startle the inhabitants; they are part of the texture of life here, quiet threads woven through the damp, luminous air.

Generations are born into this unchanging city, its form set lifetimes ago, its patterns etched into the very souls of those who live out their days beneath the crystal ceiling. Their lives run like the slow drip of water over mossy stone—a steady rhythm that needs little prompting. Progress, as it is known elsewhere, does not occur here. There is no need to change when the city itself is perfect, its overgrown, saturated world a secret enclave flourishing in a hidden chamber.

In every respect, these people are part of their city, shaped by its constraints and enriched by its beauty. They are the silent citizens of a realm defined by cool, enduring resilience, a city of moss flourishing in a sunken world. And so, they go about their lives—quiet, steadfast, inseparable from the moss and the stone—as if it is the only life they can ever know.
