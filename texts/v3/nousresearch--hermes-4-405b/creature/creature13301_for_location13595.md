Inhabiting this plateau of Wet Industry is a species sculpted by the damp, the cold, and the relentless hum of creation. They are the Tunka—a people whose forms echo the weathered shells of the city itself, whose lives pulse in slow rhythm with the deep engines beneath their feet.

**Physiology:**
They are not tall, these Tunka. Generations of damp, heavy air have given them a somewhat stocky, powerful build, their bones dense. Their skin is often the color of wet slate or the deep rust of iron-rich water, patterned with faint, naturally occurring mineral veins that sometimes catch the dim light. It is thick, almost leathery, able to shed the constant moisture and resist the chill. A fine down, almost invisible but incredibly water-resistant, covers their bodies, offering insulation.

Their hands are their most distinctive feature: broad, with spatulate fingers ending in thick, calloused pads. These are not delicate digits; they are tools, hardened by generations of manipulating heavy, cold, wet equipment, capable of gripping oily steel and resisting sharp edges. Their movements are deliberate, unhurried.

Eyes are large, dark, and protected by heavy, ridged brows and thick lashes that frequently bead with moisture. These eyes are adapted to the perpetual, diffuse twilight under the hazed sky, perhaps seeing further into the infrared spectrum to better discern the heat signatures of functioning machinery or warm pipes in the gloom. Their vision focuses slowly, deliberately.

Their faces are broad, often with a distant, contemplative expression. They possess a limited range of facial expressions by human standards, a stoicism that matches the slow, inexorable pace of their mechanical environment. Their ears, small and perhaps slightly pointed, are not for acute hearing but are highly sensitive to vibrations – a crucial sense when the air is filled with the *deafening clangor* of giant machines, allowing them to distinguish subtle shifts in the deep pulse of the city.

Clothing is functional, utilitarian. Robes or tunics of tightly woven, dark, water-resistant fibers. Hoods are common, often pulled low against the constant drip. Footwear is heavy, thick-soled, designed for treacherous, slick surfaces.

**Society and Mindset:**
The Tunka operate on "city time"—slow, generational time. Patience is their defining cultural trait. A sense of harried urgency is alien to them; their processes, like those of their vast machines, unfold with a ponderous, unavoidable certainty. Projects are conceived, initiated, and completed across decades, even centuries. This lends them an air of immense, unshakable calm.

Their language, the **"Clangor-Tongue,"** is low, resonant, and incorporates many vibratory elements, almost a tactile language through bone conduction. It is a language of sustained tones, capable of conveying complex information slowly, precisely, suited to the ambient noise of their world. They might also have a system of physical gestures, slow and deliberate, to supplement communication in particularly noisy zones or over distances where subtler voice might be lost.

Their society is built on deep, abiding connections to their work-kin and their immediate community units, often tied to a specific factory complex or industrial sector. These units are their world. Individualism, as a concept of standing out or forging a unique, rapid path, is minimal. They find identity and purpose within the slow, collective effort. Distinction comes with demonstrable skill, meticulousness, and an intuitive understanding of the ancient machines.

Divisions are less by class and more by function, by one's role within the living machine of the city. Those who tend the deepest, oldest engines are figures of quiet reverence, their knowledge both practical and arcane.

**Relationship with the Environment:**
The "bleak, functional beauty" of the Wet Industry is not bleak to them; it is the very texture of their existence. The *deafening clangor* is not oppressive; it is the breath, the heartbeat, the ambient sonification of their world, a constant they are born into and die within. Their art, should it exist, would not be representational of nature (for what nature is there but tenacious weeds and mineral stains?) but rather a celebration of precise engineering, elegant mechanics, the slow dance of colossal gears. Patterns of rust and water erosion on their dwellings might be frames of reference, points of contemplation.

They are masters of recycling, of coaxing function from ancient, weathered components. They understand stress fractures and material fatigue not as failures, but as the inevitable song of aging machinery, to be listened to and patched, its lifespan extended.

Their homes are often integrated into the industrial architecture – chambers carved from the warm shells of dormant (or semi-dormant) machines,platforms nestled within vast, cathedral-like factory halls. Comfort is warmth, dryness (relative to the outside), and the rhythmic vibration of the city's operational hum.

They are not an expansive people. The desolate nature of the plateau and the self-contained nature of their industrial existence have likely kept them largely isolated. They are content, perhaps, within the boundaries of their known world, their understanding of the universe mapped onto the exposed pipes and conduits of their metallic ecosystem, their cosmology a vast, interconnected machine.

Fauna like the scaled creatures in conduits or the insects by warm pipes are known, perhaps even named, minor elements in the grand industrial ecosystem, neither pests nor pets, just co-inhabitants.

Rain is a constant architect, sculpting their city, and they live with its results, channeling it, living with its slickness, listening to it gurgle through the deep systems. The cold is a constant adversary, met with inner warmth, communal hearths (likely powered by steam or electrical bleed-off), and their resilient physiology.

They are a people of immense, quiet endurance, guardians and operators of this ancient, eternally working plateau. Their history is the history of these structures, their future the continued, slow pulse of its machinery, echoing into an unguessable, wet, and metallic forever.
