In this sunken world, the primary inhabitants are the sessile architects themselves. They are not solitary organisms, but vast colonial beings, sprawling across every surface in interconnected networks. These are not coral, as we know them; they are something far older and stranger, their biology adapted to extract dissolved minerals directly from the water and secrete them as stone. Their growth is imperceptibly slow, a patience etched into the very bones of the world. They form the bedrock, the reefs, the towering spires—every structure is a living monument to their slow, collective industry.

Each colony, though genetically distinct, is indistinguishable from its neighbors at a distance. It is only upon closer inspection that variations emerge: minute differences in the texture of their secreted stone, the patterns of their growth, the subtle shades of their bioluminescent glow. This glow is not for illumination—it serves no purpose in the eternal dim. Instead, it is the slow exhalation of their metabolism, a visible manifestation of their life processes.

Coexisting with the sessile architects are the floating gardens. These are the strange, swollen tubers and fleshy pads that drift languidly through the water. Like the architects, they are ancient, their biology intricately adapted to this world. They possess no roots, but long, trailing filaments that brush against the architects' stone, absorbing nutrients from the water. Some possess gas bladders, allowing them to rise and fall with the slow currents, while others are anchored, bobbing gently in the stillness. Their bioluminescence is more varied than that of the architects: some glow with a steady, unwavering light, while others pulse with slow, rhythmic patterns, like the beating of a vast, invisible heart.

There are no swimmers here, no crawlers or burrowers. The only movement is the slow drift of the floating gardens, the even slower growth of the architects. There is no predation, no competition beyond the silent, endless struggle for space and nutrients.

This is a world of cooperation, of countless individual organisms working in concert to create something greater than themselves. It is a world of quiet, of near-total stillness, of life processes unfolding over millennia. It is a world that has reached its final, perfect form—a world that will endure, unchanged, until the sun itself burns out.
