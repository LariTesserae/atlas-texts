The beings who made this place belonged to a species utterly unlike any known on Earth. Their nature defined this world, from the scale of its constructions to the haunting void left by their absence. We can call them the Masons.

Physically, the Masons were beings of pure mineral life. Their bodies were composed of a complex, stable form of organic stone, a substance capable of slow but deliberate metabolism, growth, and repair that drew energy from sunlight and trace minerals dissolved in water. Imagine a creature whose "flesh" is a pale, marbled substance, slightly yielding but resilient, with the smoothness of polished limestone and a subtle, veined patterning.

The Masons had no internal skeleton; their entire frame was composed of thick strata of dense, impact-resistant organic stone, built up in layers that allowed for movement through incredibly slow, incremental adjustments in posture and position. This accounts for the sheer density and tight packing of their massive constructions: for the Masons, built environments were not obstacles to movement but constitutive parts of their very bodies. Their architecture and physiology seamlessly merged.

Their form was likely simple and monumental, perhaps resembling immense, abstract torsos anchored by broad, stable bases that allowed standing or sitting with a permanence verging on geological time. Their limbs were probably thick and trunk-like, capable of generating immense force but moving with a slowness imperceptible to ephemeral creatures. The features of their constructions reflect the Masons' essence. The faint, almost imperceptible irregularities in every block speak to the deliberate pace of their work—each quirks is not a mistake but a subtle expression of the time and care taken. For the Masons, to build was to live. Their massive structures were not just habitations but may have comprised integral parts of their physiology, with courtyards acting as shared metabolic spaces and water channels serving as circulatory systems that nourished entire families or work groups, their bodies flowing into and out of the architecture. The primitive, hand-wrought nature of the stonework is not evidence of low technology but of a technology entirely alien, a mastery of slow, deliberate shaping that has no parallel in faster-paced mechanical civilizations.

The Masons' abandonment of their world was not sudden. It had the ponderous inevitability of a geological process. Perhaps the constant weep of water gradually leached essential minerals from their bodies, requiring them to deliberately extrude new layers of stone from their surfaces and incorporate these into the structures around them. Imagine them patiently exuding thick new strata of organic stone from their surfaces, the new growth pushing away the dried husks of older layers, each cycle taking centuries. Over millennia, their bodies may have become entirely integrated into the landscape, until what was once biological form became indistinguishable from the rest of the mineral world, vanishing into the built environment.

This gradual metamorphosis would not have been death as we understand it. For the Masons, it may have been the ultimate culmination of their existence—a final merging with the monuments they had painstakingly created over eons. The structures would remain, whispering of a presence that simply became identical with the world itself, enduring not as individual entities but as emergent features of a geological system they had sculpted in partnership with the forces of rain, seeping water, and relentless sunlight.
