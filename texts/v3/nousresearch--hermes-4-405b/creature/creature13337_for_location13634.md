In the dripping gloom of the sunken basin, where the old estuary once ebbed, unseen things stir. They are not so much creatures as liquid embodiments of the place itself—as though the moisture has found its own animating spark, a willful sentience born from the endless condensation.

First, you might glimpse oily fingers of dew skittering across a rusted beam in the perpetual half-light. At night, inky drips coalesce on the mossy undersides of abandoned pipes, falling, joining, reforming into something that writhes in a pool below. Under the warped floorboards of the old worker's cottages, pearlescent rivulets come together in the dark, their combined weight leaving slug-like silver trails on the creaking wood.

This water-life, it seems, requires no lungs or hearts or bones—only liquid, endless and shifting, to take form and dissolve. They move through the Drips as disturbances in the moisture-laden air, as trickles that pulse with regular rhythm along the gutters. Some days they gather in unseen masses, their formless bodies only betrayed by the strange eddies in ever-present puddles and the way moss suddenly grows lush along a particular stretch of crumbling brick.

Others, you can see more clearly. A glinting creature the size of a cat pads across a shattered window, each footfall leaving a perfect, circular bead of clear water on the jagged glass. It has eyes like fallen drops reflecting the low light, its limpid body studded with minerals. Elseath, in the flooded foundations of the collapsed factories, something huge stirs—a slow, colossal shifting that sends waves of moisture up through the cracks in the basement floors, dampening the air even further.

These beings have never been named; perhaps they have no names. Their language, if they have one, is not spoken in words. It comes instead in patterns—an echo of drips that seem to answer one another, a momentary synchronization in the endless drizzle. Perhaps they communicate in the yielding surface of a deep puddle, or in the sudden shiver that runs through a long-abandoned gas pipe.

In the perpetual twilight, these water-formed denizens move in a dreamlike dance. They re-form with every rain, dissolve into mist when the air grows thin, and pool again when night falls. Time is nothing to them but the slow turning of the seasons—an interminable cycle of wet and wetter still. In the summer, the heat brings strange new forms of mineral and algae clinging to their liquid bodies; in winter they freeze and thaw into ever more complex crystalline lattices, the cold drawing intricate patterns into their skin.

Everywhere in the Drips, the water breathes. It rises from the deep earth as fog that drifts between the leaning buildings, it seeps through the bricks like some ancient circulatory system, and it falls again from every surface. The liquid denizens are not so much born of this water as they are the water dreaming of itself—each of them a transient manifestation of the rain, the seep, the drip. They make no nests, build no homes, but instead inhabit every puddle and pool and weeping surface. They slip between dimensions in the perpetual gloom, existing in the interstices where water becomes magic and magic becomes water again.

At times their forms are almost human—a shimmering figure that seems to beckon from the far end of an alley, its outline blurred by the ever-falling rain. But this is an illusion, an echo of the human forms they once mirrored in the reflections of rain-stained windows and pooled gutter-water. These phantoms are the Drips made flesh, and they are as fluid and formless as their homeland. To look at one too hard is to watch it dissolve back into the damp air that birthed it—only to re-form elsewhere, an eternal cycle of dissolution and rebirth.

As the sun sinks even lower in the perpetually lowering sky, the light turns the dripping world to mercury and shadow. These water-phantoms move through it, skimming the pools and slipping into crevices, their forms glinting in the weak light that filters through the stained glass of forgotten windows and moss-covered skylights. They are the stillness of algae-covered statues and the sudden rush of a drain overflowing, both at once. They are the Drips dreaming, and the dream is slow and wet and heavy—a twilight that never quite ends, a damp that seeps into everything, a landscape forever becoming something new from the endless drip of water on stone.
