In the lightless crush of the Floor, life does not breathe; it grinds. 

The primary inhabitants are the **Laminates**, the flat, crustacean-like entities mentioned in the scrolls of the quarry. They are less like animals and more like mobile shingles, their bodies composed of overlapping plates of grey slate that click and chime with every movement. They do not possess eyes—for what use is sight in a world of purple-black gloom?—but instead navigate by the "Tectonic Song." They press their flat bellies against the basalt hexes, sensing the minute vibrations of the million-ton structures above. They are the janitors of the masonry, using serrated, stone-like mandibles to scrape the lime-sweat and mineral salts from the hairline fractures. When the world shudders and the artificial rain falls, the Laminates tilt their bodies in unison, funnelling the moisture toward central pores in their shells, sounding like a thousand falling dominoes.

Deep within the forest of chimneys, where the density is highest, dwell the **Plumb-Folk**. These are not beings of flesh, but of precarious equilibrium. They appear as slender, vertiginous stacks of basalt cylinders, sometimes thirty feet tall, held together by nothing but perfectly centered gravity and the chill, damp friction of their own weight. They are the "invisible hands" responsible for the frantic assembly. They move with an agonizing, glacial rhythm, shifting a single stone once every few hours, their entire existence dedicated to the perfection of the vertical. To a human observer, they look like static pylons; only if you were to stand perfectly still for a day would you notice that the tower beside you has leaned three degrees closer to a new structural tension point. They communicate through the *ping* and *clack* of shearing flint, a percussive language of stress-points and load-bearing calculations.

In the "weird" zones, where gravity tilts and the masonry funnels into impossible spirals, live the **Angle-Mimics**. These are predatory geometries. They lack a fixed shape, consisting instead of a collection of loose, razor-sharp flint shards that hover and orbit one another in a state of constant, agitated re-configuration. They do not eat for calories, but for mass. They wedge themselves into the non-Euclidean cracks, pulling stray stones—and the occasional luckless Laminate—into their shifting centers to be crushed into new, smaller shards. They are the reason for the "cacophony of construction"; their grinding is the sound of the world’s errors being masticated into dust.

Near the vaulted ceiling, hanging like stalactites made of architectural intent, are the **Pendulum-Swifts**. They are the only creatures that move with speed. Shaped like heavy plumb-bobs, they hang from miles-long filaments of braided mineral fiber. They swing through the dark in great, sweeping arcs, their motion powered by the rhythmic pulses of the shifting city below. They wait for the sparks—the flint-grinding-flint flashes—to hunt. In that micro-second of illumination, they adjust their swing, snatching mineral-rich debris from the sides of towers before vanishing back into the bruise-purple shadows.

Finally, there is the **Great Pulse** itself. There is a growing suspicion among those who study the Floor that the masonry is not being built *for* anyone, but that the architecture is itself a single, sprawling organism. The "invisible hands" are its white blood cells, the Laminates its tactile nerves, and the groaning slate its slow, metamorphic heartbeat. In this place, to be "alive" is simply to be a stone that has not yet found its permanent place in the stack. Everything here is a worker, and the work is the world. There is no inhabitant who is not also a component; no one lives here who is not also part of the foundation.
