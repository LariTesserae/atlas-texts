The life that occupies the Iron Marsh does not possess eyes, for there has never been a sun to justify them. Instead, the inhabitants are masterpieces of thermal sensitivity and tactile percussion. They are creatures of the "Vibrational Spectrum," perceiving their world as a map of humming frequencies and shifting heat-signatures.

### The Valvesmen (The Pale Nomads)
The most significant inhabitants are the **Valvesmen**, descendants of whatever original occupants built the brass cabins, now warped by eons of isolation. They are long-limbed and spindly, their skin the color of curdled milk and as slick as a wet seal’s. They have no faces—only a vertical, heat-sensitive slit where a nose might be, and a wide, lipless mouth designed for rhythmic clicking.

They move across the turbine cliffs like geckos, their fingers and toes having evolved into multi-jointed, magnetic pads. A Valvesman does not walk; he *clings*. They live in the "Dead Zones" of the great engines—the cold spots where the heat is manageable. They are the tuners of the world. They carry long rods of resonant glass, tapping the pipes to listen for the "illness" of a blockage. To them, the Great Machine is a god that must be kept breathing. They don't eat in the traditional sense; they scrape the lipid-rich grease-moss from the gears and swallow it whole, their stomachs having adapted to digest industrial lubricants and synthetic proteins.

### The Galvanic Eels of the Basins
In the oily basins below the turbines, life is more fluid and predatory. The **Copper-Bellied Eels** thrive here. These creatures are twenty feet of braided, muscle-like cables encased in a translucent, rubbery sheath. They are the ultimate scavengers of the Steam Cycles. 

When the scalding rain falls, the Eels rise to the surface of the black water, opening their mouths to catch the falling soot. Their bodies act as living capacitors; they store the static discharge from the air, glowing with a dim, rhythmic blue light. When a Scuttler wanders too close to the water’s edge, the Eel releases a focused arc of electricity, flash-frying the prey instantly. The smell of ozone and burnt chitin is the dinner bell of the valleys.

### The Clack-Hives (The Scuttlers)
The **Brass-Backed Scuttlers** mentioned in the geography are not solitary. They are hive-minds. They cluster in the "junction boxes" of the massive pipes, building nests out of chewed silica and hardened oil. Their shells are not true bronze, but a biological ceramic rich in copper deposits harvested from the environment.

They are the janitors of the Iron Marsh. A hive of ten thousand Scuttlers can strip a fallen Spark-Kite to its conductive skeleton in minutes. They communicate through "Structural Telephony"—they press their carapaces against the metal floor and drum out messages that can be felt by other hives miles away through the shared iron bedrock. If you stand still long enough, you can feel their gossip vibrating through the soles of your boots: a rhythmic, urgent *thrum-thrum-tap*.

### The Atmospheric Drifters
Above the plateaus, in the humid space between the machinery and the Great Ceiling, life is translucent and weightless. 
*   **The Soot-Moths:** These are not insects, but fluttering membranes of charcoal-colored tissue. They have no wings in the traditional sense, but rather thermal sails that catch the upward drafts of the steam vents. They feed on the heavy metal particulates in the rain, filtering the air until their bodies become heavy and leaden, at which point they fall to the marshes to be consumed by the Scuttlers, completing the mineral cycle.
*   **The Great Bellow-Whales:** Rarely seen but always heard, these are the leviathans of the upper reaches. They are massive, gas-filled bladders of skin, miles long, that drift near the Great Ceiling. They are the source of the deepest subsonic groans. They act as living pressure-valves; they suck in the excess steam from the world-machine, process the heat, and exhale it as the fine, soot-stained mist that eventually becomes rain. They are the lungs of a world that has no trees.

### The Parasitic "Wire-Orchids"
Growing in the shadows of the lung-ferns are the **Wire-Orchids**. These are stationary predators that mimic the appearance of hanging cables or loose copper wiring. They dangle from the overhead pipes, dripping a sweet, synthetic-smelling nectar that attracts the Valvesmen or curious Scuttlers. When touched, the "wire" snaps shut—it is actually a high-tension muscle fiber—and begins to pump a caustic, rust-inducing acid into the prey, dissolving it from the inside out while it is still pinned to the machine.

### The Spirit of the Place
Life here is not "natural" by any earthly standard, yet it is fiercely persistent. There is no concept of "green" or "growth"—only **Function**. The creatures of the Iron Marsh do not view themselves as separate from the pipes and gears. To a Valvesman, his pulse is simply a smaller version of the engine’s thrum. To a Scuttler, the rust is not decay, but soil. 

They live in a state of constant, rhythmic labor, a biological choir singing in harmony with the eternal, hissing breath of the Great Ceiling. They are the ghosts in the machine, the soft things that learned to love the iron.
