The inhabitants of this rift are a people who have surrendered the concept of the "stride." To look at them is to see a biology written in the language of the vertical and the interstitial. They are long-limbed and startlingly lithe, their bodies possessing a double-jointed fluidness that allows them to fold into the boxy, windowless chambers of their city like silk tucked into a crate.

Their skin has the quality of the translucent quartz ceiling above—a pale, pearlescent grey that seems to absorb the shadowless light rather than reflect it. They do not tan or burn; instead, in the presence of the silver dew-fall, their skin takes on a faint, bioluminescent sheen, a soft velvet glow that helps them identify one another in the deeper, unlit crawlways. They are hairless, for hair would only snag on the jagged basalt or trap the dampness of the breath-mist.

They do not have feet so much as they have secondary hands. Their toes are elongated and prehensile, the soles of their feet padded with a thick, leathery callus that makes no sound on the obsidian shale. They move through the city with a terrifying, silent grace, rarely using the ladders for anything other than heavy transport. To see a group of them moving between levels is to see a slow-motion waterfall of pale limbs, a rhythmic, climbing flow that ignores gravity in favor of friction.

Because their world is a world of interiors, their eyes have evolved for the specific, diffused light of the quartz. They are large, dark, and lack visible whites, specialized for detecting the subtle textures of stone. They do not look toward the horizon—there isn't one—but rather focus on the "near-depth." They can read the health of a wall by the way the moisture beads on it; they can sense the structural integrity of a stack of stones by the way the light dies in the seams.

Socially, they are a civilization of the "Soft Murmur." In a city of a hundred thousand rooms with no doors, privacy is not a physical state, but a psychological one. They speak in frequencies that barely crest the threshold of the human ear—a series of clicks, hums, and sibilant whispers that are dampened by the heavy, humid air before they can travel more than a few feet. A family can live in a space no larger than a closet, yet maintain a profound sense of solitude through a cultural discipline of silence.

They are, by necessity and by spirit, a race of masons. Every child is born into the sound of the copper chisel. They do not view the city as a finished product, but as a slow, coral-like growth. They do not "build" so much as they "negotiate" with the rift. When the earth hums—a low-frequency vibration that signals a tectonic shift—they do not flee. They gather. They place their palms against the raw basalt, feeling for the new fractures, and immediately begin to fill the new space with their hand-cut boxes. They are the gardeners of the crack in the world.

Their diet is as sparse and efficient as their architecture. They harvest the waxy, translucent ferns, which they press into dense, flavorless cakes, and they supplement this with the protein of the flightless insects that skitter in the damp crevices. They drink the dew-fall collectively, gathered around the basins in a ritual of total stillness, waiting for the atmosphere to provide.

There is no leadership here, at least not in any traditional sense. There is only the "Grand Masonry," a collective understanding of the hive’s needs. If a wall leans, it is shored up by whoever is closest. If a crawlway becomes blocked, a dozen hands appear to clear it. They are a people who have become an extension of the geology they inhabit—deliberate, enduring, and quiet. 

They are the first witnesses to the earth's opening, and they live with the frantic, quiet intensity of those who know that their world is still cooling. They are not waiting for the future; they are busy carving it into the ribs of the mountain, one light-swallowing block at a time.
