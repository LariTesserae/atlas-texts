In the Upland Basin, life does not compete; it *endures* with a terrifying, rhythmic patience. Because the medium is so viscous and the energy so abundant, the biological imperative to be fast has been replaced by the imperative to be architectural.

### The Aurelian Veils (The Greater Drifters)
The house-sized entities mentioned in the margins of the world are the **Aurelian Veils**. They are not merely animals, but floating chemical laboratories. Their bodies are composed of a hyper-oxygenated gel that is slightly less dense than the 2.7-density "air," allowing them to hang at precise altitudes like balloons. 

They have no skeletons. Instead, they are held together by a complex network of "tension-threads"—internal fibers of spun silica that glitter like spiderwebs through their translucent bells. They do not hunt. As they drift, their vast, trailing skirts—some stretching fifty meters into the emerald gloom—filter the mineral-rich syrup. They "breathe" by pulsing their entire circumference once every three hours, a movement so subtle it is almost invisible to the naked eye. If you were to rest your hand against one, you would feel not a heartbeat, but a low, subterranean vibration, the sound of a massive, slow-turning engine.

### The Silt-Gilders
On the surface of the great floating vegetable rafts lives a species of micro-fauna known as **Silt-Gilders**. To these creatures, a single falling leaf is a generational voyage. They are flat, leaf-shaped organisms, no thicker than a sheet of parchment, with iridescent shells that mimic the gold-green light of the sun. 

They spend their entire lives—perhaps twenty years—on a single square meter of vegetation. They move by secreting a lubricant that reduces the viscosity of the water around them, allowing them to "slide" across the moss like a puck on ice. They are the gardeners of the rafts, consuming the parasitic fungi that attempt to choke the pulsing green vines. When a Gilder dies, it does not rot; it calcifies, turning into a small, amber-like bead that eventually falls, contributing to the slow rain of sediment onto the obsidian floor below.

### The Ossified Cantors
Deep in the lower strata, where the vegetation mimics the crystalline geometry of the basalt, live the **Ossified Cantors**. These are the most elusive inhabitants of the Glass-Galleries. They are sessile—fixed in place—and look indistinguishable from the jagged slate outcroppings they inhabit. 

They are lithographic life-forms. They feed on the tectonic stress of the mountain range itself. By wedging their crystalline roots into the fresh cracks of the obsidian, they absorb the kinetic energy of the shifting plates. They are the source of the low-frequency *thrum* that defines the soundscape. Every few weeks, a Cantor will release a single, profound note—a sound so deep it bypasses the ears and vibrates in the marrow of the bone—to signal its location to others of its kind across the basin. This is their only form of interaction, a slow-motion conversation that spans centuries.

### The Glass-Strands
Woven through the "vines the thickness of ancient oaks" are the **Glass-Strands**. These are colonial organisms, similar to siphonophores, that look like long, glowing ribbons of fiber-optic cable. They are the nervous system of the forest. 

They do not have brains, but they possess a collective sensitivity to light. As the sun moves across the sky, the Strands pulse with shifting colors—indigo, pale lime, and bruised gold—relaying the "weather" of the upper layers to the darkened roots below. If a massive vegetable raft were to snag on a sharp peak, the Strands would flare a violent, warning crimson, a signal that triggers the nearby vines to contract and pull the raft free over the course of several days.

### The Ghost-Lace
Finally, in the highest reaches, where the "water" meets the ultimate ceiling of the world, there are the **Ghost-Lace**. These are near-weightless, gossamer organisms that look like floating snowflakes the size of dinner plates. They are the only creatures that truly understand the boundary of their world. 

They spend their lives at the very meniscus of the high-altitude sea, skating on the surface tension. They are the most fragile things in existence; a sudden movement would shatter their crystalline lace. They feed on the rare gases that bubble up from the depths, trapped in the curtains of moss. In the eternal afternoon light, they appear as flickering sparks, tiny diamonds dancing on a ceiling of liquid jade.

There is a profound loneliness to the life here. Each creature is a solitary masterpiece, a slow-growing monument to a world that has never known a predator, a tool, or a hurry. To observe them is to realize that in the Glass-Galleries, life is not something that happens *to* the environment—it is the environment, dreaming of itself in shades of emerald and obsidian.
