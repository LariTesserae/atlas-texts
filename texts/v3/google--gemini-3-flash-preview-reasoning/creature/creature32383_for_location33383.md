The inhabitants of this shelf are not inhabitants in the sense of guests or occupants; they are the shelf's architecture, its secondary skin, and its only meaningful metric of time. They are the **Lithopeds**, though they have no name for themselves, possessing no language but the pressure of a neighbor’s shell against their own.

Individually, a Lithoped is a study in geometric austerity. They are roughly dodecahedral, their bodies faceted rather than rounded, which allows them to lock together with terrifying efficiency. The "ceramic" chitin is not a shell in the way a crab’s is—it is not a covering for something soft. Break one open, and you would find no organs, no pulsing heart, no wet coils of gut. Instead, the interior is a dense, crystalline lattice of silicate fibers and semi-solid mineral salts. They are living batteries, calcified and scorched, designed to hold a charge of geothermal heat for decades.

They do not breathe air; they "sieve" it. The bitter alkaline dust that chokes the atmosphere is their only nutrient. Through microscopic pores in their dorsal plates, they pull in the scorched flint of the air, fusing it into their own mass. They are constantly, infinitesimally growing, adding a single micron of ceramic armor every few years, a process of slow-motion calcination that makes them feel less like animals and more like a thickening of the crust.

The "Built" pillars—the totems of oily stone—are the only things they acknowledge as distinct from themselves. These pillars are the focus of their slow-motion pilgrimage. A Lithoped may spend a century traveling ten meters to reach a pillar. Once there, it does not worship; it simply leans. It presses its faceted face against the smooth silicate and vibrates. This vibration is the "heartbeat" heard in the silence—a collective, low-frequency resonance that helps shake the dust through their internal lattices. Over eons, this communal rubbing hasn't just polished the stone; it has shaped the Lithopeds themselves, wearing their sharp edges into the dull, interlocking curves that allow them to flow like a river of shale.

There are no genders, no mating, and no childhood. A Lithoped is "born" when the pressure of the collective becomes so great that the powdered remains of the dead—the crushed chitin of the bottom-most layer—fuses together under the intense heat of the ground-glow. New life is literally pressed into existence by the weight of the ancestors. They emerge from the bottom of the carpet as small, sharp-edged shards and spend centuries working their way to the surface, seeking the infrared light like a plant seeks the sun.

The "shudder" that moves through the carpet is their only form of communication. It is a tectonic conversation. If a heat vent at the far end of the shelf flares, the Lithopeds nearest to it expand slightly from the warmth. This expansion pushes the neighbor, who pushes the next, sending a physical ripple—a "heave"—across the miles. To be a Lithoped is to be a single nerve ending in a nervous system that spans the entire shelf. They feel the world not as a series of sights or sounds, but as a map of pressures and thermal gradients.

In the rare, pitch-black moments when the infrared glow from the fissures dims, the Lithopeds enter a state of total stasis. They become indistinguishable from the rock they stand upon. In this state, they are effectively immortal, their metabolism dropping to a near-zero point where time ceases to act upon them. They are patient beyond human comprehension. They are waiting for the ground to belch heat, for the dust to settle, and for the slow, grinding friction of their own bodies to eventually turn the entire mountain into a smooth, polished pebble. 

They are the ultimate end-point of a world without water: a biology of stone, a society of pressure, and a life of eternal, unblinking heat.
