In this chamber of fused iron and flora, life does not breathe; it resonates. 

The inhabitants are the **Litho-Galvanics**, entities that have long since surrendered the volatility of flesh for the slow, crushing permanence of the vault. They are not visitors to this space, nor are they its masters; they are its highest expressions, the points where the copper, the steam, and the rubbery ferns achieve a collective, dreaming consciousness.

**The Calibrated Ones**
Near the fused cast-iron doors and beneath the weeping catwalks, there are forms that, at first glance, appear to be discarded statuary or misplaced engine blocks. These are the primary denizens. They are hulking, asymmetrical bipeds—though the term "biped" implies a lightness they do not possess. Their limbs are thick columns of oxidized bronze and wet, translucent quartz, fused together by the same amber mold that illuminates the ceiling. 

They do not walk; they shift. A movement for them is a tectonic event, a slow grinding of mineral joints that takes hours to complete. They exist in a state of perpetual "tending." With fingers like blunt glass rods, they adjust the brass gauges that have turned to crystal, not because the gauges still function, but because the *act* of adjustment is a ritual that maintains the room's stasis. They do not have faces, only smooth, convex plates of polished verdigris that reflect the glowing ferns. They "see" through the vibration in the floor, their consciousness merging with the deafening, low-frequency thrum of the marrow-bone drone.

**The Tendril-Geists**
Living within the canopy of leathery ferns and frayed cable-vines are the smaller, more fluid occupants. These are the spindly extensions of the room’s nervous system. They resemble clusters of copper wire wrapped in the pale, waxy skin of the succulents. They move with the agonizing lethargy of a cooling lava flow, sliding along the rusted rafters with a faint, metallic rasp. 

They are the harvesters of heat. You might see one draped over a high-pressure steam valve, its body translucent and pulsing with a dull orange glow as it siphons the thermal energy. They do not hunt; there is nothing to kill. Instead, they position themselves where the heat is most violent, acting as living heat-sinks that prevent the chamber from detonating under its own pressure. When two meet, they do not communicate with sound, but by twining their wire-like limbs together, exchanging mineral salts and electrical charges in a silent, hours-long embrace.

**The Pulse-Moss**
The most pervasive life form is the moss itself, which blankets the petrified turbines. It is not a plant, but a carpet of microscopic, clockwork cilia. It is a colonial organism that acts as the chamber’s memory. If you were to scrape away a patch of this moss, you would find the metal beneath etched with impossibly fine patterns—records of every steam hiss and every millimetric growth of a vine over the last ten millennia. The moss feeds on the drone, converting the sound's kinetic energy into the faint, amber light that fills the air. It is the reason the room feels like a womb; the moss softens the edges of the violence, turning the industrial scream into a warm, rhythmic lullaby.

**The Nature of their Existence**
There is no hunger here, only the "need to conduct." The inhabitants are autotrophic in the most literal sense—they are transducers. They take the decay of the machines—the rust, the heat, the escaping steam—and turn it into the slow, steady growth of the forest. 

They have no language because the drone is their language. It is a singular, eternal Note. To them, the sound is not noise; it is the fundamental fact of being. They are patient beyond human comprehension. A Calibrated One might spend a century leaning against a vibrating pipe, its internal gears slowly turning to match the frequency of the steam, until the entity and the pipe are no longer two separate things.

They do not die, but they do eventually stop. When a Galvanic can no longer draw enough heat to move, it simply ceases. It stays where it is, and over the next several hundred years, the ferns grow through its ribcage, the copper rafters weep sap over its shoulders, and it becomes part of the "geology" of the vault—a new planter for a new generation of rubbery, glowing leaves. 

In this belly of industry, life is not a struggle for survival; it is a slow, beautiful surrender to the machinery of the world.
