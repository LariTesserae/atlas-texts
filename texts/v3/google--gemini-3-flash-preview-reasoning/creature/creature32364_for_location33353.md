In the Sump-Gully, the inhabitants are not merely residents; they are the connective tissue between the brass and the bark. They are known, to the extent that they are known at all, as the **Marrow-Jacks**.

### The Marrow-Jacks
The Marrow-Jacks are heavy-set, slow-moving beings whose physiology has been dictated by the pressurized humidity. They are not quite human, though they possess a vaguely hominid silhouette. Their skin is the color of wet tea-leaves—a dark, translucent amber that looks as though it has been cured in the very tannin-rich pools they tend. It is thick and slightly rubbery, designed to slide against the abrasive bark of the Weeping Wood without tearing.

They do not sweat; instead, they exude a natural, lanolin-like oil that protects them from the rust and rot of the Gully. This gives them a perpetual sheen, a slickness that makes them look like living bronze statues in the mossy-gold light.

Their movement is a study in calculated momentum. Because the "ground" is a liquifying sponge, a Marrow-Jack never lifts a foot entirely off the root-mat. They slide and shuffle, distributing their significant weight across the interwoven fibers. To see a group of them moving toward a fermentation vat is to see a slow, rhythmic dance—a collective leaning against the thick air.

### The Sensory Language
Communication among the Jacks is rarely vocal. In a world of constant *thrum-thush*, a voice is a fragile thing. Instead, they communicate through **Percussive Haptics**. They strike the iron pipes with heavy, lead-weighted rings worn on their thumbs, sending vibrations through the industrial skeleton of the Gully. A sharp *clink-tap* on a sap-conduit can signal a pressure drop four hundred yards away; a low, dragging scrape against a copper vat is a call to rest.

When they do speak, it is a low-frequency rumble that sounds like the grinding of stones underwater. They have no need for high notes; the thick air swallows them.

### The Symbiotic Vocation
The Marrow-Jacks are essentially biological gaskets. Their primary occupation is "Seaming"—using their own oily secretions and hand-pressed wads of fibrous moss to seal leaks in the steam-lines and sap-valves. They live in a state of constant, gentle maintenance. A Jack might spend three days sitting perfectly still by a single slow-turning gear, applying a drop of refined nut-oil every hour, his heartbeat syncing with the machine's rotation.

They do not eat in a traditional sense. Instead, they "steep." Within their small, burl-carved dwellings, they have shallow basins filled with a concentrated nutrient broth drawn from the fermentation vats. They submerge themselves for hours, their porous skin drinking in the chemical energy of the forest.

### The "Empty" Ecology
While the Gully lacks birds and insects, it is not devoid of other life; it simply lacks *fauna* that behaves like fauna. 

*   **The Gasket-Slugs:** These are fist-sized, translucent mollusks that live exclusively on the iron pipes. They are not animals so much as mobile patches of oxidation. They eat rust and excrete a hard, ceramic-like resin that accidentally reinforces the joints of the plumbing. The Marrow-Jacks treat them with a quiet, religious reverence, moving them by hand to areas where the iron is thinning.
*   **The Valve-Moths:** These are not moths at all, but sentient, airborne seeds from the Weeping Wood. They have broad, leathery wings that do not flap, but catch the rising heat from the steam-vents. They drift aimlessly until they find a rhythmic vibration—usually a pump or a turbine—and cling to it, their gossamer roots vibrating in sympathy with the machine until they eventually calcify and become part of the housing.

### The Elders of the Burl
The oldest among the Marrow-Jacks eventually stop sliding. When a Jack grows too heavy, or their skin too thick with accumulated resins, they choose a crook in a massive mangrove root and simply settle. Over decades, the Weeping Wood grows over their legs, then their torsos. 

These "Stationary Ones" are the Gully’s memory. Their nervous systems become entwined with the forest’s root-grid and the vibration of the iron pipes. Younger Jacks will come to them, placing a hand on the Elder’s bark-covered shoulder to "read" the pressure of the entire Gully. The Elder doesn’t speak, but they can pulse a warning through their fingertips if a turbine in the deep-water leads is beginning to wobble.

In the Sump-Gully, there is no distinction between the worker and the works. To live there is to be a part of the pressure, a slow-moving gear in a green, wet, and eternal machine.
