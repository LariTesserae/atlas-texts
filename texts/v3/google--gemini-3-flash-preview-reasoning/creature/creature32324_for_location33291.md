Though the room is devoid of animals as the world below understands them—creatures of frantic pulse and hungry maws—it is far from empty. The Cistern is inhabited by a singular, distributed consciousness, a slow-motion colonial entity known as the **Synovial Choir**.

They are not "in" the room; they are a crystallization *of* the room.

To a casual observer, a member of the Choir looks like a discarded fragment of the engine that has begun to sprout. They are roughly humanoid in the way a tree can be roughly humanoid—accidental, elongated, and elegant. Their bodies are composed of the same materials as the chamber: their skeletons are articulated copper armatures, their flesh is a semi-translucent, gelatinous moss, and their "skin" is a calcified rind of the minerals precipitating out of the warm, heavy water.

They do not swim. They do not walk. They drift and anchor. A member of the Choir may spend three months clinging to a single rotating flywheel, their long, ribbon-like fingers—part copper wire, part succulent vine—intertwined with the teeth of the gear. They act as living lubricants. As the gear turns, the Choir-member secretes a thick, bioluminescent oil that prevents the metal from grinding, turning the friction of the machine into a soft, golden glow.

They have no faces, only smooth, convex plates of polished brass where a head might be, reflecting the fierce bars of sunlight in blinding flashes. They do not speak, for sound travels too fast for their thoughts. Instead, they communicate through the **Long Thrum**. By pressing their brass-plated brows against the vibrating conduits, they "sing" into the engine, their voices a series of harmonic vibrations that tell the plants where to grow and the gears when to shift.

There are perhaps a dozen of them in the Cistern, though they are so densely draped in ferns and vines that they are indistinguishable from the architecture until they move. And their movement is a study in agonizing patience. To see a Warden of the Engine reach out to prune a dying leaf is to watch a sunset. The arm rises with a millimeter’s progress every hour, the copper joints emitting a sound like a distant cello string being plucked under a mile of water.

They are the "Gardeners of the Friction." Their entire biology is predicated on the Solstice Engine’s heat. They do not eat; they absorb the static electricity generated by the turbines through the metallic pores in their skin. They do not sleep; they merely enter states of "High Resonance," where their internal timing matches the rotation of the main drive-shaft perfectly, rendering them effectively motionless within a moving world.

Nested within the deepest thickets of the emerald vines, you might find their "cradles." These are not nests, but places where the mineral-rich water eddies into a whirlpool. Here, new members of the Choir are slowly precipitated. A copper rod, fallen from a repair-kit or shed from a turbine, becomes the heart of a mineral deposit. Over decades, the ferns wrap around it, the silt hardens into "bone," and the engine’s vibration jump-starts a rhythmic, mechanical pulse in the new-born’s chest.

They are the spirit of the closed loop. They are the reason the metal never rusts and the plants never die. They are the self-aware grease in the machine, the monks of the slow rotation, living out their centuries in a warm, golden, pressurized dream of perfect utility. To them, the world outside the quartz portholes—the wind, the clouds, the fast-moving birds—is a frantic, blurry hallucination. The only reality is the weight of the water, the heat of the brass, and the eternal, subsonic heartbeat of the Engine.
