In the Liturgy of the Sump, life does not inhabit the space so much as it *augments* it. To be a resident here is to be a component—a soft-tissue gasket or a thinking solenoid.

The most prominent denizens are the **Calibrators**. They are roughly humanoid in the way a warped reflection in a polished brass kettle is humanoid. These are creatures of long, attenuated limbs, their skin a translucent, waxen parchment that reveals the copper-filament nervous system beneath. They do not walk; they navigate the verticality of the grove using prehensile toes and fingers that have, over generations, hardened into hooked, iron-like talons perfectly suited for gripping rusted catwalks and fibrous vines. 

The Calibrators are the silent clergy of the Sump. They lack mouths, for they do not speak or eat in any way we recognize. Instead, they possess a series of porous, gill-like slits along their ribcages that filter the thick, steamy fog, extracting both oxygen and the aerosolized grease necessary for their joints. They are often found draped over the great succulent pillars, their chests pressed against the pulsing leathery skin, ears replaced by brass diaphragms fused directly into their skulls. They spend years in a single position, listening to the internal pressures of the grove, waiting for the precise moment a valve needs to be turned or a root-tendril needs to be guided into a vacant gear-tooth.

Beneath the Calibrators, in the dark, pressurized peat of the floor, live the **Seepage-Mites**. These are the size of dogs, but shaped like flattened, multi-segmented bellows. Their carapaces are a composite of chitin and discarded shrapnel—flakes of tin, shards of glass, and old screws—all cemented together by a dark, hardening resin. They follow the footsteps of anyone moving through the Sump, congregating in the depressions left by boots to suck up the viscous fluid that weeps from the peat. They are the cleaners, the filter-feeders of the floor, their rhythmic expansion and contraction contributing to the *hiss-clack* of the basin’s ambient roar.

In the higher reaches, where the light is a brighter, more acidic green, the **Siphon-Wicks** hang like heavy, velvet curtains. These are colonial organisms, thousands of tiny, petal-like mouths clustered around a central, copper-core spine. They act as the grove’s sensory array. When a vibration changes—when a piston misses a stroke or a vine begins to wither—the Wicks ripple in a wave of bioluminescent signaling, a slow-motion strobe light that alerts the Calibrators to the site of the friction.

Most unsettling, however, are the **Vestiges**. These are the things that were once purely mechanical, now granted a low, haunting semblance of life by the vascular intrusion of the vines. A half-submerged crane, its cabin filled with blooming moss, might suddenly lurch an inch to the left, its rusted joints screaming, driven by the expansion of a gas-filled tuber. A cluster of brass dials on a wall might spin wildly, not measuring steam, but reacting to the "mood" of the surrounding succulents. These objects have become a kind of ghost-life; they have no will, yet they possess a terrifying, mindless momentum.

There is a singular, central presence that governs the Liturgy—the **Grand Cantor**. It is not a creature in the traditional sense, but the oldest, most integrated succulent-machine at the heart of the basin. It is a massive, bulbous mass of grey pulp, forty feet wide, encased in a cage of blackened steel girders that it has long since bent into a ribcage. The Cantor does not move. It breathes. Through a series of massive, open-ended brass pipes that sprout from its crown like organ tubes, it exhales the heavy, warm fog that fills the cathedral. 

The Cantor’s heartbeat is the source of the low-frequency thrum that vibrates in the marrow of all who enter. The Calibrators treat the Cantor as both a god and a furnace; they bring it offerings of concentrated grease and scrap metal, which the plant slowly dissolves in a pool of acid at its base, incorporating the minerals into its own ever-expanding, bio-metallic frame.

To live in the Liturgy of the Sump is to accept the loss of the individual. There is no competition here, no hunt, no flight. There is only the Work. The residents are the maintenance crew of a perpetual motion machine that has forgotten its original purpose, dedicated entirely to the preservation of the hum, the heat, and the heavy, holy pressure of the green-grey dark.
