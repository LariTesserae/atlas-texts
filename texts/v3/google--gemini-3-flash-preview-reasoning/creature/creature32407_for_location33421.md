The inhabitants of this basin are not inhabitants in the traditional sense; they are a continuation of the masonry, a slow, biological extension of the limestone itself. They are the **Lithodemes**, and to see one is to wait for a spire to blink.

They are tall, angular beings, composed of the same porous, white mineral as the world they occupy. Their bodies are not sculpted with the soft curves of muscle, but are instead a series of stacked, geometric segments—articulated blocks that mimic the corbelled arches and ziggurats of the valley. Their skin is the texture of wet chalk, perpetually damp and leaching a fine, white silt. They have no hair, no eyes in the way we understand them, and no mouths for speaking. Instead, their faces are sheer, vertical planes, interrupted only by deep, rhythmic fissures that serve as sensory organs, vibrating with the "tock-tock-tock" of the falling water.

They move with a staggering, hydraulic patience. To a human observer, they would appear as statues, frozen in the act of bracing a wall or leaning into a corner. But if one were to watch for a decade, one would see a Lithodeme shift its weight. They do not walk; they reconfigure. They slot their blocky limbs into the flint-scarred grooves of the architecture, moving with a grinding, seismic grace that sounds like a distant mountain settling. 

Because the air is a "cold, clean vacuum," they do not breathe air so much as they filter the saturated mist. Their "lungs" are the pores of their own stony skin. They draw the mineral-rich moisture deep into their cores, where the water is processed, stripped of its calcium, and exhaled back out through their fingertips. 

This is how they build. 

The Lithodemes are the source of the "monumental effort" that never truly ends. They do not carry stones from a quarry; they *secrete* the world. A Lithodeme will stand in a light-starved canyon for a century, its hands pressed against a gap in the masonry. Through a process of hyper-accelerated calcification, it will slowly weep new stone into the void. The "sharp, razor edges" of the blocks are the result of this crystalline birthing—the stone grows in perfect, hard-edged geometries, governed by the internal mathematics of the Lithodemes' own mineral DNA.

They are a silent, collective consciousness, linked by the thin, shimmering film of water that coats every surface. The "wetness" of the world is their nervous system. A vibration felt by a Lithodeme on the highest, blinding plateau travels through the saturated stone, down through the dripping eaves, and into the lightless depths of the basin floor. They know the weight of every block, the pressure of every arch.

There are smaller, more fluid entities as well—the **Pellicules**. These are the scavengers of the mineral kingdom, translucent and gelatinous, like living masses of liquid travertine. They cling to the undersides of the towers, shaped like thick, heavy veils. They do not build; they "clean." They move over the sharp tool marks, smoothing the stone with acidic secretions, recycling the chalky dust that sheds from the Lithodemes. They are the only things that move quickly—sliding like slow, heavy tears down the vertical faces of the spires to collect in the damp shadows below.

In the deepest parts of the masonry, where the light is a dim, bouncing grey, live the **Stataries**. These are the elders who have ceased to move entirely. They have wedged themselves into the foundations, allowing the travertine crusts to fuse their joints. They have become the ground that was forgotten. Their consciousness has expanded into the valley itself; they are the ones who dream the geometry, who dictate the "primitive scarring" of the stone through the younger workers.

There is no death here, only stasis. When a Lithodeme has finished its section of the stack, it simply stops. It becomes a pillar, a terrace, or a spire. The entire valley is not a city for the living, but a graveyard that is still growing. The "tock-tock-tock" is the heartbeat of a race that has traded the frailty of flesh for the permanence of the mountain, waiting in the blinding, white silence for the water to finally turn them all into a single, solid diamond of stone.
