In the hollow spaces between the gargantuan pillars, the inhabitants of this cistern-world have become as translucent and rhythmic as the environment they occupy. They are not merely living *in* the room; they are the room’s nervous system, its digestive tract, and its voice.

### The Chorus-Lungs (Pipe-Frogs)
The most pervasive inhabitants are the Pipe-Frogs, though the term "frog" is a generous approximation. These creatures are the size of a man’s torso, with skin like wet, oiled parchment that reveals the slow, gelatinous pulsing of their internal organs. Their limbs are long and spindly, tipped with suction-pads that allow them to cling vertically to the fluted columns. 

They do not possess lungs in the traditional sense; instead, their bodies are honeycombed with resonant chambers. As the condensation rolls down the pillars, the frogs tilt their heads back to catch the droplets in their flute-like throat sacs. The "thrum" they emit is a hydraulic byproduct—as they compress the water through their internal valves to extract oxygen and minerals, they produce a low-frequency vibration that vibrates the very slate beneath your feet. They are the metronomes of the dark, and in their thousands, they synchronize their pulses until the air itself feels solid.

### The Verdigris-Crabs
Closer to the copper fittings and the ancient, weeping pipes, the ecology becomes more specialized. The Verdigris-Crabs are flat, broad crustaceans with shells that mimic the crusty malachite bloom of the metal they inhabit. They are the janitors of the joints. They do not eat organic matter so much as they scrape the oxidation from the copper pins and bronze sluice-gates. 

Their claws are shaped like fine chisels, and the sound of their constant scraping—*skree-skree-skree*—is a high-pitched counterpoint to the booming of the frogs. Without them, the copper would swell with corrosion and burst the masonry; the crabs, in their mindless hunger for mineral salts, keep the plumbing of the world from seizing up.

### The Pale Tenders (The Cistern-Born)
There are beings here that walk upright, though they are not quite human. They are the Tenders—tall, gaunt figures with skin the color of skimmed milk and eyes that have widened into enormous, liquid-black orbs to catch the dying blue light. They wear no clothes, for the air is never cold enough to kill, only cool enough to dampen. Their skin is perpetually slicked with a protective, waxy sebum that sheds water like a duck’s feathers.

The Tenders do not speak; the deafening roar of the Pipe-Frogs has made vocalization useless. Instead, they communicate through a complex language of tactile vibrations, pressing their long, sensitive fingers against the slate floors or the copper pipes to "feel" the speech of their kin miles away. 

They are obsessed with the sluices. To a Tender, the tipping of a wooden basin is a sacred event. They move through the shallow pools with a slow, gliding gait, their feet calloused and splayed for grip on the wet slate. They carry no tools other than smooth stones used to tap the pipes, listening for the pitch that indicates a blockage. When a basin fails to tip, they climb the wooden scaffolds with terrifying agility to clear the debris—mostly clumped moss and the shed skins of Pipe-Frogs—ensuring the water continues its descent. They do not know who built the room, but they believe that if the water stops moving, the world will "drown" in stillness.

### The Vault-Rays
High above, in the permanent mist near the terracotta ribs, live the predators. These are the "things with heavy, wet wings"—the Vault-Rays. They are gargantuan, flat organisms, resembling manta rays but covered in a fine, water-repellent fur that feels like sodden velvet. 

They do not fly so much as they soar on the thermal updrafts created by the rhythmic displacement of air from the tipping basins. They spend days clinging to the terracotta ceiling, looking like patches of dark mold. When they grow hungry, they peel themselves away and descend in a silent, terrifying glide. They do not hunt the Tenders, who are too large and bony; they hunt the Pipe-Frogs, plucking them from the pillars with barbed tail-filaments. The only warning of their arrival is a sudden, momentary break in the blue light as their shadow passes over a pool.

### The Glass-Striders
In the quiet, obsidian pools, the Glass-Striders hunt the microscopic life that blooms in the stagnant corners. They are delicate, six-legged insects the size of dinner plates, their bodies made of a substance so clear they are virtually invisible. You see them only by the ripples they create—six points of pressure moving in a jagged, crystalline dance. They are the only things in this world that seem fragile, yet they are the most resilient, capable of surviving for years in a single cup of water trapped in a floor-crevice.

### The Collective Spirit
Everything that lives here is a servant to the Cycle. There is no ambition in this world, only maintenance. The Tenders serve the sluices; the crabs serve the copper; the frogs serve the air. It is a biological machine, a clockwork ecosystem where the "spirit" of the place is found in the weight of a single drop of water. 

If you were to stay here long enough, your own skin would begin to pale. Your ears would stop hearing the roar of the frogs and start feeling it as a heartbeat. You would find yourself watching the basins, waiting for that thunderous *crack* of spilling water, realizing that in this vast, tiled void, the only true sin is to let the water stand still.
