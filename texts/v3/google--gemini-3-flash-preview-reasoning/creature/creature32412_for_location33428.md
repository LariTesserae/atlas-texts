To understand the Hollow-Backs, one must first understand that they do not perceive the mountain as a static object, but as a slow, erupting wave. To them, the "violent" rock is still mid-explosion. They live in the heartbeat of the stone.

### The Physiology of the Void
The name *Hollow-Back* is a misnomer born of the first glance. Their carapaces are not empty, but concave—a deep, obsidian bowl formed by the upward-curving plates of their spines. These bowls serve as their primary sensory and digestive organs. Within the hollow of their backs lies a secondary "skin," a membrane as thin as a soap bubble and iridescent with a shifting, oily sheen. This membrane is a lung, a stomach, and an ear. It catches the bruised purple light and the leaden condensation, vibrating with the subsonic thrum of the mountain’s tectonic shifts.

Their legs are not limbs for walking so much as they are organic pitons. Terminating in clusters of diamond-hard cilia, their feet "knit" into the microscopic pores of the basalt. When a Hollow-Back moves, it does not step; it un-bonds and re-bonds its molecular structure to the rock face. This is why their movement is so agonizingly slow. To move an inch is to undergo a thousand tiny fractures and healings.

### The Glass-Scribes and the Chroniclers
The "tools" they carry—those slivers of volcanic glass—are not merely for gathering water. Among the huddled mass, there are those specialized as *Scribes*. Their glass needles are etched with runes so fine they are invisible to the human eye, and they spend decades carving the history of the wind into the basalt walls. 

They are recording the "Voice of the Needle." The subsonic thrumming that vibrates through the cleft is their language, a low-frequency friction produced by the rubbing of their abdominal plates against the schist. They are singing a song that began before the peak broke the cloud line, a slow-motion hymn to the weight of the sky.

### The Nursery of Veils
Deep within the living upholstery, where the bodies of the elders overlap to form heavy, chitinous shingles, lie the *Translucents*. These are the young, though "young" is a relative term for a creature that may take a century to reach the size of a cat. 

The young lack the basalt-mimicking armor of their parents. Instead, they are composed of a substance resembling soft, wet quartz. They are almost entirely liquid, held together by a shimmering, gossamer silk. They do not eat; they absorb the "greasy film" of the mountain through their skin. They hang from the bellies of the adults like living stalactites, dripping a pale, bioluminescent fluid that provides the only true light in the cleft—a faint, sickly green glow that reveals the pulsing veins beneath their skin.

### The Great Siphon (The Anchor)
At the very center of the cleft, where the pressure of the wind is most fierce, lives the *Anchor*. This is the oldest and largest of their kind, a creature so massive and so ancient that it has literally become part of the geological record. Its legs have fused entirely into the mountain’s root; its back-bowl is a wide, dark lake of gathered condensation.

The Anchor does not move. It has not moved in a thousand years. It serves as the biological processor for the colony. The other Hollow-Backs bring their glass troughs to the Anchor, pouring their gathered moisture into its dorsal lake. In return, the Anchor emits a steady, rhythmic heat—a byproduct of its immense, slow-burning heart—that keeps the moisture in the cleft from freezing into killing ice.

### The Shared Dream
There is no aggression here because there is no "self." The multifaceted eyes that watch you are not looking at you as a predator looks at prey, or even as a scientist looks at a specimen. They are looking at you as a mountain looks at a mayfly. 

To the Hollow-Backs, your frantic, rapid breathing and your twitching, high-speed movements are a blur of impossible violence. You move so fast you are practically invisible to them—a ghost of heat and motion. They do not hunt you because you are a flicker in time, a spark that is gone before their optic nerves can even register your shape.

They exist in a state of perpetual, collective meditation. They are waiting. They are waiting for the mountain to finish growing, for the wind to finally smooth the schist, and for the purple light to turn into the white of a dying sun. They are the patient architects of a world that moves too slowly for us to ever truly inhabit. 

When you leave the Needle-Point, you feel a crushing loneliness. Not because you were alone, but because you were surrounded by a thousand lives that considered your entire existence to be nothing more than a momentary shiver in the air.
