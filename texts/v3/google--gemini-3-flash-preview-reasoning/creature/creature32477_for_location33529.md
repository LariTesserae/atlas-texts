The inhabitants of the Spire of Oure are a people who have surrendered the concept of the horizontal. To an Ourean, the idea of a "plain" is a mathematical abstraction, a terrifying myth of a world where one could walk a thousand paces and never rise or fall. They are a vertical species, their physiology and culture dictated by the unrelenting demand of the Spire’s geometry.

Physically, the Oureans are lean and elongated, as if the Spire’s own upward trajectory has stretched their bones. Their skin is the color of wet slate, perpetually damp and toughened by a lifetime of friction against the nascent obsidian. Their hands are their most remarkable feature: the palms are broad and padded with thick, rubbery callouses, while the fingers are unusually long, the tips reinforced by nails that are kept short, thick, and square for wedging into the cooling fissures of the rock. They do not walk so much as they *adhere*. Even in their sleep, tucked into the carved stone cells that line the ribs of the Spire, their toes remain slightly curled, instinctively seeking purchase.

Because the Spire is a place of deafening sound—the roar of the falling water and the tectonic thrum of the growing rock—the Oureans are a silent people. They have largely abandoned spoken language, which is easily swallowed by the wind and the white noise of the abyss. Instead, they communicate through a complex system of tactile signs and high-pitched, melodic whistles that pierce through the roar like the cry of a hawk. A conversation between two neighbors on different tiers looks like a dance of flickering fingers and rhythmic swaying, a visual language that mirrors the trembling of the bioluminescent moss around them.

They are not builders in the traditional sense; they are "Gardeners of the Stone." The elite among them, the *Lithos-Shapers*, spend their lives listening to the subterranean groans of the Spire. They use primitive chisels to guide the growth of the emerging rock, scoring lines into the softening obsidian to encourage it to crystallize into usable platforms or protective eaves. They don't fight the mountain; they prune it. When a new rib of stone thrusts out from the void, it is greeted with a silent, ritualized celebration, as the community descends (or ascends) to weave the first rope bridges and anchor the first timber frames into the cooling mass.

The Oureans share their vertical world with the **Vitreous Kites**, the only other large-scale life forms. These are not birds, but something more primitive—aerial scavengers with skeletal frames made of hollow, glass-like cartilage and wings of transparent, veined skin. They do not flap; they are masters of the updrafts created by the Spire’s thermal heat. The Kites are scavengers of the "sweat," landing on the vertical faces to lick the mineral-rich moisture with long, sandpaper tongues. The Oureans view them as sacred barometers; when the Kites dive into the lower mists and refuse to return, the people know a "growth-spasm" is coming—a violent shift in the rock that could shear a village from the Spire’s side.

Deep within the massive, sail-sized ferns, there exists a hidden layer of life: the **Dew-Striders**. These are spindly, multi-legged arthropods the size of a human hand, with translucent bodies that refract the neon glow of the moss. They act as the Spire’s janitors, skittering across the vertical forests to consume the decaying edges of the ferns before they can rot and lose their grip on the stone. The Oureans tolerate them, for the Striders’ silk—a waterproof, incredibly strong filament—is harvested and spun into the safety lines that every child wears from the moment they can crawl.

There is a profound, meditative patience to the life here. The Oureans eat a diet of compressed moss-cakes and the protein-rich grubs that live in the fern-roots, all washed down with the "weeping" water of the Spire, which tastes of iron and ancient cold. They have no concept of "down" as a destination, only as a disappearance. Their dead are not buried, for there is no soil; instead, they are wrapped in weighted fern-fronds and committed to the void, a final return to the empty sky from which the Spire is still trying to escape.

To live on the Spire is to be a part of a slow-motion explosion. The people are the moss’s companions, the stone’s witnesses, and the wind’s choir, clinging to the only solid thing in a universe that has not yet finished being born.
