The inhabitants of the Cleft are not merely survivors of the vertical; they are creatures of the **Grip**. 

### The Silent Kindred
The humans here—if they can still be called that—are long-limbed and deceptively slight, their bodies refined by generations of hauling their own weight against the obsidian. Their skin has the matte, translucent quality of a fish’s belly, revealing a map of blue-black veins beneath. Because the air is a constant, heavy shroud of moisture, they do not sweat; instead, they have developed a fine, oily coating on their palms and soles that reacts with the wet basalt, creating a chemical suction. 

They do not speak with their throats. In a place where the sound of a voice might shatter the fragile equilibrium of a floating water-bead or signal a hunter, they have moved their language to their hands and the vibrations of the ropes. Their fingers are impossibly long, the knuckles reinforced with thick pads of callus. They communicate in a rapid-fire blur of tactile signs—"Touch-Talk"—often conducted by gripping the same section of hair-rope and sending rhythmic pulses through the line. A vibration can travel three hundred feet up the Weaver’s spine to warn of a shifting mist-bank or the ripening of a fern-patch.

Their clothing is a second skin, woven from the damp, heavy fur shed by the Glimmer-moths. It is never truly dry. It clings to them, dark and sodden, insulating their heat against the biting chill of the wet slate. To a stranger, they would look like ghosts or statues, often remaining motionless for hours, blended into the shadows of the overhangs, waiting for the precisely right moment to move.

### The Pulse of the Moths
The **Glimmer-moths** are the true architects of the Cleft’s atmosphere. They are not solitary; they are a carpet. Their damp fur acts as a living sound-dampener, absorbing the echoes of the city until the air feels thick, like cotton in the ears. The inhabitants treat them with a reverence bordering on the domestic. A family’s wealth is often measured by the thickness of the moth-colony clinging to the exterior of their stone nest. 

The moths are the city's warmth. In the "deep-night"—the hours when the bruised sky turns to absolute ink—the people crawl beneath the living blankets of moths. The insects’ slow, rhythmic pulsing generates a low, friction-based heat. In return, the humans feed them "Stone-Milk," a calcium-rich secretion scraped from the weeping grooves of the basalt. This symbiosis is the heartbeat of the Cleft: the moths provide the silence and the heat; the humans provide the mineral nutrients the insects cannot harvest from the air.

### The Six-Legged "Glimpse-Cats"
The lidless, six-legged mammals—known by the locals as **Vakes**—are the Cleft’s erratic shadows. They are the size of hounds but move with the boneless fluidity of cephalopods. Their lidless eyes are massive, reflecting the bruised purple light of the sky like twin mirrors. 

The Vakes are not pets, but neither are they entirely wild. They are the cleaners. They scavenge the vertical city for organic waste, their suction-cup feet "thup-thupping" softly against the wet stone in a sound the humans have learned to ignore. There is a terrifying understanding between the two species: a Vake will never attack a human on a bridge, and a human will never block a Vake’s migration path. To do so would be to invite the creature’s only defense—a high-frequency shriek that, while silent to the Vake, can cause a human’s inner ear to bleed, sending them plunging into the abyss.

### The Weaver
Beneath the city, in the lightless depths where the Cleft narrows to a needle-point, lives the namesake of the fracture. The inhabitants believe the **Weaver** is a singular entity, a gargantuan crustacean or arachnid that anchors the entire mountain. They believe the oily hair-ropes they use to lash their houses to the rock are actually strands of the Weaver’s shed silk, harvested by the bravest "Depth-Divers" who descend into the absolute dark.

The Weaver is never seen, but it is felt. When the wind howls at the peaks above, the ropes of the city hum. The inhabitants believe this is the Weaver "tuning" the Cleft. Their entire religion is one of tension—maintaining the right tightness in the ropes, the right balance on the wicker terraces, the right pressure of the hand against the stone. 

### Life in the Drip
The culture of the Cleft is one of extreme, meditative stillness. Because the water-spheres drift through the air, mealtime is a slow, graceful dance. A family will sit on a wicker terrace, nets in hand, watching a pearl of water the size of a melon drift toward them. They do not snatch at it; they wait for the local thermal to bring it within reach. They eat the "Skin-Ferns" raw; the leaves taste of iron and cold sugar.

There is no concept of "falling" as a mistake; falling is seen as a return to the Weaver. When an inhabitant grows too old to maintain their grip, or when their oily skin finally dries, they do not weep. They simply stop gripping. They let go of the hair-rope, disappearing into the heavy mist without a sound, their body becoming part of the silent, high-speed engine that runs the world below. 

In the Cleft of the Weaver, life is not a struggle against the height—it is a long, quiet intimacy with the vertical.
