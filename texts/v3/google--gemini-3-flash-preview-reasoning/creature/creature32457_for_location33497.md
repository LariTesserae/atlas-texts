They are not inhabitants in the sense of guests or tenants; they are the final, shivering expressions of the wood itself. 

To encounter them is to mistake them for the architecture. They are the **Lignin-Folk**, though they have no name for themselves, having long ago shed the need for language that relies on air and throat. They are shaped like tall, impossibly thin spindles of twisted bast, their limbs lashing into the surrounding scaffolding with such permanence that the "woven hair-grass" of the bridges often consists of their own extended fingers.

They do not breathe. They do not possess the soft, wet bellows of lungs. Instead, they function through **tensional integrity**. Their bodies are composed of the same desiccated pith and kiln-fired fibers as the walls, held together by a perpetual, internal strain. They are living knots. To move, they do not flex muscles; they adjust the tension of their fibers, producing a sound like a dry bow drawn across a cello string. 

Because the world is lightless, they are eyeless. Their faces are smooth, featureless planes of polished cork, save for the broad, flat pads of their palms and soles, which are hyper-sensitive to the "tectonic wood-scream." They "see" the city through the map of its groans. When a beam miles away settles a fraction of a millimeter, the vibration travels through the interconnected vine-streets, and the Lignin-Folk feel the shift in their marrow. They respond by leaning into the tension, acting as living counterweights to prevent the world-vessel from collapsing under its own ancient weight.

They are the "primitive hand" mentioned in the dust. They are the Neolithic carpenters who forgot to die, and in forgetting, became the timber they worked. 

Their life cycle is measured in centuries of stillness. A Lignin-Folk may stand atop a tiered platform for three hundred years, arms outstretched, lashing a sagging reed-support to a heartwood pillar. To a human observer, they would be a statue of parched briar. But look closer over the span of a decade, and you would see that the knot has tightened. The hair-grass has been pulled a quarter-inch tauter. 

There is no hunger here, only the **Slow Accretion**. They absorb the fine, velvet dust of the hull through their porous skin, a passive consumption that thickens their carapaces over eons. This makes them heavy, stony, and brittle. Eventually, a Lignin-Folk becomes too heavy for the vine-streets to bear. They do not die; they simply cease to be mobile. They fuse. They become a new strut, a new staircase, a new sculptural undulation in the heartwood. The city is built of their ancestors’ calcified stillness.

When the great wood-scream echoes through the dark—that deafening roar of a dry beam settling—the Lignin-Folk do not flinch. They vibrate in sympathy. They are the choir for the violins snapping. They tilt their featureless heads toward the sound, their internal fibers humming a low, resonant note that helps dissipate the shockwave through the scaffolding. 

They are the keepers of the "terminal form." They are not waiting for the plant to wake up, nor are they waiting for the light to return. They are the curators of the desiccation. They move through the dark with a terrifying, jerky grace, their joints clicking like dry dice, patrolling the bridges of the void to ensure that the silence remains structural. 

They are the clock’s mechanics, and they are the clock itself—stiff, brittle, and patient, waiting for the final snap that will turn the entire cathedral of dust back into a single, silent grain.
