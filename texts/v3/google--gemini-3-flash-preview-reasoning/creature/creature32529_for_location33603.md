In a place where the sound of the earth’s pulse can be felt in the marrow, life does not thrive; it persists, slow and heavy, as an extension of the industrial geology. 

To look upon the inhabitants of this saturated world is to see the physical manifestation of the damp and the pressure.

### The Litho-Parasites
The armored silverfish mentioned in passing are the **Silt-Skaffs**. They are not insects in the way a sun-warmed garden knows them. They are broad, flat, and heavy, their carapaces forged from the same ferruginous minerals as the plateaus. Their bodies are articulated plates of oxidizing iron-grey, designed to press flat against the copper-clad pipes. They move with an agonizing, twitching lethargy, grazing not on foliage, but on the "sweat" of the machinery—the thick, mineral-rich scale that builds up on the warm ventilation cowls. 

They are deaf, for hearing is a liability in a world of constant thunder. Instead, they sense the "health" of the steam-engines through their bellies. A Skaff can detect a micro-fissure in a mile-long piston weeks before it fails, congregating in the thousands over the heat-leak like a scab of living metal.

### The Vapor-Moths
The **Ghost-Drifters** are the only things that seem to defy the gravity of the place. Translucent and wide-winged, their bodies are little more than gelatinous lungs, filtering the microscopic spores of the silver-grey lichen from the thick air. They do not fly so much as they are "negotiated" by the updrafts. They spend their lives in the golden-grey haze between the chasms, their wings glistening with a permanent oily sheen that allows the water to bead and roll off. They are the only creatures that see the sun, though only as a bright, featureless bruise in the white sky.

### The Valve-Keepers (The Living Souls)
The human inhabitants—those few "living souls" scattered across the miles of conduits—are the **Lents**. They are a people of the Long Breath. 

Physically, the Lents have become molded by the pressure. They are short, barrel-chested, and dense, their skin the color of wet silt and mapped with the fine, blue traceries of veins that have adapted to the low-oxygen, high-moisture air. They do not walk with a spring in their step; they move with a heavy, deliberate gait, their boots spiked with brass to grip the slick, moss-covered catwalks.

Their culture is dictated by the **Great Piston**. Because a single stroke of the machinery takes a lunar cycle, the Lents do not measure time in hours or days. They measure it in *displacement*. A child is "born during the Third Ascent of the Western Ram"; an elder "wears out during the Great Compression."

Communication among them is not vocal. The monolithic roar of the valleys has rendered speech obsolete. Instead, they speak a language of **Tapping**. Using heavy, copper-tipped staves, they strike the resonant iron of the catwalks and pipes. They communicate in vibrations—a rhythmic code that travels through the metal skeletons of the city faster and clearer than any voice could travel through the thick mist. They are a silent people who feel the news in the soles of their feet.

### The Ecology of the Moss
The dark, slick moss is the true master of the mesas. It is a carnivorous, mineraloid growth that feeds on the "blood" of the city—the lubricating oils and iron-rich steam that vent from the hissing valves. It is thick, spongy, and deep; in some places, it has overgrown the catwalks to such an extent that the metal is merely a skeletal suggestion beneath a foot of wet, black velvet. The Lents harvest the moss, pressing the moisture from it to find the concentrated minerals they need for their own slow metabolisms.

### The Silent Ones
Deep within the plateaus, near the glacial hearts of the engines, there are rumors of the **Anchors**. These are Lents who have ceased to move entirely. They have positioned themselves in the niches of the great gears or against the warm, vibrating brass of the secondary boilers, and simply stayed there. Over years, the silver-grey lichen begins to grow over them, pinning them to the architecture. Their heartbeats, it is said, eventually synchronize with the low-frequency thunder of the world. They become living sensory organs for the machine, and the machine, in turn, keeps them warm, fed by the steam, and eternal within the golden-grey afternoon.

In this place, life is not a fire that burns bright; it is a slow oxidation. It is the steady, inevitable growth of a crystal, or the patient erosion of a stone. Everything here is waiting for the next stroke of the piston, and the one after that, in a world where the act of breathing is the same as the act of drowning, and the machine is the only God that answers back.
