To live in the Cistern of Stilled Breath is to exist as an extension of the karst itself. The inhabitants, the **Vellic**, are not merely occupants of the hollow; they are its slow, soft pulse. 

They are a race of beings who have surrendered the concept of the skeleton. Their bodies are long, tapering columns of pale, translucent muscle, standing perhaps seven feet tall when fully extended, though they rarely are. Instead, they drape themselves over the undulating limestone like living damp-cloths. Their skin is a feat of evolutionary mimicry—milky, semi-opaque, and textured with the same fine, pebbled grain as the water-worn stone. To look upon a resting Vellic is to see only a slightly more supple fold in the cathedral wall.

They possess no eyes. In the profound, blue-tinted gloom, sight is a wasted energy. Instead, their entire dorsal surface is covered in a carpet of microscopic, sensitive cilia that detect the slightest change in the weight of the air. They do not hear sound so much as they feel the pressure of the dripping water. The *plink-plink-plink* of the limestone tears provides them with a sonar map of their world; they move through the Cistern by navigating the rhythm of the drops, sliding their bodies into the silent spaces between the falls.

The Vellic do not eat in a way that involves pursuit or violence. They are the shepherds of the gastropods. With fingers that are long, multi-jointed, and tipped with soft, spatulate pads, they move among the millions of snails with a terrifying tenderness. They do not hunt; they "cull the weight." When the density of the snails threatens the equilibrium of a particular moss-mound, a Vellic will reach out—a movement that may take an hour to complete—and gently lift a white-shelled crustacean. 

They use the river stones mentioned in the ancient piles to crack these shells, but the act is performed with a liturgical precision. The stone is not swung; it is pressed. The resulting sound is a dull, wet snap that the moss immediately swallows. They consume the soft interiors with a radula-like tongue, then add the shell to the spirals. These spirals are not art, nor are they architecture. They are ballast. The Vellic possess a primal, instinctual understanding of the karst’s fragility. They arrange the shells to distribute weight across the peat floor, ensuring that the water drainage remains perfectly balanced. They are the engineers of the stasis.

Their "stilled breath" is literal. A Vellic breathes perhaps four times in a twenty-four-hour cycle. Their lungs are not bellows, but passive absorption chambers that draw oxygen from the wet velvet air through osmosis. Because they move so little and breathe so rarely, they produce almost no heat. A hand placed upon a Vellic would feel no different from the stone it clings to, save for a slight, rubbery give.

Communication among them is a matter of silk and moisture. They utilize the heavy curtains of larval gossamer as a tactile telegraph. A Vellic at one end of the Cistern may pluck a single thread—a vibration so subtle it would be invisible to a human—and the message travels through the liquid pearls of condensation to another Vellic hanging a hundred feet away. These messages are not about ideas, but about the *state*. *The water is heavy. The moss is deep. The balance is held.*

There is a terrifying patience to their lives. A Vellic may spend a decade sitting in a single limestone niche, using their thorn-needles to pin leaves into a pool, watching the mineral deposits slowly petrify the edges of the vegetation. They do not play; they do not fight. If two Vellic meet on a narrow slick of rock, they do not bypass one another. They merge their bodies, sliding over and through each other in a slow, gelatinous exchange that can last for days, sharing the chemical memories of the stone they have touched before parting.

They are the curators of a museum that never opens. They believe—insofar as their slow, cool minds can believe—that if the rhythm of the drops were to change, or if the air were to stir, the world would end. Thus, they live as the ultimate conservatives of biology. They are the guardians of the pause, the living ghosts of a mountain that decided, eons ago, that it had seen enough of the sun.
