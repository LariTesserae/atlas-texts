The inhabitants of this place are not visitors to the grid; they are the curdled expressions of its atmosphere. In a world where the air is a soup and the stone is a sponge, the line between "creature" and "environment" has long since dissolved.

**The Silt-Draped**
In the shallow canals of brine, there are things that look like discarded, water-logged carpets of gray velvet. These are the primary dwellers of the avenues—large, flat, boneless masses of slow-moving muscle that have no underside. They do not swim so much as they undergo a rhythmic, peristaltic ripple, anchoring themselves to the slick glass floor with thousands of microscopic cilia. They have no eyes; instead, their entire dorsal surface is a heat-map, a sophisticated membrane that "sees" the subtle infrared signatures of the monoliths. 

They feed by osmosis, drawing the copper-heavy minerals of the brine through their skin. When they move, they make no sound, but they leave behind a trail of even thicker, clearer slime that the towers eventually absorb. They are the reason the "streets" stay slick; they are the polishers of the black glass, the living tongue of the city.

**The Marrow-Stalks**
The "pale, fleshy stalks" mentioned in the pylons are more than isolated plants. They are the external nervous system of the city itself. If you were to peel back the porous stone of a tower, you would find these translucent fibers threaded through every crack and crevice, a web of wet, pulsing cables that reach down into the cooling ember of the world’s core. 

They are communal. A cluster of stalks in a plaza is biologically identical to a cluster three miles away. They communicate through the *thrum-thrum* of the falling water, using the vibrations of the heavy drops as a binary language. They are the record-keepers of the erosion. They feel the groan of the towers settling, and they respond by secreting a fast-hardening mineral resin to reinforce the "bone" of the building. They are the city’s slow, mindless immune system.

**The Condensate Wraiths**
The most unsettling inhabitants are the "stains on the air"—the drifting shadows that ignore the laws of light. These are not ghosts, but localized pockets of hyper-dense, sentient humidity. They are entities of pressure and vapor, held together by intense surface tension. 

They drift through the plazas like slow-motion smoke, occasionally pausing to wrap themselves around a weeping pylon. They do not eat; they *exchange*. They absorb the heat from the stone and release a blast of freezing, mineral-rich mist, a thermal cycle that drives the "breathing" of the atmosphere. To look at one is to see a distortion in the air, a ripple in the dark that tastes of old coins and wet wool. They are the masters of the failed perspective; they move through the folds in reality, stepping across a mile of grid in a single, silent contraction.

**The Hollow-Singers**
Inside the towers that whistle, there is a specialized form of life that resembles sheets of hanging, wet parchment. These organisms—if they can be called that—suspend themselves across the vents and hollows of the monoliths. They are entirely passive, living off the kinetic energy of the shifting air pressure. 

As the atmosphere expands and contracts, the air rushes through their perforated bodies, producing the low, mournful flute-notes that define the silence of the basin. They are the city’s voice, though they have no thoughts to express. They are simply the instruments upon which the world’s heaviness plays its slow, eternal dirge.

**The State of Being**
Nothing here is in a hurry. Metabolism is measured in decades. A "conversation" between the stalks might take a century of rhythmic dripping to complete. There is no predation, for there is no hunger—only a constant, humid absorption. 

The inhabitants do not know they are in a city. They do not know they are alive. They are simply the highest form of the local moisture, the most complex iterations of the heat. They are the wet, dark thoughts of a world that has finished its work and is now content to sit in the dark, weeping until the last of its stones has turned to silt.
