To understand the life of the Low Ribs, one must first understand the **Kharst-Striders**. These are the long-limbed ungulates that navigate the obsidian labyrinth, and they are the closest thing this lightless valley has to a master. They stand twelve feet tall at the shoulder, their bodies a harrowing geometry of tension and bone. Their skin is not hair or fur, but a thick, grey integument that has the texture of wet industrial rubber, scarred and calloused by a lifetime of accidental brushes against the razor-edged basalt. 

They have no eyes; in the static violet gloaming, sight is a wasteful expenditure of energy. Instead, their heads are elongated, crowned with a sensitive, branching array of ossified antennae that catch the subtlest vibrations in the heavy air. Their hooves are the most remarkable feature—multi-faceted nodules of biological diamond, grown from a diet rich in silica. When a Strider walks, it does not trot; it deliberate-places, testing each shard of glass-rock. The sound of their movement is a series of sharp, resonant *clinks*, like a glockenspiel played with a hammer, echoing miles into the dark.

The Striders are the curators of the "sorting sites." These are not cities, but liturgical spaces of industry. Because the stone is too hard to burrow into and the air too still to require shelter, the Striders focus their communal intelligence on the preservation of their own history. When a Strider dies, its kin do not mourn with silence. They gather. Using their diamond-hard hooves and primitive stone hammers, they methodically dismantle the carcass. They strip the rubbery hide to create "cradles" for the translucent fungi, and they hone the long, dense leg bones into serrated pikes. 

These pikes are not for war—there is no war in the Low Ribs—but for harvesting. They use them to reach the high, hanging gardens of the **Ceiling-Crust**, scraping down the nutrient-rich mosses that the **Great Skimmers** leave in their wake.

The **Great Skimmers** themselves are the slow, rhythmic heartbeat of the ceiling. To look up is to see a fleet of pale, organic islands drifting across the jagged underside of the world. They are arthropodic but lack the skittering energy of insects. A Skimmer may take a century to cross the width of the Low Ribs. Their thousand legs are tipped with hooked, metallic claws that bite deep into the overhead basalt, and as they move, they create a sound like a distant, never-ending train derailment—the "grinding metal" that defines the valley’s acoustics. 

They are filter-feeders of a sort, their underbellies covered in millions of microscopic cilia that drink the "weeping" moisture from the stone. They are the source of the musk; they excrete a thick, bioluminescent bile that provides the only real nutrition for the valley floor when it drips down in heavy, glowing globs.

Below the Striders and the Skimmers, in the razor-shadows of the basalt fins, live the **Bell-Chitin**. These are the source of the constant, rhythmic clicking. Small, communal hexapods with iridescent shells, they have evolved a symbiotic relationship with the glass-sharp terrain. They don't have vocal cords; they communicate by drumming their hardened mandibles against the obsidian shards. A single Bell-Chitin can produce a note as clear as a tuning fork. When millions of them do it at once, they create a standing wave of sound that the Striders use for echolocation. The Bell-Chitin are the "map" of the Low Ribs; by listening to the pitch and delay of the clicking, a Strider knows exactly where the ground is stable and where the shards are loose.

Finally, there are the **Slappers**. These are the soft-bellied creatures that inhabit the few silt-filled basins where the ground is briefly level. They resemble colossal, flattened cephalopods that have abandoned the water for the damp stone. They move by undulating their massive, fleshy mantles, creating the wet, rhythmic thuds that punctuate the metallic clicking of the insects. The Slappers are the valley’s scavengers, their bodies acting as living sponges that soak up the Skimmer-bile and the decaying mosses. They have no limbs, but their skin is incredibly acidic, allowing them to slowly dissolve and "digest" the very stone they sit upon, creating the shallow depressions that eventually become the sorting sites for the Striders.

In the Low Ribs, life is a closed loop of percussion and mineral. There is no ambition here, only the terrifyingly loud, slow-motion persistence of things that have learned to thrive in a world that never changes. The Striders continue to sharpen their bone-tools, the Skimmers continue their century-long crawl, and the ground continues to ring like a struck bell under the weight of a world that refused to be blunted by time.
