The inhabitants of the High Fold are not so much a population as they are a slow, rhythmic extension of the geology itself. They are the **Osians**, a people whose very biology seems to have been transcribed from the wet basalt and the waxy ferns that surround them.

Physically, they are tall and startlingly thin, their bodies composed of long, graceful lines that mimic the verticality of the mountain faces. Their skin is their most remarkable feature: it is not truly opaque, but a semi-translucent, pale grey-green, possessing the matte texture of a river-smoothed stone that has been dipped in oil. It does not sweat; it *breathes* the humidity. They are osmotic beings, drawing the heavy moisture of the air directly through their membranes, their circulatory systems fueled more by the mineral-rich "weepings" of the obsidian than by any caloric intake we would recognize. 

They move with a terrifying, liquid deliberation. To an outsider, an Osian stepping across a razor-edged chasm would look like a disaster in slow motion, but their feet—long, finger-like, and bottomed with a dense, rubbery callus—grip the obsidian fractures with the certainty of a mountain vine. They do not wear shoes; to do so would be to blind themselves to the texture of the world. Their clothing is minimal, consisting of heavy, wrap-around kilts and cowls woven from the inner pith of the giant ferns, dyed a deep, permanent indigo that never fades in the constant mist.

In the High Fold, there is no spoken language. The "infinite, low-frequency thrum" of the water is their metronome, and their communication has evolved to exist beneath it. They communicate through **Litho-tonics**. By placing a hand or a specialized obsidian rod against the basalt walls of their dwellings, they send rhythmic vibrations through the crust of the mountain. An Osian miles away, standing on a solitary watchtower, can feel the "voice" of a neighbor through the soles of their feet—a tactile language of pulses that describes the flow of the water or the arrival of a new spore-drift.

This is why their city is so sparse. To an Osian, physical proximity is noise. Being too close to another person is like standing too close to a shouting man; it blurs the senses. They live in their massive, dry-stone pyramids not for shelter from the elements—for the warmth is constant—but for the solitude required to "hear" the mountain. 

They are a people of singular focus: the **Masons of Stasis**. Because the environment does not decay, their work is cumulative. An Osian might spend forty years chipping a single lintel with an obsidian flake, not because the stone is hard, but because they are waiting for the exact moment the mountain’s internal vibration tells them the stone is ready to be parted. They do not hunt, for there is nothing to kill. They do not farm, for the moss provides. Instead, they *arrange*. They spend their centuries-long lives moving basalt blocks into alignments that harmonize with the weeping water, turning the entire High Fold into a massive, silent instrument.

They have no concept of "youth" or "age" as a decline. An elderly Osian simply becomes more calcified, their skin thickening and turning more opaque until, eventually, they cease to move altogether. They do not die in the way organic things do; they simply stop navigating the vertical and become a permanent fixture of the masonry. Many of the "cyclopean walls" that bridge the peaks contain these ancestors, who have transitioned from the movers of stone to the stone itself.

They are lonely only to our eyes. To themselves, they are part of a grand, damp equilibrium. They stand on their wet, hammered-stone promenades, hands resting on basalt railings, eyes—wide, lidless, and the color of rain—fixed on the horizon. They are not waiting for a wind to blow; they are the guardians of the stillness, the slow-beating heart of a world that refused to crumble.
