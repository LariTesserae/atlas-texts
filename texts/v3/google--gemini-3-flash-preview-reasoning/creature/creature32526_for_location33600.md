The inhabitants of the Roof are defined by a physiology of conservation. They are the **Lithos-Kin**, a people whose bodies have become a mirror of the vertical world they inhabit.

They are barrel-chested and broad-shouldered, their torsos dominated by oversized, hyper-efficient lungs designed to wring life from the thin, bruised air. Their skin is the color of wet slate, thick and pebbled with a natural callus that resists the biting wind and the abrasive touch of basalt. They do not sweat; to lose moisture is to die. Instead, their skin possesses a microscopic texture that encourages condensation, allowing them to drink through their pores from the morning mist.

Their eyes are their most striking feature—oversized and protected by nictitating membranes, the irises are a deep, light-absorbing obsidian to counter the blinding glare of the sun. They move with a strange, liquid economy. On the cantilevered walkways, a Lithos-Kin does not walk so much as they flow, keeping their center of gravity low, their four-fingered hands often brushing the masonry for tactile orientation.

### The Social Weave
Society here is not governed by laws, but by the **Gravity of the Whole**. Because a single loose stone can compromise a terrace that took three centuries to build, the people are pathologically cooperative. There is no concept of "private" space in the hive; a room is merely a thickening of the communal artery. 

They communicate in a language of low-frequency hums and rhythmic taps against the stone. Because the thin air swallows high-pitched sounds, their voices are bass-heavy, vibrating through the floorboards and the soles of their feet. To a Lithos-Kin, to speak is to be felt as much as heard.

### The Oru: The Walking Hearths
Sharing the terraces are the **Oru**, the six-legged pack beasts mentioned in the annals. They are the biological anchors of the city. Their matted white wool is not just hair, but a complex, mossy ecosystem that traps heat. In the freezing nights, the people do not retreat to fires—for there is little to burn—but huddle against the Oru. The beasts are slow, ruminating on the pale lichens scraped from the cliffside, their three pairs of legs allowing them to maintain a grip on a seventy-degree incline even while sleeping. Their milk is thick, grey, and tastes of minerals, providing the primary caloric intake for the city’s millions.

### The Sky-Sails
High above, the raptors—the **Aether-Whales**—are the only things that truly own the indigo sky. With wingspans of thirty feet, they are nearly weightless, their bones hollower than the air itself. They are not merely predators; they are the scouts. The Lithos-Kin have a symbiotic relationship with them, tracking the birds' flight patterns to predict the shifting micro-climates and the arrival of the rare, life-giving fog banks. The shed feathers of these birds are the only "soft" material in the city, used to line the cradles of infants to protect them from the stone’s eternal chill.

### The Micro-Ecology
In the cracks of the masonry lives a hidden world of **Glint-Mice**. These furred rodents possess teeth hardened by silica, allowing them to gnaw on the very rock to reach the damp marrow of the mountain. They are the cleaners of the hive, consuming the organic waste of the millions above and turning it into a rich, dark loam that collects in the deep crevices, providing the only soil for the stunted, grey-leafed shrubs.

These shrubs, the **Iron-Roots**, are the city’s slow-motion clock. A single branch may take forty years to grow an inch. They are never harvested for fuel; instead, they are trained and braided over centuries to form living railings and reinforcement for the dry-stone arches.

### The Quiet Industry
Life is a cycle of **The Chipping**. Every citizen is a mason. Every day, a portion of one’s life is spent with a hammer of dense diorite, shaping the next stone for the next terrace. There is no urgency to finish, for the city is considered a living organism that must grow at the same pace as the mountain’s own geological rise. 

When a person dies, they are not buried—there is no earth for it. Instead, they undergo the **Final Masonry**. Their body is placed in a deep dew-well where the mineral-heavy waters slowly petrify the remains over decades. Eventually, they are lifted out, now a pillar of salt and stone, and built into the foundations of the new towers. In this way, the city is literally made of its ancestors, a hive of the living supported by the calcified weight of the dead.

Here, the boundary between the inhabitant and the habitat has dissolved. The people are the stone that breathes; the mountain is the body that endures.
