The inhabitant of the circular dwelling is a creature of high-tensile wire and parched membrane, a being that looks less like a biological organism and more like a kite fashioned from sun-dried parchment and copper wire. 

They are the **Hiss-Kin**, though they have no name for themselves that requires a larynx. To look at one is to look at a study in frantic economy. Their skin is not skin at all, but a layered series of overlapping, semi-translucent scales—a flexible armor of organic mica that shimmers with a dull, oil-slick iridescence. Underneath this shell, there is no blood. Instead, a network of pressurized, dry gasses circulates through their extremities, keeping their limbs stiff and spring-loaded. They do not sit; they perch. They do not walk; they recoil and release.

Their faces are narrow, dominated by large, multifaceted eyes shielded by permanent, transparent nictitating membranes to protect against the scouring grit. They have no mouths in the traditional sense, only a vertical slit protected by a filter of fine, vibrating cilia. They breathe the ozone-rich air, pulling the static charge directly into their nervous systems.

### The Metabolism of Motion
The occupant of the hut lives in a state of perpetual, vibrating tension. To be still is to starve. When they are seen outside, hunched over the stone walls, they are often performing a rhythmic, swaying dance. This isn't ritual; it is survival. By rubbing their mica-scale limbs against one another—a process that sounds like a thousand knives being sharpened at once—they generate the friction-heat necessary to keep their internal gases pressurized. 

They eat the "caloric dust"—the ground-up remains of static-moss and lichen—but they do not digest it with acid. Instead, their internal cavities are like miniature grinding mills, using the different grades of sand kept in their gourds to pulverize the minerals until the raw atomic energy of the friction can be absorbed by their porous bones.

### The Domesticity of the Scream
Inside the woven glass-fern dome, the inhabitant lives in a masterpiece of kinetic engineering. The furniture is grown, not made. Benches of basalt are layered with thick pads of static-moss that have been "tamed"—trimmed so their vibrations harmonize rather than irritate. 

The inhabitant’s "bed" is a hammock of woven wire-strider silk, suspended in a drafty corner where the wind through the basalt flutes is loudest. They sleep with their eyes open, their bodies twitching in micro-seizures that capture the ambient roar of the Crags, converting the sound-waves into a low, simmering warmth that keeps them from shattering in the drop of the night-wind.

The hand-tools scattered outside are extensions of their own biology. The scraper of rib-bone is used to groom the buildup of oxidation from their scales; if the bruised-copper tarnish becomes too thick, they lose their ability to absorb the purple lightning of the discharge-orchids. They treat their bodies like precision instruments, constantly polishing, grinding, and tuning.

### The Social Friction
They are not entirely solitary, though their interactions are abrasive. When two Sibilants meet, they do not speak. They stand close together and allow their electrical fields to overlap. They exchange information through bursts of "spark-talk"—rapid-fire discharges of static that smell of burnt tin and ozone. 

They are the gardeners of this screaming height. The walls they build aren't just for shelter; they are acoustic baffles. By rearranging the shale, they "tune" the plateau, directing the wind-shriek into specific frequencies that encourage the glass-ferns to grow in certain patterns. They are composing a symphony of wind and grit, turning the chaotic momentum of the Crags into a structured, livable resonance.

### The Scuttle-Moths and Wire-Striders
Sharing this space are their "livestock" and pests. The **Scuttle-moths** are kept like hounds, but they are driven by hunger for the Hiss-Kin’s excess electrical discharge. They flutter around the dweller’s head, their chitinous wings snapping like whips, acting as mobile lightning rods that protect the dwelling from the larger bolts of the discharge-orchids.

The **Wire-striders** are the scavengers of the plateau's edges. They are spindly, six-legged nightmares of clicking joints that follow the Hiss-Kin, waiting for them to shed a scale or drop a tool. They move with a stuttering, stop-motion gait, their bodies so light that a strong gust can lift them hundreds of feet into the air, where they spread their limbs and sail like dandelion seeds before slamming back down onto the basalt, unharmed and immediately scurrying.

### The End of the Cycle
The inhabitant of the Crags is a creature that knows it is being slowly erased. The very wind that feeds them also scours them down. Eventually, a Sibilant’s scales become too thin, their internal pressure too low to resist the atmospheric weight. When this happens, they do not die in the way wet things do—they do not rot. 

They simply stop swaying. 

Without the motion, the static charge dissipates. The internal gases cool and contract. The mica-scales become brittle. In the next grit-storm, the inhabitant does not stand against the wind; they join it. They shatter into a cloud of bruised-copper dust, becoming the very "lushness" that the next generation will grind into gourds and consume, a kinetic legacy in a world that refuses to be still.
