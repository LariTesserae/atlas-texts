The inhabitants of the Cleft, the **Oru-Ka**, are a people who have forgotten the concept of a horizon. To an Oru-Ka, the world is not a map spread out before the feet, but a series of vectors—up, down, inner, and outer. 

Physically, they are lean and wiry, their bodies elongated by generations of reaching and climbing. Their skin is the color of wet river-slate, often tinged with a faint, bioluminescent lichen that they cultivate in their pores as a mark of status and a means of identification in the emerald gloom. Their hands are disproportionately large, the fingers tipped with broad, flat pads that allow them to adhere to slick cedar and damp moss with the security of a gecko. They do not wear shoes; their feet are prehensile, the big toe set wide like a thumb, allowing them to traverse the swaying spider-silk bridges with a fluid, four-handed gait that makes them look less like humans and more like pale, drifting ghosts of the canopy.

They are a people of the "Deep Ear." Because the Cleft is a vertical echo chamber, the Oru-Ka have developed an auditory culture. They rarely speak in the way surface-dwellers do. Their language is a complex system of percussive clicks, tonal whistles, and sharp, bird-like chirps that can pierce the roar of the mist. An Oru-Ka can tell the weight, speed, and intent of a neighbor three levels up simply by the rhythm of their footsteps on the willow-whip.

Beneath the Oru-Ka, in the hierarchy of the Cleft, are the **Kree-tiks**. These are the long-tailed rodents mentioned in the leather harnesses, but "rodent" is a clumsy term for them. They are more like aquatic ferrets, with sleek, oily fur that sheds water like a duck’s back. The Kree-tiks are the nervous system of the Cleft’s commerce. They are semi-intelligent, capable of understanding complex directional whistles. They act as couriers, their clawed feet allowing them to run inverted along the undersides of the bridges, bypassing the traffic of the Oru-Ka porters. In exchange for their labor, they are fed the protein-rich grubs harvested from the moss-mattress, and they sleep in communal warmth-huddles inside the Oru-Ka’s woven pods.

In the mid-canopy, where the ferns are widest, live the **Harrow-wings**. These are the "feathered gliders," creatures with the hollow bones of a bird but the leathery, translucent skin of a bat. They are not domesticated, but they are tolerated neighbors. The Oru-Ka view them as omens; the Harrow-wings possess an uncanny sensitivity to air pressure. When the gliders descend to the lower marsh-thicket, the Oru-Ka know a "Down-Pour"—a rare, violent deluge from the peaks—is coming, and they lash themselves to the structural willow-whips of their homes.

The **Thrum-beetles**, the fist-sized iridescent insects, are the Cleft's heavy machinery. The Oru-Ka do not tame them so much as they bait them. By smearing a specific fermented mint-paste on heavy logs or stone blocks, the Oru-Ka can lure the beetles into dragging materials up the vertical heights. The drone of a thousand Thrum-beetles is the "low-flying aircraft" sound that defines the workday, a vibration that can be felt in the very marrow of one's bones.

Deep in the rotting mulch of the "floor," where the light never reaches and the air is thick with the musk of fermentation, live the **Mutes**. These are Oru-Ka who have chosen, or been sentenced, to tend the great vats. Over time, the humidity and the fumes of the ale-mash affect their lungs, deepening their voices to a guttural rumble. They live in a slow-motion world of sludge and heat, tending the wooden pulleys that hoist the finished spirits to the thirsty cities above. They are the guardians of the Cleft's base, living in symbiosis with the giant red frogs, whose skin secretions they use to seal the fermentation casks.

Nothing in the Cleft exists in isolation. The Oru-Ka do not "own" the Cleft; they are part of its metabolism. They eat the lichen, the beetles eat the waste, the frogs eat the beetles, and the water—always the water—washes the memory of it all down into the granite roots of the mountain. Life here is a frantic, beautiful, and soggy machine, clicking and whistling in the silver dark.
