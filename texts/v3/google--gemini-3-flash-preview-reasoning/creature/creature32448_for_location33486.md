The inhabitants of the Verdigris Terrace are the **Limn**, a race of lithic, semi-translucent beings who are less a population and more a slow-motion extension of the geology. To see one at rest is to see a smooth, pale boulder; to see one move is to witness a glacier’s patience condensed into a single, fluid gesture.

### Anatomy of Stasis
The Limn do not have skeletons of bone, but of pressurized fluid and cartilaginous conduits, mirroring the internal structures of the giant ferns. Their skin is the color of the pale limestone, textured like wet chamois, and perpetually slick with a film of the Terrace’s mineral-rich sweat. They are vaguely humanoid in the way a shadow is humanoid—stretched, softened, and missing the harsh angles of joints. 

They have no mouths, for they do not eat. Instead, they possess wide, porous pads on their feet and palms. They feed by "rooting"—standing for days in the shallow basins, drawing the emerald algae and the "bruised purple" mosses directly into their bodies through osmosis. When they are full, their translucent torsos take on a deep, vitric green hue, and the slow pulse of their internal fluids becomes visible, matching the bioluminescent heartbeat of the pools.

### The Hydraulic Mind
The Limn do not speak. The heavy, cushioned silence of the Terrace is sacred to them. They communicate through the very medium that defines their home: water. By standing in the same interconnected basins, they release complex chemical signatures—pheromonal "tastes"—into the water. A Limn at the north end of the Terrace can "feel" the intent of a companion at the southern edge by the way the water’s mineral composition subtly shifts against their skin.

Their intelligence is not one of invention, but of maintenance. They are the ones who sort the river stones at the base of the ferns. These are not decorative; they are hydraulic regulators. By placing stones in specific patterns, the Limn guide the "sweat" of the cliff into the basins, ensuring that no pool ever overflows and no fern ever goes thirsty. The grooves in the central slab are where they hone their shell-scrapers, which they use to gently shave away excess calcium deposits from the fern-stalks, preventing the plants from becoming too brittle to sway.

### The Life Cycle of Stone
There are no graves because a Limn does not die in the traditional sense; they simply stop moving. Over a period of decades, a Limn who has reached the end of their vitality will find a preferred depression in the limestone and settle into it. Their internal fluids thicken, their translucency clouds into an opaque cream, and the moss begins its "slow, green ascent." 

Eventually, the Limn becomes the stone. The "thumbprint" basins that dot the Terrace are the physical imprints of ancestors who have long since merged with the shelf. The Terrace grows thicker, inch by agonizing inch, not through sedimentation, but through the voluntary calcification of its inhabitants.

### The Gentle Labor
At any given moment, a visitor might think the Terrace is empty. But if you were to wait—to truly wait, for hours or days—you would see a "boulder" at the edge of your vision slowly unfold. A long, boneless arm might reach out to adjust a single pebble, the movement so deliberate that it does not even ripple the water’s surface. 

They are the curators of the stillness. They treat the Terrace like a living clock, one where the ticking is the sound of a falling drop and the hands move once a century. They do not fear the moss or the damp, for they are the masters of the humid stasis. They are the reason the balance is perfect: they are the breath that the mountain has been holding since the dawn of time.
