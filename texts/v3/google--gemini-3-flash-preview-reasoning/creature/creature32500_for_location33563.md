The true inhabitants of the basin are the **Litho-Pelagians**, a lineage of beings so synchronized with the stillness that they have nearly become a sub-set of the local geology. 

To look at one is to see a creature that has surrendered the very concept of a skeleton. Their bodies are long, tapering columns of dense, muscular tissue, roughly the height of a tall man but possessing the fluid posture of an upright slug. Their skin is the exact texture of the basin’s weeping walls—a porous, grey-mauve membrane that is perpetually slick with the surrounding humidity. They do not have nostrils; instead, their entire torso acts as a gill, pulsing with a rhythm so slow it takes a full minute to complete a single inhalation of the heavy, velvet air.

They move with a terrifying, liquid grace. When a Litho-Pelagian traverses the mineral mounds, it does not step so much as it pours its weight from one point to another. Their limbs are vestigial, spindly things, tipped with four long, spatulate fingers that end in nails as hard and sharp as the volcanic glass tools they leave behind. These fingers are not for grasping prey—there is no prey—but for the meticulous "gardening" of the silicate jellies.

These beings are the keepers of the silt. You might find one standing motionless for days beside a particular mineral ridge, its long, pale fingers slowly scraping a microscopic layer of salt into one of the thick-walled clay bowls. They eat by osmosis, pressing their palms or the soles of their feet against the nutrient-rich slimes, absorbing the mineral tea directly into their bloodstream. 

Their faces are hauntingly minimal. They possess no mouths, for there is nothing to chew and no reason to speak. Their eyes are large, lidless orbs the color of curdled milk, sensitive not to light—which never changes—but to the subtle shifts in thermal energy. They see the world as a map of warmth; they can watch a drop of water travel down a wall by the faint heat generated by its friction against the stone.

They are not solitary, but they are profoundly private. When two Litho-Pelagians meet in the narrow gaps between the mounds, they do not touch or acknowledge one another with sound. Instead, they stand perfectly still, centimeters apart, allowing their heavy, humid auras to mingle. They communicate through the rhythmic vibration of their gill-chests—a low-frequency thrum that harmonizes with the *drip-pluck-hiss* of the basin. To them, a conversation is simply a shared resonance, a brief alignment of their internal clocks before they move on.

Lower down, in the porous honeycombs of the limestone walls, live the **Glass-Grubs**. These are the translucent, moth-like creatures mentioned before. They are the scavengers of the "discarded" time. They wait for a Litho-Pelagian to finish scraping a ridge, then they descend to feast on the microscopic debris left behind. Their bodies are so thin they are practically two-dimensional, sliding into cracks in the rock no thicker than a coin. When they move, they make a sound like dry parchment rubbing together—the only "sharp" noise in the entire basin.

Finally, there is the **Deep-Pulse**. It is unclear if it is a creature or a geological fluke, but something massive lives beneath the floor of the basin, down where the mineral tea is brewed. Every few weeks, the standing pools ripple outward in perfect concentric circles. The Litho-Pelagians respond by pressing their bodies flat against the mounds, becoming one with the plum-colored stone, waiting for the vibration to pass. They do not fear it; they respect it as the source of the warmth.

In this place, to live is to accumulate. The inhabitants do not think of themselves as individuals, but as layers of the basin that have gained the ability to move. They are the slow, conscious expressions of the limestone itself, waiting for the day—millions of years hence—when the basin finally fills completely, and they are pressed, silent and unmoving, into the eternal record of the stone.
