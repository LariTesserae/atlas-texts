Here, the sky and the sea have reached a violent compromise. The world is a vertical deluge, a regional expanse of gargantuan, smooth-backed monoliths that rise like the humps of petrified whales from an invisible floor. These are the Aethel-Pillars, mountains so ancient that every jagged edge was surrendered to the water millions of years ago. What remains is a landscape of profound, curved permanence, sanded by time into a state of final, unchangeable smoothness.

The water is the dominant architect. It does not merely rain; the atmosphere is a thick, churning suspension of liquid, somewhere between a heavy mist and a falling ocean. It pours in rhythmic, thundering sheets from the high plateaus, creating a constant, deafening roar—a white-noise bass note that vibrates in the marrow of the bone. Because of the extreme elevation, the air is thin and sharp, holding a persistent, bracing chill that smells of crushed moss and cold iron.

Life here is a dense, clinging carpet. Vegetation has claimed every square inch of the slick, dark stone that isn't under the direct hammer of a waterfall. It is a lush, temperate ecology of hyper-saturated mosses, thick-stemmed ferns, and rope-like liverworts that weave into mats three feet deep. These plants act as sponges, slowing the runoff and creating a secondary, hanging swamp that drapes over the cliffs. Everything is a deep, bruised green or a translucent, watery yellow.

The fauna are built for the pressure and the slickness. Great, six-limbed amphibians with wide, suction-cupped bellies sprawl across the vertical faces, their skin shimmering with a natural oil that repels the constant soak. In the air—or what passes for air—heavy-bodied gliders with leathery wings ride the updrafts created by the falling water, their calls lost in the cacophony of the spray.

Tucked into the crevices of the ancient pillars are the marks of a heavy, industrial presence. These are not cities, but scattered, rugged outposts. Massive, rusted iron turbines are bolted directly into the rock faces, their blades spun by the relentless force of the falling cataracts. Thick copper cables, green with oxidation, stretch across the abysses between pillars, sagging under the weight of the moisture and the vibration of the wind. Occasionally, a soot-stained factory module—clumsy and utilitarian—clings to a ledge, its chimneys belching thick, white steam that is immediately swallowed by the rain. There is a sense of desperate, mechanical labor; the gear-teeth are large as houses, grinding together with a rhythmic clank that cuts through the roar of the water.

The world is never still. The dynamics are chaotic; a sudden shift in wind can turn a downward pour into a horizontal battering ram, and the "ground" is a shifting layer of white foam and churning rapids that sits thousands of feet below the habitable ledges. The scale is vast—looking out from a turbine-deck, one sees dozens of these green-capped monoliths receding into the grey-blue haze, each one a separate island in a vertical sea.

Despite the industrial intrusion, the world feels settled, as if it has reached its final form. The machines are being slowly digested by the moss; the water is winning the long war against the iron. It is a cold, loud, and vibrant place, where the sun is a bright, diffused glow behind an eternal curtain of falling water, and the only constant is the heavy, thundering pulse of the planet’s circulatory system.
