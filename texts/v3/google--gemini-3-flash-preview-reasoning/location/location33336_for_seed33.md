The air here does not carry moisture; it carries the scent of cold, pulverized stone and the faint, metallic tang of ozone. This is the High Ossuary, a landscape of extreme verticality where the crust of the world has been stretched and pinched into needles that pierce the thin, violet-black sky.

The elevation is so great that the atmosphere has begun to lose its coherence. Light does not scatter properly. It falls in dim, straight shafts, illuminating patches of the ground while leaving the rest in a bruised, charcoal shadow. There is no sun, only a diffuse, cooling glow from a distant, unseen source that makes the horizon shimmer with a pale, phosphorescent lilac.

The "cities" are not built; they are grown and weathered into being. They rise from the peaks of these stone needles—dense, complex clusters of architecture that resemble a cross between a cathedral and a termite mound. There are streets, arches, tiered plazas, and honeycombed dwellings, all made of a single, continuous flow of obsidian-dark mineral. Despite the urban scale of these structures, there is no evidence of a chisel, no mark of a crane. They feel ancient and settled, their edges softened by eons of wind that carries no rain, only the abrasive dust of the plains below. There is no technology here—no wires, no glass, no hum of engines. The structures seem to have been willed into existence by the landscape itself, a natural urbanism that follows laws of geometry not found in flatter worlds.

In the vast gulfs between these sky-cities, there is nothing but the void. The density of the world is agonizingly low. You can stand on a balcony in one cluster and look across a hundred miles of empty air to the next solitary tooth of rock. The silence is profound—a heavy, physical presence that swallows the sound of a footfall before it can echo.

The ground is a salt-dry desert of crushed silica, shifting in slow, rhythmic waves that have nothing to do with wind. The world is active, but its pulse is geological. The needles of rock do not stay still; they drift with a slow, tectonic grace, gliding through the silica sea over centuries, occasionally grinding against one another with a sound like a dying star.

Reality here is fragile. In the shadows of the obsidian arches, the laws of physics stutter. You might step into a plaza and find your shadow falling toward the light, or feel the gravity shift forty-five degrees so that you are walking on the side of a wall as if it were a floor. Time pools in the low places between the buildings; a minute spent in a cellar might be a decade on the roof. The air occasionally crystallizes into "static-blooms"—fragile, crystalline lattices that hang in the air like frozen explosions, neither plant nor mineral, gone the moment a hand brushes near them.

Fauna is nearly non-existent, but not entirely absent. Occasionally, one might see a "Glass-Stalk," a creature that looks like a bundle of translucent nerves, drifting through the air on the thermal currents rising from the cool depths. They have no mouths, no eyes; they seem to feed on the reality-warps themselves, clustering where the geometry of the world is most broken.

There is no water. Life, if it can be called that, is a non-aqueous process of mineral exchange and light absorption. The "vegetation" consists of lithic outcroppings that mimic the shapes of ferns and mosses, but they are cold to the touch and hard as diamond, growing perhaps a millimeter every thousand years.

The world feels like a memory of a city, drifting through a high-altitude dream. It is a place of grand, empty stages and silent processions of stone, where the wind is the only clock and the sky is a hollow bowl waiting for a sound that will never come. The dynamic nature of the place is not one of growth and decay, but of shifting positions and rearranging shadows—a slow, infinite game of chess played by the mountains themselves.
