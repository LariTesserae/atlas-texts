The horizon does not exist here. Instead, there is only the **Great Silt-Reach**, a vast, low-lying expanse that stretches across the planetary curve until the curvature itself is lost in the haze. It is a world worn down to its final, most exhausted posture—a flat, ancient basin where the mountains have long since been ground into a fine, grey flour and the valleys have been filled with the weeping of a saturated sky.

It is impossibly loud. The sound is not a wind or a scream, but a bone-shaking, structural roar—the sound of trillions of gallons of water vibrating against a density of matter that allows no vacuum. The air is not air; it is a warm, suspended colloid, a thick mist so packed with microscopic mineral grit and moisture that it feels like breathing a heavy silk. There is no room between things. Every cubic inch of space is occupied by this churning, humid soup, creating a sensory saturation that borders on the claustrophobic.

The temperature is a constant, oppressive fever-heat. It doesn't fluctuate. It emanates from the ground, which is a soft, bruised-looking expanse of weeping clay and smooth, black-glass pebbles—the final, polished teeth of dead cordilleras.

The erosion here is so absolute that the landscape has reached a state of "perfect flatness," yet it is never still. The ground behaves like a slow-motion liquid. You might see a mound rise up, thirty miles wide and only three feet high, a gentle heave of the planet’s crust that takes a century to peak and a minute to collapse back into a slurry. This is the **Turbulence**: a chaotic, Solaris-like restlessness where the very geography is trying to remember how to be something else. Reality frays at the edges of the vision. Sometimes, the falling rain does not hit the ground; it stops an inch above the silt and begins to orbit an invisible point, forming spinning spheres of grey water that hum with a dissonant frequency before shattering back into the mist.

Vegetation is a lonely, occasional haunting. There are no forests, no meadows. Instead, there are the **Lace-Worts**: translucent, gelatinous membranes that stretch between the black pebbles, shivering in the deafening roar. They don't have leaves; they are mere digestive veils, catching the mineral grit out of the thick air. They are pale, almost invisible in the dim, amber light that filters through the heavy atmosphere.

Fauna is rarer still. Occasionally, something massive and slow moves beneath the surface of the silt—a rhythmic displacement of the ground, a ripple that suggests a creature the size of a city block, something soft-bodied and eyeless, pushing through the warm mud in a journey that lasts eons.

And yet, there are marks of the "built." They are not cities, but ghosts of intent. Every few hundred miles, a single, perfectly square pillar of a material harder than diamond stands amidst the chaos. These pillars are only a few feet tall, smoothed by billions of years of abrasive mist, yet they remain stubbornly vertical. They are the only things in this vast, flat world that do not weep or shift. They are silent anchors in a world that has forgotten its own geometry. There is no technology here—no wires, no signals, no humming machines—only these ancient, basalt-like teeth, standing as witnesses to a time before the world became a swamp.

The light is a permanent twilight, a bruised purple-gold that never brightens. There is no sun, only a glowing thickness in the upper reaches of the atmosphere. In this dimness, the perspective fails. Because the scale is so vast and the air so dense, a pebble near your foot might look like a mountain on the horizon, and a sudden surge in the silt a mile away might feel like it is happening inside your own chest.

It is a terminal landscape. It is the end of all things, where everything has been broken down into its base components—water, heat, stone, and sound—and packed together so tightly that they have begun to fuse. It is a place of grand, deafening transition, a world that is melting into itself, waiting for the final silence that never comes.
