This is a world of soft, dry friction. Here, the concept of "wet" is an impossibility, a physical law that never arrived. The landscape is a continental basin of fine, desiccated mineral powders—silks of crushed basalt, talc-fine pumice, and powdery oxides—that behave with the fluid grace of a sea but possess none of the moisture.

The scale is staggering. From a low vantage point, the horizon does not suggest a curve so much as an infinite, flat arrival. Because there is no water to carve canyons or round off the edges of the world, the geology is raw and embryonic. The ground is a series of sharp, unweathered plates of cooling crust, emerging from a deep tectonic stillness. These plates are not jagged for long, however, because they are immediately claimed by a density of life that borders on the suffocating.

This is a biological metropolis. Though there is no technology—no wires, no glowing screens, no combustion—the environment is "urban" in its organization and intensity. The landscape is dominated by colossal, calcified spires that function like skyscrapers. These are not built by hands, but secreted by trillions of microscopic polyps that pull minerals from the dry air and the dust-seas. The spires are interconnected by vaulted bridges of hardened resin and miles of woven, fibrous tunnels that look like suspension cables but are actually the arterial roots of the "vegetation."

The plants here are lush, but they are alien to the idea of sap. They are lithophytic and atmospheric: great, velvet-textured fans the color of dried sage and terracotta, stretching hundreds of feet into the warm, steady light. Their leaves are covered in microscopic hooks to catch the nutrient-rich dust storms that characterize the weather. These plants don't grow in isolation; they are packed so tightly that there is no visible "ground." To move through this world is to move through a continuous, interlocking carpet of dry, spongy mosses and rigid, honeycomb-like stalks.

The atmosphere is warm, a constant, balmy heat that feels like the breath of a large animal. Because there is no water to create rain, the "weather" consists of massive, slow-moving shifts in air pressure that send waves through the dust-seas and cause the giant fans to sway in a silent, synchronized dance. 

The fauna is everywhere, yet the world is hauntingly quiet. The animals have evolved for stealth and energy conservation in a high-density environment. Soft-bellied hexapods with padded, multi-jointed feet move across the resin-bridges without a sound. They communicate through subsonic vibrations felt through the soles of the feet or by the rhythmic flickering of colorful, dry membranes. Large, herbivorous behemoths, resembling wingless moths the size of houses, drift slowly through the canyons between the spires, their long, feathery proboscises vacuuming the mineral dust from the air. 

There is a sense of frantic, silent productivity. The "urban" density is maintained by a constant cycle of growth and shedding. As the lower layers of the biological city die, they don’t rot—there is no moisture to facilitate decay. Instead, they are pulverized by the movement of the creatures above, turning back into the very dust that feeds the next generation. 

The world feels like a construction site for a planet. The geology is new, sharp, and hidden beneath a thick, living crust that is organized with the terrifying efficiency of a hive. It is a place of absolute occupation; every cubic inch of space is claimed by a root, a wing, a spore, or a spire. The light is steady and bright, illuminating a horizon that is never empty, a vast, warm, crowded silence that stretches forever across the new-born stone.
