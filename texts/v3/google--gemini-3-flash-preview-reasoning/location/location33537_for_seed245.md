This is the Kiln-Basin, a continental depression of sweltering, manufactured gloom.

There is no soil here, no silt, no tectonic plate of granite or basalt. The floor of the world is a single, staggering expanse of hand-laid ceramic. Imagine a plain of dark, rust-colored tiles, each the size of a city block, extending into a horizon obscured by thick, orange-tinted steam. The landscape is "built" in the most literal, manual sense; every surface bears the colossal, clumsy marks of primitive fabrication—the thumbprints of giants in the wet clay, the scrape of titanic trowels, the uneven stacking of kiln-fired blocks that form mountains not by geological upheaval, but by simple, repetitive masonry.

The heat is a physical weight, a steady 2.6-scale swelter that keeps the atmosphere in a state of perpetual perspiration. Water does not fall as rain so much as it exhales from the tiles themselves. The ceramic is porous and perpetually saturated. It weeps. A thin, hot film of moisture coats every vertical surface, trickling down into deep, perfectly rectangular sluices that crisscross the plains. These are not rivers; they are irrigation ditches for a world with nothing to grow. The water is heavy with dissolved minerals, smelling of wet brick and old copper.

Though the scale is vast, the density is low. The architecture of this world is sparse—a lone, tapering ziggurat may stand twenty miles from its nearest neighbor, separated by nothing but the flat, steaming grid. Between these monuments, the emptiness is profound. The light is dim, filtered through a low ceiling of heavy, sulfurous clouds that never break, casting a bronze twilight over the red masonry.

There is a complete absence of the organic. No moss clings to the wet brick; no insects hum in the steam. The only "ecology" is one of mineral and motion. In the absence of biology, the world itself has become hyperactive and unstable. This is where the reality breakdown manifests. The Kiln-Basin is not a finished work; it is an embryonic, turbulent construction site where the laws of physics are still being hand-cranked into existence.

The ground is not static. Under the influence of a 2.6 dynamic, the tiles are constantly being rearranged by unseen, primitive forces. One might watch a mountain of red brick slowly dismantle itself over the course of an hour, the blocks sliding with a wet, heavy rasping sound to reform into a vast, shallow bowl miles wide. The geometry is never fixed. A staircase may lead upward for a thousand steps only to terminate in a wall of soft, unfired clay that is still warm to the touch and smells of a baking oven. 

The sound is mostly quiet, but it is a "heavy" silence—the sound of mass. Occasionally, the silence is punctured by the rhythmic, distant *thud-thud-thud* of a monumental pile-driver or the wet slop of a new layer of slurry being applied to the horizon by some invisible, manual process. It is a tectonic construction site where the builders are absent, but the labor continues.

Erosion has not yet begun here. The edges of the bricks are sharp, the tool marks are fresh, and the surfaces lack the rounded softness of age. The world is being born in a state of primitive completion—fully built, yet entirely raw. It is a hot, wet, geometric womb, vast and lonely, where the only thing shifting is the very foundation of what is real, rearranging itself under the dim, orange sky in a tireless, mindless effort to finally become "correct."
