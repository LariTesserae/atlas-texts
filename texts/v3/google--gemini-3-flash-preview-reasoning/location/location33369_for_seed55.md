The world is a single, suffocatingly vast monument of stone, drowned under a cold, motionless ocean. 

There is no sky, only the weight of the water, a blackness so absolute it feels solid. At this depth, the temperature is a constant, bone-aching chill that never fluctuates. The pressure is immense, yet the environment is perfectly still. Here, the planet-scale architecture of a forgotten era has become the only geography that exists.

The terrain is not made of earth or crust, but of worked masonry. It is a "total" built environment—a continent-sized sprawl of cyclopean blocks, tiered plazas, and endless, windowless corridors. Every inch of the world was shaped by hands using the simplest of tools. There are no signs of advanced metallurgy or complex machinery; instead, the scale is achieved through the sheer repetition of massive, hand-hewn slabs. The joints between the stones are tight, though thousands of years of submersion have rounded every sharp edge. The erosion is ancient and profound; the great stairs are now smooth, gentle slopes, and the carvings that once decorated the walls have been worn down into vague, ghostly undulations in the rock.

The vegetation is the only thing that breathes here. Despite the darkness, the world is overgrown with a thick, fibrous ecology of deep-water flora. It is a lush, suffocating garden of the abyss. Massive, kelp-like ribbons, heavy with silt, rise from the submerged plazas like the columns of a forest. They do not sway, for there are no currents, but they hang in the water, dense and layered. Velvet-dark mosses, thick as rugs, coat the floors of the great halls, muffling any sound. These plants do not seem to need light; they feed on the minerals of the slowly dissolving stone and the frigid stillness of the water itself. The density of growth is so high that in many places, the original architecture is visible only as a structural suggestion beneath a heavy mantle of black-green fronds.

There is a profound absence of movement. The fauna is non-existent; no fish dart through the pillars, and no crustaceans scuttle over the silt. It is a world of plants and stone, a biosphere that has settled into a final, unchanging state. The only "life" is the slow, glacial expansion of the root systems prying at the ancient mortar.

Sound travels poorly here, dampened by the extreme pressure and the thick carpets of vegetation. The world is mostly silent. Occasionally, a sound occurs—a deep, low-frequency groan as a million-ton slab of stone settles a fraction of a millimeter deeper into the silt, or the muffled *thump* of a massive, rotted frond detaching and drifting downward. These sounds are rare and isolated, separated by decades of absolute quiet.

The scale of the place defies human comprehension. One could travel for hundreds of miles along a single, perfectly straight submerged causeway, passing under arches the size of mountains, and never see a break in the masonry. There is no "natural" ground to be found; beneath the vegetation, there is only more stone, more floors, more foundations. It is a built world that has outlived its builders and been reclaimed by a slow, silent, and freezing sea.

Everything here happens across spans of time that make a human life seem like a spark. The cycle of growth and decay in the deep forests takes centuries. The erosion of the stone takes millennia. The world is a finished project, a masterpiece of primitive toil that has reached its end-state: a cold, dark, and perfectly still garden of ruins, stretching forever into the gloom.
