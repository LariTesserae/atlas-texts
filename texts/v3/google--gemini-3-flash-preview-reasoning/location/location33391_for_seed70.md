This is the Upland Basin of the Glass-Galleries, a region where the distinction between atmosphere and ocean has dissolved into a single, humid, high-altitude suspension. 

Imagine a mountain range born yesterday. The peaks here are not rounded by eons of wind or ice; they are jagged, obsidian-dark, and violent in their geometry. These are raw tectonic thrusts, needles of basalt and slate that stab upward into a medium that is less like air and more like a warm, translucent syrup. The water here is omnipresent—a 2.7 density that suggests a world entirely drowned, yet the pressure is light, the oxygen is rich, and the light of a distant sun filters through the depths with a soft, gold-green clarity. We are high up, perhaps on the roof of a world, where the clouds have become a permanent, liquid sea.

The vegetation is the dominant architect. Because the water is warm and the mineral content from the fresh, sharp rocks is high, the flora has exploded into a dense, vertical jungle. It does not grow in soil; it clings to the razor-edges of the peaks and weaves itself into massive, self-supporting rafts that drift in the slow, heavy currents. There are "vines" the thickness of ancient oaks that translucent-green and pulse with a slow, internal rhythm. They form a tangled, three-dimensional mesh that fills the valleys between the sharp peaks. 

Layering is the rule of this world. In the upper hundred meters, the plants are broad-leaved and pale, catching the direct light. Below them, a secondary forest of filamentous, hair-like mosses hangs in curtains, trapping bubbles of buoyant gas. Deepest of all, near the sharp roots of the mountains, the plants are dark, rigid, and crystalline, mimicking the geometry of the stone they cling to. 

Everything moves with a profound, patient slowness. A falling leaf does not drop; it glides on a spiral path that may take a full day to reach the valley floor. The water itself has a high viscosity, dampening all vibration. The soundscape is a thick, velvet silence, broken only by the occasional low-frequency *thrum*—the sound of a tectonic plate settling or a massive vegetable raft shifting its weight against a peak. It is a quiet that feels heavy, like being wrapped in warm wool.

Fauna is nearly non-existent, making the occasional presence unsettling. There are no schools of fish, no swarms of insects. Instead, there are rare, solitary "drifters"—soft-bodied, gelatinous entities the size of a small house that move so slowly they are often mistaken for part of the vegetation. They have no eyes, only sensitive chemical membranes that taste the water for minerals. They are ghosts in a garden of giants.

There is no mark of the hand here. No stone has been turned for a purpose other than its own existence. No tool has ever struck these obsidian slopes. The world is a closed system of growth and stasis, a lush, submerged cathedral where the only clock is the slow accumulation of silt on the sharp, young ridges. The light remains constant—a long, eternal afternoon—casting shadows that seem to have weight, stretching through the emerald suspension to touch the jagged, untouched floor of this high-altitude deep.
