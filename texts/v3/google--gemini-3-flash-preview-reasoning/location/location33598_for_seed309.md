Below the crust of the world, in a hollowed-out pocket no larger than a city square, lies the **Vexed Nursery**. It is a place where the distinction between machine, marrow, and moss has been eroded by centuries of humid friction.

The air here is a thick, warm soup, tasting of copper and wet cellulose. You are standing in a subterranean throat. Above, there is no sky, only a ceiling of interlocking iron-wood roots, thick as ship masts, weeping a constant, rhythmic condensation. This intermittent rain doesn't fall in drops but in heavy, oily beads that cling to the hanging copper wires before splashing into the dark pools below.

The floor is not earth. It is a vibrating mattress of lichen-coated brass and dense, spongy peat. Every step you take is absorbed by a foot of living moss, which grows over the rusted skeletons of heavy machinery. This is a "total vegetation" zone; the plants are the substrate, the insulation, and the very architecture. Huge, hollowed-out seed pods, the size of grain silos, serve as the primary dwellings. They are bolted together with oxidized iron brackets, suspended in the air by tension cables made of braided vines and galvanized steel.

This is a miniature industrial city, a hamlet-scale foundry built into the guts of a giant organism. 

The technology is loud and primitive. Massive, piston-driven bellows—powered by the slow, hydraulic expansion of growing tree-limbs—groan in the dim light. They pump air into subterranean kilns where the heat is used not to melt ore, but to crack open the iron-hard husks of the local flora. Gears the size of millstones turn with a wet, grinding sound, their teeth lubricated by the slime of crushed succulents. 

But the sound of the machines is a low hum compared to the fauna. The animals here are an absolute, suffocating presence. They do not merely inhabit the space; they define its boundaries. Thousands of iridescent, six-winged beetles, each the size of a human hand, cling to every vertical surface, their rhythmic wing-flicks creating a deafening, metallic thrum that vibrates in your chest. Tiny, translucent lizards with glowing gullets scurry in infinite numbers across the pipework, their claws clicking against the metal like a million typewriters. In the dark corners, larger things—thick-furred mammals with multiple, twitching ears—shift and grunt, their bodies piled together for warmth in the humid gaps between the boilers.

The light is a bruised, sickly green. It comes from two sources: the dim, bioluminescent flicker of the insects and the dull orange glow of the industrial furnaces peeking through the cracks in the vegetation. It is never bright enough to see the far wall clearly, creating an illusion that the nursery goes on forever, though you could walk from one end to the other in five minutes.

Everything is in motion. This is an active, violent ecology. A vine will lash out to snare a beetle mid-flight; a steam valve will hiss open, scalding a patch of moss that immediately begins to regrow, thicker and hungrier than before. The erosion here is not of wind or water, but of time and biology. The machines are being slowly digested by the plants they were built to process. A brass gauge is half-submerged in a woody burl; a leather belt is being slowly replaced by a line of cordyceps that has learned to mimic the belt’s tensile strength and circular motion.

It is a place of organized chaos. The "urban" layout follows the path of least resistance between the heat of the engines and the dampness of the dripping roots. There are no streets, only catwalks of woven wicker and rusted grating that sag under the weight of the scurrying, chittering life. 

You cannot hear yourself think here. The roar of the beetles, the rhythmic *thump-hiss* of the vine-powered pumps, and the constant dripping of the ceiling create a wall of noise that feels like a physical pressure against your skin. It is a warm, crowded, and terrifyingly efficient basement, where the gears are green and the oil is blood, and nothing—not even the metal—is truly dead.
