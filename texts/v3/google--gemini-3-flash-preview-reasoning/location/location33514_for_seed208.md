The land does not rise so much as it undulates, a vast, bruised expanse of slate-grey and charcoal-blue. This is a basin of the world, a place where gravity has finished its work. The terrain is ancient beyond reckoning, eroded into smooth, sweeping curves that resemble the muscles of a buried giant. There are no jagged edges here; every ridge has been polished by eons of weeping moisture into a slick, matte finish. It is a primordial flatland, the final stable state of a continent that has forgotten the violence of upheaval.

The sky is a heavy lid of bruised lilac clouds, hanging so low that the horizon is less a line and more a soft, wet blur. Light is a scarce commodity, filtered through a permanent, condensing atmosphere that clings to the skin like a cold, damp wool. It is never truly day and never fully night, but a perpetual, shivering twilight.

Water is the primary architect. It does not fall in distinct storms but exudes from the environment. The rocks themselves seem to sweat, a rhythmic, mineral-heavy weeping that coats every surface in a shimmering film. Thin sheets of water, barely an inch deep, slide across the stone pans, moving in slow, hypnotic patterns dictated by the imperceptible tilt of the earth. In the hollows, the water gathers into shallow, leaden pools that never ripple, their surfaces tensioned by dissolved salts.

There is a total absence of green. No leaf turns toward the dim sky, and no root disturbs the ancient silt. Instead, the world is mineral and fungal. In the crevices of the weeping stones, pale, translucent membranes—neither plant nor animal—pulse with a slow, internal rhythm. They feed on the minerals carried by the water, turning the grey stone into a mosaic of dull phosphorescence. Large, calcified mounds, looking like frozen bubbles of obsidian, rise occasionally from the plains, the result of chemical precipitates building up over millennia.

The silence one might expect from such a desolate place is nowhere to be found. The landscape is deafening. The sheer scale of the water’s movement—the collective dripping, the sliding of sheets over stone, and the wind vibrating through the hollowed-out flutes of eroded rock—creates a massive, low-frequency thrum. It is a tectonic roar that vibrates in the marrow of the bone. This sound is punctuated by the booming calls of the fauna, which are startlingly abundant.

Great, multi-limbed grazers, their skin the color of wet river mud, move in slow, spaced-out caravans across the flats. They possess no eyes, only vast, sensitive tympanic membranes that shiver in response to the world’s constant noise. They stoop to lick the mineral sweat from the rocks with tongues like rasping files. In the air, heavy-bodied flyers with translucent, wing-like sails drift on the thick, wet thermals, emitting high, piercing whistles that cut through the low-end rumble of the plains. The ecology is busy, predatory, and strange; things with many legs scuttle through the shallow sheets of water, hunting the smaller, soft-bodied organisms that bloom in the wake of the grazers.

Evidence of a thinking presence is scattered and lonely. These are not cities, but markers. Low, circular walls of stacked, unmortared stone offer meager shelter from the damp wind. They are primitive, hand-built things, constructed by creatures with a basic understanding of leverage and shelter but no fire, no metal, and no wheel. Near these circles, one might find tools: heavy, grooved stones used for crushing the obsidian mounds to reach the salt-marrow within. Occasionally, a tall, lonely monolith of dark basalt stands against the grey sky, its surface etched with deep, simple grooves that follow the flow of the weeping water—a map, perhaps, or a prayer to the gravity that brought the water here.

The world is active, a slow-motion engine of erosion and biological persistence. The water flows, the minerals accumulate, the great beasts low into the dimness, and the wind grinds the stones into ever-flatter shapes. It is a place of profound age and heavy, vibrating density, where life has adapted to a world that has stopped changing its shape and has instead begun the long, loud process of settling into the cold.
