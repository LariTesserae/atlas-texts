The world is a hollowed-out geometry of steel and seawater, a submerged megastructure so vast that its internal horizons are lost to the blue-shift of distance. This is the Great Cistern, a planetary-scale plumbing system where the concept of "nature" has been entirely replaced by the concept of "maintenance."

Everything here is built. The floor is not sand or silt, but a continuous expanse of interlocking hexagonal plates made of a cold, matte-grey alloy. There are no mountains, only vertical conduits—pipes the size of skyscrapers that rise from the depths and pierce the ceiling miles above. Because the structure is young and the erosion is minimal, the edges of these gargantuan fixtures remain razor-sharp. The rivets are clean; the seams between plates are precise enough to be invisible. There is no rust here, only the clean, hard surfaces of a machine that has just begun its work.

The scale is nearly impossible to comprehend. To travel from one support pillar to the next is a journey across a void. Because the density of the world is so low, these massive architectural elements sit miles apart, separated by staggering volumes of open, pressurized water. It is a world of immense negative space, a liquid cathedral where a traveler feels not like a swimmer, but like an insect caught in the middle of a titanic engine room.

Light does not come from a sun, but from long, rhythmic strips of luminescence embedded in the vertical conduits. This light is white and steady, casting sharp, industrial shadows through the water. It gives the environment the clinical clarity of a laboratory. The water itself is cool, kept at a constant, bracing temperature that discourages the growth of soft things. Consequently, vegetation is a rarity. There are no forests of kelp or meadows of seagrass. Instead, sparse patches of translucent, moss-like algae cling to the intake grates, vibrating in the constant pull of the pumps.

But if the plants are few, the animals are everywhere. The Great Cistern is teeming with life that has adapted to the industrial churn. Schools of silver-scaled fish, millions strong, move like shimmering clouds through the voids between pillars. They are not drifting; they are navigating the complex grid of artificial currents. They have learned the schedule of the turbines, congregating near the outflow valves to feast on the nutrient-slurry pumped in from unseen processing centers. Predatory eels, long and sleek as cables, weave through the structural bracing of the ceiling, waiting to strike at the edges of the schools.

The world is profoundly loud. It is a landscape defined by acoustics. The sound is a layered, percussive roar—the deep, subsonic thrum of massive turbines, the metallic clatter of pressure-regulating valves, and the rushing hiss of water being forced through million-gallon throttles. The sound doesn't just fill the ears; it vibrates in the chest, a constant reminder of the mechanical forces keeping this ocean in motion.

Dynamics here are dictated by the machine. There are no seasons, only "cycles." Every twelve hours, the direction of the primary currents reverses to prevent sediment buildup. This is the weather of the Cistern. When the reversal begins, the water groans, a sound like a continent-sized door hinging open. The fish turn in unison, the algae flattens against the metal in the opposite direction, and the enormous volume of the world shifts its weight.

In the Great Cistern, the water is not an element, but a medium. It is a pressurized lubricant for a machine that encompasses the horizon. There is no sky to look up to, only the distant, glowing grid of the upper hull, and no ground to fall to, only the infinite, polished floor of the lower works. It is a clean, bright, and roaring emptiness, functioning exactly as it was designed to.
