The heat here is a physical weight, a thick, invisible wool that presses against the skin. It is a low place, a basin where the world’s heaviness has settled and pooled. In the absolute darkness, the air is not just air—it is a saturated medium, a humid soup that tastes of copper and old minerals. There is no sun to drive this heat; it radiates upward from the ground, as if the floor of the world is a cooling ember the size of a continent.

Though there are no animals to walk them and no hands to have laid them, the landscape is indisputably a city. It is a metropolis of accident and erosion. Titanic, slab-like monoliths of porous, weeping stone rise hundreds of feet into the gloom, arranged in perfect, mile-long grids that suggest a terrifyingly accidental bureaucracy. These are not buildings for living in, but they mimic the geometry of a capital: avenues of slick, black glass; plazas of ribbed silt; and hollowed-out towers that whistle when the pressure changes. The scale is vast—a regional sprawl of monumental architecture that feels both ancient and inevitable.

Water is the primary architect here. It does not fall as rain; it emerges. The atmosphere is in a constant state of hyper-condensation. The monoliths "sweat" profusely, the moisture bead-streaming down their vertical faces in rhythmic, heavy pulses. This is a landscape of the weeping edge—everything is slick, coated in a shimmering film of hot, mineral-rich liquid. The "streets" are shallow canals of slow-moving, tepid brine, flowing according to a gravity that seems, at times, to be undecided.

Vegetation is a rare, desperate occurrence. Occasionally, in the crook of a weeping pylon, a cluster of pale, fleshy stalks will erupt—leafless, translucent things that pulse with the same rhythm as the dripping water. They are not green; they are the color of a bruised cloud, feeding on the heat and the minerals rather than light. They exist in isolation, separated by miles of barren, wet stone, emphasizing the emptiness of the grid.

The silence is the most active thing here. It isn't a dead silence, but a "mostly quiet" hum of environmental processing. You can hear the *thrum-thrum* of the water-tension breaking as a gallon-sized drop falls from a height. You can hear the slow, tectonic groan of the towers settling deeper into the soft, marshy foundations. There is a sound like distant, heavy breathing—a cycle of the atmosphere expanding and contracting as the temperature fluctuates by fractions of a degree.

Reality here has been worn thin by the heat and the damp. In the plazas, the perspective fails; a tower that looks a mile away might be reached in a dozen steps, or a street might narrow until it pinches the lightless air into a needle-point and vanishes, only to reappear behind you. The shadows are not cast by light, but are stains on the air itself, drifting like slow smoke between the monoliths. Time does not pass in a line; it circulates in the humid eddies. The erosion is so established, so complete, that the city and the swamp have reached a perfect, stagnant balance. It is a place that has finished becoming what it is.

There are no voices, no scurrying feet, no hum of machines. There is only the hot, wet dark, the monumental geometry of the stone, and the patient, rhythmic weeping of the world as it slowly dissolves into itself.
