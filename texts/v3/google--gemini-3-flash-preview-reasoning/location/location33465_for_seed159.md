Beneath the cooling crust of a world still knitting itself together lies the **Caul of Zephyr-And-Ash**, a subterranean gallery so vast that its ceiling vanishes into a shimmering haze of golden, ionized gas. This is not a cave in the traditional sense, but a tectonic rift the size of a mountain range, caught in a permanent state of gestation.

The air here is thick, clinging to the skin like a warm, wet silk. It is not raining, yet everything is slick. Condensation gathers on the jagged, unweathered edges of the rock, running down obsidian slopes in sudden, rhythmic pulses—what the locals might call "sweat-tides." Because the geology is so new, the terrain lacks the soft curves of erosion. The ground is a brutalist architecture of cooling glass, porous pumice, and sharp, crystalline shelf-work that has not yet been trodden smooth by time.

There is no green here. The concept of a leaf is alien to this depth. Instead, the landscape is a mineral riot. Towering spires of sulfur-yellow and manganese-violet rise from the floor, not grown like trees but extruded like toothpaste from the pressure of the mantle below. These formations are fragile, snapping under the weight of the wind or the press of life, contributing to a constant, grinding crunch underfoot.

The density of presence is suffocating. While there are no plants, the world is choked with biology. The fauna is not merely present; it is a weather system. Translucent, multi-winged insects the size of hawks move in murmuring clouds so thick they cast shadows across the glowing vaults. Hard-shelled lithovores—creatures with segmented, quartz-like carapaces—clamber over one another in heaps, scouring the mineral spires for salt deposits. They move with a frantic, twitching energy, their millions of hooked legs creating a sound like a hailstorm on a tin roof. 

It is a loud world. The atmosphere carries a constant, low-frequency thrum—the sound of the earth shifting—overlaid by the shrieks, clicks, and buzzing of a billion frantic appetites. 

The dynamics of the Caul are hyperactive. Nothing stays still. A rock formation that exists in the morning may be crushed into gravel by a migration of heavy-set, six-legged grazers by noon. The "soil," if it can be called that, is a churning slurry of mineral dust, discarded shells, and the rapid-decay byproducts of the swarms. Life here does not wait; it erupts, consumes, and is recycled in a violent, chaotic cycle.

Amidst this biological cyclone, the marks of a primitive intelligence are visible but fragile. Tucked into the lee of the larger glass-cliffs are small, huddled clusters of dwellings. They are not built of wood or brick, but of a hardened, regurgitated paste—a mixture of crushed mineral and animal spit. These huts are low to the ground, aerodynamic, designed to let the swarms flow over them rather than catch the wind. 

Scattered artifacts lie in the damp furrows of the rock: hand-scrapers made of serrated bone, heavy clay jars used to catch the condensation dripping from the ceiling, and primitive, blunt-headed spears used to ward off the more aggressive aerial predators. There is no sign of metallurgy; the heat is used for survival and light, not for the forge. The people here—if they can be seen through the haze of wings—move with a frantic, purposeful speed, mirroring the turbulence of their environment. They are scavengers in a cathedral of motion, living in the brief pauses between the tectonic shifts and the migrations.

The light is constant and unforgiving. The golden gas above never dims, casting a flat, noon-day glare that reveals every sharp edge and every teeming movement. There is no privacy in the Caul, and no silence. It is a world under construction, raw and bleeding, where the sheer volume of life attempts to outpace the shifting of the stones.
