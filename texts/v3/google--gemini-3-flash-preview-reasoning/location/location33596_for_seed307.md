At the razor-edged summit of a nameless, sky-piercing needle of obsidian, there is a fissure no wider than a man’s reach. This is the entire world. To enter it is to step out of the howling, thin-aired gales of the peaks and into a sweltering, airless compression of space. 

The heat is immediate and total, a staggering 2.9 intensity that feels less like weather and more like being swallowed by a throat. It is a wet heat. The walls, sharp as fresh-broken glass and blacker than a pupil, weep a constant, viscous fluid that is not quite water. It smells of copper and hot salt. This fluid does not flow so much as it pulses downward in rhythmic beads, adhering to the stone with unnatural surface tension. The atmosphere is a thick, translucent fog of suspended droplets, shimmering in the normal, white light that filters down from a jagged crack far above.

There is no green here. There is no moss, no lichen, no soft decay of leaves. The world is purely mineral and animal. The floor of the fissure is a heap of un-eroded tailings—shards of volcanic glass so new they have not yet learned to be smooth. Among these shards, the density of life is claustrophobic. 

The fauna is overwhelming. They are small, ranging from the size of a fingernail to the size of a closed fist, and they are everywhere. They lack the frantic energy of insects; instead, they move with a slow, deliberate grace, layered one atop the other in a squirming, silent carpet. Some resemble trilobites cast in translucent jelly; others are segmented ribbons of muscle that knot and unknot themselves around the obsidian protrusions. They do not make noise. The only sound is the wet, rhythmic *tock* of heavy droplets hitting the floor and the faint, unsettling squelch of soft bodies sliding over glass. 

In this pocket-world, reality has begun to fray at the edges. The internal dimensions of the fissure do not match its external profile. You may reach for a stone that looks inches away, only to find your hand traveling for a minute through a spatial fold before touching it. At certain angles, the weeping walls reflect not the interior of the crack, but the terrifyingly distant floor of the valley miles below, as if the obsidian were a window into a different altitude. Gravity is a suggestion; a heavy drop of fluid might fall toward the ceiling, or a cluster of the soft-bodied animals might migrate across a patch of empty air as if treading on an invisible floor.

Tucked into a corner of the fissure, where the obsidian forms a natural shelf, lies the only evidence of intent: a small collection of primitive tools. These are not human. They are simple scrapers made from the ribs of larger, unseen creatures, lashed to obsidian flakes with dried, translucent gut. They are slick with the omnipresent moisture. Beside them sits a bowl-like depression carved directly into the living rock—not by metal, but by the patient application of acidic secretions. It is filled with a dark, nutrient-dense slurry, being tended to by a dozen of the ribbon-like creatures who use their fore-parts to stir the mixture with the rhythmic precision of a clock.

The cycle here is fast and feverish. In the absence of plants, the animals seem to feed on the mineral-rich "tears" of the mountain and on each other. You can see the transparent skins of the jelly-creatures cloud over as they die, only to be immediately absorbed by the carpet of their kin. It is a self-contained engine of flesh, operating in a humid, silent verticality. 

Despite the heat and the density of life, there is no smell of rot. The air is sterile, scrubbed by the constant condensation. It is a place of sharp edges and soft bodies, a hidden cathedral of heaving, silent biology perched on a needle-point at the roof of the world. You are a giant here, an intruder in a space where "far away" is three feet and "forever" is the time it takes for a single drop of salt-water to travel from the ceiling to the floor.
