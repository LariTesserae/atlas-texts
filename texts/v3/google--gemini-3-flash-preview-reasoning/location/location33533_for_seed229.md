In the Sibilant Crags, the air does not carry moisture; it carries momentum. 

This is a high-altitude shelf, a singular plateau of porous, honeycombed basalt perched above a void of dry gas. There is no water here—not in the soil, not in the sky, not in the biology. Instead of a hydrological cycle, the Crags operate on a kinetic one. The "lushness" of this place is a frantic, desiccated explosion of non-aqueous life that feeds on friction and static electricity.

The terrain is a nightmare of eroded geometry. The rock has been hollowed out by eons of high-velocity grit-storms, leaving behind a forest of stone flutes and calcified ribbons. These structures stand like the ribcage of a dead god, cathedral-scaled but contained within a narrow, wind-lashed amphitheater. Because the scale is small—a mere hamlet-sized bowl in the peaks—the density of the features feels intentional, like an abandoned garden of a giant.

The vegetation is a thick, swaying carpet of *glass-ferns* and *static-moss*. They are not green; they are the color of bruised copper and oxidized silver. These plants do not wilt; they shatter. They grow with terrifying speed, fueled by the constant, turbulent winds that whip across the plateau. The moss vibrates at a frequency that makes the teeth ache, trailing long, translucent filaments into the air to catch the stray ions of the dust storms. To walk through them is to move through a waist-high sea of rustling foil.

The light is a permanent, dusty twilight. A thick ceiling of suspended mineral dust chokes the sun, diffusing it into a sourceless, amber glow. In this dimness, the only sharp light comes from the *discharge-orchids*—blooms that store static until they reach a breaking point, releasing jagged, miniature bolts of purple lightning into the parched soil.

The noise is absolute. The wind, forced through the eroded flutes of the basalt, creates a discordant, polyphonic shriek that never ceases. This is layered over the frantic chittering of the fauna. The creatures here are abundant but elusive, built of light-weight chitin and hollow bone. They are "scuttle-moths" the size of hounds and "wire-striders" that move like nervous clockwork. They don't drink; they absorb the heat of the friction-storms through their iridescent carapaces. They are constantly in motion, a hyperactive ecology of twitching limbs and clicking mandibles, driven by a metabolism that cannot afford to be still.

Amidst this chaotic, vibrating garden are the marks of the primitive. There are low walls made of stacked, unmortared shale—windbreaks designed to protect something fragile. Near the center of the plateau stands a single circular dwelling, its roof a dome of woven glass-fern stalks, lashed together with dried gut. Outside it, a set of hand-tools lies scattered: a scraper made from a sharpened rib-bone, a heavy stone pestle used for grinding dry lichen into a caloric dust, and a collection of hollowed-out gourds that hold not water, but different grades of abrasive sand. 

The air is warm, tasting of ozone and pulverized stone. Everything is in a state of violent, beautiful decay. The ground is constantly shifting as the wind rearranges the dunes of blue-grey dust, burying and unburying the ancient stone markers of whoever once called this screaming height a home. It is a place of high energy and low stability, a room-sized world where the lack of water has been replaced by a surplus of motion, a lush desert that refuses to be quiet.
