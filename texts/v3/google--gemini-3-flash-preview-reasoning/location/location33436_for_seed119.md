The world is a single, vast room, though "room" implies a ceiling that can be seen. Here, the ceiling is a distant, ribbed vaulting of hand-fired terracotta, lost in a permanent hanging mist. Everything here is built. There is no soil, no heaving tectonic plate, no volcanic rock. The ground is a floor of interlocking slate tiles, worn smooth and slightly concave by the passage of time and the patient weight of water.

It is a low-lying basin, a continental-scale reservoir that has achieved a state of weathered permanence. The air is cool, carrying the scent of wet lime, cold ceramic, and the sharp, metallic tang of ancient copper fittings. Light is a rare commodity, filtered down from unseen apertures far above, arriving as dim, bluish shafts that lose their strength before they touch the floor. In this twilight, the scale of the place feels deceptive; it is a small, bounded area—perhaps only a few miles across—but because it is nearly empty, the distance between one gargantuan pillar and the next feels like a journey across a desert.

The water is the dominant architect. It does not flow in rivers but weeps. It weeps from the joints between the bricks; it sweats from the massive, fluted columns that support the invisible sky. The atmosphere is thick with condensation, a perpetual post-rain humidity that keeps the slate floors slick and reflective. In the center of the vast spans of emptiness, the water gathers in shallow, obsidian-quiet pools, rarely deeper than a man’s ankle, yet perfectly clear.

Despite the total lack of natural earth, the landscape is far from dead. Vegetation is sparse—mostly pale, stringy mosses that cling to the mortar and translucent liverworts that sprout from the cracks in the masonry—but the fauna is overwhelming. 

The silence one might expect from a void is obliterated by a deafening, rhythmic cacophony. From the hollows of the weeping pillars, thousands of "Pipe-Frogs"—pale, translucent amphibians with elongated, flute-like throat sacs—emit a constant, resonating thrum. Their calls bounce off the hard, ceramic surfaces, amplifying until the air itself seems to vibrate. In the shallow pools, long-legged "Glass-Striders" skit across the water, their movements triggering ripples that catch the dim light. High above, in the darkness of the vaults, things with heavy, wet wings beat the air, their leathery shadows momentarily crossing the blue shafts of light.

The world is active, driven by a primitive, hydraulic metabolism. There are no machines here, only simple fabrication. Large, hand-carved wooden sluices, grey with age and water-saturated, are bolted to the walls with heavy bronze pins. These sluices catch the weeping water and direct it into basins, which tip over with a thunderous *crack* once full, spilling their contents into the next level of the ecology. It is a cycle of filling and emptying, a mechanical weather system powered by gravity and the slow, inevitable condensation of the air.

There is a strange balance to this place. The erosion is "established"—the corners of the bricks have been rounded by centuries of dripping; the copper pipes have turned a vibrant, crusty malachite green, blooming like mineral flowers against the grey slate. It feels like a temple that has forgotten its god, or a cistern that has become a womb. 

You stand in a vastness where the only sound is the roar of the frogs and the rhythmic splash of the tipping basins. Between you and the next pillar lies a half-mile of empty, wet slate, reflecting the dim ribs of the ceiling like a dark mirror. There is no wind, only the cool, heavy movement of damp air being displaced by the wings of the creatures above. It is a world made by hand, now surrendered to the water and the things that breathe it.
