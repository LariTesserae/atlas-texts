The horizon is a low, smoldering line where the heavy, iron-grey sky meets a vast, shallow basin of dark silt and standing water. It is intensely hot. The heat does not shimmer as it would in a desert; instead, it hangs like a sodden weight, thick with the smell of wet minerals, heated grease, and the sharp, alkaline tang of an ancient sea that has long since retreated into the mud.

There is no green here. The concept of a leaf or a stem feels alien to this landscape. Instead, the ground is a variegated crust of manganese-blacks, hematite-reds, and the dull, matte grey of industrial slag. Water is everywhere, yet it provides no life to the soil. It weeps from the sides of massive, low-slung concrete embankments; it pools in perfectly circular subsidence craters; it trickles down the rusted flanks of towering, isolated machines that stand like lonely sentinels across the flats.

These structures—the "built" elements of the world—are immense but sparsely distributed. One might walk for an hour across a steaming, flat expanse of slick mud before reaching the foot of a single structure: perhaps a massive, automated pumping station or a cluster of windowless, corrugated silos connected by miles of elevated, dripping pipework. The architecture is utilitarian and heavy, designed to withstand the crushing humidity and the relentless oxidation of the air. These are not ruins, but they are not inhabited by people. They are active, humming with a deep, subsonic vibration that one feels in the marrow of their bones rather than hears with their ears. Every few minutes, a rhythmic *clack-hiss* echoes from a distant pressure valve, a lonely sound that travels far across the open, unobstructed miles.

The scale of the place is deceptive. Because there are no trees or varying elevations to provide a sense of perspective, the massive industrial towers appear smaller than they are until one is standing in their colossal shadows, looking up at rusted gantries lost in the low-hanging fog. 

While the landscape is devoid of flora, it is teeming with life. In the absence of plants, the fauna has become the dominant force of the ecology, a vast and overwhelming biomass that has adapted to a mineral-and-machine diet. The mud is not merely mud; it is a living carpet of billions of trilobite-like crustaceans, their chitinous shells clicking softly as they sift the silt for chemical nutrients. Clouds of heavy-bodied, translucent insects drift through the steam, their wings beating a silent, frantic rhythm. They do not buzz; they move in great, shifting veils that dim the already weak light.

Larger creatures, low-slung and multi-legged, move with slow, deliberate grace between the distant industrial hubs. They have thick, leathery hides the color of wet slate, designed to shed the constant condensation. They congregate near the outflow pipes of the great factories, basking in the scalding, mineral-rich runoff. There is no predation in the traditional sense, or if there is, it is slow and communal. The animals seem to be part of the landscape’s machinery, scavengers of energy and heat, recycling the output of the iron towers.

Everything is damp. The air is so saturated that the distinction between breath and water feels thin. Every surface is slick with a fine film of moisture that never evaporates, only cycles—condensing on the cold iron of the machines, dripping into the mud, and being heated by the geothermal pulse of the basin to rise again as steam. 

The world is quiet, but it is not still. It is a place of slow, heavy momentum. The erosion is complete; the sharp edges of the world were ground away eons ago, leaving behind a stable, rhythmic cycle of heat, moisture, and metal. It is a functional, working landscape, a vast and lonely factory-wetland where the only sound is the dripping of water and the endless, patient scuttle of a trillion legs across the dark, warm earth.
