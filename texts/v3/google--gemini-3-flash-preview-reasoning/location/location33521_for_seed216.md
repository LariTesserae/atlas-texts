The world is a hollow sphere of woven green, a planet-sized basket orbiting a dim, dying ember of a star. There is no stone here, no soil, no mantle of iron. Everything that exists is a testament to an impossible, prehistoric labor: the Grafting.

Imagine a cage of living timber, the diameter of a world, where the bars are kilometers-thick banyan trunks and the mesh is a tangled continent of interlocking vines. This is the Lattice. Because the density of the world is so low—mostly empty air trapped within a sphere of vegetation—the gravity is light and strange, pulling not toward a center, but toward the nearest mass of living wood.

### The Atmosphere and Light
The air is perpetually warm and heavy, saturated with a humidity that feels like a physical weight. It is a post-rain world that never dries. The light is a bruised, eternal twilight. The star outside is weak, its rays filtered through layers of translucent leaves and thick, weeping resins before they reach the interior. The result is a verdant gloom, a cathedral of mossy shadows where colors are reduced to shades of jade, charcoal, and ochre. 

Because the structure is so vast and the interior so empty, weather happens in the gaps. Great internal mists form in the hollows between the major boughs, miles-wide clouds that drift in the low gravity, occasionally colliding with a forested wall to release a silent, vertical deluge.

### The Terrain of the Woven
To walk here is to walk upon a floor that is also a ceiling and a lung. The "ground" is a mat of peat and living moss, meters thick, grown over a substrate of braided branches. There is evidence of the Hand everywhere, though the tools used were primitive. You can see where ancient ancestors used bone saws and stone weights to bend the gargantuan saplings into specific shapes. The world is *built*—it is a piece of megastructural wicker-work—but it was built with the patience of a gardener, not the precision of an engineer. 

Massive knots, the size of cities, serve as junctions where thousands of primary trunks meet. These knots are scarred with the marks of manual labor: primitive pulleys made of vine-rope, massive clay basins fired in the heat of decomposing compost to catch the weeping sap, and ladders carved directly into the bark.

### The Silence and the Sound
The world is eerily quiet. The vegetation acts as a perfect acoustic dampener. The sound of a falling branch—a mile-long splinter of wood—is swallowed by the moss before it can echo. What remains is a low-frequency thrum, the sound of the Lattice swaying in the solar winds, and the wet, rhythmic *drip-hiss* of moisture condensing on the undersides of the world and falling into the void.

### Ecology and Fauna
Life is abundant but specialized for the vertical and the hollow. Fauna here do not run; they cling, glide, or drift. Giant, soft-bodied gastropods the size of houses slide along the resin-slicked trunks, their shells made of hardened sap. Flocks of featherless, membrane-winged creatures—more like flying lemurs than birds—navigate the thermal drafts of the interior, their cries muffled by the humid air.

In the dampest crevices, where the water collects in stagnant, hanging ponds held together by surface tension and low gravity, bioluminescent larvae pulse with a pale, sickly light. They are the primary source of illumination in the deep interior, casting long, swaying shadows of the primitive scaffolds left behind by the Lattice-weavers.

### The Breakdown of Reality
As one moves toward the higher elevations—the outer layers of the sphere where the weave is tightest—physics begins to stutter. The sheer scale of the construction, combined with the lack of a solid core, creates pockets of "soft reality." In these areas, the vegetation doesn't just grow; it mirrors the thoughts of those nearby. A traveler might find the vines twisting into the shape of a familiar face, or a path opening through an impenetrable thicket just as the desire to pass becomes acute.

Time, too, is weathered and eroded. Without a sun that rises or sets, the inhabitants mark time by the "Pulse"—the slow, planetary-scale throb of sap rising from the roots to the leaves. A single year is a single breath of the Lattice.

### The Built World
Everything is fabricated, yet nothing is mechanical. There are no machines, only complex grafts. A "door" is a flower that has been bred to remain closed until its petals are stroked in a specific way. A "reservoir" is a giant, hollowed-out gourd, grown over centuries to hold a lake’s worth of rainwater. 

There is a profound sense of balance here, but also of exhaustion. The Lattice is old. The primitive ropes that hold the great grafts together are fraying. The wood is petrifying in places, turning into a brittle, glass-like substance that chimes when the wind hits it. The world is a masterpiece of prehistoric intent, a high-altitude marsh floating in the void, slowly drying out as its central star fades into sleep.
