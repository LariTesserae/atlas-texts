The air here is a thick, motionless bake, smelling of scorched linen and pulverized clay. It is a dry heat—total and absolute—where the concept of moisture feels like a forgotten myth or a linguistic error. There is no mud, no mist, no dew; the atmosphere is a clean, parched clarity that carries light without refracting it through vapor.

You are standing in the *Hollow of the Weavers*, a space no larger than a village square, yet it feels infinitely dense. Every square inch of the environment has been fashioned by hand. The ground is not earth, but a vast, interlocking mosaic of hand-pressed terracotta tiles, each one slightly different, etched with thumbprints and primitive solar symbols. There is no natural substrate here; beneath the tiles are more tiles, and beneath those, a foundation of sun-dried bricks stretching down into a warm, silent dark.

The architecture is sharp and fresh, as if finished only hours ago. The edges of the stacked slate walls are keen enough to draw blood, showing no signs of wind-wear or age. These walls do not form houses so much as they form a labyrinthine garden of enclosures—low-slung partitions and waist-high altars that segment the space into a thousand tiny, private niches. 

Despite the lack of water, the landscape is violently lush. The vegetation is non-biological in the way we understand it. From the cracks between the tiles, stiff, fern-like structures erupt in explosive density. They are made of a material resembling cured leather and hammered copper. They do not wilt; they do not sway. They grow in patient, geometric fractals, their "leaves" being thin sheets of translucent mica that vibrate almost imperceptibly to harvest the heat of the air. These plants are packed so tightly together that you must push through them like a crowd, their dry surfaces rasping against your clothes with the sound of shuffling paper.

This is where reality begins to fray. 

In the center of the grove, the rules of Euclidean space are treated as suggestions. A row of hand-carved pillars supports a roof that isn’t there; yet, when you stand beneath them, the sun vanishes and you are bathed in a cool, artificial indigo shade. Objects do not fall; they drift toward the nearest vertical surface as if pulled by a localized, fickle gravity. You might see a primitive clay bowl, crudely fired and lopsided, floating three feet off the ground, rotating once every hour in a slow, hypnotic cycle.

The fauna are abundant but strangely hushed. Small, chitinous creatures with too many joints scuttle across the terracotta. They have no mouths and no eyes; they seem to navigate by sensing the heat-shadows cast by the mica-ferns. They move with a stuttering, stop-motion cadence, appearing in one spot and then another, bypassing the space in between. Occasionally, a large, moth-like entity with wings of woven dry grass will drift through the air, its flight path a jagged, non-linear scribble across the sky.

Everything is quiet. The sound is muffled, as if the dense "vegetation" and the porous brick walls are swallowing every vibration. Even your own footsteps on the tiles produce only a dull, hollow thud that dies instantly. 

There is a sense of immense, manual labor behind everything. You can see the twine—thousands of miles of hand-spun vegetable fiber—lashing the stone slabs together, anchoring the floating bowls to the floor, and weaving through the copper-ferns to guide their growth. It is a world built with fingers and grit, a primitive masterpiece of survival against a physics that is slowly coming undone. 

Time moves with a heavy, syrupy slowness. A shadow might take a full day to cross a single tile. The growth of the mica-leaves is visible only if you stare for hours, watching the copper-colored veins slowly extend their reach. It is a warm, static, and beautifully broken room, a cathedral of sun-baked clay where the absence of water has been replaced by a surplus of intention.
