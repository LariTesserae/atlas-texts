This is a world of verticality and vibration, a high-altitude expanse where the distinction between solid, liquid, and sound has begun to dissolve.

### The Terrain and Scale
You stand upon the **Opaline Spires**, an extreme elevation landscape that defies the traditional logic of mountain ranges. The scale is vast—a regional basin situated miles above any sea level, where the air is thin but saturated with a heavy, cool moisture. The ground is not soil, but a dense, interlocking carpet of weathered silica and translucent shale. 

The erosion here is "established," meaning the land has long since settled into its impossible shapes. Great arcs of stone, smoothed by eons of high-velocity mist, bridge the gaps between needle-thin peaks. These spires are so dense and numerous that they resemble a forest of mineral stalks, packed tightly together, leaving only narrow, winding canyons of light between them.

### The Material and Water
There is no green here. No moss, no lichen, no cellulose. The world is purely mineral, yet it feels "wet" in a way that is almost invasive. The rocks do not merely sit in the rain; they weep. Water is squeezed from the pores of the stone by the sheer atmospheric pressure of the heights, creating a "weeping rock" effect where every vertical surface is coated in a shimmering, downward-moving film of moisture. 

Because the temperature is cool but the light is intensely bright, this water does not always evaporate. Instead, it clings to the minerals, refracting the light into blinding, prismatic halos. In the basins between spires, the water collects in shallow, vibrating pools that never settle; they are kept in a state of constant, rhythmic agitation by the environment’s pervasive sound.

### The Weirdness: Harmonic Reality
Reality here is governed by resonance. The "weirdness" manifests as a breakdown of standard physics in favor of acoustic properties. The spires act as tuning forks. As the wind rushes through the dense clusters of stone, the landscape produces a sound that is not just a background noise, but a physical force—a "loud," low-frequency thrum that you feel in your marrow.

In this environment, gravity is occasionally superseded by vibration. You might see droplets of water "falling" horizontally, drawn along the lines of a particular sound wave, or witness a heavy boulder hovering inches off the ground, trapped in a sonic null-zone. The light, too, is affected; at certain times of day, the brightness is so intense that shadows become physical objects—dark, cold silhouettes that you have to step around, as they possess a temporary solidity.

### The Built and the Tech
Scattered throughout this crystalline wilderness are the marks of a primitive, transient presence. These are not cities, but "Resonance Stations"—small, circular structures built of stacked, unmortared flat stones. They are architectural ears, designed to focus or dampen the overwhelming sound of the spires.

The technology is rudimentary, limited to what can be fashioned by hand from the surrounding mineral wealth. You find primitive "sound-gouges"—heavy flint tools used to carve channels into the weeping rocks to redirect the flow of water. There are also "hanging chimes" made of resonant shards tied with braided mineral fibers, used perhaps to signal across the vast, open gulfs between the peaks. The builders are absent, but their marks are fresh; the stone basins they carved to catch the weeping water are clean and functional.

### The Dynamics and Ecology
Though there are no plants or animals, the landscape is intensely active. It follows a "biological pace" through a process of rapid mineral growth and collapse. The silica formations are not static; they grow in crystalline fractals, fed by the mineral-rich weeping water, only to be shattered by the next great acoustic surge. 

The "weather" is a cycle of light and resonance. When the sun hits the zenith, the brightness becomes an active participant in the geography, baking the moisture into a thick, luminous fog that hums. This is a world of constant motion: the downward creep of water, the upward growth of salt-lattices, the shifting of "hard" shadows, and the eternal, deafening song of the wind through the stone teeth of the world. 

It is a place of cold, bright, and loud equilibrium—a cathedral of glass and water where the air itself feels like a prism, and the ground beneath your feet is a living instrument.
