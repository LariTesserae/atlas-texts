The horizon does not curve so much as it simply persists, a straight line drawn across the eye’s reach with a geometric cruelty. This is a world of the **Plateau of Poured Earth**, a continental expanse where the very ground is a manufactured thing. There is no soil here, no loose gravel, no chaotic jumble of tectonic uplift. Instead, the world is a single, vast, low-lying basin of vitrified ceramic and kiln-fired terracotta, laid down in gargantuan, interlocking slabs that stretch for thousands of miles.

The scale is so immense that the landscape generates its own localized weather systems. In the heat of the day—a relentless, dry 2.7-level heat—the ceramic floor absorbs the sun’s energy until the air above it begins to wobble and blur. This thermal activity creates "pavement winds," hot gusts that scour the flat surface, carrying nothing but the scent of baked clay.

Though the environment is "Total Built," there is no technology here. There are no wires, no humming grids, no glowing lights. It is a primitive megastructure, as if a civilization of potters and masons had been tasked with replacing the crust of a planet using only heat and earth. The surface is raw and "emerging"; the plates are new, their edges sharp and unweathered. There has not been enough time for the wind to round the corners of the expansion joints or for the rain to pit the glassy finish of the terracotta. It is a world in its first epoch, a fresh casting.

Water is a rare, rhythmic visitor. It does not pool in lakes but exists as a thin, intermittent film. In the cool of the pre-dawn, condensation wicks out of the deep seams between the plates, creating a network of "joint-rivers"—shallow, inch-deep streams that flow for miles along the geometric lines of the world before the rising sun licks them dry. Occasionally, a flash-rain occurs, a sudden dumping of moisture that turns the vast floor into a mirror, reflecting the sky with terrifying clarity before the water vanishes into the thirsty cracks.

Vegetation is a lonely occupant. There are no forests, only "seam-dwellers." Sparse, wiry succulents with skin like cured leather grow exclusively in the grout-like gaps between the slabs. They are grey-green and stiff, their roots reaching deep into the dark, damp recesses beneath the ceramic crust. They do not wave in the wind; they are too rigid for that. They simply endure, spaced hundreds of yards apart, marking the vastness with their singular, lonely presences.

There is no fauna. No insects buzz in the heat; no birds cross the white-hot sky. The silence is profound, broken only by the dynamic groans of the world itself. Because the structure is so large and the heat so intense, the plates are constantly expanding and contracting. The sound is a sequence of slow, tectonic pops and deep, resonant thrums—the sound of a continent-sized kiln settling.

The light is flat and honest. Without the soft diffusion of forests or the jagged shadows of mountains, the sun moves across the sky like a searchlight over a ballroom floor. Shadows are long, crisp, and stark, providing the only sense of relief in a landscape that offers no natural shade. 

Everything here is intentional, yet abandoned. The world functions as a massive, passive machine for catching light and moving water, a biological-scale clockwork made of fired clay. It is a place of absolute stillness and terrifyingly slow motion, where the only thing "becoming" is the slow, inaugural wear and tear of time on a brand-new world. You could walk for a month in any direction and the scenery would not change: only the rhythmic pulse of the heat, the morning dampness in the cracks, and the geometric certainty of the floor beneath your feet.
