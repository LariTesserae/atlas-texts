The air does not move. It is a cool, thin medium, lacking the humidity of breath or the scent of damp earth. Here, the concept of a "cycle" has been discarded; there are no seasons, no tides, and no wind to carry the dust. The landscape exists in a state of terminal equilibrium, a final architecture achieved after eons of patient wearing-away.

We begin at the heights. The elevation is agonizingly vertical. The world consists of colossal, smooth-sided pillars of obsidian-colored mineral, rising from a floor so distant it is lost in a soft, grey haze. These are not jagged, young mountains; they are the bones of a planet that has finished eroding. Their surfaces are polished to a dull, satin sheen by the slow passage of time itself, rather than by wind or water. They stand like the teeth of a gargantuan comb, spaced with mathematical regularity across a territory that spans hundreds of leagues.

There is no water. In the deep basins between these pillars, there is only a fine, particulate silt—a dry, alkaline powder that behaves like a fluid but offers no life. It is a sea of dead grit, perfectly level, reflecting the pale, constant light of a sun that seems fixed in a perpetual afternoon.

The density of life here is suffocating, yet it is not lush. The flora is nearly non-existent—only thin, wire-like filaments of grey-blue moss that cling to the shadows of the pillars, never growing, never dying. But the fauna is an oceanic presence. The world is crowded with things that breathe but do not move.

Clinging to every vertical surface, stacked layer upon layer like barnacles on a ship’s hull, are the *Quietants*. They are chitinous, multi-limbed creatures the size of horses, with shells the color of bruised plums and translucent membranes that shiver at a frequency too high for the ear to catch. They are packed so tightly that the stone beneath them is invisible. They do not hunt; they do not mate; they do not migrate. They exist in a state of biological stasis, drawing sustenance from the thin mineral gas that leaks from the pillar cores. There are billions of them. They are so numerous that their collective heartbeat, if they have one, creates a sub-audible thrum that acts as the world’s only foundation of sound. 

The silence is heavy. It is a physical weight, dampened by the millions of soft, shivering bodies. A shout would be swallowed within a few meters, muffled by the density of the living walls.

Reality here has begun to fray at the edges of the pillars. Because the environment has remained unchanged for so long, the boundaries between objects have grown thin. In certain lights, you can see where a creature’s limb has fused permanently into the obsidian stone, or where two creatures have bled into one another, occupying the same physical coordinates without distress. Space is folded here; a step taken toward the edge of a precipice might find your foot landing a mile away on a different spire, as if the world has grown tired of maintaining the illusion of distance. Time does not pass linearly; it pools. A falling pebble does not accelerate; it descends at a constant, leisurely pace, eventually coming to rest on the silt sea without a splash, as if the law of gravity has been negotiated down to a suggestion.

Among this crowded, silent verticality, there are marks of the primitive. Small, precarious platforms of hand-hewn slate are wedged into the crevices between the Quietants. These structures are tethered to the stone with ropes made of woven hair and dried gut. They are not dwellings so much as observation posts. You might find a single, crude stone bowl or a scraper made of a sharpened rib-bone resting on a ledge—artifacts of a people who have long since stopped trying to build upward and have instead accepted their place as parasites of the stasis. There are no machines, no fires, no metals. The technology is limited to what can be scraped together in a world where nothing new ever arrives.

The light is steady, a silver-gold clarity that never dims. It illuminates the vast, crowded spires, casting long, unmoving shadows across the silt. To look out from a high ledge is to see an infinite forest of stone and flesh, a crowded necropolis of the living. It is a world that has reached its final page and decided to stop reading. Nothing will ever happen here again, and the millions of creatures clinging to the heights seem perfectly, terrifyingly content with that fact.
