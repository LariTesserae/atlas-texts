Here, the sky is not a void but a heavy, slate-colored curtain that never quite lifts. The air is cool, carrying the sharp, metallic tang of ozone and the soft, persistent scent of wet peat. It is a world of verticality, defined by heights so extreme that the base of the landscape is a theoretical myth, lost miles below in a crushing gloom.

The geology is unsettlingly new. The peaks are not the rounded, weathered nubbins of an old earth, nor are they the jagged spires of traditional tectonics. They are *emerging*—raw, geometric extrusions of basalt and obsidian that feel as though they were birthed only yesterday. The edges are razor-sharp, untouched by the smoothing hand of time. There are no scree slopes, no tumbled boulders; the stone rises in clean, impossible facets, looking more like gargantuan, unpolished crystals than natural mountains. Because erosion has not yet begun its work, the world feels unfinished, a skeletal frame for a reality still in the process of being assembled.

Nestled against these raw heaves of stone is an urban sprawl of terrifying ambition. This is not a compact city, but a sparse, industrial network that stretches across the chasm-riven region. Massive, soot-stained factories are bolted directly into the vertical faces of the new-born rock. They are connected by a web of suspension bridges, heavy iron pipes, and humming electrical conduits that groan under their own weight. The architecture is layered and dense where it exists, yet separated by vast, terrifying gulfs of empty air. Great brass-rimmed ventilation fans, three stories tall, turn with agonizing slowness, clearing the mist from the iron catwalks only for it to roll back in moments later.

The moisture here is a quiet, pervasive thing. It doesn't rain so much as it *collects*. Condensation slickens every surface, dripping from the undersides of rusted gantries and forming dark, freezing pools in the shallow depressions of the unweathered stone. Small, fast-moving rivulets snake down the obsidian faces, appearing and disappearing into the industrial drainage systems.

Despite the cold and the industrial grit, life is relentless. Vegetation clings to the machines as much as the rock. A species of thick, violet-veined moss carpets the pipes, insulated by the warmth of the steam running within. Long, translucent ferns hang from the undersides of the bridges, drawing sustenance from the constant mist. The fauna is equally abundant, though strange. Enormous, slow-beating moths with wings the size of sails drift between the factory spires, their abdomens glowing with a faint, pulsing amber light. Small, six-legged scavengers with metallic shells scuttle along the rivets of the buildings, feeding on the grease and the moss, their chattering cries echoing through the canyons.

The sound is a constant, physical presence. It is a low-frequency thrum—the combined vibration of a thousand massive engines, the hiss of escaping steam, and the deep, sub-sonic tectonic groan of the mountains themselves as they continue to extrude upward. It is a loud world, but the noise is rhythmic and patient, a mechanical heartbeat that seems to regulate the flow of time.

However, the logic of the place is fraying. This is where the reality breakdown manifests. Gravity is not a constant law but a local suggestion. In the spaces between the industrial towers, one might see a pool of water clinging to the *underside* of a bridge, or a trail of smoke falling downward into the abyss like a lead weight. Shadows occasionally detach from their owners, lingering for a few seconds too long before snapping back into place. The industrial lights—dim, flickering bulbs encased in heavy glass—sometimes cast colors that don't exist in the natural spectrum, illuminating patches of the mist that seem to turn solid or transparent at random.

Everything moves with a peculiar, heavy slowness. The growth of the moss, the rotation of the great fans, and the very extrusion of the mountains happen on a timescale that feels stretched. You could watch a gear turn for an hour and feel as though only a second has passed, or watch a new spire of rock break the surface of a plateau and realize that generations of workers have lived and died in the time it took for that stone to rise an inch. It is a cold, dim, and booming realm of industry pinned to the ribs of a world that hasn't finished being born.
