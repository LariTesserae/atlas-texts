The land here does not peak in jagged summits; instead, it heaves upward in massive, smooth-backed plateaus of ferruginous siltstone, worn down by eons of weeping. We are at a great height, where the air should be thin and cold, yet the atmosphere is thick, warm, and heavy with a suspended sea of vapor. It is a world of permanent saturation. The rock does not merely hold water; it breathes it. Every vertical surface of the great mesas is a hanging garden of dark, slick moss and silver-grey lichen—the only vegetation that can endure the constant, warm deluge of condensation.

Between these monumental ridges lie valleys of immense scale, draped in a complex, industrial urbanity that seems to have grown into the geology rather than being built upon it. This is not a city of streets and storefronts, but a city of infrastructure—a sprawling, multi-layered grid of copper-clad pipes, massive brass ventilation cowls, and heavy iron catwalks that bridge the chasms. The density of the architecture is high, yet it feels strangely empty. There are miles of pressurized conduits and rhythmic, hissing valves for every one living soul. It is an urbanity of systems, not of people.

The sound is the first thing that unmoors the mind. It is a deafening, monolithic roar that never ceases. It is not the sound of wind or voices, but the sound of the earth’s own circulatory system being forced through the throat of an industrial titan. The valleys act as resonance chambers for the colossal steam-engines buried deep within the plateaus. These engines move with a glacial, agonizing slowness; a single stroke of a mile-long piston may take an entire lunar cycle to complete, yet the sheer scale of the displacement creates a sound that vibrates in the marrow of one’s bones. It is a low-frequency thunder, a rhythmic groaning of metal against stone that makes the very mist tremble.

Water is the protagonist of this place. It does not fall as rain but emerges from the stone as a constant, warm perspiration. It gathers in shallow, obsidian-colored pools on the flat roofs of the industrial blocks, then spills over the edges in long, silent veils to the valley floor thousands of feet below. The air is so humid that the transition between gas and liquid is blurred; one breathes the landscape as much as one sees it. 

The technology here is heavy and tactile. Great oxidized wheels, three stories tall, turn so slowly that their movement is undetectable to the naked eye, though the scream of their dry bearings adds a piercing shriek to the background roar every few hours. There are no delicate electronics here; everything is governed by pressure, weight, and the slow, inexorable expansion of steam. The light is filtered through the constant shroud of moisture, creating a perpetual, golden-grey afternoon. Shadows are soft and vast, cast by the ribs of the great pipe-bridges onto the mist below.

Life is a rarity, an afterthought. Occasionally, one might see a pale, flat-bodied scavenger—something akin to a giant, armored silverfish—clinging to the underside of a warm pipe, or a flight of translucent, moth-like creatures drifting through the updrafts of a steam vent. But for the most part, the ecology is one of metal, water, and heat. 

The world operates on a timeline that ignores the frantic pace of organic life. The erosion is complete; the machines have settled into their final resting places; the water has found its permanent paths through the stone. It is a landscape in a state of terminal equilibrium. The heat is constant, the sound is absolute, and the slow pulse of the great engines provides the only clock. In this high, wet, and thundering place, the distinction between a built city and a natural mountain has finally, after millions of years, ceased to matter.
