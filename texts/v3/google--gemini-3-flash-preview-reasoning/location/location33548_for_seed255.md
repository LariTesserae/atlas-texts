In this place, the concept of "ground" is a fiction. There is only the Floor, an endless, interlocking expanse of hand-fitted basalt hexes and slate shingles that stretches into the dark. There is no soil, no silt, no sand. If you were to pry up a stone, you would find only another layer of masonry beneath it, and another beneath that, a crust of intentionality thousands of feet thick.

The world is a cold, damp architectural hive, a valley-sized basin of verticality where the sun never reaches. The air carries a permanent chill (1.4) and the smell of wet lime. Because there is no sky—only a distant, vaulted ceiling of massive, unrefined slabs—the light (0.5) is a heavy, bruised purple-black. Vision is a luxury afforded only to those who bring their own light or wait for the rare, rhythmic sparks of flint-grinding-flint that occur when the structures shift.

The density (2.1) is overwhelming. The landscape is a forest of chimneys, pylons, and dry-stone towers that stand so close to one another that a traveler must often turn sideways to pass. These structures are not ruins; they are "emerging" (0.5 erosion). The edges of the stones are razor-sharp, newly quarried and stacked without mortar. There is a raw, embryonic quality to the masonry, as if the city is being frantically assembled in the dark by a million invisible hands.

Water (0.9) does not flow in rivers here. Instead, it "sweats." The cool temperature causes a persistent condensation to bloom on the smooth faces of the basalt. It gathers in the hairline fractures between stones, forming thin, vertical veins of moisture that trickle downward. Occasionally, a rhythmic pulse in the deep masonry causes a collective shudder, shaking the condensation loose in a sudden, artificial rain that lasts for minutes before the world returns to its state of damp stasis.

The silence you might expect from a stone world is absent. The environment is loud (1.9) and aggressively active (1.9). The sounds are percussive and tectonic: the *clack-clack-clack* of stone settling on stone, the deep, resonant groan of a million tons of slate compressing, and the sharp *ping* of a flake of flint shearing off under pressure. It is a cacophony of construction. The world is a machine made of rocks, perpetually reconfiguring its own weight.

There is no green here. Biology has been replaced by geometry. The only thing resembling life is a rare, lithic fauna (1.4)—flat, multi-legged crustacean-like entities with shells made of the same grey slate as the floors. They move with the sound of shuffling plates, scavenging the mineral salts left behind by the evaporating "sweat" of the walls. They are the only witnesses to the "weirdness" (1.7) of the geography—like the places where the gravity seems to tilt, or where the masonry begins to spiral into impossible, non-Euclidean funnels that lead nowhere.

Everything here is "built" (2.8), but the technology is "primitive" (1.1). There are no wires, no glowing screens, no steam pipes. The complexity of the world is achieved through the sheer, brute-force repetition of simple shapes. It is a masterpiece of the lever, the wedge, and the plumb line. It feels like a cathedral built by insects, or a mountain range designed by a mad mason who feared the chaos of a natural slope.

To walk here is to be a ghost in a quarry that never sleeps. You move through a labyrinth of sharp corners and damp surfaces, listening to the mountain-sized heartbeat of stones grinding against one another, waiting for the next spark to briefly illuminate the terrifying scale of the labor that surrounds you. It is a world under construction, yet there is no sign it will ever be finished, or that it was ever meant for anyone to inhabit.
