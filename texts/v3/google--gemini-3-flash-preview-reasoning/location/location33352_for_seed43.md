This is the Basin of the Scalloped Kilns. 

It is a subterranean hollow, a singular vertical chamber the size of a village square, but its floor is not earth and its walls are not stone. Every surface, from the deepest sump to the vaulted ceiling three hundred feet above, has been built. It is a world of fired clay, hand-laid brick, and stacked terracotta, all of it ancient and smoothed by the passage of time into a state of "established erosion." The sharp corners of the bricks have been rubbed into soft, organic curves by centuries of contact; the stairs are no longer geometric, but resemble shallow, rhythmic waves frozen in burnt orange mud.

The air here is warm, holding the steady, radiating heat of a baker’s oven that has been cooling for a thousand years. It is damp, but not wet. There are no rivers, only the "Leaking Rains"—intermittent beads of condensation that gather on the ceiling tiles and fall in rhythmic, heavy drops, or the thin, silver veining of moisture that crawls down the grout-lines after a storm on the surface far above. This moisture is precious and meticulously managed; every brick is angled to guide these stray droplets into hand-carved runnels and small, circular basins the size of a bowl.

Though the environment is entirely artificial, it is not sterile. The vegetation is sparse—clings of pale, stringy lichen in the dampest crevices and the occasional tuft of brittle, sun-starved grass growing from a crack in a high lintel—but the fauna is overwhelming. 

The Basin is a vertical hive of activity. Thousands of small, warm-blooded creatures—half-rodent, half-lizard, with skin the color of parched clay—scuttle across the vertical faces. They live in the "density" of the architecture, nesting in the gaps where bricks have crumbled or behind the decorative friezes. Their movement is constant. They leap between the hundreds of rope bridges and wooden scaffolds that crisscross the void. These structures are primitive, lashed together with coarse fiber and weighted with stones, showing no sign of advanced metallurgy or complex machinery. Everything is hand-lashed, hand-carved, and manually operated.

The sound is a cacophony. Because the chamber is a closed vessel of hard, fired surfaces, every noise is amplified and reflected. The chittering of the clay-dwellers, the constant *tink-tink-tink* of water hitting terracotta basins, and the rhythmic creak of the rope bridges combine into a loud, vibrating drone that feels physical against the skin. 

Light enters from a series of "Oculi"—large, circular vents at the very top of the chamber that look up toward the surface world. During the day, thick, solid shafts of golden light cut through the hazy air, illuminating the dust motes and the frantic activity of the creatures. Where the light hits the floor, the warmth intensifies, creating thermal updrafts that toss the hanging bridges. 

There is no "nature" here in the traditional sense. Even the mounds that resemble hills are actually vast heaps of discarded pottery shards, smoothed by time into sloping dunes of ceramic gravel. The ecosystem is in high motion: predators—large, silent owls with brick-patterned feathers—swoop from the upper arches to pluck scavengers from the walls. The "weather" is the shifting of the sun-shafts and the occasional humid mist that rises when the Basin floor grows too warm.

It is a place of frantic, crowded life contained within a rigid, man-made shell. It feels like the inside of an ancient, abandoned machine that has been colonised by the wild, where the distinction between "building" and "geology" has completely dissolved. The world is a room, and the room is a world.
