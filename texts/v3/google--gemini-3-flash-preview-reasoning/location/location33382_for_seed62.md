Here, the sun does not so much rise as it does ignite, a total and unblinking glare that saturates the high altitudes in a sterile, white brilliance. Despite this ferocity of light, the air remains stubbornly cool, thinned by elevation and tasting of ozone and wet stone. 

The terrain is a jagged, adolescent geography. The mountains are sharp-toothed and steep, their ridges defined by clean, dark basalt that has not yet been softened by the long work of wind or rain. These are young peaks, thrust upward by some recent tectonic impatience, leaving deep, narrow valleys that hold the cold like bowls. 

Water is a rare, precious currency here, arriving not in downpours but in the patient accumulation of the mist. Every morning, the high-intensity light hits the cooling rock, and the resulting condensation clings to the landscape in heavy, silver beads. There are no rushing rivers, only "still-flows"—shallow, glassy ribbons of water that sit trapped in the troughs of the rock, moving so slowly they appear as solid as the obsidian beneath them.

Into this sharp, vertical world, the vegetation has crowded with a desperate, silent density. It is a lushness of the small and the waxy. Carpets of phosphorescent moss, three feet thick, swallow the jagged edges of the stone. They act as a sound-muffling insulation, ensuring that the world remains in a state of absolute, heavy silence. Broad, silver-veined leaves, as stiff as parchment, overlap in scales along the cliff faces, positioned to catch the relentless light. The greenery is not soft; it is turgid and pressurized, gorged on the intermittent dampness.

Amidst this green sprawl lie the industrial artifacts. They are not ruins, for they do not seem to be decaying; rather, they are "presences." Massive, cast-iron pistons, the size of cathedral pillars, emerge directly from the mountainsides. They are coated in a dark, self-lubricating oil that smells faintly of nutmeg. These machines move with a glacial, terrifying slowness—a single stroke might take a decade to complete. There is no grinding of gears, no hiss of steam. They operate on magnetic tolerances so precise that their movement is undetectable to the ear. 

Scattered between the peaks are smaller structures: solitary, windowless kiosks of ribbed steel and glass, and long, elevated walkways that lead to nowhere, ending abruptly in mid-air above the lush valleys. These are the marks of a builder who valued production over habitation. There are no houses, no streets, only the scattered nodes of a wide-bore, automated industry that seems to be mining the very air or the slow-moving minerals within the young rock.

Life that moves is nearly non-existent. There are no birds to catch the thermals, no insects to hum in the moss. Occasionally, one might spot a "glass-strider"—a spindly, translucent creature that spends its entire multi-year lifespan traversing a single industrial pipe, moving so slowly it appears to be a decorative carving. 

The world feels like a clock whose weights are too heavy to drop. The bright, cold light fixes everything in place, turning the valley into a high-exposure photograph. The only change is the infinitesimal slide of a piston or the microscopic expansion of a silver leaf. It is a landscape of immense, latent power, held in a state of perpetual, sun-drenched hush.
