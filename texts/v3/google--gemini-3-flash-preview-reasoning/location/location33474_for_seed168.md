The air here is thin, warm, and utterly devoid of moisture. It does not carry the scent of rain or the humidity of a swamp; instead, it smells of heated copper and the sharp, dry ozone of a high-altitude vacuum. We are on a series of stratospheric plateaus, rising so high that the sky above is a deep, bruised violet, yet the light remains a constant, clinical white, illuminating every detail with unmerciful clarity.

The geology is raw, embryonic. These mountains have not been softened by the patient hands of wind or water. They are jagged, unweathered shards of obsidian and pyrite, thrust upward by a tectonic event that seems to have occurred only moments ago and then stalled. There is no sand here, no silt—only the primary, broken geometry of a world under construction. The peaks are like the teeth of a saw, perfectly sharp, their edges glinting with a metallic luster that suggests the crust is composed of alloys rather than silicates.

Despite the absolute lack of water, the landscape is crowded with a terrifyingly lush vegetation. This is not biology as we know it; it is a non-aqueous, metallurgical flora. Dense thickets of copper-hued ferns grow in the sheltered crevices of the black rock. Their fronds are not soft, but are instead composed of thousands of micro-fine, interlocking gears and perforated brass-like membranes. They do not sway, for there is no wind, and they do not grow, for the world is locked in a state of frozen equilibrium. They simply *are*—vast, intricate, and static.

In the wide, hollow basins between these needle-sharp peaks, the scale of the world reveals itself. The distance between one cluster of life and the next is enormous, a yawning void of empty space that makes the heart feel small. Each grove of metallic plants sits like a localized industrial accident, a dense concentration of complex, machine-like growth isolated by miles of barren, unformed stone.

The "tech" of this world is not something built, but something inherent. The trees resemble vertical heat-sinks, their bark a series of cooling fins designed to radiate the warmth of the constant sun. Low-lying shrubs appear as clusters of glass-like vacuum tubes, their interiors glowing with a faint, stagnant phosphorescence. There are no buildings, no roads, and no artifacts, yet the entire ecology feels like a factory that has been switched off mid-cycle. The ground beneath the groves is littered with "leaf-fall" that looks like discarded punch-cards made of thin, brittle gold.

Silence is the absolute law here. There are no animals to cry out, no insects to buzz, and no rustle of wind through the leaves. The only sound is the occasional, tectonic *tink* of metal expanding under the warmth of the sun—a solitary, percussive note that emphasizes the stillness. 

It is a world of stagnant production. It feels as though, if one were to find the right lever or provide the first drop of a celestial lubricant, the entire landscape would begin to chug, rotate, and hiss. The gears of the ferns would turn, the piston-like trunks of the larger trees would begin to pump, and the raw, unformed mountains would finish their ascent. 

But there is no movement. The light stays at its zenith. The copper leaves do not oxidize. The world is a masterpiece of industrial potential, forever paused at the moment of its creation, a lush jungle of machinery waiting for a spark in a universe that has forgotten how to burn.
