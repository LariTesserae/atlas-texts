Here, the sky is not a distant canopy but a searing, white intimacy. At this elevation, the atmosphere has thinned to a translucent veil, allowing a light so fierce and absolute that it feels structural, as if the brightness itself is holding up the heavens. It reflects off every surface with a punishing, crystalline glare, for here, every surface is wet.

The terrain is an impossible synthesis of the embryonic and the ancient. It is a world without stone. The "geology" of this place is entirely botanical—a vast, undulating crust of hyper-dense mosses, fused liana-networks, and titanic, plate-like fungi that have calcified into a substrate as hard as limestone but as porous as bone. Because the erosion is only just beginning, the landscape lacks the soft curves of aged earth; instead, it is a realm of sharp, vertical heaves and raw, jagged green ridges that look like they were thrust upward only moments ago. Deep, narrow fissures split the verdant crust, revealing dark, dripping interiors where the "bedrock" is nothing but tangled, prehistoric roots.

Water is the primary inhabitant. It does not merely fall; it emerges. The air is so saturated that it feels heavy in the lungs, a cold, suspended marsh that constantly condenses against the skin. Every cliff-face of compacted fern-matter weeps a thousand tiny, silver threads of runoff. The ground—if it can be called that—is a saturated sponge. Step heavily, and a pool of ice-cold water swells around the foot; stand still, and the slow, hydraulic pressure of the moss will begin to swallow the print.

The scale is vast and lonely. You can stand on a high, tufted promontory and look out across a valley the size of a province, yet see no more than three or four distinct features. The density of the world is low; it is a landscape of profound emptiness, a green desert. The vegetation, while total in its coverage, is uniform and low-slung, broken only by the occasional "monolith"—a singular, towering spire of woody growth that resembles a cathedral spire wrapped in wet velvet.

The silence one might expect from such a remote highland is absent, replaced by a sound that is physical, a roar that vibrates in the marrow. It is the deafening, multi-tonal scream of the wind rushing through the high-altitude vents of the landscape, punctuated by the rhythmic, industrial throb of the machines.

Scattered miles apart, separated by yawning gulfs of mist and green, are the industrial harvesters. These are the only signs of the "built" world: massive, iron-black structures that seem to have been dropped from a great height into the moss. They are not cities, but solitary factories—perhaps atmospheric condensers or pressure-mining rigs. Their great brass pistons move with agonizing slowness, heaving against the weight of the air, venting plumes of supercooled steam that vanish instantly into the bright white sky. The tech is heavy, clanking, and utilitarian—massive gears coated in frost and green slime, turning with a tectonic deliberation that matches the slow growth of the world around them.

There is almost no movement of life. A rare, pale-winged scavenger might occasionally drift on the updrafts, a mere speck against the blinding sun, but otherwise, the land belongs to the plants and the machines. 

Everything here happens across vast durations. The machines take hours to complete a single stroke. The weeping water takes decades to carve a centimeter into the root-rock. The light remains fixed and brutal for spans that feel like days. It is a cold, bright, shouting wilderness, a place where the earth is still trying to decide what shape it will take, even as the heavy iron of industry begins to rust into its new, wet skin.
