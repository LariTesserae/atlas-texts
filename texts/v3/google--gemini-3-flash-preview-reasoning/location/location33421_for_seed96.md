It is a world of white stone and verticality, a high-altitude basin where the concept of "ground" has been forgotten. Here, the landscape is not grown, but stacked. 

The environment is composed entirely of millions of hand-hewn blocks of a pale, porous limestone. These blocks form a dense, architectural thicket of ziggurats, narrow spires, and steep-stepped terraces that pack the valley from wall to wall. There is no space between these structures; they lean against one another, forming deep, light-starved canyons that terminate in flat, blindingly bright plateaus. Because the erosion is so young, the edges of these stones are razor-sharp. The tool marks—the rhythmic, primitive scarring of flint-on-stone—are still crisp and visible on every surface, suggesting a monumental effort that ended only recently, or perhaps moves so slowly it appears paused.

Despite the height and the thin, cool air, the world is saturated. The stone itself acts like a sponge. Water does not flow in streams here; it exhales from the masonry. The atmosphere is a heavy, luminous mist that clings to the sharp angles of the towers. Every surface is slick with a thin, shimmering film of moisture. From the undersides of the countless protruding eaves and corbelled arches, water gathers into heavy, slow-forming droplets. 

The sound is a rhythmic, percussive silence—the "tock-tock-tock" of water falling from one stone tier to the next, echoing up from the lightless depths of the masonry. There is no wind to break the pattern, only the cool, damp breath of the stone.

There is a total absence of green. No moss softens the jagged corners; no lichen mottles the white surfaces. The world is sterile, a mineral kingdom. The only "ecology" is the slow, chemical growth of the water itself. In the places where the weeping is most constant, small, sharp crusts of travertine and calcium carbonate have begun to form, creating tiny, fragile stalactites that mimic the primitive geometry of the buildings. These are the only things that "grow"—slow, pale mineral accretions that thicken the walls by millimeters every century.

The light is pervasive and punishingly bright, diffused by the high-altitude haze into a shadowless glare. It reflects off the wet, white stone until the entire valley seems to glow from within. Even in the narrowest alleys between the stacks, the light bounces downward, illuminating the "streets" which are merely the roofs of the level below.

Everything here is built, yet there is no sign of the builders. There are no doors, only openings; no furniture, only smaller stacks of stone. The tech is fundamental—gravity, friction, and the sheer mass of the blocks holding the world together. There are no pulleys or cranes left behind, only the sense that this entire regional expanse was assembled by hand, one cold, wet stone at a time.

It is a patient, static place. The only dynamic element is the water’s slow migration through the rock, a crystalline metabolism that operates on a geological timescale. The air tastes of wet chalk and cold, clean vacuum. It is a monumental archive of labor, a high-altitude marsh of stone, perfectly quiet and perfectly full.
