The air is a heavy, warm velvet, so saturated with moisture that the distinction between breath and drink feels negligible. This is a low-lying basin, a shallow thumbprint in the earth where the world has settled into a permanent, sweltering stasis. There is no wind here. The atmosphere does not circulate; it simply leans against the surfaces of the world, heavy and wet.

The space is small, perhaps the size of a village square or a large cathedral’s nave, hemmed in by high, weeping walls of porous limestone. Within these boundaries, the density is absolute. There is no open ground. The floor of the basin is a labyrinthine crowd of bulbous, mineral-heavy formations—sedimentary mounds that have grown upward like slow-motion bubbles, each slick with a continuous film of warm water. They are the color of bruised plums and wet sand, layered with calcified ridges that have taken ten thousand years to settle into their current, unchanging shapes.

There is no green here. The world is utterly devoid of chlorophyll. In place of plants, a thick, non-biological carpet of mineral crusts and gelatinous, heat-loving slimes coats every surface. These are not alive in any way a gardener would recognize; they are chemical precipitates, slow-growing salts and silicate jellies that feed on the rich, warm mineral tea that seeps from the basin’s floor. The shapes are organic—frilled like coral, swollen like gourds—but they are as hard as teeth or as soft as wet clay.

The light is a dim, perpetual amber, as if filtered through miles of thick cloud or deep water. It casts no sharp shadows. Everything is rounded, softened by aeons of relentless, gentle erosion. The edges of the world have been licked smooth by the humidity. 

Tucked into the narrow, winding gaps between the mineral mounds are the faint marks of a primitive presence. These are not ruins of a great civilization, but the patient leftovers of a quiet one. A small, hand-hewn alcove has been scraped into a limestone wall, just deep enough to shield a fire that hasn’t burned for a century. Nearby, a handful of flat stones have been laid across a particularly deep pool of standing water to form a short, uneven bridge. A few tools remain, half-embedded in the mineral crust of a ledge: a scraper made from a sharpened volcanic glass, a heavy stone hammer with a finger-groove worn into its grip, and several clay bowls, unglazed and thick-walled, positioned to catch the rhythmic drip from the ceiling. 

The sound is the only clock. *Drip. Pluck. Hiss.* The water moves from the weeping rocks to the standing pools in a polyrhythmic sequence that never falters. It is a quiet place, save for this liquid percussion. Occasionally, a heavy bubble of gas might roll to the surface of a silt-heavy pond and break with a wet *thwack*, but otherwise, the silence is profound.

Fauna is nearly non-existent. Once every few hours, a pale, translucent creature—something like a wingless moth or a soft-shelled crustacean—might crawl with agonizing slowness across a mineral dome, its movements so rhythmic and steady that it seems part of the geology. It leaves a faint trail in the slime that the condensing water erases within minutes.

Nothing here is in a hurry to become something else. The erosion has reached an equilibrium where the minerals deposited by the weeping walls exactly match the minerals carried away by the slow, subterranean drainage. It is a world in a jar, a warm, dim, crowded sanctuary of stone and water, where the very idea of a "season" or a "change" feels like a fever dream from a distant, louder world.
