The ground here does not know the concept of a curve. It is a vast, low-lying expanse of razor-edged obsidian and shimmering, unweathered silica. The terrain is "young" in the sense that it feels as though it were rendered only moments ago; every fracture in the dark mineral floor is a perfect geometric split, unsoftened by wind or moisture. There is no sand, no silt, and certainly no mud. The world is bone-dry, yet it is not a wasteland.

Rising from the sharp-edged floor is a forest of impossible density, a lushness that defies the absence of water. These are not plants of cellulose and sap, but structures of structured light and hyper-advanced polymers that mimic the architecture of ferns, weeping willows, and thickets of brambles. They are warm to the touch, radiating a gentle, consistent heat that keeps the air at a pleasant, static temperature. The "leaves" are thin sheets of iridescent crystal, layered so tightly that they form shimmering, multi-colored walls that stretch for miles across the flat horizon.

Because there is no water to provide weight or tension, these growths follow laws of physics that feel slightly askew. A vine might grow perfectly straight into the air for thirty feet before turning a sharp ninety-degree corner, continuing its path as if gravity were merely a suggestion. The scale is immense—an open, regional plain where the crystalline canopy creates a false sky beneath the real one. 

The light is constant and bright, pouring down from a sun that seems too large and too still. It catches on the sharp edges of the ground and the translucent "foliage," creating a world of high-contrast brilliance. There are no shadows that move; because the light source and the landscape are locked in a frozen equilibrium, the shadows are permanent stains of deep violet on the white and black floor.

Despite the visual density, the silence is profound. There is no rustle of leaves, for there is no wind. There is no trickle of streams, for there is no liquid. Occasionally, a low-frequency hum vibrates through the soles of one’s feet—not a mechanical sound, but the sound of the reality itself maintaining its state. It is the sound of a processor at rest, the background radiation of a world that is being actively calculated. 

Fauna is nearly non-existent, though occasionally one might spot a "glitch-wisp"—a small, flickering cluster of voxels that skitters between the crystal trunks. These entities do not breathe or eat; they seem to be maintenance routines, drifting through the lush, dry thickets to ensure no two objects occupy the same space.

The technology here is not built; it is inherent. There are no ruins, no wires, no machines. Instead, the very atoms of the landscape seem to be infused with a post-physical intelligence. The "plants" may actually be antennae or data-storage units of unimaginable capacity, growing in fractal patterns to maximize surface area for a purpose beyond biological survival.

Time does not seem to pass. The "lushness" does not grow, nor does it wither. A crystalline frond, if snapped (though it would require immense force to break its perfect molecular bond), would not regrow; however, in this static realm, nothing ever seems to break. It is a museum of a moment, a high-resolution, dry paradise where reality has reached a state of perfect, unchanging completion. It is a vibrant, crowded, and utterly lonely masterpiece of a world, standing sharp and brilliant under a timeless sun.
