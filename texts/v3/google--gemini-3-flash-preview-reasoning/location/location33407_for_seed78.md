In the Great Lean, there is no horizon, only the intersection of verticality and the void. 

Imagine a sky that is not a ceiling, but a hollow, infinite well of charcoal-colored air. Across this emptiness stretch the Pillars: colossal, needle-sharp stalks of a singular, hyper-vegetative species that serves as the world’s only substrate. These are not trees as we know them, but gargantuan, obsidian-barked shoots that thrust upward from an unseen depth, tapering into points so sharp they seem to pierce the thin, freezing atmosphere. They are young in the way a mountain range is young—jagged, unweathered, and still aggressively ascending.

The scale is impossible to hold in the mind at once. A single Pillar might be five miles thick at its base and rise forty miles into the thin, cold air. Between these Pillars lie gulfs of nothingness so vast that a storm system can form, rage, and dissipate entirely within the space between two stalks, never touching either one. 

Because the air is so thin and the elevation so extreme, it is perpetually cold. The moisture here does not fall as rain; it arrives as a "frost-bloom"—a sudden, heavy condensation that flash-freezes into rime. The bark of the Pillars is perpetually damp and slick, coated in a shimmering layer of ice that holds the faint, bruised light of a sun that never quite seems to rise. In the deep hollows of the bark, where the plant’s own internal heat creates a micro-thaw, small pockets of liquid water collect, forming hanging ponds the size of lakes, suspended in the vertical valleys of the trunk.

The sound is the first thing that overcomes a traveler. It is never quiet. The wind here does not whistle; it roars, channeled through the gargantuan gaps between Pillars like a physical weight. Combined with this is the "Growth-Crack"—the sound of the Pillars themselves expanding. Because the ecosystem is so young and active, the wood is constantly splintering and shifting as it pushes higher. It sounds like a slow-motion shipwreck occurring on a planetary scale: a rhythmic, tectonic booming that vibrates through the soles of your feet.

Life here has adapted to the verticality and the gloom. The fauna is abundant but largely unseen in the shadows. There are "Grip-Gliders," six-limbed creatures with hooks of organic iron that they use to scale the icy bark, and "Vane-Birds" with wings made of stiff, translucent membrane that catch the updrafts. They communicate in shrill, percussive barks that cut through the wind’s low drone. Everything clings; everything hooks. To fall is to be lost to the lightless void below for a lifetime.

Human presence is a series of fragile afterthoughts. Small, primitive dwellings are tucked into the natural fissures of the bark, constructed from the shed needles of the Pillars and bound with fibrous, frozen resins. These are not cities, but precarious camps, scattered hundreds of miles apart. The technology is tactile and manual: bone-pulleys, hand-woven rope-bridges that sway over the abyss, and stone scrapers used to harvest the bioluminescent moss that provides the only reliable light. There is no fire here—the air is too thin and the wood too damp—so the people live in a state of perpetual, shivering dimness, huddling for warmth against the Pillar’s own slow, internal pulse.

The world feels unfinished. The edges of the bark are sharp enough to cut; the gaps between the land-masses are terrifyingly empty. It is a landscape of frantic, cold growth, a continental-scale garden of thorns reaching for a dark God, where the only rule is to hold on tight, and the only constant is the booming of the wood as it climbs higher into the freezing night.
