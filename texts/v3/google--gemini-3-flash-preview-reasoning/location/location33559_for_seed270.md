In the deep interstices of a cooling, ancient engine—a space no larger than a jeweler's chest—lies a landscape of flattened, weary perfection. This is the Lithic Interstice, a pocket-world tucked between two massive plates of rusted iron and oxidized copper. It is a place where time has sanded every edge down to a dull, comfortable curve, and where the concept of "up" is merely a matter of which metal ceiling one is currently scurrying across.

The terrain is a vast, level plain of pulverized hematite and fine, grey-blue silt. There is no moisture here; the air is a crisp, desiccated nitrogen mix, chilled by the massive heat-sinks that hum somewhere miles away in the "outer" world. The ground feels like velvet-coated slate, worn smooth by eons of vibration. Because the scale is so compressed, a single discarded brass screw, half-buried in the silt, looms like a jagged, copper-hued mountain range, its threads forming spiraling terraces for the inhabitants.

Vegetation is entirely absent, replaced by the slow, crystalline growth of "static-flowers"—delicate, fractal structures of zinc and sulfur that sprout near electrical leaks. They don't grow so much as precipitate, shivering in the constant, low-frequency thrum of the machine. 

The most striking feature of the Interstice is the sheer, suffocating density of life. The fauna here is not biological in the sense of soft tissue and blood, but a teeming, clicking ecology of "litho-scurriers." They are tiny, multi-legged crustacean-like entities with shells of polished magnetite. They are everywhere. They carpet the ground in a shifting, iridescent tide, their carapaces clacking together with the sound of a thousand spilled marbles. They move with a frantic, twitchy energy, driven by the need to harvest the faint electromagnetic pulses that bleed through the floor.

The sound is a constant, industrial roar—not a chaotic noise, but a rhythmic, mechanical chanting. The deep, bass throb of the engine plates provides the heartbeat, while the high-pitched skittering of the magnetite swarms provides a frantic, overlapping melody. It is loud enough to vibrate the silt into standing wave patterns, creating miniature dunes that shift and reform every few seconds.

Scattered across this miniature desert are the "Relics." These are the industrial markings of a lost purpose: a row of vertical copper filaments that glow with a faint, steady amber light; a series of miniature, spinning turbines that draw power from the draft; and small, rectangular housings that might have once been sensors but now serve as fortified citadels for the larger, slower-moving fauna. These structures are built into the landscape, integrated by time and oxidation until they are indistinguishable from the "natural" geology of the machine.

Despite the darkness of the engine’s interior, the Interstice is lit by a "normal" light—a diffused, ambient glow emanating from the static-flowers and the glowing copper filaments. It creates a perpetual, cool twilight, casting long, sharp shadows behind every grain of sand and every skittering leg.

Dynamics are high here. The world is never still. The swarms are in a constant state of migration, following the shifting currents of electricity like birds following the sun. Predator-prey cycles play out in the blink of an eye as larger, multi-segmented "wire-snappers" lunge from the shadows of the screw-mountains to seize smaller scurriers. There is a sense of frantic, crowded industry, an ecosystem that has perfected itself within a closed loop, operating with the terrifying efficiency of a watch that refuses to stop ticking even after its owner has turned to dust. 

Everything in the Interstice is old, polished, and packed tight. It is a dense, vibrating theater of movement, hidden in the cracks of a world that has long since forgotten it exists.
