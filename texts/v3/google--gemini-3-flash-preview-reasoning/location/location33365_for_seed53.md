At this altitude, the sky is no longer blue; it is a hard, bruised violet, so thin that the sun does not glow so much as it pierces. The light is a constant, blinding white pressure, reflecting off surfaces with a surgical intensity that makes shadows feel like physical holes in the world. We are standing in the **Glass Thicket**, a high-altitude depression cradled between three jagged, nameless peaks that tear at the underbelly of the atmosphere.

The terrain is a dense, vertical labyrinth. It is not made of wood or leaf, but of obsidian and translucent quartz-veined granite. These are the "Spires"—thousands of narrow, hexagonal pillars, none thicker than a man’s torso, standing packed together like the bristles of a brush. They rise ten to twenty feet into the biting air, so close to one another that a traveler must turn their shoulders to pass through the gaps. Their surfaces are not jagged, but eerily smooth, worn down by eons of wind-blown grit into a state of "established" erosion—they look less like broken rocks and more like ancient, weathered teeth.

There is no green here. The only thing resembling life is a "fungal-crust," a biology of slow, mineral-eating mats that cling to the shaded sides of the spires. These mats are the color of dried blood and rusted iron, brittle to the touch, smelling faintly of sulfur and cold copper. They do not grow so much as they crystallize, expanding by microns each year.

The world is cold—a dry, sucking cold that steals the breath—but it is not dry. Despite the thin air, the environment is curiously damp. Clouds do not pass overhead; they collide with the peaks and snag in the thicket of spires. They leave behind a heavy, shimmering condensation that coats the obsidian in a film of water. In the brilliance of the sun, this moisture doesn't evaporate immediately; it beads and trickles down the stone faces, collecting in tiny, clear pockets at the base of the pillars. These "weeping wells" are the only source of water, reflecting the violet sky like scattered coins.

The sound is a rhythmic, metallic tinkling. It is the sound of "ice-shedding." Every few minutes, the intense sun melts the flash-frozen condensation from the previous night, causing sheets of needle-thin ice to slide off the stone and shatter against the floor. It creates a tonal, percussive environment—a quiet, crystalline chatter that never stops.

Human presence is scattered and ancient. This is not a place of cities, but of "stations." Tucked into the natural niches of the pillars are small, primitive shelters built of stacked flat-stone and lashed together with thongs of cured, leathery fungus. These are "wind-breaks," barely large enough for a person to crouch in. Beside them, one might find the tools of a vanished or very sparse people: scrapers made of chipped volcanic glass, heavy stone mallets for crushing mineral-mats, and shallow bowls carved directly into the bedrock to catch the drip of the spires. The technology is tactile and manual, born of necessity and the absence of fire-wood.

The landscape is active, governed by a rapid, violent dynamic of light and frost. As the sun moves, the shadows of the spires sweep across the ground like the hands of a thousand clocks. Where the shadow hits, the water freezes instantly with a sharp *crack*; where the light returns, it weeps. The air is always in motion, a constant, freezing updraft that carries the scent of wet stone and the sound of the shattering ice. 

It is a place that feels finished, a settled machine of stone and light, where the only movement is the clockwork transition between liquid and solid, and the only residents are the shadows weaving through the dense, glittering teeth of the mountain.
