This is the **Interior of the High-Crank Sump**.

It is a space no larger than the inside of a grand piano, yet it possesses the vertiginous gravity of a mountain peak. There is no stone here, no soil, no sky. Every surface is a product of heavy industry: cold-rolled lead plates, galvanized steel struts, and thick, vibration-dampening rubber gaskets. The geometry is all right angles and sharp, lethal edges. Because erosion has not yet begun its work, the metal is unnervingly pristine—burrs from the factory molds still cling to the edges of the bolts, and the milling marks on the floor-plates are deep enough to catch a fingernail. It is a place that has just been finished, or perhaps a place that is being birthed, second by second, in a state of permanent newness.

The air is a freezing, pressurized soup. At this elevation—thousands of feet up within a larger, unseen superstructure—the atmosphere is so cold it feels brittle, yet it is saturated with moisture. Water does not fall as rain here; it weeps. It beads on the ceiling-plates and hangs in heavy, trembling globes before dropping into the machinery. The walls sweat a grey, industrial condensation that smells of ozone and wet iron. 

Sparse, pale lichens—the only vegetation—cling to the threading of the giant bolts, huddling in the few places where the heat of the mechanical friction offers a fraction of a degree of warmth. They are translucent and waxy, appearing more like spilled sealant than living things.

The space is impossibly crowded. It is a dense thicket of moving parts. Pistons the size of a human thigh pump in rhythmic, violent proximity to one another, their shafts slick with a mixture of freezing water and black grease. Copper pipes, wrapped in fraying insulation, weave through the gaps like frozen intestines. There is no room to stand upright; to move is to risk being caught in the indifferent crush of the works.

And then there is the sound. It is not a noise, but a physical weight. The Sump is a hollow resonant chamber for the entire industrial mass above it. The sound is a deafening, monolithic roar—a singular, unvarying C-sharp that vibrates the marrow of the bones. It is the sound of ten thousand turbines screaming in unison, smoothed out into a flat, terrifying wall of vibration. Because the environment is in a state of frozen equilibrium, the sound never wavers. It does not ebb; it does not flow. It is a permanent condition of the geography.

Within this cacophony, the fauna thrives in a terrifying, claustrophobic abundance. They are "Sump-Mites"—creatures the size of fists, plated in silver chitin that mimics the surrounding steel. They are everywhere. They carpet the pipes, huddle in the recesses of the gear-teeth, and hang in clusters from the weeping ceiling. They do not move across the landscape so much as they *are* the landscape. They vibrate in sympathy with the great roar, their millions of tiny legs drumming against the metal. Their eyes are small, glowing pinpricks of bioluminescence, providing the only light—a dim, shifting charcoal-blue that barely illuminates the churning pistons. They eat the grease, the lichen, and each other, a frantic and overwhelming biomass packed into a space far too small to contain them.

In the High-Crank Sump, time has no meaning because nothing changes. The pistons strike their marks, the mites vibrate, the water condenses, and the roar continues. It is a masterpiece of total construction, a tiny, freezing, screaming pocket of industrial perfection that has never known the touch of a wind or the sight of a sun. It simply *is*, humming in the dark at the top of the world.
