The world is a vertical forge, a soaring architecture of obsidian and basalt that has not yet learned the meaning of a curve. Here, the geography is a series of razor-edged exclamation points, thrusting upward into a sky so thick with charcoal-colored haze that light is reduced to a bruised, infrequent ghost. It is a place of extreme, jagged elevation—spires the size of cathedrals rise from the floor of the world not over eons, but over hours, pushed upward by a restless, shivering crust.

The heat is a heavy, pervasive weight. It does not radiate from a sun, but from the ground itself, a dry and suffocating warmth that tastes of sulfur and pulverized stone. There is no water; the very concept of a liquid is alien to this parched mineral kingdom. Instead of rivers, there are slow-moving flows of fine, superheated dust that pour down the flanks of the growing mountains like hourglass sand, filling the deep, lightless trenches below.

Because the erosion is near zero, the landscape is terrifyingly sharp. Every edge is a surgical blade. The stone has not been softened by wind or rain; it is raw, neonatal geology. The surfaces are tactile and hostile—vesicular glass, crystalline needles, and slabs of matte-black rock that vibrate with the internal pressure of the planet.

The dynamics of the place are hyperactive. The ground is never truly still. You can hear the groaning of the strata as it buckles and snaps, a rhythmic *thrum-crack* that echoes through the sparse, open valleys. A mountain may grow a hundred feet in a single afternoon, its birth accompanied by a shower of sparks as flint strikes flint, only to be swallowed by a sudden subsidence the following day. It is a landscape in a state of violent, creative flux, a Solaris-like engine of stone that builds and destroys itself with indifferent speed.

Life is almost entirely absent, save for a few stubborn, non-aqueous anomalies. Clinging to the leeward sides of the obsidian fins are crusts of a pale, fungal-like mineral. These are not plants, but self-organizing chemical colonies that feed on the heat and the sulfurous gases, growing in brittle, fractal patterns like frost on a windowpane. They are the only "vegetation" this world knows—colorless, stiff, and smelling of spent matches.

In the wide, dark spaces between the rising peaks, there are signs of something else. They are not cities, nor even villages, but scattered, solitary structures. These are primitive, megalithic arrangements—slabs of stone leaned against one another to form lightless voids, or tall, tapering chimneys that vent the internal heat of the world. They are built without tools, perhaps stacked by the tectonic movements themselves or by some pre-intelligent impulse to order the chaos. They sit like abandoned sentinels, hollow and silent, scattered miles apart across the dusty plains.

The sound is a paradox. For long stretches, there is a profound, airless silence, the quiet of a tomb. Then, the silence is shattered by the sound of a mountain breaking. A sharp, crystalline snap reverberates through the darkness, followed by the dry hiss of a landslide. There is no wind to carry the sound, so it travels through the ground, vibrating in the soles of the feet long before it reaches the ears.

Everything is scaled to the monumental. The valleys are vast, empty amphitheatres where the darkness feels thick enough to touch. Because there is no fauna—no insects, no birds, no scurrying things—the only movement is the slow, grinding evolution of the earth itself. It is a world of immense, lonely potential, a place where the clock of geology has been set to a frantic pace, yet there is no one there to witness the mountains rising and falling in the dark. It is a raw, hot, and magnificent wasteland, perpetually coming into being and perpetually falling apart.
