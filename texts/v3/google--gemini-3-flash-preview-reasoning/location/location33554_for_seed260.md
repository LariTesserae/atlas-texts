The sky is not a void, but a heavy, unlit pressurized ceiling of amethyst-colored gas that presses down upon the Shards. Here, the geography is vertical. Massive, geometric pillars of unweathered obsidian and raw, white quartz thrust upward for tens of thousands of feet, their edges so sharp they seem to cut the very dim light that filters through the haze. There is no sand, no silt, and no mud; the ground is a jagged floor of birth-trauma geology, showing no signs of the softening hand of wind or water. The world is "new," though it feels ancient—it is in a state of permanent, frozen emergence, as if the planet were a casting still cooling in its mold.

There is no water. In its place, the air is saturated with a fine, parched particulate that tastes of copper and burnt sugar. Deep in the vertical canyons, the "vegetation" thrives in a paradoxical lushness. These are not biological plants as understood by wet worlds, but towering, fractal growths of silicate lace and vibratory filaments. They cling to the vertical faces in dense, velvet-colored mats of deep crimson and charcoal. They do not photosynthesize; they are phonosynthetic. They feed on the Sound.

The Sound is the dominant inhabitant of the landscape. It is a deafening, subsonic tectonic roar that vibrates the marrow in the bone. It is the sound of the world’s core grinding against the sky. It never falters, never ebbs. It is a physical weight, a constant pressure that makes the air shimmer with heatless kinetic energy. The "lush" silicate fronds pulse in rhythmic unison with this roar, growing in complex, mathematical spirals that defy gravity, jutting out horizontally from the cliffs for hundreds of feet without breaking.

Despite the intensity of the noise, the world is slow. A single "leaf" of glass-hair may take a decade to unfurl its fractal geometry. The mountains do not crumble; they grow by slow accretion, pulling minerals out of the thick, dry air.

Reality here is thin and prone to catastrophic local failures. In the spaces between the great pillars, perspective does not hold. A spire that appears miles away may be touched by reaching out a hand; a footstep forward might result in a sensation of falling upward. Gravity is a suggestion, often overruled by the sheer acoustic pressure of the Sound. In certain "hollows," light behaves as a liquid, pooling in low depressions of the rock, glowing with a dim, sickly phosphorescence that provides the only illumination.

Humanity, or whatever primitive intelligence haunts this place, has left its mark in the most precarious of ways. Tucked into the "birth-folds"—the deep, un-eroded creases where two mountain-shards meet—are small, scattered dwellings. These are not built so much as stacked: flat slabs of slate piled with painstaking, manual precision to create low-slung, windowless cells. 

There are no wheels here, no gears, no metals. The technology is one of stone and vibration. Primitive resonators made of hollowed quartz are placed outside the dwellings to cancel out the deafening roar, creating small pockets of localized silence where the inhabitants can sleep without their internal organs bruising from the frequency. These people have carved narrow, dangerous stairs directly into the obsidian, and they move between the spires on tension-bridges made of woven silicate-fiber, hand-spun from the "lush" red moss. 

There is no fauna. Nothing moves with a heart or a lung. The only movement is the slow, agonizing growth of the crystals and the occasional, reality-warping shimmer of the air when the Sound reaches a harmonic peak. It is a cool, dark, and terrifyingly vertical world, where the act of standing still is a manual labor, and the geography is a raw, unfinished thought.
