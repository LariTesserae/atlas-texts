The horizon is not a line, but a soft, frayed edge where the pale sky meets the endless, weathered crown of the Great Lignin. 

At this elevation, the air is thin, cool, and perpetually damp, smelling of ozone and ancient, wet sawdust. There is no stone here. There is no soil. The world, from the deepest foundation to the highest spire, is composed entirely of vegetable matter. You stand upon a plateau that was once the stump of a tree the size of a province, now eroded by eons of wind into a flattened, silver-grey plain of petrified cellulose. It is so old that the grain of the wood has become a geological feature, with ridges of hard heartwood forming mountain ranges six inches high and valleys of soft rot creating sprawling, moss-clothed basins.

Water does not flow in rivers; it seeps. It manifests as a pervasive, shimmering condensation that clings to the underside of giant, overhanging fern-brakes and drips with rhythmic, heavy thuds into the hollows below. Occasionally, a brief, freezing rain sweeps across the open spaces, turning the silver wood into a dark, slick mirror before vanishing back into the grey mist.

The scale of the landscape is deceptively open. Because the terrain is a single, continuous organism—an eroded, multi-layered forest-city—the eye can track a single brass-lined aqueduct for miles as it spans the gaps between titanic, dying trunks. Yet, despite the vastness, the world feels hollow and sparse. The "urban" elements are not crowded tenements, but monumental industrial monoliths spaced leagues apart. These are the Sap-Canneries and the Fiber-Mills: massive, soot-stained structures of copper and iron bolted directly into the living wood. 

The tech is heavy, loud, and rhythmic. Massive pistons, driven by the slow, hydraulic pressure of the world-tree’s own circulatory system, hammer against the wood with a sound that vibrates in the marrow of your bones. A constant, low-frequency thrum—the heartbeat of a million interconnected gears—competes with the screaming whistle of steam vents. It is a loud world, filled with the groans of shifting timber and the clatter of mechanized harvesters that crawl like iron beetles along the distant, vertical ridges.

But here, reality has begun to fray at the edges of the industrial sites. 

The 2.6 magnitude of weirdness manifests in the way perspective fails to hold. As you look toward a distant industrial spire, the distance seems to stretch and contract with your breath. Shadows do not always follow the sun; they linger in the depressions of the wood long after the light has moved, pooling like black liquid. In certain "thinned" zones near the great turbines, the vegetation grows in impossible geometries—ferns that fold into four-dimensional knots and moss that glows with a light that feels cold to the touch. Sometimes, you may see a mechanical crane lifting a load of timber, only for the crane to vanish, leaving the timber suspended in mid-air, continuing its ascent as if the machine were still there, merely translated into a different frequency of existence.

Fauna is nearly non-existent. There are no birds in the cool, damp air, and no insects in the moss. The only movement, aside from the grinding machinery and the slow growth of the plants, is the occasional "Scour-Wisp"—small, shimmering distortions in the air that drift like tumbleweeds, smoothing the wood as they pass, contributing to the ancient, eroded flatlands.

The world feels finished, a primordial machine-garden that has reached its final, stable state. It is a place of grand, lonely industry, where the hiss of steam and the drip of condensation are the only prayers offered to a sky that never quite clears. The plants are the architecture, the substrate, and the power source; the machines are the parasites; and the silence between the loud, mechanical beats is where the logic of the universe quietly unspools.
