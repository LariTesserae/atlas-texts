The environment is a vast, sunken basin known as the Low Ribs, a subterranean valley located miles beneath the primary crust, yet so wide and vaulted that it defies the claustrophobia of a cave. The ceiling is a distant, jagged underside of a continent, weeping a constant, rhythmic condensation that never gathers into rivers, but instead maintains a permanent state of dampness across the stone.

The terrain is aggressively young. There are no rounded pebbles or weathered slopes here. The ground is a tectonic wreckage of obsidian-hued basalt and silica, shattered into razor-edged plates and sharp, vertical fins. To walk here is to navigate a labyrinth of glass-like shards. Because there is no wind and no significant flow of water to grind these edges down, the landscape remains as sharp as the day it fractured, a geological freeze-frame of a colossal ancient collapse.

It is cool, a steady, unyielding chill that never fluctuates. The air is heavy and smells of wet mineral and the sharp, musky scent of chitin. Light is a rare commodity, filtered down through impossible vertical chimneys that reach toward the surface, arriving only as a dim, violet-grey gloaming that never shifts its angle. There are no shadows that move; the dimness is as static as the stone.

Vegetation is a desperate afterthought. In the crevices of the sharpest rocks, thin, pale mosses cling to the moisture, and occasionally, a cluster of translucent, woody fungi erupts from a pocket of accumulated silt. These plants do not provide a canopy or a floor; they are mere accents on a world of grey and black.

Yet, the Low Ribs are not empty. They are drowning in life.

The silence one might expect from such a cold, stone cathedral is utterly obliterated by the fauna. The sound is a constant, overwhelming cacophony—a textured wall of noise that never abates. It is the sound of millions of clicking mandibles, the wet slap of soft bellies against damp stone, and the echoing, multi-tonal shrieks of creatures that use the vast scale of the basin as a resonator. The density of the *individuals* is sparse—you can look across a mile of jagged basalt and see only a dozen large shapes—but their presence is felt everywhere. 

High above, clinging to the "ceiling" of the world, are the Great Skimmers, pale, flat-bodied organisms the size of ships that move with a glacial, crawling pace, their thousand legs scratching against the stone in a sound like grinding metal. On the floor, long-limbed, eyeless ungulates pick their way across the sharp ridges with hooves hardened to the density of diamond. They are the primary architects of the world's "tech." They possess a rudimentary, communal intelligence. Though they build no shelters—there is no weather to hide from—they use the sharp basalt to scrape and shape the bones of their dead into long, serrated lances and scraping tools. These items are found scattered in "sorting sites," clusters of primitive manufacturing where stones have been used to hammer other stones, leaving behind piles of shaped shards and cleaned hides.

There is no sense of time here. The moisture drips at the same rate every hour, every century. The violet light never fades to black or brightens to noon. The animals do not migrate; they simply exist in a state of high-volume equilibrium. It is a loud, crowded, primitive world where the sharp edges of the earth are never blunted, and the roar of the living is the only thing that fills the vast, cold void.
