The ground here is not earth, but a vast, rusted plain of interlocking plates, worn as smooth as river silt by cycles of time no longer recorded. Every inch of the horizon is manufactured, a global machine that has forgotten its function. It is a landscape of "total build," where the mountains are slumped heaps of corroded girders and the valleys are the shallow, concave basins of cooling ponds long since overflowed. 

The elevation is low, a sprawling, flat expanse of industrial decay that suggests the entire world has settled under the weight of its own exhaustion. The atmosphere is a heavy, perpetual gloaming, hovering just above total darkness. The light does not come from a sun, but from the low-voltage, amber flicker of dying indicator lamps and the occasional, violent arc of blue electricity jumping between sagging cables.

It is a wet world, but the moisture is not refreshing. It is a world of weeping surfaces. A fine, cool mist—half-water, half-lubricant—condenses on every cold metal face, gathering into oily droplets that run down the ribs of massive, dormant turbines. The ground is a patchwork of shallow, black lagoons where the water sits stagnant over submerged circuitry, reflecting the occasional flash of a Short-circuit. 

The sound is the defining characteristic of this place. It is not a noise you hear, but a noise you inhabit. It is a deafening, rhythmic thrum—the groan of titanic tectonic plates made of iron, the shriek of high-pressure steam venting from rusted fissures, and the relentless, booming heartbeat of some deep-core pump that refuse to die. The sound is so loud it creates standing waves in the puddles, shivering the surface of the water into complex geometric patterns.

Vegetation is a desperate, sparse afterthought. There are no trees, only "wiry-moss"—a bioluminescent, copper-threaded lichen that clings to the sheltered undersides of pipes. It feeds on the heat of the machines and the chemical runoff of the rains. In some places, long, pale filaments of fungal growth hang from the overhead gantries like weeping willows, swaying in the hot, mechanical drafts that occasionally pulse through the air.

There is no animal life. Nothing with a pulse could survive the concussive pressure of the sound or the toxicity of the mist. Instead, the world is animated by its own mechanical ghosts. The "fauna" here are the machines themselves: gargantuan pistons that slowly rise and fall over the course of an hour; automated gantries that slide along rusted rails with a scream of metal on metal, carrying nothing to nowhere; and the slow, rhythmic opening and closing of massive ventilation louvers that look like the gills of a dying god.

The erosion here is profound. This is a primordial industrialism, a state of being so old that the difference between "natural" and "artificial" has collapsed. The rust is the new soil; the slumped ruins of factories are the new geology. The world is active, but it is the activity of a clockwork mechanism that has been overwound and then left in a damp cellar for a billion years. It is a landscape of immense, lonely scale—a valley of iron where you can walk for days without seeing anything but the repeating patterns of rusted bolts and the shivering, oil-slicked water, all while the sky-shattering roar of the machine reminds you that you are standing on the skin of a titan that has forgotten how to sleep.
