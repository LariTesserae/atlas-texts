The air here is a thick, cool suspension, less like oxygen and more like a slow-moving broth of distilled humidity. It clings to the skin with a weight that suggests the atmosphere is perpetually on the verge of collapsing into a solid state. This is a low place, a basin of cold stone and oxidized iron where the sky is not a horizon, but a distant, vaulted ceiling lost in a charcoal haze.

The floor is a mosaic of eroded basalt and rusted metal plates, worn down into soft, undulating curves by a thousand years of weeping water. Shallow pools, no deeper than a thumb’s length, cover nearly every surface, their tension so high they bulge over the edges of the stone like lenses. There is no soil here, and nothing green. In place of plants, the landscape is crowded with a dense, mineral biology. Translucent, gelatinous siphons—tubes of a material somewhere between glass and jellyfish flesh—rise from the puddles in tight clusters. They pulse with a rhythmic, internal peristalsis, drawing the floor-water upward to the unseen heights above.

Between these siphons grow the "rust-polyps," hard, calcified structures the color of dried blood and burnt orange. They resemble coral but lack the sharp edges; they have been sanded smooth by the constant, dripping condensation. The density is claustrophobic; one must weave through these vertical pipes and mineral mounds, feeling the slick, cold surfaces brush against one's clothes.

The "built" elements of the world are not separate from the terrain but are the bones of it. Massive, industrial pistons, four times the girth of a human, tilt at odd angles out of the silt. They are ancient, their steel surfaces pitted and velvet-soft with oxidation, yet they are not dead. Every few minutes, a deep, sub-audible vibration shudders through the floor—a slow, mechanical heartbeat. A piston will retract an inch with a sound like a heavy sigh, releasing a puff of freezing, white steam that instantly vanishes into the damp air. Copper wires, thick as ship’s cables, drape between the mineral pillars like dead vines, weeping beads of verdigris-tinted water.

The fauna here is abundant and silent. Soft-bodied, pale creatures, shaped like flattened bells, drift through the air at eye level, propelled by the same slow rhythms as the pistons. They graze on the mineral salts that crust the edges of the iron plates. Near the ground, small, multi-legged arthropods with shells made of hammered brass scuttle through the puddles. They do not click; their movements are muffled by the film of oil and water that coats everything. They gather around the leaking joints of the ancient pipes, drinking the pressurized lubricants that seep out in iridescent ribbons.

Light is a scarce resource. There is no sun, only a pervasive, dim luminescence that emanates from the siphons themselves—a faint, ghostly indigo that barely illuminates the path. Shadows aren't sharp; they are soft, muddy blurs that shift as the floating fauna pass through the glow.

It is a world of quiet, relentless process. The machines are not making anything for a consumer; they seem to be part of the water cycle itself, cooling the air to wring out the moisture, which then feeds the siphons, which eventually drips back down from the ceiling to be pumped again. The sound is a layering of liquid textures: the *tink-tink* of water on metal, the wet *slurrup* of the siphons, and the distant, metallic groan of a gear turning somewhere deep beneath the basalt. Everything is in motion, but nothing ever leaves this hall. It is a closed, damp, and perfectly balanced engine of minerals and cold steam.
