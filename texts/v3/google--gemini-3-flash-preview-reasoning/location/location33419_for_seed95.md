At this altitude, the air is thin but carries a surprising, heavy warmth, as if the sun has been soaking into the ground for a billion years and the earth is only now breathing it back out. This is the **Plateau of the Still-Housed**, a region so high that the clouds often drift *underneath* the ridges, yet so ancient that not a single sharp peak remains. Every mountain has been sanded down by eons of wind into a series of gentle, repetitive domes—mammillary hills of polished, dark-ochre stone that look less like geology and more like the vertebrae of a sleeping god.

The landscape is defined by an impossible density of life that makes no sound. 

While the vegetation is sparse—clinging only as thin, translucent lichens or patches of hair-like, white filaments in the shadows of the domes—the fauna is overwhelming. The plateau is carpeted in the *Slow-Movers*. They are soft-bodied, mound-shaped creatures the size of haystacks, covered in a skin that feels like damp suede. There are thousands of them, tens of thousands, huddled so close together in the troughs between the stone domes that one could walk for miles by stepping from one velvet back to another. 

They do not vocalize. They do not scurry. They pulse. A rhythmic, collective expansion and contraction occurs once every ten minutes, a low-frequency thrum that you feel in your marrow rather than hear with your ears. Because they are so numerous and so quiet, the world feels crowded but lonely, a massive congregation of silent witnesses.

Water here is a subtle, pervasive presence. It does not flow in rivers; it lives in the "weeping cracks" of the ancient stone. Because the air is warm and the elevation is high, moisture condenses on the cool, velvet skin of the Slow-Movers during the night. By midday, this condensation trickles down their sides, collecting in shallow, tea-colored pools in the depressions of the rock. These pockets of dampness are the only places where the white-filament grass grows, creating small, shivering oases of pale fringe amidst the vast expanse of ochre and grey.

There is no technology here, nor any memory of it. The "built" elements are skeletal and enigmatic. Scattered across the domes are the *Lace-Towers*—slender, three-story structures made of sun-bleached bone or perhaps a very hard, fibrous resin. They have no doors, only intricate, honeycombed apertures that allow the wind to whistle through them at a pitch almost too high for human ears. They are ruins, but they haven't crumbled; they have simply been hollowed out by time, standing as empty husks of a biology that reached upward before settling into the slow, horizontal sprawl of the current era.

The light is steady and mercilessly clear, typical of the high places, casting long, soft-edged shadows that move with agonizing slowness. Everything here operates on a deep-time scale. The erosion has reached its final state; the mountains can get no flatter, the stones no smoother. The Slow-Movers might live for centuries, their only movement the gradual migration toward the sunward side of a dome, a journey that might take a generation to complete.

The atmosphere is one of profound, heavy patience. It is a world that has finished its work and is now simply existing in the warm, damp afternoon of its lifespan. To stand in the center of the plateau is to be surrounded by a million heartbeats you cannot hear, under a sky that never changes, waiting for a sunset that seems a thousand years away. The only dynamic element is the occasional, sluggish blink of a Slow-Mover’s lidless eye, or the way the white-filament grass shivers when a pocket of moisture finally evaporates into the thin, warm air.
