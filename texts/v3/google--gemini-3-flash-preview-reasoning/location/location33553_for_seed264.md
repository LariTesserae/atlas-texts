The horizon is a long, flat line of bruised copper and oxidized iron. This is a low country, a basin of vast proportions where the very concept of "soil" has been forgotten, replaced by a lithic crust that is at once geological and mechanical. The ground is a rhythmic expanse of weathered plates, immense interlocking discs of basalt-colored alloy, and stretches of pulverized rust that crunch like dry snow underfoot.

It is a world of stagnant industry. There are no buildings here, no ruins of cities, no evidence that a hand ever turned a wrench. Instead, the industry is intrinsic to the planet’s crust. Massive, half-buried camshafts, thick as redwood trunks and miles long, rotate with a tectonic slowness beneath the surface. They do not seem to be part of a grand design or a functional factory; they are simply the way the earth is made. Great, rusted pistons rise from the low-lying flats, heaving upward with a hiss of pressurized steam before settling back into the earth with a shuddering thud that can be felt in the marrow.

The air is warm—a heavy, humid heat that smells of ozone and wet pennies. Moisture does not fall as rain so much as it gathers. It condenses on the cold, dark undersides of the great gears and drips into shallow, oil-slicked basins. These pockets of water are the only breaks in the mineral monotony: dark, iridescent pools where the liquid is heavy and tastes of minerals and salt. There is no green here. Not a single blade of grass or tuft of moss breaks the surface. The only things that could be mistaken for life are the slow-growing dendrites of copper-sulfate crystals that bloom in the spray of the steam vents, turquoise and jagged, spreading across the iron flats like a slow-motion frost.

The scale is oppressive in its openness. Standing in the center of a "valley"—which is really just a slight depression between two colossal, unmoving flywheels—one can see for miles. The landscape is sparse, populated only by these titanic, repetitive shapes. A single, jagged spire of obsidian-like glass might stand alone in a plain of red dust for three leagues before another feature appears.

It is never quiet. The world is a cacophony of "established" sound, a permanent, unchanging roar. There is the rhythmic *clack-hiss* of the steam vents, the deep, sub-bass grinding of metal against stone, and the constant, singing hum of high-voltage currents running through the crust. Yet, despite the noise and the movement, the landscape is utterly static. Nothing is being produced. Nothing is being consumed. The pistons have been firing in the same sequence for a billion years; the gears have turned a trillion times without moving a single inch of the horizon. It is a frozen equilibrium of motion, a dynamic system that achieves zero net change.

Fauna is nearly non-existent, though occasionally, one might see a "skitterer"—a creature of articulated chitin and wire-thin legs, possessing no eyes, only vibration-sensitive pads. They cling to the vibrating surfaces of the great machines, feeding on the static discharge and the warmth of friction, moving with a frantic, nervous energy that contrasts sharply with the slow, eternal grinding of their world.

The light is steady and unremarkable, a "normal" day-lit glow that reflects off the polished faces of the larger gears, casting long, sweeping shadows that move in time with the planet’s mechanical heartbeat. There is no wind, only the displacement of air caused by the machinery—sudden, warm gusts of metallic-smelling exhaust that ruffle the dust and then fall still.

In this place, time feels like a physical weight. Because nothing ever changes, the distinction between a second and a century feels academic. The rust is deep, the motion is eternal, and the silence of life is buried beneath the deafening, purposeless labor of the earth itself.
