Beneath the crust of the shifting earth, nestled in a jagged fracture of recent tectonic origin, lies the Scald-Pocket. It is a space no larger than a cathedral, a singular, claustrophobic knot of industry and geology compressed into a lightless hollow. 

The heat is the first thing to manifest. It is a thick, 2.9-level presence—a dry, baking radiance from the surrounding rock, mitigated only by the intermittent, sizzling moisture that beads upon the overhead conduits. The air is heavy, smelling of ozone and wet iron. It is a damp heat, not from a river, but from the constant, rhythmic expulsion of steam from a thousand micro-valves. Condensation clings to the sharp, unweathered edges of the obsidian-dark rock; the stone here is young, broken cleanly by recent pressure, showing no signs of the centuries of softening that water usually brings. The edges are razor-like, biting into the dense architecture that has been wedged into the crack.

The space is a miniature metropolis of machinery. It is an urbanity of pure function, devoid of inhabitants. Every square inch of the cavern floor and walls is layered with infrastructure: narrow, tiered walkways made of unoxidized brass; dense clusters of vertical pistons that cycle with a slow, hypnotic grace; and miles of bundled copper cabling that pulse with a dim, internal amber light. The density is overwhelming—a thicket of organized construction that leaves no room for a stride. To move through it is to squeeze between hot, vibrating metal plates and over hissing grates.

There is no animal life here. No insects skitter in the shadows; no birds roost in the rafters of the pipes. The ecology is purely mechanical and vestigially botanical. Sparse, pale mosses—translucent and heat-tolerant—bloom in the few places where condensation drips consistently onto the jagged stone. They do not look like surface plants; they look like spills of milky wax, feeding on the mineral runoff and the warmth, their tiny fronds trembling whenever a nearby engine cycles.

The atmosphere is quiet, yet intensely active. There is no roar of a city, but rather a complex, layered "breathing." A valve to the left lets out a sharp, rhythmic *tiss-tiss-tiss*; overhead, a heavy flywheel hums at a frequency that vibrates in the marrow of one's teeth. Deep within the walls, something heavy and hydraulic groans, a slow shifting of weight that suggests the entire pocket is a single, integrated machine performing a task for a world that has forgotten it. 

The light is dim, sourced from the dull cherry-red glow of overheated components and the occasional flicker of a status lamp on a control board that no one watches. These lights cast long, sharp-edged shadows against the fresh-cut rock, creating a flickering strobe effect as the mechanical parts move in their endless, automated dance. 

This is a place of cycles. Though there are no seasons, there is a "weather" of pressure. Every few hours, the steam output increases, the temperature climbs even higher, and the mosses swell with the sudden humidity. Then, a drainage sequence begins—a series of clacking shutters opening to swallow the excess water—and the pocket returns to its baking, expectant hush. 

The rules here are industrial and indifferent. The machines are not breaking down; they are in the prime of their operation, their surfaces clean and their movements precise. The "young" nature of the erosion suggests this space was opened and filled only recently—a secret, high-functioning heart beating in a freshly opened wound in the earth, working tirelessly at a process that requires neither witness nor master. It is a dense, humming, lonely miracle of heat and motion.
