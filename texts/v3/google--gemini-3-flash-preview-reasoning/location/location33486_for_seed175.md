This is the Verdigris Terrace, a high-altitude shelf tucked into the lee of a weathered, unnamed peak. At this elevation, one might expect a thin, freezing wind, but the Terrace is inexplicably warm—a humid, heavy heat that feels as though the stone itself is exhaling. The air is thick enough to taste, flavored by the constant, gentle transpiration of the flora and the mineral scent of weeping rock.

The terrain is a study in patience. The ground is not jagged or broken; it has been smoothed by eons of water into a series of shallow, interconnected basins. These are not sharp-edged pools, but rounded, organic depressions in the limestone, looking more like well-used thumbprints in clay than natural erosion. The rock is a pale, porous cream color, but it is rarely visible beneath a slick, shimmering coat of algae and velvet-textured mosses that range from deep emerald to a bruised, translucent purple.

Water is the primary resident here. It does not fall in a rush or flow in a stream. Instead, it seeps. The cliff face at the back of the Terrace—a wall of ancient, pockmarked stone—simply sweats. Droplets bead on the surface, swelling with agonizing slowness until they are heavy enough to slide down the vertical face, joining the films of water that coat every surface. The sound is a rhythmic, sporadic percussion: the *tink* of a drop hitting a still pool, the muffled *thwip* of water sliding off a broad, waxy leaf. There is no wind to stir the air, and no birds to break the silence. The quiet is heavy, cushioned by the moisture.

The vegetation is lush but strangely spaced. There is no undergrowth, no tangled thicket. Instead, the plants stand like solitary monuments. Massive, fern-like structures, three times the height of a person, rise from the stone basins at wide intervals. Their stalks are thick and pulpy, translucent enough to see the slow ascent of fluids within them. These "trees" do not have bark; they have a skin that feels like wet suede. At the top, they erupt into a few enormous, heavy fronds that sag under their own weight, dripping continuously into the pools below. Between these giants, the ground is mostly clear—open stretches of mossy stone and shallow water, providing a sense of cathedral-like scale. You could walk between the plants with arms outstretched and never touch a leaf.

There is a subtle, unsettling intelligence to the landscape’s layout. Small piles of smooth, hand-sorted river stones sit at the base of several plants—not as foundations, but as if placed there for a purpose. Near the center of the Terrace, a flat slab of rock bears the marks of primitive labor. There are shallow grooves worn into the stone where something—a hand-tool of harder rock, perhaps—has been repeatedly sharpened. Beside it lies a discarded scraper made from a large, fossilized shell, its edge honed to a lethal thinness. There are no shelters, no fires, and no graves. Only these few, silent witnesses to a manual craft that requires no metal and leaves no scar on the land.

The weirdness of the place reveals itself in the light. Though the sun is high and the sky is a clear, thin blue, the atmosphere on the Terrace refracts the light into a soft, hazy glow that seems to come from all directions at once. Shadows are blurred and weak. In the deeper pools, the water doesn't just reflect the sky; it seems to hold a pale, bioluminescent charge, a faint pulsing that matches the slow, tectonic rhythm of the environment. 

Nothing moves quickly here. If a leaf were to fall, it would take a minute to reach the ground, drifting through the dense, warm air like a feather in oil. The "fauna" is entirely absent—not an insect, not a worm, not a fly. The plants do not need to be pollinated by movement; they seem to communicate through the root-system that laces through the porous limestone, or perhaps through the chemical signatures they release into the standing water.

The rules of this world are simple: stay still, and you become part of the geology. The moisture is so persistent that if one were to sit on the stone for a day, the moss would begin its slow, green ascent up one's limbs. The Terrace does not tolerate haste. It is a place of perpetual, humid stasis, where the only transition is the infinitesimal deepening of the stone basins and the slow, upward crawl of the water through the veins of the giant ferns. It is a silent laboratory of wet stone and green light, existing in a balance so perfect it feels like a held breath.
