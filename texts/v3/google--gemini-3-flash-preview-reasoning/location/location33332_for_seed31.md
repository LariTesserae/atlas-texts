The air at this altitude is thick, not with thinness or cold, but with a suspended, warm humidity that clings to the skin like a wet silk veil. We are in the High Fold, a region where the planet’s crust has recently—in geologic terms—shattered upward, creating a landscape of terrifyingly sharp geometry. The peaks here are not rounded by eons of wind; they are jagged, obsidian-dark, and razor-edged, looking more like fresh fractures in glass than mountains.

Despite the sharpness of the rock, the world is dripping. This is a place of weeping stone. Water does not merely fall as rain; it seems to exhale from the pores of the mountains themselves. Countless silver threads of water, some no wider than a finger, slide down the vertical faces of the black rock, creating a constant, shimmering movement that belies the absolute stillness of the air. The water collects in high, perched basins and overflows in silent, heavy curtains into the valleys below.

The vegetation is a riot of emerald and deep teal, though it lacks the chaotic tangles of a lowland jungle. Because the rock is so young and hasn't yet crumbled into deep soil, the plants have adapted with primitive, stubborn elegance. Massive, succulent-leaved ferns with stalks as thick as tree trunks grip the jagged ridges. Mosses, three inches deep and saturated like sponges, coat every horizontal surface, softening the lethal edges of the stone. There are no flowers here, and no fruit—only vast, waxy leaves that capture the constant mist and channeled spores that drift in the humid updrafts.

Spread across this vertical empire is a city that defies the primitive nature of its tools. It is an urban sprawl of breathtaking scale, yet it feels lonely. The architecture is a triumph of manual labor and dry-stone masonry. Massive blocks of dark, unweathered basalt are stacked into soaring terraces and cyclopean walls that bridge the gaps between the sharpest peaks. There are no arches, only heavy lintels and stepped pyramids that echo the natural fracture lines of the mountains.

The settlement is vast, extending as far as the eye can see through the haze—a network of platforms, hanging gardens, and stone causeways—but the density is low. The dwellings are separated by wide stretches of moss-covered rock and plunging waterfalls. It is a city designed for a population that values distance. There are no markets, no crowded squares. Instead, there are grand, empty promenades of hammered stone, slick with moisture, leading to solitary watchtowers that overlook the clouds.

Technologically, the inhabitants have mastered the weight of stone and the flow of water, but nothing else. There is no smell of smoke, for there are no fires; no sound of metal, for there are no forges. The tools left on the benches are made of the same obsidian as the mountains, chipped to a prehistoric sharpness. Fabric is rare, replaced by woven fibers of fern-bark, heavy and waterproof.

The most striking feature of the High Fold is the silence. There is no fauna. No birds circle the jagged spires; no insects hum in the waxy thickets; no lizards skitter over the stone. The only sound is the infinite, low-frequency thrum of falling water—a sound so constant it becomes a form of silence.

Time here feels like a locked gear. The erosion is so minimal and the environment so stable that the landscape appears caught in a permanent state of "just after." It is always just after the rain, just after the mountains rose, just after the city was finished. The plants do not wilt, and the stone does not smooth. The warmth is constant, a humid embrace that never fluctuates into a breeze or a chill. It is a monumental, beautiful stasis—a high, wet, lonely civilization carved into the teeth of a young world, waiting for a wind that never blows.
