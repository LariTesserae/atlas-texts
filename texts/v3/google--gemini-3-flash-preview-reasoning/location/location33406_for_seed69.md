The sky does not exist here. Instead, there is the Great Ceiling—a vast, rusted expanse of corrugated iron and leaded glass, miles above, dripping with a perpetual, warm condensation that falls as a heavy, soot-stained rain. This is a world of high plateaus made not of stone, but of compacted, weathered machinery, stacked so deep that the concept of "ground" has been forgotten.

In the 0.0 darkness, the landscape reveals itself through heat and sound. It is a warm, humid pitch-blackness, smelling of wet copper, ozone, and rotting loam.

### The Terrain of the Iron Marsh
The elevation is dizzying. You stand on the edge of a massive turbine housing, a weathered metal cliff face smoothed by eons of acidic rainfall. Below, the "valleys" are vast basins of stagnant, oily water, fed by the weeping of the colossal pipes that bridge the chasms. These pipes, some wide enough to house cities, are the primary arteries of the landscape. They hum with a deep, subsonic vibration that shakes the marrow of your bones.

Erosion here has a mechanical character. The metal is not just rusted; it is "melted" by time, its hard edges rounded into organic-looking mounds. Huge gear assemblies, once precise, have softened into iron hills. Pituitary-sized rivets have become stepping stones through the black-water mires.

### The Vegetation of the Dark
The "lushness" of this place is a tactile, humid density. In the absence of light, the flora has abandoned the leaf. Instead, the landscape is choked with towering, translucent lung-ferns and rubbery, obsidian-black vines that pulse with the rhythm of the hidden engines. They don't seek the sun; they seek heat. They wrap themselves around the warm steam-valves and heat-exchangers that sprout like scattered monuments across the plateaus.

Mosses, thick as heavy carpets and sodden with grease, coat every horizontal surface. If you touch them, they are hot to the touch, thriving on the thermal bleed of the world-machinery. There are forests of "whistle-reeds"—tall, hollow tubes of silica and carbon that grow in the wind-tunnels between structures, creating a perpetual, haunting flute-music as the hot drafts blow through them.

### Industrial Ecology
The tech is not "built" into the world so much as it *is* the world. Isolated, titanic engines—some the size of mountain ranges—occupy the centers of vast clearings. These are the scattered "buildings" of the world: lonely, electrified cathedrals of brass and steel. They are mostly silent, save for the occasional, earth-shaking *thrum* of a piston firing once every decade, or the rhythmic, hissing release of a million-gallon steam vent.

Electricity is a wild element here. Static discharge crawls across the surface of the wet vines like glowing blue insects. The "fauna" have adapted to this. There are "Spark-Kites"—flat, leathery organisms with wingspans of thirty feet—that drift on the thermal updrafts, trailing long, conductive filaments to catch the ambient electricity of the air.

In the marshes, things with too many legs and shells of hammered bronze scuttle through the oily reeds. They feed on the mineral rich "sludge" that leaks from the joints of the ancient pipes. They communicate not with sight, but with clicks and clacks that echo off the iron cliffs, contributing to the deafening, layered cacophony of the landscape.

### The Sound and the Motion
It is never quiet. The sound is a thick, textured fabric: the constant *thrum-hiss* of the steam, the rhythmic *clack-clack* of the fauna, the roar of the black waterfalls pouring off the edges of the machine-plateaus, and the high-pitched whistling of the reeds. It is a loud, active world, constantly processing, constantly moving.

The dynamics of the world are driven by the "Steam Cycles." Every few hours, the internal pressure of the earth-machine reaches a breaking point. The ground shudders, the air grows ten degrees warmer, and the weeping rocks begin to spray scalding mist. The fauna retreats into the hollows of the pipes, and the lung-ferns inflate, soaking up the moisture. Then, the "rain" begins—a heavy, dark downpour that washes the soot from the iron and feeds the black marshes below.

### The Weirdness of Scale
To walk here is to be an ant on a rusted locomotive left in a jungle for a billion years. You might find a "scattered" structure—perhaps a small, pressurized cabin of brass, its glass frosted over, sitting alone on a ridge of gears. It is an artifact of a civilization that didn't build this world, but merely tried to survive within its gears.

There is no horizon, only the receding layers of gargantuan silhouettes—pistons, cranes, and cooling towers—fading into the warm, wet dark. It is a landscape that is both a graveyard and a womb, a place where the distinction between the mechanical and the biological has been eroded by time, heat, and the endless, weeping water.
