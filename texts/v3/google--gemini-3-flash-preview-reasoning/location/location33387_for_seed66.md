The highlands of the Ocher-Conduct do not begin or end; they simply persist across the curvature of the planet, a vast, elevated continent of weeping metal and sweating stone. The air here is perpetually thick, not with the scent of pine or rot, but with the humid, mineral tang of wet brass and the heavy, warm condensation of a thousand thousand industrial exhausts.

The scale is overwhelming. To stand on one of the great Corrugated Vales is to look across a distance where the horizon is lost to a haze of heat-shimmer and falling spray. The ground beneath is not soil, but a weathered, geological sediment of rusted iron filings, crushed quartz, and the smoothed-over debris of a billion years of mechanical friction. There is no green here. Not a single leaf, no carpet of moss, no vine. The world is a spectrum of hematite reds, copper greens, and the dull, wet grey of lead.

Everything is wet. The high-altitude atmosphere, warmed by the slow, rhythmic heat of subterranean furnaces, constantly bleeds moisture. It clings to the underside of the massive, elevated conduits that span the valleys like the ribs of a planet-sized beast. It weeps from the joints of the great slow-pistons. It gathers in shallow, warm pools atop the flat roofs of the factory-districts, where it is agitated into a constant ripples by the thrumming of the earth.

The architecture is built on a scale that defies individual comprehension. It is a city that has become a mountain range. Buildings do not sit upon the land; they are extruded from it, layered over one another in a dense, industrial sedimentary record. Massive, soot-stained towers rise miles into the bright, normal light of the high sky, their surfaces etched by eons of wind and acidic mist until they resemble the weathered spires of a canyon. Between them, the infrastructure is a chaotic, saturated web of pipes, catwalks, and slow-moving gears, all slick with a permanent film of condensation.

Despite the lack of flora, the world is violently alive. The fauna is not merely present; it is a deluge. Without the competition of plants, life has poured itself into the animal form.

Creatures the size of cathedral bells, armored in chitin that mimics the dull sheen of the industrial environment, cling to the sweating pipes in clusters so dense they look like living grapes. In the air, vast clouds of gossamer-winged insects, each the size of a human hand, move in slow, swirling cyclones that catch the light like drifts of falling mica. They do not buzz; they hum, a deep, resonant vibration that syncs with the rhythmic groan of the machines. On the lower levels, in the warm, mineral-rich runoff of the weeping rocks, heavy-bodied bipeds with skin like wet leather move in endless, slow-motion migrations, grazing on the mineral crusts and the fungal-like bacterial mats that form in the shadows of the vents.

The sound is a constant, crushing presence. It is the voice of a world that is both a machine and a hive. There is the rhythmic, tectonic *thud-hiss* of the great steam-bellows, which move with the agonizing slowness of glaciers, taking hours to complete a single breath. Layered over this is the chittering, clicking, and low-frequency lowing of the billions of organisms that inhabit the nooks of the iron city. It is a loud world, but not a frantic one. The dynamics are patient. The great migrations of the leather-skinned beasts take decades to cross a single district. The erosion of the brass pilings by the constant drip of warm water is measured in centuries.

The tech is not sleek. It is industrial, heavy, and weary. It is a technology of boilers, chains, and immense, grinding flywheels that have been polished smooth by the passage of time and the touch of a trillion claws. The machines seem to have no central purpose other than their own continued, slow movement—pumping warm water up to the high peaks only for it to weep back down, fueling the humid atmosphere that the overwhelming fauna requires to breathe.

Here, the distinction between the "built" and the "natural" has eroded entirely. A bird-thing might weave a nest from copper wire and shed scales, wedging it into the crook of a pressure gauge that hasn't been read in a millennium. A massive, slow-moving crawler might spend its entire three-hundred-year life ascending a single industrial spire, licking the condensation from the metal as it goes.

It is a warm, bright, and crowded cathedral of industry, where the machines provide the weather, the stones provide the water, and the animals provide the soul, all moving at the patient, grinding pace of a world that has already found its final, enduring shape.
