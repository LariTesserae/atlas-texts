This is the **Scald-Reach**, a region of extreme elevation where the bones of the earth are still hot from their birth.

Intermittent plumes of white vapor erupt from hairline fractures in the black rock, hissing softly before cooling into a fine, slick dew that coats every surface. The water collects in small, perfectly still pockets of obsidian—natural bowls of dark liquid that mirror the sky with such clarity they look like holes in the ground.

There is no green. Biology has not yet found a foothold here, or perhaps it has been burned away. The only "growth" is mineral: vast, sprawling blossoms of salt and sulfur that crystallize around the steam vents in pale yellows and chalky whites. They look like frozen, brittle fungi, but they are cold and hard to the touch.

Massive copper-clad conduits and iron gantries bridge the chasms, connecting one spire to the next. The density of construction is startling; in the narrow gaps between the great needles of rock, thousands of dwelling-pods and processing units are stacked like the cells of a hive, clinging to the vertical faces with massive, heat-resistant bolts.

The technology here is heavy, mechanical, and strangely quiet. Enormous flywheels, three stories tall, turn with a glacial, hypnotic slowness, harvesting the thermal energy of the stone. Thick cables, slick with condensation, hum with a suppressed electrical charge, vibrating just enough to shake the droplets of water from their undersides. There are factories here—vast, windowless blocks of corrugated steel—that seem to be breathing, exhaling rhythmic puffs of steam that join the natural mists of the peaks.

Yet, for all this industrial density, the Scald-Reach is hauntingly silent. The machines are precision-engineered to be hushed, or perhaps the heavy, humid air swallows sound before it can travel. The inhabitants, if they are seen at all, move with deliberate, heat-dampened patience along the high catwalks. The most significant changes occur over decades: the slow, millimeter-by-millimeter rise of a new spire pushing up from the depths, or the gradual accumulation of mineral scale that eventually chokes a copper pipe, requiring a silent, months-long replacement.

It is a place of heavy stillness and extreme height, a dense, industrial hive perched on the razor-edge of a world that is still cooling, still hardening, and still very much alone.
