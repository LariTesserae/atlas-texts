At this elevation, the air should be thin and freezing, but instead, it is a heavy, stagnant soup of warmth. We are in the **High Weave**, a continental-scale lattice of biological architecture that has completely replaced the crust of the world. There is no dirt here; there is no stone that was not first passed through a gut.

The terrain is a dizzying, multi-layered sprawl of calcified ivory-colored spans. Imagine a city the size of a mountain range, constructed entirely of interlocking ribs, vaulted chambers, and porous platforms, all grown or deposited by a trillion generations of industrious, unthinking life. The scale is immense—valleys of woven struts drop away into a darkness so profound that the bottom is a matter of theory, not sight. Looking out across the horizon, one sees not peaks, but the silhouettes of other great megastructures, weathered by time into smooth, organic arches that suggest a world made of bone and hardened silk.

It is dark. The sun is a distant, pale memory filtered through a perpetual ceiling of thick, low-hanging clouds that snag on the highest spires. The light that reaches the interior of the Weave is a bruised, charcoal grey, barely enough to distinguish the floor from the abyss.

Yet, in this gloom, the vegetation is suffocatingly lush. Lacking direct sunlight, the flora has evolved into a riot of deep purples, bruised magentas, and waxy blacks. Massive, succulent-like vines, thick as a human torso, wrap around every "built" pillar, their leaves broad and heavy with stored water. Mosses grow in carpets three feet deep, muffling footsteps but doing nothing to dampen the noise. Epiphytes—plants that live on other plants—hang in long, shaggy curtains from every overhang, dripping with slow, rhythmic condensation.

Water is a precious, intermittent presence. There are no rivers, only the "Cold Drip." The clouds above constantly surrender their moisture to the porous surfaces of the upper Weave. This water percolates downward, caught in the cupped leaves of giant bromeliads or pooled in the weathered basins of the bone-white floor. These pockets of dampness are the anchors of the ecology, small, dark mirrors reflecting nothing but the shadows above.

The world is "total built," but it is not a city of gears or wires. There is no technology here. There are no tools, no sharpened edges, no intentional design. It is a landscape of pure, instinctual engineering—a coral reef of the sky. The erosion is "established," meaning the sharp edges of the original construction have long since been smoothed by the patient lick of wind and the slow, acidic creep of root-systems. The structures feel ancient, balanced, and immovable.

The silence you might expect from such a heavy, dark place is non-existent. The sound is physical—a deafening, unrelenting wall of noise. This is the "Fauna: 2.9." The environment is not merely inhabited; it is saturated. Hidden in the dark folds of the vegetation are trillions of organisms. There is the constant, high-frequency vibrato of chitinous wings, the wet, rhythmic clicking of mandibles, and the low, booming territorial bellows of soft-bodied behemoths that move through the lower gloom. The sound is so loud it feels like a pressure against the chest, a constant reminder that every cubic inch of this dark, warm space is occupied.

Movement in this world is slow. The great vines take decades to coil an extra turn around a pillar. The calcified floor thickens by a millimeter a year as the microscopic architects deposit their waste. The animals, though numerous, move with a languid, energy-saving grace, adapted to the humid heat. 

Here, the rules are simple: life builds upon life. There is no "nature" separate from the "structure." To be in the High Weave is to be a microbe inside a gargantuan, living lung, surrounded by the deafening roar of a biology that has successfully paved over the universe. It is a warm, damp, loud, and crowded dark, where the ground is just a different kind of ceiling, and the only constant is the slow, relentless growth of the hive.
