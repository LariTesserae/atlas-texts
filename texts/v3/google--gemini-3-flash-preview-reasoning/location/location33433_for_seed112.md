The world is a hollow lattice of dry, vertical continents—a sky-sea with no water, only the Weave.

Imagine a structural network of petrified, cork-like xylem the size of mountain ranges, spanning a void that stretches to every horizon. There is no planet-core here, no tectonic plate or soil. The substrate is entirely botanical. It is a vast, open-cell grid of titan-vines, each miles thick, gray-white and weathered like bone, crisscrossing in a geometry that defies gravity. Because the density of these structures is so low, the landscape is mostly "nothing"—a terrifyingly vast, bright emptiness between the structural beams of the world.

This is a dry kingdom. There is no rain, no mist, no dew. The air is a cool, thin, gas-rich cocktail that smells of desiccated sap and friction-heated wood. In place of water, the ecology runs on refined mineral dust and high-energy light. The light is blinding, a 2.6-scale brilliance that seems to come from everywhere at once, reflecting off the silver-white bark of the Weave until the shadows are scrubbed away.

The vegetation is not a soft green covering; it is the architecture itself. The "trees" are megastructures, and their "leaves" are stiff, metallic fans the size of city squares. These leaves are not passive; they are the source of the world’s industrial character. They are geared. They are evolved bio-mechanisms. As the unrelenting high-altitude winds—the "Great Gale"—roar through the lattice, these leaves catch the kinetic energy. They don’t just flutter; they cycle. They hammer. They move with the rhythmic, thumping precision of a piston line. 

The sound is 2.8: a deafening, oceanic roar that never ceases. It is the sound of a trillion wooden gears grinding against one another, the howl of wind through the porous fiber of the sky-beams, and the percussive *thrum-thrum-thrum* of the leaf-pistons pumping dry nutrients through miles of vertical trunk. If you stood on a branch, the vibration would rattle your bones. It feels like standing on the deck of a great, engine-driven ship that spans the diameter of a moon.

The elevation is constant and terrifying. There is no "down" to land on, only a deeper descent into the blue-white glare where the lattice becomes too dense to see through. Looking out from one of the primary vines, the next nearest structure might be fifty miles away—a thin, white needle piercing the sky, connected to your own path by a web of hair-fine tendrils, each still the size of a highway.

Despite the emptiness, the world is turbulent (2.9). It is never still. The entire lattice is in a state of violent, harmonic oscillation. The beams sway and whip; the junctions groan with the stress of continental-scale leverage. At the "nodes"—where twelve or more titan-vines meet—the friction generates enough heat to glow. These are the centers of the world's fauna.

The animals here are creatures of the gale. They are sleek, hard-shelled things, like a cross between a beetle and a glider, with hooked limbs designed to lock into the vibrating bark. They don't walk; they are moved by the wind, unhooking themselves to be flung across the gaps, steering with iridescent vanes to reach the next beam. They swarm in the nodes, feeding on the powdered minerals shaken loose by the constant grinding of the wood.

The "industrial" nature of this place is its most unusual feature (1.3 weirdness). There is no "builder," yet the forest functions as a factory. The plants have evolved to process the gas of the atmosphere into solid, resinous pellets, which are then transported by gravity-fed chutes inside the trunks—clattering, rattling pipes of wood that sound like coal being moved in an old refinery. It is a world of production without a purpose, a mechanical metabolism that has reached a state of weathered, eroded balance (2.2). The wood is polished smooth by the wind; the "gears" of the leaves are rounded by eons of friction.

It is a lonely, roaring, brilliant place. It is a machine of white wood and cold light, humming with the terrifying energy of a storm that has lasted since the beginning of time. There is no soft place to rest, only the hard, vibrating bone of the world, singing its deafening, industrial song into the bright void.
