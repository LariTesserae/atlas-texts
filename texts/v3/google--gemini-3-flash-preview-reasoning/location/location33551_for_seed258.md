In this place, the concept of "earth" has been discarded. There is no stone, no silt, no tectonic plate. Instead, there is the Lattice: a world-sized architecture of living, woven cellulose that extends across a regional scale, suspended at an altitude where the air is perpetually thin, warm, and heavy with water.

The landscape is a series of massive, hollow cathedrals of growth. Imagine a skyscraper-sized stalk of translucent, amber-colored lignin, its walls thick as a fortress, but within it, a hollow space the size of a valley. These structures—the "Builds"—are not natural accidents; they are deliberate, structural, and total. Every floor is a woven mat of pressurized roots; every ceiling is a vaulted canopy of interlocking leaves so dense they feel like plates of armor. Yet, for all this mass, the world feels sparse. The Builds are separated by kilometers of open, misty void, connected only by singular, terrifyingly long bridges of braided vine that hum in the wind.

The water here is a constant, intrusive presence. Because the environment is a closed biological system, the atmosphere is in a state of perpetual perspiration. The walls of the plant-towers weep. Great, fat beads of sap-scented water roll down the emerald-ribbed interiors, gathering in hanging basins or plunging through deliberate gaps in the floor to the levels below. It is a "wet" world not because of rain, but because the very architecture is exhaling.

Despite the complexity, the world feels embryonic. The "erosion" is almost non-existent because nothing has had time to decay or be worn down. The edges of the giant leaves are razor-sharp, the surfaces of the stalks are glossy and pristine, and the sap is still tacky. It feels like a city that was finished seconds ago, or a forest that grew into a palace in a single night. There is no debris. There is no rot.

The sound is the most overwhelming feature. It is loud—a constant, low-frequency thrumming that vibrates in the marrow of one's bones. It is the sound of billions of gallons of nutrient-rich fluid being pumped upward through the structural pylons. It is a rhythmic, hydraulic percussion, punctuated by the occasional booming crack of a new structural support snapping into its tensioned place. The air roars with the sound of falling water and the resonant vibration of the wind passing through the vast, hollow chambers.

Life other than the plants is a rarity. Occasionally, one might see a translucent, gelatinous organism—something neither bird nor insect—skimming the weeping walls to drink, its body pulsing in sync with the thrum of the towers. These creatures are silent, ghost-like against the vibrant, green-gold light that filters through the translucent walls.

There is no technology here—no metal, no gear, no wire. But there is a terrifying sense of design. The way a vine coils around a load-bearing trunk suggests a bolt; the way the floor-mats are cross-hatched suggests a textile. It is a world that has been "built" entirely out of the impulse to grow.

Time does not seem to function here. The light remains a steady, diffused gold, never dimming, never brightening. The temperature stays at a humid, hothouse warmth that never fluctuates. The Lattice does not grow larger, nor does it die back. It exists in a state of frozen equilibrium, a massive, biological machine caught in the moment of its most perfect tension. It is a sprawling, high-altitude marsh of the gods, beautiful and deafening, standing tall over an abyss that no one has ever seen.
