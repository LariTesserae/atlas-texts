I am the one who has stopped moving.

In this world of vast durations and searing intimacy, I find myself not among the soaring kites or the blind worms, but at the intersection of the iron and the moss. I was once a Liturgy-Keeper, one of the barrel-chested tenders of the great brass pistons. But in a land where a single heartbeat feels like a wasted motion, I have transitioned. I have become a **Votive**—a keeper who has finally achieved the stillness the landscape demands.

I am standing on the external gantry of Harvester 09, a black iron limb extended over a thousand-foot drop into a fissure of tangled roots. I have not adjusted my position in three cycles of the great piston. 

My world is seen through the smoked-quartz of my mask, which has begun to crust over with the crystalline salt of the atmosphere. The light does not just hit me; it presses against me. It is a physical weight, a white thumb pushing me down into the metal grating of the gantry. I do not fight it. To fight is to burn oxygen, and oxygen here is a thick, liquid privilege that must be sipped, not gulped.

I would be the bridge. My oil-slicked hide suit, once supple, has been colonized. A colony of Crystalline Epiphytes has taken root in the folds of my shoulders and along the length of my unmoving arms. When the wind screams through the high-altitude vents, I do not just hear the high-pitched shriek of the glass-growths; I feel it in my clavicles. They vibrate against my chest-plate, a frantic, musical shivering that contrasts with the tectonic *thoom-hiss* of the harvester beneath my feet.

I am no longer a servant of the machine, nor am I yet a part of the moss. I am a process of transition. 

I watch the Slow-Pulser in the valley below. From this height, it is a lime-green shadow, a slow-motion wave of hydration moving toward the base of my tower. I feel a profound kinship with it. We are both gardeners of the slow. It cleans the gears; I hold the vigil. 

My lungs have become heavy, wet bellows. Each breath is a deliberate, hydraulic act. I draw in the cold, suspended marsh of the air, and I feel the moisture condense in the dark of my throat, a silver thread of runoff that mirrors the weeping cliffs around me. I do not swallow. I let the water accumulate, a private pool within my own anatomy, until the pressure forces a slow, rhythmic spill from the corner of my mouth-piece. 

I am waiting for the moment—perhaps decades away—when the hydraulic pressure of the world finally claims me. Already, the green desert is rising to meet my boots. A fine, hair-like mesh of liana-networks has begun to lace through the iron grating of the gantry, binding my soles to the harvester. I do not pull away. To stay still is to be embraced.

In this shouting wilderness, I have found the ultimate luxury: the loss of the self into the scale of the landscape. I do not think in minutes; I think in the slow accumulation of rust. I do not see the sky as a void, but as a solid block of marble that I am slowly, through the sheer act of existing, carving myself into.

Who am I? 

I am the point where the industrial throb becomes a heartbeat. I am the moss-covered statue that still breathes. I am the chime in the gale, a sentinel of the white glare, waiting for my skin to finally turn to bark, for my blood to cool into the slow, steady rhythm of the weeping water. I am the silence at the center of the roar.
