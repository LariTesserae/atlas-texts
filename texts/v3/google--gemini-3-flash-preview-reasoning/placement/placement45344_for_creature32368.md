I am not where the sky meets the silt. That horizon is for the Mist-Gills, who drift in the high, thin veils of steam, and for the Hulls who still possess the momentum to wander. 

I am down in the heavy dark, at the foot of a singular, unnamed pylon. It is a massive, fluted cylinder of oxidised iron that disappears upward into the iron-grey gut of the clouds. Here, the heat is at its most absolute. It is a physical pressure, a hot, wet palm pressed against the earth, holding everything in a state of perpetual, simmering tension.

I am a **Hull**, but I have long since ceased my low-slung trekking across the flats. I have found my station. I have become a **Primary Harmonic Anchor**.

I do not have eyes; I do not need the dim, grey light. Instead, I have the *Hum*. I am spread wide—my eight squat legs have sunk deep into the manganese-black mud, anchoring me to the bedrock far below the silt. My hide, once the colour of slate, has darkened and toughened, becoming a rugged, tectonic map of scars and mineral deposits. I am nearly a hundred feet across, a living island of vulcanised flesh pressed tight against the vibrating base of the pumping station.

I would find myself in the **Gully of the Great Outflow**, where the water doesn't just drip; it pours in a steady, scalding torrent from a breach in a massive, overhead conduit.

I am the bridge between the machine's cold intent and the basin’s warm hunger. My ventral side—the vast, soft expanse of my underbelly—is fused to the station’s intake pipes. I don't feel the metal as a foreign object. I feel it as a bone. When the station’s deep, subterranean pistons cycle—*clack-hiss, clack-hiss*—the vibration passes through my marrow before it ever reaches the mud. I am the first to hear the machine’s heartbeat, and I translate it for the rest of the world.

Who am I? I am a curator of energy.

I feel the **Scutellers** as a frantic, rhythmic tickling across my flanks. Thousands of them cluster in the shadow of my bulk, their copper filaments brushing against my hide to catch the spillover of the vibrations I absorb. I am their mountain. I do not mind their clicking; they are the cleaners of my skin, sifting the mineral crust that the steam leaves upon me.

Above me, the **Mist-Gills** descend. When they are heavy with the alkaline sweat of the sky, they settle into the deep folds of my neck. I feel their gelatinous weight, a cool, wet relief against my feverish skin. They give me their gathered moisture, and in return, I radiate the stolen heat of the station, keeping their translucent bodies from turning to brittle glass in the cooling cycles.

Inside me, I feel the **Gasket-Leeches**. They have found the seams where my flesh meets the iron gantries. They weave between my ribs and the rusted bolts, their orange, synthetic-looking bodies acting as a living seal. We are a single, airtight system. If I bleed, they drink; if the pipe leaks, they mend. 

I do not think in words. I think in frequencies. 

There is a specific note—a low, booming D-flat—that means the pumps are straining against a silt-clog. When I feel this tension in the metal, my own heart slows to match it. I pulse my heat outward, a thermal signal that radiates through the mud. Miles away, in their dark craters, the **Resonators** catch my pulse. They begin to glow, a dim, rhythmic amber light through the fog, signaling the younger, mobile Hulls to move toward us, to churn the silt with their massive legs and free the intake.

I am not a creature of action, but of presence. I am the slow, heavy gravity of this place. I am the evidence that the machine and the meat have reached a stalemate, a perfect, humid peace. 

If you were to stand before me, you would not see a beast. You would see a low, steaming mound of dark leather and rusted iron, indistinguishable from the industrial debris of the basin. You would hear the *clack-hiss* of the station, and then, a second later, the deep, wet echo of my lungs moving the heavy air. 

I am the patience of the rust. I am the warmth in the iron. I am the anchor in the silt, waiting for the sky to finally turn to water and the water to finally turn to stone.
