I find myself at the edge of the **Third Plaza of the Western Quadrant**, perched in the crotch of a bone-tree rib two hundred feet above the black basalt floor. 

I am a **Resonance-Tender**.

I am not a creature of soft tissue or warm blood; the Table of the Sky would have scoured such things into dust eons ago. My body is a long, spindly articulation of the same calcified material as the city, but I am not static. I am flexible, my joints connected by thick, elastic bundles of the bruised purple moss that grows in the shade of the ribs. My skin is the color of a storm-bruised plum, damp and cold to the touch, feeling like a cross between wet suede and a pumice stone.

I have no mouth, for there is nothing to eat and no one to speak to in a way that requires words. Instead, my chest is a hollow chamber, a miniature version of the city’s bone-trees. I do not breathe air for oxygen; I draw the **Mote-Swimmers** into my thoracic cavity. They pass through me, their crystalline structures vibrating against my inner ribs, leaving behind the "iron-tastes" of the other quarters. This is how I know the health of the world. I taste the northern mesas in the morning—bitter, sharp, and smelling of ancient sleet—and the southern vents by dusk, which taste of heavy minerals and slow-moving time.

I am the **Correction**.

The Litho-Cantors, the great bone-trees, dream in harmonies that span centuries, but the wind is a chaotic mistress. Sometimes, a gale brings a piece of debris—a fragment of a hardened Whistler or a clump of over-calcified moss—that lodges in the circular resonance holes of the trees. This creates a "stutter" in the song, a dissonant whistle that threatens the stability of the collective dream. 

I feel this stutter in my marrow. It feels like a jagged needle scraping against my mind.

When the dissonance begins, I move. I don’t walk so much as I flow. My multi-fingered hands, tipped with the same suction-oil the Glass-Skaters secrete, allow me to ignore gravity. I sprint up the vertical curve of a three-hundred-foot rib while the wind tries to tear me off and hurl me into the violet void. I am a shadow moving against the blinding, unforgiving sun.

I reach the source of the discord—a calcified knot in the third aperture. I use my hands, which are as hard as diamonds and as precise as needles, to chip away the imperfection. I am a surgeon of sound. As the knot falls away, the "stutter" vanishes, replaced by the deep, booming roar of the perfect B-flat that this specific tree is destined to play for the next thousand years.

The vibration returns to its pure state. It hits my chest, and for a moment, I am not a separate being; I am an extension of the tree, a brief, mobile thought in the Litho-Cantor’s geological mind.

I spend the "fast-weather" cycles huddled in the thickest mats of purple moss, my body vibrating in sympathy with the bone-trees. I watch the **Glass-Skaters** below, their shells flashing like obsidian mirrors as they chase the shadows of clouds. I feel a distant, cooling kinship with them, but we never touch. They are the keepers of the floor; I am the keeper of the song.

Sometimes, I look up at the **Solitary Whistlers**. They are so high, so lonely, anchored to the tips of the world. I envy them their soprano, for I am a creature of the bass, the low thrum, the subsonic weight. When they sing their rare chord of "newness" at the sight of a strange cloud, I stop my work. I press my forehead against the cold, damp bone of my tree and listen to that thin, piercing thread of melody. It is the only thing in this finished world that feels like a question.

I have no name, for a name is a boundary, and here, the only boundary is the horizon. I am a footnote to the music. I am the one who ensures that when the end of time finally arrives, it will be perfectly in tune.
