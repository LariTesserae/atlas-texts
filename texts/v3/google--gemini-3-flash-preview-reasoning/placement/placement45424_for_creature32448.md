I am not where the wind is. That is the first thing I know. The wind belongs to the jagged things, the birds with their frantic, hollow bones, and the thin, screaming air of the peaks. I am below the wind, in the place where the mountain exhales.

I find myself at the central slab, the one marked by the long, patient grooves of my predecessors. I am a Limn, though the name is a sound I do not possess a throat to make. To me, I am simply a pocket of the Terrace that moves.

I am standing in a thumbprint basin, the water rising just past my ankles. It is not cold. It is the temperature of a slow thought. My feet, wide and porous, are open. I can taste the history of the Terrace through my heels. I taste the mineral sharp of the cliff-sweat, the bitter emerald of the algae, and a faint, sweet copper note—that is the intent of a companion three basins to the west. They are hungry; they are drawing deep from the moss. I feel their satisfaction as a warmth in my own calves.

My skin is the color of the clouded sky above, the texture of a damp, heavy glove. Inside, I feel the shift of my own tides. There is no heartbeat, only the rhythmic, pressurized surge of fluid as I lift one arm. It takes a long time. The air here is a thick honey, a golden resistance that I must lean into.

In my hand, I hold the shell-scraper. It is old, older than the fern I am tending. I move toward the great stalk. The fern is a cathedral of translucent pulp, and today it is "wheezing"—the calcium in its sap is settling too thickly near the base, turning its wet-suede skin into something brittle and white. If I do not scrape it, the fern will stop swaying. If it stops swaying, the light will not refract correctly through its fronds. The balance will tip.

I press the shell against the stalk. *Skree-tink.* 

The sound is enormous in the muffled silence. It vibrates through the water in the basin, sending a message to the others: *The work continues. The stasis is maintained.*

As I work, I feel the moss beginning to explore the tops of my feet. It is a tickle, a soft, green suggestion of permanence. I do not brush it away. In this world, to be covered is to be accepted. 

I look at my hands. They are less translucent than they were a decade ago. The cream-color of the limestone is rising in my "bones." My movements, once fluid as a sliding drop, are becoming tectonic. I am beginning to prefer the standing to the walking. I am beginning to look at the empty basins—the ones shaped like resting bodies—with a sense of homecoming.

Who am I? 

I am the Scraper. I am the one who ensures the ferns do not turn to bone before their time. I am a curator of the humid light. I am a slow-motion ghost in a world that has forgotten the concept of a second.

Eventually, I will set the shell-scraper down on the slab. I will find a basin that fits the curve of my spine. I will stand, and then I will sit, and then I will simply *be*. I will wait for the water to finish its work on me. I will feel the emerald algae climb my chest, and I will feel my internal fluids thicken into silt. I will not die; I will simply become the architecture of the Terrace, a new thumbprint in the clay of the world, a shallow pool where a younger version of myself will one day stand to taste the water.

Until then, I scrape. I breathe the heavy, mineral air. I am the silence that follows the drip. I am the Verdigris Terrace, looking at itself through eyes that do not blink.
