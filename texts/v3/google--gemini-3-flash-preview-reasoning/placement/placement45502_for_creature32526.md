I would be a **Resonance-Setter**.

At first, I am only a sensation of weight. My consciousness does not begin in my head, but in my feet—broad, flat, and bare—pressed against the shivering basalt. I feel the mountain before I see it. It is not a silent thing to me; it is a low, subsonic roar, a billion tons of tectonic ambition pushing toward the stars.

My body is thick, built like a pillar. My chest is a bellows, slow and deliberate. I do not breathe the air; I harvest it. Each inhalation is a conscious act of filtration, my oversized lungs extracting the meager molecules of life from the bruised indigo void. My skin is the color of a storm-tossed lake, and it is damp. I am a sponge for the mountain’s breath. I stand in the path of the dawn-fog, and I feel the water beads forming on my shoulders, sliding into the microscopic grooves of my skin, keeping me supple in a world that wants to turn everything to glass.

My role is one of calibration. Because the city is dry-stone, held together by nothing but friction and the terrible, crushing embrace of gravity, it must be "tuned."

I spend my hours with my ear pressed to the masonry. I am listening for the **Stress-Hum**. When the mountain exhales, or when a new terrace three miles away adds a thousand tons of weight to the strata, the stones beneath my feet begin to sing. If the song is sharp, there is a fracture coming. If the song is a dull, rhythmic thrum, the city is healthy.

I carry a mallet of black diorite, its head polished to a mirror finish. When I find a stone that has lost its voice—a stone that has shifted out of alignment with the Gravity of the Whole—I do not move it with force. I tap. 

*Clack.*

I wait. The sound travels down through the layers of the hive, through the foundations of the dead, into the very roots of the massif. I listen for the echo. I am looking for the path of least resistance. I tap again, a different rhythm, a low-frequency pulse that vibrates in my own marrow. I am persuading the stone to settle. I am the mediator between the architecture of man and the architecture of the planet.

Around me, the city moves in its beautiful, agonizing slow-motion. I see a team of masons three hundred feet below me. They look like dark lichen clinging to the cliff. They are lifting a single block of basalt, the size of an Oru’s head, using a system of braided Iron-Root pulleys. It will take them the entire afternoon to place it. I watch them, and I hum a low, grounding note that carries through the rock to their feet, a signal that the shelf they stand on is steady. They do not look up, but their pace synchronizes with my vibration. We are one clock.

At night, when the sun vanishes and the indigo turns to an absolute, terrifying black, I do not retreat to the interior. I find a crevice near the Outer Eave and call to an Oru—a matriarch I have known for forty of her years. Her name is a specific vibration, a thrum that mimics the sound of wind through a hollow pipe. 

She comes, her six hooves clicking with practiced grace on the vertical incline. She settles beside me, a mountain of warm, matted wool that smells of minerals and ancient dampness. I sink into her side. Her body heat is a gift, a slow-burning furnace in a world of ice. I close my nictitating membranes, but I do not truly sleep. I feel her heart beat—a slow, heavy *thud-thud* that matches the internal rhythm of the mountain.

I am a Lithos-Kin, but more than that, I am a part of the masonry. I know that one day, my breath will stop, and my lungs will no longer need to fight the thin air. I will be taken to the dew-wells. I will feel the mineral water seep into my slate-dark skin, replacing my cells with salt and silica. I will become heavy. I will become silent. 

And then, they will lift me. They will carry my petrified form to the Outer Eave, and they will set me into the foundation of a new walkway. I will finally be what I have spent my life studying. I will be the stone that does not move. I will be the floor for the next generation of Resonators to walk upon.

I am the building, and I am the builder, and we are all still rising.
