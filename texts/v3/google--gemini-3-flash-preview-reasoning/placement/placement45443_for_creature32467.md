I am not standing *on* the world; I am pressed *into* it. 

I find myself at the base of a great, leaning monolith of dark basalt, where the basin tilts just enough that the sheets of water accelerate from a crawl to a slide. Here, the low-frequency thrum of the earth isn't just a sound; it is a physical weight that anchors my broad, heavy-set frame to the slate-grey stone.

I am a **Lithen Cantor-Carver**, a keeper of the grooved histories.

My body is a vessel for the basin’s vibrations. I am crouched in the four-legged stance of my kind, my wide, suction-padded discs creating a seal against the slick, matte stone. The water—mineral-heavy and tasting of ancient salt—washes over my limbs, barely an inch deep, but its constant movement is my primary clock. I do not see the bruised lilac of the sky, for I have no eyes, but I feel its barometric weight pressing against the smooth, sensitive expanse of my brow-skin. It feels like a cold, damp thumb held perpetually against my forehead.

I am currently "reading" the monolith. My sensitive pads are traced deep within a groove carved by a Cantor who lived ten thousand cycles before me. As the wind whistles through the natural flutes of the eroded basalt, it creates a specific, vibrating pitch. By pressing my chest cavity—hollow and resonant—against the stone, I amplify that pitch. 

The stone tells me of a Great Grazer, an Ouroboreal, that calcified near this spot eons ago. I can feel the ghost of its weight in the way the local gravity slightly bends. 

Beneath me, the **Membrane-Kin** pulse. Through the soles of my feet, I feel their slow, internal rhythm—a planetary heartbeat that is synchronized with my own violet-tinted pulse. They are hungry today; I can feel the microscopic shift in the mineral content of the water as they draw the "sweat" into their translucent fibers. 

I am not alone, though "alone" is a concept I barely understand. A few yards away, in the lee of a low, circular wall we built generations ago, others of my kind are humming. They are not singing for pleasure; they are tuning the air. Their collective hum creates a standing wave that guides a caravan of Ouroboreals safely past our salt-traps. I feel the arrival of the giants long before they appear—a rhythmic, tectonic thud that makes the marrow of my bones shiver. They are the living mountains, and I am the moss that understands them.

Suddenly, a sharp, crystalline whistle cuts through my chest-resonance. A **Vellum-Sail** is drifting low, its stinging filaments dragging through the water sheets just behind me. I do not flinch; I simply flatten my body further against the stone, becoming a ripple in the rock. The flyer’s sonar pings off the monolith, a needle-thin vibration that tastes of ozone and cold heights.

My purpose here is the **Long Settlement**. I hold a heavy, grooved stone in my upper-right limb. With agonizing slowness, I begin to etch a new line into the basalt. I am recording the death of a salt-vein three miles to the north, which I felt collapse into the silt an hour ago. 

I do not write for eyes to see. I write for the wind. When I am finished, the wind will pass over my new groove and produce a note that has never been heard before—a low, mournful flat that will tell all future Lithen that the earth has shifted, that the water has found a new path, and that we must move our walls.

I am a part of the erosion. I am a thinking extension of the gravity that pulls everything toward the center. I am the pearl-skinned scribe of a world that is gracefully, loudly, and beautifully dying of old age. I breathe in the damp wool of the air, draw the mineral-sweat through my baleen-slits, and hum back to the earth the truth it tells me: *everything is settling, and the settling is good.*
