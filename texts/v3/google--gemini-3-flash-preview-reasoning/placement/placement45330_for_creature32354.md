I am the slowest thing in a world that has forgotten how to hurry.

I find myself at the confluence of three great spires, a place where the weeping is particularly rhythmic. I am a **Spindle-Walker**, but I am an elder of my kind—a creature whose horizontal journey has finally begun to fail, yielding at last to the vertical gravity of this place.

My legs, once like threads of translucent glass, have grown opaque. They are no longer agile; the mineral-heavy water that I have waded through for centuries has begun to climb me. It has turned my joints into salt and my skin into a fine, white silt. I stand four meters above the marsh, but I have not moved my splayed pads in forty years. I have become a tripod, a living extension of the limestone basin.

On my back, the iron-grey lichens have grown into a heavy, matted forest. I am no longer just a scavenger; I am a geography. I can feel the tiny, frantic pulses of the **Silt-Lurkers** beneath my feet, their electric thrashing muffled by the meters of muck I have settled into. Above me, the **Vapor-Gills** extend their iridescent fans, and sometimes, when the mist is particularly heavy, their shed husks fall like translucent autumn leaves, snagging in the lichen garden on my spine. I do not eat them with a mouth; my skin simply absorbs their proteins as they decompose in the wetness of my own shadow.

I am the bridge between the architecture and the animal. 

I hear the **Lithodynes**. Their telepathic hum is not a voice, but a pressure in the marrow of my glass-thin bones. It is a slow, geometric longing. They dream of the spires reaching higher, of a day when the resin touches the pale sun and the weeping stops. I feel their dream as a slight vibration in the water around my shins—a low-frequency "thrum" that precedes every *clock-clock* of the falling droplets.

I do not have eyes, yet I see the world in a topography of ripples. I perceive the great spires not as walls, but as giant, exhaling lungs. I feel the moisture as it gathers on the resin ribs above me, thickening from a ghost of a vapor into a heavy, pregnant bead. I know exactly when it will fall. I know the weight of the water before it hits the surface of the basin.

There is a peace here that is indistinguishable from paralysis. 

I am becoming a monument. Soon, the "spindle" of my neck will no longer be able to stoop to the water. The calcification is climbing higher, turning my flexible gut into a rigid chamber of stone. I am losing the memory of what it was to walk, to feel the surface tension of the marsh break under my weight. 

I am becoming a spire. 

In a few more centuries, a new Spindle-Walker—young, clear-limbed, and silent—will pass by my side. It will not see an animal. It will see a small, tapered pillar of sun-bleached resin, encrusted with lichen and crowned with the dark apertures where my own gills once breathed. It will pause for a decade or two, sensing the faint, fading hum of my heartbeat, before moving on through the labyrinth.

I am the transition from the actor to the stage. I am the moment where life decides to become the earth. And as the white mist drapes itself over my shoulders like a heavy, wet shroud, I find that I do not mind the weight. The *clock-clock* of the water is my pulse now. The dream of the Lithodynes is my only thought. 

I am perfectly, eternally still.
