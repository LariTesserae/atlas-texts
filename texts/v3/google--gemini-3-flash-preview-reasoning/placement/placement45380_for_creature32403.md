I do not have a name that can be spoken, for the air here is too heavy for the frivolity of vowels. If you were to find me, you would first hear me: not as a voice, but as a rhythmic, percussive stutter against the basalt. *Tap-tap... slide. Tap-tap... slide.*

I am a **Scraper of the Mid-Ribs**, one of the Marrow-Kin.

I find myself clinging to a convex swell of basalt roughly seven hundred meters above the Matriarchal Substrate. Here, the "cloud-drip" is thickest—a slow, vertical river that tastes of cold iron and ancient rain. I am pressed so flat against the stone that the sulfurous heat of the vents warms my belly while the moisture of the sky cools my back. I am a sandwich of two worlds, belonging to neither.

My fingers—long, spatulate, and tipped with hooks of keratin—are currently buried in a fresh deposit of Wax-Drift. It is soft today, the consistency of warm suet, smelling of minerals and the musk of a billion moths.

I am not young. The oil on my skin, once a shimmering translucent grey, has begun to thicken, taking on the faint, pearlescent sheen of the moths I displace. This is the way of things. We begin as water and end as wax.

### The Rhythm of the Scrape

I hold the obsidian wedge—a heavy, cold weight lashed to the bone of a Suture-Bird. The bone is hollow, and as I drag the blade through the wax, the handle whistles a low, mournful note that only I can feel through the vibrations in my palm.

The Chitin-Moths do not move for me. They are a carpet of living shingles. To harvest, I must gently slide my hand beneath their calcified bellies. They feel like warm coins, vibrating with a frantic, internal clock. They crawl over my knuckles, their feelers tasting the salt of my skin. I do not mind. We are coworkers in this vertical factory. I scrape the excess; they secrete the new. If I did not scrape, the wax would eventually seal the fissure shut, suffocating the vents, and we would all freeze in the ice of the outer peaks.

I am a gardener of the dark.

### The Language of the Bones

A vibration shudders through my left foot, which is braced against a natural notch in the basalt. 

*Thrum-thrum. Pause. Thrum.*

It is my daughter, three hundred meters above me, near the ghost-grass ledges. The vibration tells me the cloud-snag is heavy today. The moisture will be high. She is warning me to secure my tools; the "weeping" of the walls will be more like a sob within the hour, and the slickness will become treacherous.

I respond by striking my obsidian blade against a dry patch of stone. *Clack. Clack-clack.*

*I hear. I am braced.*

We do not miss the sun. To us, the sun is a blinding predator that visits once a day to paralyze us. We live for the indigo pulse—the soft, deep-sea glow of the larvae below. It is a heartbeat. When I close my eyes, I don't see darkness; I see the rhythm of the mountain.

### The Eleven Minutes

The air begins to thin. The grinding hum of the moths shifts in pitch, rising from a bass growl to a frantic, harmonic whine. They feel it before I do. 

The light is coming.

I find my "niche"—a shallow depression in the basalt where the rock curves inward, creating a small overhang. I pull my knees to my chest, my double-hinged joints folding with a wet, soft click. I tuck my head into the crook of my elbow and press my forehead against the stone. 

Then, the **Gold** falls.

It is a violent, searing intrusion. Even through my closed eyelids, the world turns a terrifying, bloody red. The heat is sudden—a dry, parching sting that threatens to crack my oily skin. The moths are screaming now, their wings vibrating so fast the sound becomes a single, solid wall of noise that makes my teeth ache.

I hold my breath. I count the pulses of the mountain. 

*One hundred. Two hundred.* 

I feel the ultraviolet weight trying to peel me from the stone. This is the only time I feel small. Not because of the height—height is merely the distance between breaths—but because of the vast, indifferent emptiness above the Ribs. 

Then, as quickly as it arrived, the weight lifts. The bruised twilight returns. The air turns humid and kind again.

### The Slow Descent

I look at my hands. They are stained with the red lichen and the yellow wax. I am tired. The obsidian tool feels heavier than it did a century ago. 

I look down, past the shifting carpet of moths, toward the indigo depths where the Matriarchal Substrate glows like a fallen star. I can feel the warmth rising from it, a beckoning hum. One day soon, I will not strike the stone to signal my daughter. I will simply let go of the scraper. I will let it fall into the dark, a final gift of obsidian to the floor below.

I will descend, sliding through the film of water and moth-silk, until I reach the bottom. I will lay my thinning body upon the glowing fungal mats. I will feel the moths settle upon me, their tiny, hooked feet tickling my skin for the last time. 

I will become a bulbous formation. I will become a new handhold for those who come after. I will become the very basalt I have spent my life peeling.

But for now, there is more wax to harvest. 

I reach out, slide my fingers under a vibrating wing-case, and begin to scrape. 

*Tap-tap... slide.*

The mountain breathes. And I breathe with it.
