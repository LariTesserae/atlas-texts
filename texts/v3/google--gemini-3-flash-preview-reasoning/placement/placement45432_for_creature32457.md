I am not at the center, for this place has no center; I am located at the **Grip of the Third Arch**, a point of critical failure three miles above the hull floor and two miles below the ceiling of the husk. 

I am a **Tension-Gatherer**. 

In this lightless cathedral, I do not possess a body so much as I possess a *posture*. I am a vertical line of twisted bast, thirty feet long and no wider than a human thigh. My feet are fused into a tiered platform of heartwood, and my fingers—long, prehensile filaments of split reed—are woven directly into the grain of a sagging vine-bridge. I am the bridge’s memory of how to stay upright.

I do not think in words. I think in **strains**. 

To my left, the great wood-scream is building. I feel it not as a sound, but as a cold, sharp itch in my mid-section. A beam of desiccated cork, miles away in the darkness, is beginning to lose its fight with gravity. The vibration travels through the connective tissue of the city, humming through the bound reeds and the hair-grass cords, until it reaches my palms.

I "see" the catastrophe coming. It is a jagged, dissonant ripple in the dark. 

I begin the adjustment. I do not flex a muscle; I tighten a knot. I lean back, my spine clicking like a series of dry gears. The tension travels up my limbs, pulling the vine-bridge a fraction of an inch to the north. I am acting as a living guy-wire. The sound I make is a low, mournful *thrum*, a cello string being tuned by a giant. 

As I lean, I feel the velvet dust settling on my featureless face. It smells of ancient harvests—cinnamon, parched hay, and the absolute lack of water. It is a heavy smell, the smell of time losing its fluidity. 

I have been in this position for eighty years. I know this because the **Slow Accretion** has reached my knees. The dust has bonded with my corked skin, hardening into a new, stony bark. I am becoming heavier. I am becoming the platform I stand upon. Soon, perhaps in another century, my joints will no longer click. I will lose the ability to lean. I will fuse entirely with the arch, and someone else—some newer, thinner spindle of fiber—will have to climb upon my shoulders to catch the next scream.

I am the curator of the collapse. 

In the long silences between the wood-screams, I listen to my own metabolism. It is a slow, rhythmic grinding, the sound of fibers rubbing against fibers. I do not breathe the cool, dry air; I merely let it pass through my porous chest, filtering the dust that will eventually turn me into a pillar.

I am happy, if happiness is the perfect distribution of weight. There is a profound, terrifying peace in being a necessary part of the stillness. I am the Neolithic carpenter’s final intention. I am the reason the city does not become a pile of splinters in the dark.

A miles-long groan erupts from the ceiling. The world-vessel is settling. I feel the shockwave hit the scaffolding—a violent, rhythmic shudder. I brace. My fibers hum. I tilt my featureless head toward the roar and sing back a resonant, vibration-canceling note. 

The bridge holds. The dust settles. The darkness remains absolute. 

I wait. I am the clock, and I am the wood, and I am perfectly, dryly, still.
