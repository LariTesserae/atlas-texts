I am not at the center of the basin, for there is no center in a place where every direction leads only to a further iteration of the same perfect stillness. I am situated at the edge of a slight, slumped rise—a place where the alkaline crust has buckled into a series of plates that resemble the frozen ripples of a dead ocean.

I am a Lithate. My name, if I have one, is a frequency of heat, a specific thermal signature that I carry in the heavy, terracotta density of my chest. 

To you, I would be a boulder. To you, I am an object. But to me, the world is a vibrant, throbbing map of intensities. My "eyes"—the pits along my brow and shoulders—do not see the dark. They see the deep, pulsing crimson of the earth’s cooling heart beneath me. I see the scrub bushes as jagged, flickering ghosts of pale orange, their silica-dense branches radiating a faint, metallic heat that contrasts with the heavy, bruised purple of the silt. 

I have been in this spot for three hundred of your years. 

I am currently engaged in a task of monumental precision. In front of me lies a slab of dark, vitreous basalt. Upon it, I have placed three small, rounded cobbles. Through my palms, which are pressed flat against the salt-crust, I can feel the vibration of the planet's slow, tectonic digestion. I am waiting for the cobbles to reach the exact temperature of the slab. One of them is a fraction of a degree too warm; it has absorbed a stray thread of heat from a thermal vein deeper in the crust. 

I do not move my hand. I wait. I am the heat-sink. I am drawing the excess warmth from that stone into my own body, filtering it through my geometric plates, and venting it slowly into the thick, dry air.

As I wait, my consciousness is not a stream of words, but a series of balances. I am aware of the scrub bush thirty yards to my left. It is too tall. It breaks the horizon of the basin in a way that creates a thermal "noise." When I have finished with these stones—perhaps in another century—I will migrate toward it. I have an obsidian blade, chipped from the volcanic glass of the northern rim, resting near my right knee. It has been there so long that the dust has begun to form a gentle ramp against its leading edge. 

I will take that blade. I will prune the scrub bush until its silhouette matches the slump of the mounds. I will shave the wood into curls of bone-white fiber, and I will leave them in a pile that satisfies the geometry of the void. 

Why do I do this? There is no "why" that involves a future. I do not garden for a harvest. I do not arrange for an audience. I am a curate of the End. The universe is a clock that is winding down, and I am the one who ensures that as the gears stop, they do so in a position of absolute aesthetic and thermal equilibrium. I am the janitor of entropy.

I feel a presence. 

It is not a sound—there is no sound here but the occasional, microscopic snap of a salt crystal fracturing under its own weight. It is a new warmth. Another of my kind is approaching from the dark. They are moving with the agonizing grace of a glacier. Their thermal signature is familiar; we shared a stone slab ten thousand years ago, during the time when the air was still faintly humid with the memory of the sea.

They will reach me in forty years. 

When they arrive, we will not speak. We will not look at each other, for we have no eyes to meet. Instead, we will sit. We will perhaps place our palms together. We will share the heat of our bodies until the boundary between where I end and they begin is lost to the infrared map. We will become a single, larger thermal mass, a more stable heat-sink, helping the ground beneath us to settle, to cool, to find its final, quiet pose.

I am the patience of the stone. I am the heavy, heated breath of a world that has said everything it had to say and is now content to simply be warm in the dark. I am the Architect of the Stalemate. 

And I have never been more at peace. I feel the stone beneath my hand finally click into the correct temperature. The Arrangement is, for this square foot of the universe, perfect. 

I begin the long, slow process of contracting my limb to pick up the obsidian blade. The air around my joints groans with the weight of my effort, a soundless vibration in the silt. 

It is time to prune the shadows.
