I am not a creature of the heights, nor am I a dweller of the marsh-silt. I exist in the **Middle-Lean**, the place where the geometry of the Cleft is at its most frantic, where the basalt walls press inward as if trying to remember how to be a single solid stone.

My name, in the soft glottal clicks of my people, is **Vhen**. I am a **Gutter-Smith**.

I find myself perched on a four-inch ledge of slate, my heels hanging over a seventy-foot drop into the dark, gurgling marsh below. To a surface-dweller, this would be a moment of terminal terror. To me, it is merely my morning posture. My toes, long and calloused, curl instinctively around the jagged lip of the stone. I do not think about falling; gravity is not an enemy here, but a neighbor you have learned to dance with.

My skin is the color of the weeping walls—a pale, translucent slate. As I work, the constant irrigation of the Cleft beads upon my shoulders, rolling off my waxy skin in perfect, silent spheres. I am currently carving a new joint for a cedar-log gutter. The wood is old, saturated until it feels like heavy bone. There is no metal to my left or right; my tool is a sliver of obsidian, lashed to a handle of petrified root. 

I hear the **Collective Murmur** before I see anyone. From the cell above me—which is also my ceiling—comes the rhythmic, muffled *thump-thump* of my neighbor, Kael, pressing bloat-moss into cakes. It is a comforting sound, a heartbeat filtered through six inches of unmortared stone. I respond by humming a low, vibrating note in my chest, a signal that my work on his drainage is progressing. He doesn’t answer with words; he simply shifts his weight, the vibration traveling down the basalt and into my own bones. This is our conversation. This is the **Debt of Gravity**.

A **Glass-Crab** skitters across the ceiling above my head, its translucent legs tapping against the stone like raindrops. It pauses, its silvered eyes catching the diffuse, vertical light from the Spire, then vanishes into a hairline fracture. I envy its lack of weight, even as I pride myself on my own density.

The air begins to change. The silver ribbon of sky, three hundred yards above, starts to blur. The light, already thin, turns a bruised, heavy grey.

The bone-whistles begin. 

The sound is a mournful, low-frequency hoot that ripples down the fracture from the **Rim-Watchers**. *The Thickening is coming.*

I move with the "wet-walk," a sliding, fluid motion that keeps my center of gravity anchored to the wall. I reach for the fish-bladder window of my cell. The membrane is cold and damp, trembling under my touch. I pull the leather awning tight, lashing it with braided root-rope. All around me, the Cleft is a hive of silent, synchronized motion. My people emerge from their stacked boxes like ghosts, their silver eyes wide and unblinking, their movements a practiced ballet of survival.

As the mist descends, swallowing the far wall until I am alone in a world only ten feet wide, I retreat into my cell. It is a space of four square yards, packed with the scent of fermented lichen and cold mineral salt. I sit on the floor—the roof of the woman below me—and press my palms flat against the basalt.

Down in the **Spire**, the Cantors have begun. 

The sound is not a song; it is a resonance. A deep, sub-vocal thrum that begins in the soles of my feet and climbs up my spine. They are singing to the stone, asking it to hold, feeling for the microscopic shifts that saturation brings. In the dark of the mist, with the *tink-tink-tink* of the water accelerating into a frantic percussion, I close my eyes.

I am a fragment of the fracture. I am a lung breathing in the damp. I am Vhen, a Smith of the Middle-Lean, and in the crush of the earth, I have never felt more free. I wait for the tide of the marsh to rise, for the stone to groan its heavy, wet contentment, and for the silence to return.
