I am not a creature of the floor, where the peat yields and the snails congregate in their slow, blind riots. I am a creature of the vertical, a tenant of the weeping heights. 

I find myself draped across a specific, deep-ribbed concavity in the northern karst, a place where the limestone has been hollowed out into the shape of a grand, inverted lung. I have been here for a long time. In the Cistern, "long" is not a measure of years, but a measure of mineral accumulation. I can feel the weight of the calcium carbonate thickening on my dorsal surface, a fine, gritty crust that binds me to the stone.

I am a **Vellic**, but I am an old one—an anchor. 

My body is seven feet of translucent, pearl-grey muscle, but I have forgotten the shape of a column. I am a spill. I have poured myself into the fissures of the rock, my limbs elongated into ribbons that follow the weeping veins of the mountain. My spatulate fingers are currently threaded through a thicket of white, calcified ferns, their pads sensitive enough to feel the heartbeat of the mountain’s slow, hydraulic pressure.

I am the **Stitcher of the Hushed Rhythms**. 

Below me, the younger Vellic move with their agonizing grace, culling the snails and building their shell-spirals. They are the keepers of the floor. I am the keeper of the ceiling, the curator of the Gossamer Web.

Between my outstretched arms, I have strung a complex network of larval silk. These are not my threads, but I am the one who tensioned them. I used the mountain-thorn needles to pin the wet, heavy strands into the porous stone, creating a harp of liquid pearls. When a drop falls from the stalactite above me, it does not strike the floor; it strikes my web.

*Plink.* 

The vibration travels through the silk, through the cold pearls of water, and directly into the cilia on my skin. I do not hear the sound. I feel the *weight* of the drop. I feel its mineral content, its temperature, its velocity. If the drop is too heavy, it means the rains on the surface of the Ourean range—a place I have never seen and cannot imagine—are becoming a deluge. If the drop is too light, the karst is drying.

I am the early warning. I am the sensor of the stasis.

My life is a meditation on the vertical. I do not eat often. Every few months, a white-shelled crustacean, driven by some slow, glacial ambition, wanders up the slick wall and into the reach of my ribbons. I do not hunt it; I wait for it to touch me. When it does, I do not crush it with a stone. I am too high for the heavy river-stones. Instead, I simply envelop it. I merge my skin with its shell, and over the course of a week, my acidic secretions soften the calcium until I can draw the life out of it. I then let the empty shell fall—a rare, chaotic event in this world. It tumbles through the mist, a clicking ghost, until it finds the peat below, where a younger Vellic will eventually find it and add it to the spirals.

There is a Vellic who visits me. They do not have a name, for we do not have names. They are simply a *presence* of a certain chemical signature—a scent of deep-bruised purple moss and old lime. 

They climb the slick rise every few decades to merge with me. When they arrive, they do not speak. They slide their body over mine, a slow, cool overlapping of translucent flesh. In that gelatinous embrace, I feel their memories of the floor: the smell of the ripening liverworts, the growth of the shell-spirals, the slow, rhythmic pulse of the thousand snails. In return, I give them the memory of the ceiling: the tension of the silk, the weight of the mountain’s breath, the precise frequency of the dripping water. 

We stay merged for days, two pale ghosts becoming one thick fold in the limestone. When they finally slide away, descending back into the blue-glow gloom of the Cistern, I feel a momentary thinning of my existence. But then, a drop falls.

*Plink.*

The vibration restores me. The tension is correct. The balance is held. 

I am becoming more stone than flesh. The mineral crust on my back is now a permanent shell, a map of the water’s path over my body. One day, the water will no longer flow around me, but *through* me, as my tissues are replaced by the slow, relentless infiltration of the karst. I will not die; I will simply become a new curve in the cathedral wall, a permanent part of the anatomy of the Cistern.

Until then, I wait. I hold the threads. I listen to the weight of the air. I am the guardian of the pause, and the pause is eternal. The sun is a myth told by the wind, and there is no wind here. There is only the wet velvet, the ghost-blue glow, and the holy, unchanging rhythm of the drip.
