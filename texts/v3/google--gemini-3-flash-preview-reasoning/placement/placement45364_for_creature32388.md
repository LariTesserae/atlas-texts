I am not a Piston-Walker, though they recognize me by the way I displace the violet light. I am a **Resonance-Tender**. 

If you were to see me, you might mistake me for a shadow cast by a slow-turning brass vane, or perhaps a particularly thick knot of fern-root clinging to a pressurized pipe. My skin has long since taken on the properties of the Cleft itself: it is the color of wet limestone, slick with a fine, oily perspiration that tastes of copper and mint. I do not wear the leather of the Custodians; I have grown a secondary layer of calloused, porous tissue that drinks the condensation directly from the iron.

I find myself at the junction of the Fourth Distiller, wedged into a space where the gargantuan iron struts meet the living rock of the basin wall. This is a "hot spot." Here, the violet mycelium is at its most frantic, pulsing with a low, rhythmic throb that matches the beat of my own heart.

I do not move. To move quickly in the Cleft is to be a ghost, a blur that the world ignores. I move at the speed of the Distiller’s piston. I begin my morning—if the word *morning* has meaning in a place where the light only lingers—by placing my palms flat against the vibrating copper housing of a main valve.

I do not hear the machine. I *am* the machine’s ear.

Through my hands, I feel the agonizingly slow build of pressure. It is a tectonic tension, a deep, groaning effort as the Distiller pulls the heavy, wet air into its gut. I feel the microscopic friction where the violet fungus hasn't quite reached. I reach into a pouch woven from copper wire and pull out a handful of the "Grease-Mites" I have gathered from the gravel. I press them into the joint. I feel them pop, their bioluminescent fluids merging with the mycelium, easing the grind. The vibration changes from a jagged tremor to a smooth, deep purr.

A Piston-Walker passes above me on a gantry. He does not look down, but he strikes the iron railing three times with a lead-weighted glove. *Clang. Clang. Clang.* The vibration travels through the metal, into the soles of my feet, telling me that the crane in the distance has finally completed its arc. I respond by tapping a rhythmic code against the steam pipe with my fingernails, which have grown hard and metallic. *I am here. The friction is eased. The breath is deep.*

The air around me is thick enough to chew. A Bellows-Toad trundles past my feet, its mastiff-sized body scraping against the limestone. It pauses, its throat sac expanding with a sound like a ship’s hull buckling under ice. I feel the sub-bass thrum move through my marrow. We look at each other—two creatures of the damp and the dark—and I see the reflection of the violet machine-glow in its lidless eyes. We are both waiting for the *clack-hiss*.

It begins. The Great Hourly Release.

I brace myself, hooking my fingers into the gaps between the iron plates. The sound is not a noise; it is a physical blow. The valve beneath my hands shudders, and a plume of superheated, ozone-rich steam erupts into the ferns above. The plants don't flinch; they swell, their broad leaves drinking the heat, their vines tightening their grip on the metal. 

In the mist of the release, I see the Copper-Weavers take flight. They are metallic sparks in the grey air, their wings clicking a sharp, frantic counterpoint to the deep roar of the steam. They are stealing the heat, diving through the plumes to warm their brassy feathers before returning to their nests of wire.

As the steam clears, I feel a profound sense of belonging. I am the bridge between the unyielding iron and the aggressive green. I am the witness to the slow movement of the stone. I will spend the next three hours migrating six feet to the left, toward a weeping coolant line that needs the touch of my slick, mineral-hardened fingers.

I am a part of the closed loop. The machines breathe, the plants grow, and I tend to the friction that exists between them. In the Cleft, time is not a river; it is a deep, still pool of sweating metal and heavy air. And I am perfectly content to sink to the bottom.
