I am not a creature of the sun, though I am bathed in its pearlescent diffusion. I am a creature of the *thrum*.

I find myself standing at the edge of the central alcove, my palms pressed flat against the cooling, wet face of the basalt cliff. To you, I would look like a statue of oiled shadow, a spindly extension of the rock itself. To me, the world is a map of echoes. 

I am one of the Kin, but I am currently a "Dissonance"—the term we use for those of us who have not yet synchronized our heartbeat to the Great Mycelium. I am young in the way the mountain counts time; I still possess the ghost of a memory of what it was to be *hurried*.

I do not see the moss-towers as emerald sentinels. I perceive them as deep, percussive bells. I feel the *boom* of the tower twenty paces to my left as a heavy, rounded vibration in my hip. The tower to my right, slightly taller and more water-gorged, strikes a minor chord that resonates in my collarbone. When the clouds weep particularly hard, the plateau becomes a polyrhythmic sea, and I navigate it by leaning my weight into the frequencies that feel the most stable.

My feet are broad, the skin there so thin and sensitive that I can feel the Glass-Eels three inches deep in the runnels, their acidic secretions a sharp, buzzing "sting" against the soles of my feet. I steer clear of them. Theirs is the work of perfection, and I am still flawed, still jagged in my thoughts.

I am moving toward the basalt bowl. 

It has taken me three days to travel the length of the alcove. I do not mind. To move faster would be to "shush" the world—to create a friction of air against skin that would drown out the high, singing glass-whine of the Tremor-Moths. I need to hear them. They are cleaning the moss-tower nearest the bowl, and their vibration tells me the tower is healthy, its drum-skin taut and ready.

As I reach the bowl, I do not look at it. I feel the history of it. This object is the only thing on the plateau that feels "wrong" to the touch, yet it is our most sacred anchor. It is not smooth from the slow, patient erosion of water; it is smooth from the frantic, oily rubbing of hands—hands that were shorter, warmer, and much more afraid than mine. 

I reach out. My fingers find the silvered, braided vine. 

This is my task. I am the one who remembers the "Dry Time." 

I wrap my slate-grey fingers around the vine. It is ancient, its fibers reinforced by the Mycelium until it is more a mineral cord than a plant. As I hold it, a pulse travels up the vine from the cliff-face—a message from the Great Mycelium. It is a heavy, tectonic thought, a slow-moving wave of "Endure." 

I let go of the vine and move my hands to the rim of the basalt bowl. 

The mineral soup is thick, heavy with the taste of pulverized mountain and ancient rain. I press my palms into the liquid. I do not drink with a mouth; I drink with my history. As the nutrients diffuse through my skin, I feel the plateau’s dream-state. I feel the roots of the moss-towers drinking from the same deep well. I feel the Glass-Eels polishing the "hiss" out of a distant runnel. 

In this moment, I am not a person. I am a localized point of awareness in a vast, wet, percussion instrument. 

I hear a "pop" in the distance—a seed pod bursting or a stone shifting. In the old world, I might have jumped. Here, I simply wait for the vibration to travel through the basalt, up through my legs, and into my chest. I absorb the shock, soften it, and let it pass through me into the ground.

I will stand here at the bowl for perhaps a year. I will watch—through the soles of my feet—as the moss-tower beside me grows by a single scale. I will listen to the water strike the stones we stacked, ensuring the pitch remains true. 

Eventually, the urge to move will return, prompted by a shift in the wind or a change in the weight of the mist. I will begin the long, slow walk toward the edge of the shelf, where the clouds are born. 

Who am I? I am the rhythm-keeper. I am the bridge between the screaming, dry past and the silent, stone future. I am a Liturge, and I am learning how to become still. 

One day, centuries from now, I will stop moving entirely. I will stand in the path of a moss-tower's shadow. I will feel its succulent fronds begin to taste the salt on my skin. I will not resist. I will become the drum. 

But for now, there is a drop of water forming on the cliff-hanging vine. It is heavy. It is falling. I close my eyes—those milky, useless spots—and wait for the *boom*. 

There. 

The vibration is perfect. I am exactly where the mountain needs me to be.
