The air in the High Garden of Irongate is thin and perpetually cool, a constant seep of damp that clings to the skin and makes the rust bloom faster. You stand on a wide avenue paved not with stone, but with a dark, compacted soil that has the texture of petrified cork. It gives slightly underfoot, a resilience that is deeply unsettling in a place that feels so solid.

The city of Irongate was built here, millennia ago, on a high, windswept plateau. But the city is gone. Its inhabitants, its purpose, its very reason for being are dust. What remains is its ghost, overwritten by a singular, all-consuming verdant life. The Great Banyan, the city-eater, the world-tree, has taken everything.

Its trunks are the new architecture. They are immense, columns of living wood the color of burnt umber, wider than old-growth redwoods, soaring hundreds of feet into the sky before exploding into a canopy so dense it forms a second, rustling ceiling. But these are not mere trees. If you press your ear to the bark, you hear a faint, grinding hum—the tick of sap-clocks, the whir of unseen mechanisms deep within the xylem. The Banyan is industrial. Its roots are its plumbing, its factories, its power grid.

The great, pale roots do not follow the old streets; they have become them. They snake across the plateau in broad, flat highways, their surfaces worn smooth by unimaginable time and the passage of… nothing. Nothing has walked here in a long age. Some roots have grown over and around the skeletal frames of old foundries, the rust-pocked iron girders now serving as trellises. In other places, the roots have fused with the stonework of collapsed towers, creating hybrid structures of wood and granite that lean against each other like tired old men.

Water is a ghost here, too. There are no rivers, no lakes. Moisture condenses in the high, cool air, gathering on the undersides of vast, leathery leaves the size of tabletops. It drips, a slow, rhythmic percussion that is the city’s only predictable sound, tracing damp paths down the trunks and pooling in the hollows of the root-highways. Sometimes, these pools are deep and clear, reflecting the diffuse, grey light that filters through the canopy. Other times, they shimmer with an oily, iridescent film, a byproduct of the Banyan’s internal industry.

The soundscape is dominated by two things: the wind, a high, mournful whistle that plays through the gaps in the canopy, and a low, persistent roar. It is not the sound of a machine, but of a thousand machines working in unison. It is the sound of photosynthesis at an industrial scale, the grind of a million leaf-plates shifting to track the sun, the deep, resonant thrum of the Banyan’s heartwood pumping nutrients up to the sky. It is loud, a presence in itself, a pressure against the eardrums that never quite ceases.

Fauna is a myth. A few pale, long-legged insects with bodies like polished obsidian pick their way across the bark, their movements slow and deliberate. They seem more like part of the Banyan’s maintenance crew than living creatures. Birds do not sing here. The ecosystem is closed, a perfect, self-sustaining loop of plant and machine.

And the air feels… thin. Not just in terms of oxygen, but in substance. The world here feels stretched. If you stare at a particular junction where three roots intertwine, the pattern might seem to repeat itself a few feet away, or a shimmering afterimage of it might hang in the air for a second too long. The Banyan has been here so long, has become so deeply integrated with the plateau, that it has started to fray the edges of reality. The space between its roots feels deeper than it should. Shadows move with a slight, lagging delay. It is a world settling into its final, vegetative form, and in its extreme age, the rules have grown soft and permeable. The roar of the Banyan is the sound of reality being slowly, inexorably digested.
