# The Roaring Abyssal

## The World as Water

There is no horizon here — only the place where the grey-blue surface meets the grey-blue sky, indistinguishable, a smudge of light in all directions. This is a world that is almost entirely ocean: a planet-spanning sea that fills a continental basin perched at extraordinary elevation on a larger world, as though some ancient cataclysm tilted the crust and poured the hydrosphere into one high plateau. The thin atmosphere above is clear and cold. The water below is miles deep, mineral-poor, and so transparent that sunlight — the ordinary, undramatic light of a distant star — penetrates hundreds of meters before losing its grip. From below, looking up, the surface is a pale membrane, rippling faintly, a ceiling of diffused glow that reaches down into the steel-blue column like a cathedral of optical depth. Below that glow: darkness, but slowly arrived at. Not the instant blackness of a murky sea. A long, patient fade.

The water is cold. Not frozen — the world's interior is still warm enough, and the currents keep the liquid moving — but relentlessly, pervasively cold. A few degrees above freezing at the surface. Colder still in the abyssal trenches. The kind of cold that doesn't shock but infiltrates, that makes its home in your core and never leaves.

The ocean floor is young. That's the first thing a geologist would notice, if one could be lowered to the bottom: everything is sharp. The ridges are jagged, unsmoothed, studded with fresh volcanic glass — obsidian spines and pillow lavas still bearing the rope-textures of their cooling. The seamounts rise in steep, angular profiles, their flanks unsoftened by the millennia of sediment that would have rounded them on an older world. Hydrothermal vents cluster along the mid-basin ridges, exhaling mineral-laden plumes that briefly shimmer with heat before the surrounding cold swallows them. New crust is being born here — slowly, by the standards of a human lifetime, but rapidly by geological measure. The world's mantle is restless beneath this high ocean, pushing new rock upward, splitting old rock apart, building fresh sharp terrain even as everything else on the surface remains almost perfectly still.

## The Factories

They are there if you know where to look. From the surface, nothing — only the same endless water, the same grey light, the same faint chop. But descend, follow the cables.

The cables come first. Thick as a person's torso, barnacle-crusted, sheathed in layers of mineral deposit and biological grime, they run along the seafloor in long straining lines that stretch for hundreds of kilometers between anchor points. They connect the scattered installations like the nervous system of some enormous creature that gave up on having a body. Follow them and you begin to encounter structures.

The factories sit on the seamount ridges, anchored into fresh basalt with massive pylons. Each one is the size of a small city — not a metropolis, but a purpose-built industrial town, a place where things are made or extracted or processed. They are built from steel and concrete and composite alloys, heavy-walled to withstand the pressure, studded with intake valves and exhaust ports and the massive housings of turbines. The turbines are the heart of each installation: enormous machines that draw power from the thermal vents, converting the planet's internal heat into electricity, into mechanical force, into the energy that drives whatever production this world exists to perform. What they produce, exactly, is not obvious from the outside. The structures are sealed, monolithic, functional. No ornamentation. No signage. Pure industrial architecture — every angle a load-bearing angle, every surface a working surface.

Some of the installations show signs of long habitation: docking cradles for submersible vehicles, airlock-style chambers, the occasional dome or tower that might serve as a control center. But most of these are dark, their vehicles absent or docked in standby positions that haven't changed in years. The factories run without visible operators. They are automated, or remotely controlled, or — perhaps — simply running on their own, following the last set of instructions they received from whoever built them and left. Scattered between the major installations are smaller artifacts: survey markers, abandoned sensor arrays, the partial remains of earlier structures that were never completed or that failed and were left in place. One seamount bears the corroded skeleton of what might have been a crane, its arm still extended over a chasm, as if reaching for something it never grasped.

The construction is industrial-level — not primitive, not futuristic. Riveted steel. Electrified systems. Mechanized in the way of early twentieth-century heavy industry, but built to endure conditions that no terrestrial factory has ever faced. Whoever designed these things understood pressure, understood cold, understood the corrosive patience of saltwater. They over-engineered. They built to last. And they have lasted — sitting here on the seafloor, running, processing, producing something no one is present to collect.

## The Sound

It is the first thing and the last thing. It is everything between.

The sound begins as a vibration in the water, a pressure wave that enters the body before the ears register it. The factories are loud — their turbines, their pumps, their pressurization systems and exhaust cycles produce a constant industrial roar that saturates the water column for hundreds of kilometers in every direction. But the factories alone don't account for the totality of it. The ocean itself contributes: the groaning of tectonic plates, the crackling of fresh-formed rock as it cools, the bass-frequency boom of hydrothermal vents releasing gas. And the water, being water, carries all of it, transmits it with terrifying efficiency across distances that would silence it in air. Sound doesn't diminish here the way it should. It refracts, reflects off thermoclines, channels through deep sound channels, bounces between the seafloor and the surface and back again. The result is a permanent, deafening, layered roar — a world-spanning noise that never drops below a grinding hum and often rises to a physical force, a thing you feel in your chest and your sinuses and the fluid of your inner ear.

Close to a factory, the sound is almost unbearable for a surface-dwelling organism. It is not a single noise but a compound of thousands: the whine of turbines, the clang of mechanical arms, the hiss of pressurized vents, the deep throb of water being displaced by enormous pistons. You don't hear it so much as become part of it — your body resonates, your thoughts scatter and reform, you learn to think in the gaps between frequencies. Far from any installation, on some empty abyssal plain a thousand kilometers from the nearest ridge, the sound is still there: a low, omnipresent pressure, a bass note so deep it's more felt than heard, the ghost-vibration of a world that is, acoustically, never silent. Never even quiet. The quietest moment in this ocean is louder than the noisiest moment in most.

The fauna has adapted. Of course it has. Life here communicates — if it communicates at all — in frequency bands above or below the industrial roar, in ultrasonic clicks that ride above the noise floor or infrasonic pulses that thread between the machinery's harmonics. Some creatures have simply given up on sound as a means of contact and rely entirely on chemical signaling, touch, or the detection of pressure waves too subtle to register as noise. The world's acoustic landscape is, paradoxically, both the loudest place imaginable and a place where silence has been reinvented in pockets and niches, hidden frequencies, whispered conversations that the thundering machinery cannot hear.

## The Inhabitants

There are no plants. Almost none. The cold, the depth, the relentless acoustic violence — these are not conditions that favor photosynthesis. What vegetation exists is microscopic: scattered phytoplankton in the upper water column, cyanobacterial films on a few sunlit rock faces near the surface, barely enough to register. The base of the food chain here is not photosynthetic but chemosynthetic — microbial mats that feed on the mineral-rich outflows from hydrothermal vents, converting sulfur compounds and dissolved metals into organic matter. These mats are the foundation. Everything else builds on them.

The fauna is moderately abundant — not teeming, not barren. It is distributed unevenly, concentrated around the vents and the factories (which provide their own thermal effluent and, it seems, their own nutrient outputs) and sparse everywhere else. Near the installations, the ecosystem is richer. Large filter-feeders — creatures resembling manta rays but armored, their wing-spans reaching ten or fifteen meters — cruise the current corridors, straining microbial clouds from the water with feathery baleen-like structures. They move slowly, deliberately, their bodies adapted to the cold with thick layers of insulating gel. Around the vents, armored grazers cling to the rock faces, scraping microbial mats with radula-like mouths, their shells encrusted with mineral deposits that make them nearly indistinguishable from the basalt they feed on. Predators patrol the middle water: eel-like, elongated, bioluminescent along their flanks — not brightly, but in dim, shifting patterns of blue and green that might be communication, might be lures, might be something with no terrestrial analogue. They hunt in the acoustic shadow of the factories, using the noise as cover, striking at prey that has been driven half-deaf by proximity to the machinery.

In the open water, far from any structure or vent, life thins to almost nothing — but not quite nothing. There are clouds of tiny organisms, krill-like, drifting in slow gyres. There are jellyfish-analogues, translucent bells pulsing gently, trailing tentacles for meters behind them. There are microbial communities suspended in the water column, invisible to the naked eye but collectively forming a faint haze that gives the deepest water a slightly milky quality. Life here is sparse but persistent, adapted to cold and noise and pressure and darkness, making its living on the chemical trickle from below and the faint light from above.

Nothing here is exotic or alien in the way that the word *alien* usually implies. The creatures are unusual — their adaptations are shaped by conditions without terrestrial parallel — but they are recognizably biological, recognizably animal, operating on principles of metabolism and predation and reproduction that would make sense to any marine biologist, if not to any specific species they'd ever studied. This is not a world of impossibilities. It is a world of harsh, cold, loud, barely-supported life, doing what life does: persisting.

## The Equilibrium

Nothing changes. Or rather: everything changes so slowly, and with such perfect balance, that the effect is the same as no change at all.

The currents follow the same paths they have followed for centuries. The thermal vents emit at the same rate. The factories process at the same output — their gauges, if you could read them, showing the same numbers they showed last year, and the year before, and the decade before that. The fauna follows the same migratory circuits, the same feeding schedules, the same generational rhythms. New seafloor is born along the ridges, yes — but it is born at the same rate that old seafloor subducts at the margins, and the net shape of the ocean floor doesn't change. The temperature doesn't change. The salinity doesn't change. The light, filtered through an atmosphere that never varies, doesn't change.

It is a frozen equilibrium. Not frozen in the sense of being dead or static — the machines still roar, the creatures still hunt, the vents still smoke — but frozen in the sense that every force is perfectly counterbalanced. Nothing builds up. Nothing depletes. The energy that enters the system equals the energy that leaves. It is, in the thermodynamic sense, as close to a steady state as a living, industrial, geologically active world can achieve. And it has been this way for longer than anyone is here to measure.

There is a quality to this — sitting in the deafening cold, watching the faint blue bioluminescence of an eel-analogue drift past a factory pylon, hearing the turbines grind their eternal note — that is not quite peace and not quite despair. It is something older than both. It is the feeling of a place that has found its final configuration, its lowest energy state, and settled into it with the patience of stone and the inevitability of gravity. The world does not want. The world does not change. The world roars, and is cold, and is vast, and is water, and continues.

## Scale and Emptiness

The scale is difficult to convey because there are no landmarks. On land, distance is measured by what you pass — a hill, a river, a town. Here, there is only water. Swim for a week in any direction and you will have passed through water, then more water, then water again. The seafloor, if you're near it, may change — a ridge may rise and fall, a vent field may appear in the murk and fade behind you — but between the ridges, between the installations, between the scattered concentrations of life, there are abyssal plains that stretch for thousands of kilometers in every direction, flat as a table, featureless as a mirror, empty as only a volume of water with nothing in it can be empty.

The density parameter — 0.1, nearly nothing per volume — manifests as this: the profound, almost aggressive sparseness of the world. You could drop a city into this ocean and lose it. The factories are large, but the ocean is larger, and between any two installations there is a gulf of nothing that would take weeks to cross. The fauna is present, but distributed across such an enormous volume that an observer in any given cubic kilometer of water is overwhelmingly likely to be alone. Alone in the cold. Alone in the noise. Alone in the blue-dark water that goes on in every direction, above and below and side to side, without boundary or interruption, a volume so large that it becomes its own kind of substance — not empty, because it's full of water, but empty in every way that matters to something looking for a wall to touch or a landmark to navigate by or another heartbeat in the dark.

The vastness and the sparseness together produce a particular psychological effect, or would, if anyone were here to feel it: the sense that the world is not so much a place as a condition — that *being submerged in cold water in the dark in the noise* is not something that happens *in* a landscape but something that happens *to* a body, endlessly, without variation, without purpose, without end.
