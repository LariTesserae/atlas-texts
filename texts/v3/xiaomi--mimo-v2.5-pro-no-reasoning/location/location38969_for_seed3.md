The climb is vertical. The world is built on the sheer, uneroded faces of a mountain that has no business existing. This is not a peak worn by time; it is a geological argument, a recent and violent thrust of raw basalt and obsidian that tore its way into the sky. It is unfinished. Great, crystalline lattices of quartz, still forming, jut from the rock like the bones of a new world, catching what little light there is and bending it wrong. The air at this altitude is thin and perpetually cool, a damp chill that clings to everything.

The city of Vael is carved into this impossible cliff. It is less a settlement and more a parasite of industry, a vertical hive of scaffolding and pneumatic lifts and damp cobblestone roads that spiral precipitously around the mountain's girth. Buildings are stacked precariously, their foundations drilled directly into the primordial rock. Soot and grime streak down the raw stone walls, giving the entire structure a bruised, weeping appearance. Pipes, thick as ancient trees, hiss with steam and chug with strange fluids, running along every surface, connecting workshops to gushing cisterns. The sound is a constant, low hum layered with the clang of hammers on anvils, the hiss of hydraulics, and the distant, mournful groan of a great engine deep in the mountain's heart.

This is a place of stark, confusing scales. From the open-air terraces, one can see for miles across a desolate, fog-shrouded valley below. The city itself is sparse, its density focused in pockets of industry and habitation, leaving great swathes of the cliff face empty, raw, and silent. But within those pockets, life is thick. The damp, cool air and the constant leaking from pipes have nurtured a strange and abundant ecology. Luminescent fungi, which glow with a soft, sickly green light, coat the shadowed walls and underside of every ledge. Pale, eyeless lizards scuttle through the crevices, and vast, web-spinning spiders, their bodies the size of a human fist, hang motionless in the gloom between buildings. The fauna here has adapted not to the world, but to the city, treating the pipes and girders as a new kind of forest canopy.

The world is broken. Reality does not sit correctly on this new mountain. In the dim, perpetual twilight cast by the low, heavy clouds and the city's own greasy gas-lamps, things shimmer. The edge of a stone step might seem to fray into nothing for a moment before snapping back. A worker’s shadow, cast by a flickering industrial bulb, might move a half-second out of sync with its owner. The light that filters through the industrial smog is itself unreliable; it seems absorbed by surfaces rather than reflected, making the shadows deep and absolute. Sound behaves strangely too. The clang of a distant forge might arrive at your ear a moment before the sight of the hammer falling, a subtle dissonance that keeps one's nerves perpetually on edge.

The residents, a hardy and grim populace, seem to accept this as the natural order. They move with a slow, deliberate patience, their movements economical. They are building their world on a foundation of pure instability, and they have learned not to rush. They go about their work in factories that pump out more pipes and scaffolding, their faces lit by the eerie glow of the parasitic fungi they cultivate for light. The air smells of wet rock, ozone, hot metal, and a strange, sweet decay. It is a city of ghosts in the machine of a mountain that is not yet finished being born, a monument to an industry built on a fault line of the real, all set against the immense, patient, and slowly dripping silence of the high, raw peaks.
