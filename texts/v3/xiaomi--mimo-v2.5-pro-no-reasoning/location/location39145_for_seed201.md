The air is thin and sharp, a blade of cold crystal. You are not on a mountain so much as inside a wound in it — a high-altitude cirque, a natural amphitheater scooped from the flank of a greater peak by an ice age that has barely ended. The light is the first thing you must adjust to. At this elevation, the sun does not warm, it strikes. It pours into the bowl from above, a liquid, surgical brightness that etches every edge and casts shadows that are not grey but a deep, solid indigo. There is no haze. The sky is a colour for which your eyes have no name, a violet-black sea hovering just above the rock.

The rock itself is new. The glacier that carved this place retreated not long ago, in the slow language of stone. Everything is sharp. The walls of the cirque are a series of fractured, angular shelves, where slabs of granite and darker gneiss lie tumbled like discarded pages. The erosion is not rounded or softened; it is a history of clean breaks and recent falls. Dust from these constant, silent fractures coats everything in a fine, pale silt, the colour of bone. Water exists here not as a river but as a nervous system. Meltwater trickles from weeping seams in the rock, collects in crevices, and forms a network of tiny, ephemeral streams that vanish into gravel fans. At the cirque's nadir, a shallow, milky pool sits, so cold it seems to hold the light rather than reflect it, its surface disturbed only by the occasional drop from an overhanging icicle.

And yet, life clamours. This is the paradox of the place: a small, cold, bright wound in the world that is ferociously, visibly alive. The "lushness" is not in height but in concentration. Every ledge, every pocket of soil, is stuffed with a low, tenacious riot of cushion plants, saxifrages, and alpine flowers — purple, yellow, and white — growing so tight they form a living skin over the stone. Mosses, star-shaped and vivid green, map the damp faces of north-facing rocks. This vegetation is the foundation for the fauna. The air itself is thick with movement. Insects are everywhere: clouds of tiny, dark flies, iridescent beetles that shimmer like oil slicks, and butterflies whose wings are patterns of warning against the cyan sky. Birds are constant presences — pipits that flit from stone to stone, their calls sharp and thin, and a single, circling raptor whose shadow periodically sweeps the floor of the cirque like a slow hand. In the larger crevices, you catch movement: a white-tailed hare, a family of pikas with their alarm calls like tiny whistles. Life here is not subtle. It is a dense, visible, churning web, made all the more apparent by the emptiness of the air and the starkness of the rock.

Against the eastern wall, almost hidden, are the marks of something not-geological. Three or four small, rough shelters have been built from the local stone, their walls little more than carefully stacked slabs to break the wind. They are recent, but abandoned. Near one, a circle of soot-darkened stones marks a fire-pit. A few scraps of woven fibre, stiff with cold, are caught on a thorn bush. These are not ruins; they are memories of a presence, left by someone who understood how to survive here, but did not stay.

The sound is the quiet. It is not an absence, but a positive force. The dominant noise is the faint, high-pitched hiss of wind moving over the sharp rock edges, and the occasional, distant *clink* of a stone settling. The biological sounds — the insect hum, the bird calls — are distinct and close, set against this vast, ringing silence. The activity is in the small scale: the slow opening of a flower, the frantic search of a beetle, the silent sweep of the raptor. The rock is still breaking, the ice is still melting, the plants are still growing, the hunters are still hunting. Everything is in motion, but it is the motion of a clockwork mechanism, intricate and contained within the small, bright bowl of the world.
