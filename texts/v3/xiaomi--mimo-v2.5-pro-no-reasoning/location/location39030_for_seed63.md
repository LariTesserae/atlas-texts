The air is a physical weight, a pressure that lives in the bones. It is not merely hot; it is a simmering medium, thick with a haze of mineral dust so fine it never settles. To breathe is to draw in the powdered ghost of mountains. The ground, far below a forgotten sea, is a cracked and vitrified plain of salt and fused silica, but you are not on the ground. You are high, far too high, on a ledge or a plateau that is itself a feature of a greater, unseen ascent. The darkness is not absolute. It is a deep, bruised purple, lit from within and above by the slow, majestic pulse of a nebula, a permanent aurora of impossible colors that stains the rock without providing true illumination. This light reveals a world at cathedral scale, but it is a single, cramped cathedral, a chamber of stone whose vault is the star-laced void itself.

The dominant sound is a low, resonant hum. It is not wind, for the air is too still and thick for wind. It is the collective vibration of the place, the song of a billion tiny lives. They cling to everything. Not plants, not in any sense you would recognize. They are the dominant fauna, the overwhelming presence. Chitinous, six-legged things, the size of a human fist, with shells of obsidian and milky, translucent salt. Their density is staggering. They carpet the rock faces, the ledges, the spires of fused silica that erupt from the plateau like rotten teeth. They are packed so tightly that their collective movement is a single, slow, peristaltic wave across the landscape. Their legs are tipped with tools of pure biology—drills, rasps, polishers. They are the erosion. For millennia, they have been the weather, the wind, and the water.

They are miners, and the world is their ore.

The few structures are not built by them, but are of them. Great, spiraling heaps of shed carapace and mineral waste, the midden-mounds of a thousand generations, cemented into bizarre, organic ziggurats by secretions that glow faintly in the nebular light. These are the focal points, the reasons for the motion. The creatures swarm up and down these structures in intricate, silent highways. They are not building in a way that implies planning or thought, but in a way that implies geology. They are a living, crawling part of the planet's crust, a mobile, thinking mineral.

The strangeness is in the purpose. They are not farming or hunting, for there is nothing to farm or hunt but each other, and they do not seem to do that. They are excavating. They drill into the very rock of the plateau, their rasping appendages making a sound like fine sandpaper on slate, a whisper just beneath the threshold of hearing until you are surrounded by it. They are pulling something from the deep rock—slivers of a darker, non-reflective crystal that seem to drink the faint light. They carry these slivers, one per creature, up to the very tips of the tallest ziggurats and leave them there, in perfect, geometric patterns.

The ecosystem is a closed, frantic loop of consumption and construction. The creatures eat the rock, digest the silica, and use it to build their homes, their bodies, their world. The heat is not from a sun, but from the geothermal pressure of this world's core, a heat that powers the slow, volcanic chemistry that the creatures themselves seem to replicate and accelerate. The faint marks are the scrapes of their legs on a billion surfaces. The single feature is this plateau, this one great altar in the sky.

And on the tips of the ziggurats, the dark crystal slivers begin to glow, one by one, with a light that is the absolute antithesis of the nebula above. It is a cold, sharp, violet light that seems to pinprick the darkness. The creatures, having placed their burden, turn and begin the long crawl back down for another. They are not just inhabitants of the landscape. They are the landscape's nervous system, its hands, its slow, geological thought. The plateau is not a place. It is a single, living organ, and you are standing on its skin.
