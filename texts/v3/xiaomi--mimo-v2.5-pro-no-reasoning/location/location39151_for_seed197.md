# The Gullet

---

You hear it before you understand where you are.

Not a sound *from* a place. A sound that *is* the place — a standing roar, a droning harmonic that lives in your sternum and makes your vision swim at the edges. It comes from everywhere at once. It is not one thing. It is a thousand things layered so densely they become a single texture of noise: the wet percussion of something enormous dripping in irregular rhythm, the stridulation of ten thousand mandible-bearing things scraping their wings against living walls, the deep subsonic *pulse* of a structure that breathes, the crystalline singing of taut fibers vibrating at frequencies that have no right to exist inside something organic. You cannot hear yourself think. After a while, you stop trying. Thought becomes gesture, becomes instinct, becomes the act of moving forward through a place that expects you to move through it.

---

## The Threshold

The entrance is a mouth. There is no more honest word.

You came across low ground — a warm, flat marshland of grey mud and standing pools, ankle-deep, studded with the broken stumps of what might once have been trees before something larger replaced them. The sky was overcast, not with clouds but with *canopy* — the frayed outer edges of a growth so vast it has its own weather. Moisture condensed on every surface. Not rain. Condensation. The air itself weeping. The ground was soft and warm and everywhere you stepped, something small and quick skittered away from under your foot — things with too many legs, things with not enough legs, things that left faintly luminous trails in the mud that faded by the time you turned to look.

And then the ground changed. The mud gave way to something fibrous, spongy, resistant — a mat of interlocking roots so dense it had become *flooring*. And ahead of you, the growth rose. Not a tree. Not a wall. A *lip*. A curving, ridged outcropping of woody tissue, dark as old scab, moist at its edges, ridged with channels that carried moisture upward and carried something else — a faintly sweet, faintly acrid sap-smell — downward. The lip was tall. Perhaps four meters at its highest. And beyond it, the sound — already loud from the marshland — became something else entirely. Became *presence*.

You stepped inside.

---

## The Interior

The space you enter is not a room. It is a *chamber* in the geological sense — a cavity inside a living body so large it has mistaken itself for geography.

The walls are plant matter. There is no other material. No stone, no soil, no mineral. Every surface — floor, wall, ceiling, every protrusion and depression — is *growth*. Cellular tissue in layers so compressed and aged that it has the density of softwood, dark amber and grey-green, shot through with vascular channels that glisten when the light catches them. And there is light. Normal light. That's perhaps the most disorienting thing about this place — the light is *ordinary*. It comes from above, filtering through the layered canopy-crown of the outer structure, dappled, warm, sufficient. As if this were simply a clearing in a forest. As if the walls were not walls.

But they are walls. The chamber is roughly oval, perhaps sixty meters across at its widest, and the ceiling — the inner surface of the upper canopy-crown — curves overhead at a height of maybe fifteen meters, hanging with pale root-tendrils that sway in air currents generated by the structure's own slow respiration. The floor is uneven, rising into low mounds and dipping into shallow depressions where moisture gathers — not pools, exactly, but *slicks*, thin films of condensation that collect and evaporate and collect again on a cycle that has nothing to do with weather and everything to do with the creature you are standing inside of.

The emptiness is striking. The chamber is mostly *air*. The walls press close — you are never more than thirty meters from a surface — but between the walls and across the floor there is vast, echoing, *sparse* space. A few standing columns of growth rise from the floor like stalagmites, dark and ridged, some of them forked, some of them curved, all of them slowly — imperceptibly over minutes, visible over hours — *moving*. Growing. Adjusting. Reaching toward light that isn't where it was a week ago. And on every surface, in every crevice, on every column and tendril and ridge: life. Life in extravagant, relentless abundance.

---

## The Fauna

They are the texture of the sound.

You cannot see them all. You cannot see *most* of them. But you can hear them, and hearing them is like standing inside a broken engine that has decided to sing.

The walls are *perforated* with small openings — holes ranging from the diameter of a finger to the diameter of an arm — and from these holes, things emerge and retreat on cycles you cannot track. Insects, primarily. Beetles the size of your fist with carapaces that shift color in the dappled light — not camouflage, something else, something communicative, flashing bands of bioluminescent blue-green along their wing-cases as they crawl across the chamber ceiling in loose, spiraling columns. Smaller things too: flies with iridescent wings that beat at frequencies that contribute, individually, a thin whine to the overall roar, and collectively produce a *wall* of high-pitched sound that is the first thing your ears lose the ability to separate from silence. Moths, pale and powdery, that drift through the root-tendrils like slow confetti, their wing-beats producing a sound you feel more than hear, a soft percussion against the subsonic hum of the structure itself.

And then the larger things.

Something moves along the far wall — a creature roughly the size and shape of a large monitor lizard, but *wrong* in ways that are hard to articulate. Its limbs are too long. Its head is too narrow. Its skin is the exact color of the wall it moves across, and when it stops, it becomes nearly invisible — not because it is camouflaged but because the wall seems to *accept* it, the tissue rippling slightly around its resting body as if absorbing it into the surface. It watches you with eyes that are too far apart. It does not seem alarmed. It seems *patient*.

There are birds. Or there are things that fly like birds — small, fast, chittering, with translucent wing-membranes stretched over elongated fingers, roosting in clusters along the upper reaches of the chamber, their calls contributing a shrieking, bubbling counterpoint to the insect-drone. They dart and wheel through the root-tendrils with a precision that suggests they can see the air currents — or that the air currents are *for* them, channeled by the structure deliberately.

In the slicks of moisture on the floor, amphibian things. Flat, wide, barely an inch thick, with skin so dark it is almost black, absorbing the dappled light. They don't hop or crawl. They *ripple*, moving by undulation across the wet surfaces like slow, deliberate inkblots. When two of them meet, they pause, press their flat bodies together for a moment, and then separate, each continuing on its own trajectory. It is unclear whether this is mating, communication, or something that has no analogue in any ecology you recognize.

There are more. There are always more. For every creature you identify, five more move at the edge of perception — a rustle in a wall-hole, a shadow on a ceiling, a vibration that is not the structure and not the insects but something *else*, something moving through the fibrous substrate of the walls itself, traveling inside the living tissue like a pulse through a vein.

---

## The Deeper Chambers

Beyond the first chamber, the structure continues.

Not in a way that makes spatial sense. The passage that leads inward — a curving throat of dark, moist tissue, narrowing and then widening again — does not take you to a *second* chamber so much as it takes you to a different *version* of the same chamber. The proportions shift. The ceiling is higher. Or the floor is lower. Or the columns have changed — fewer here, but thicker, and they don't connect to the floor anymore but hang from the ceiling like stalactites, their tips trailing filaments that brush the ground and seem to *listen* when you pass. The light is the same — still ordinary, still dappled, still warm — but it comes from a slightly different angle, as if the sun outside has moved by an amount of time that does not correspond to how long you spent walking.

This is the weirdness. This is where the place becomes *unreliable*.

The structure does not obey consistent rules of proportion. A corridor that should be ten meters long takes thirty steps to cross. A chamber that appeared round from the entrance resolves into an oblong when you reach its center. Distances *flex*. Not dramatically — you are never teleported, never turned around without knowing it — but the geometry breathes, expands and contracts, as if the structure's living tissue is subject to forces that have nothing to do with the rigid spatial logic of dead wood and mineral stone. You begin to suspect that the place is not *shaped* but *shaping* — that the architecture is not fixed but ongoing, that the room you are standing in was this shape at the moment you entered it and is, even now, becoming something else.

The animals seem unaffected. They move through the shifting spaces with absolute confidence, taking paths that are clearly long-established, well-worn, habitual. A colony of the flat black amphibian-things occupies a particular depression in the floor of the third chamber, and they have clearly occupied it for generations — their trails have worn shallow channels in the fibrous floor, channels that glow faintly with bioluminescent residue. They know this place. They have adapted to its uncertainty. You have not.

And the sound changes as you go deeper. The insect-drone remains, a constant foundation, but new layers emerge: a rhythmic *clicking* from deep within the walls, like a clock made of bone. A high, oscillating tone — almost musical — from a cluster of creatures you cannot see, nested somewhere in the upper reaches of a chamber whose ceiling has risen so high it has become dark. And beneath everything, the subsonic pulse of the structure itself, slower than a heartbeat, more regular than breathing, a *throb* that you feel in the soles of your feet and in the wet air against your skin and in the faint, persistent vibration of the walls.

---

## What It Is

The structure is old. The erosion — the weathering, the settling — is visible in the way its outer surfaces have *hardened*, the fibrous tissue compressing over centuries into something that approaches lignite, dark and glassy and nearly mineral. But the interior remains alive. Warm. Moist. Growing. The establishment is not stasis — it is *equilibrium*. The structure has reached a balance with its environment, with its internal fauna, with the damp air and the low ground and the intermittent moisture that feeds it, and it maintains that balance through constant, active adjustment. Growth and decay. Expansion and retraction. The creatures eat the structure and the structure grows around the creatures and the condensation feeds everything and the sound — the deafening, inescapable, *omnidirectional* sound — is the signature of that process, the noise of a living system so dense, so layered, so full of interlocking agents and reactions and cycles that it has produced, as a byproduct, a roar that rivals thunder.

No one built this. No one tends it. There are no tools here, no devices, no infrastructure in the human sense. But the word *urban* is not wrong. This is a city. It is organized. It has districts — the bright outer chambers where the light-loving insects mass in spiraling columns, the dark inner chambers where the amphibian-things pool in their bioluminescent trails, the vertical shafts where the flying things roost and breed, the deep passages where something very large and very slow and never quite visible moves on a cycle measured in weeks. It has infrastructure — the vascular channels in the walls that distribute moisture and nutrients and sap, the root-tendril networks that connect distant chambers, the system of holes and passages that allows the fauna to move through the structure's body. It has *layers* — strata of growth, of habitation, of history, compressed into the walls like sedimentary rock, each one a record of a previous equilibrium, a previous set of residents, a previous version of the sound.

It is a city made of flesh, governed by biology, deafening and alive and *unreliable* in its geometry, and it has been here longer than you can measure, and it will be here longer than you can imagine, and the sound — the sound — will outlast everything.
