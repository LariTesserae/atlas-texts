# The Vatry

You hear it before you see it — a subsonic hum that lives in your molars, a thrumming so constant it becomes silence's neighbor. Then the ridge falls away and there it is: the Vatry.

It is a building, but to call it a building is like calling a cathedral a house. The structure climbs out of the high saddle between two eroded peaks at roughly four thousand meters, a canted series of enormous rectangular volumes stacked and cantilevered against the slope like a child's blocks left mid-fall. Each volume is perhaps sixty meters on a side, oriented at slightly different angles, their walls made of riveted iron plates now the color of old blood, of oxidized copper sheeting gone green-black, of glass panes — thousands of them — most still intact, clouded with mineral deposits and the slow stain of condensation. The whole assembly has the quality of something that was built all at once by an intent that has since evaporated. No one maintains it. It maintains itself, which is different.

---

**The exterior.** The slope below the Vatry is bare scree — high-altitude rubble, pale grey and ochre, drifted against the lower walls in slow avalanches of frost-heaved stone. No vegetation touches the ground here; the elevation is too severe, the wind too constant. But the building itself seethes with green. From every joint, every gutter, every corroded seam, plant matter extrudes: thick curtains of moss in jade and emerald, epiphytic ferns uncurling from bolt-holes, woody vines with stems as thick as a forearm threading through broken windowpanes and gripping the iron ribs of the structure from inside. The vegetation doesn't grow *on* the Vatry so much as *out of* it — as though the building were a substrate, a cliff face, a geological fact. Which, after eight hundred years, it more or less is.

Moisture runs everywhere. The warm air pooling inside the volumes meets the thin, cold atmosphere at every glass surface and condenses into sheets of water that slide down the interior walls, collect in gutters that have long since rusted through, and spill outward in thin cascades that freeze at night and thaw by noon — a permanent slow dripping that has carved dark streaks down the iron facades and stained the scree below in long fans of rust-red and mineral white. Pockets of standing water sit in the depressions of the cantilevered roofs, shallow brown pools where algae bloom and insects breed. It is not a wet place, exactly. It is a *damp* place — damp the way a terrarium is damp, a warm breath held against cold glass.

---

**The interior.** You enter through a gap where a wall panel has corroded and fallen, and the noise doubles — no, redoubles, because now you are inside the machine.

The hum has sources. Deep in the lower volumes, still-functioning turbines turn with geological patience. They have not accelerated or decelerated in anyone's memory. Their housings are furred with moss; birds — small, dun-colored, with anisodactyl feet adapted for gripping metal — nest in the cooling vents. The turbines generate power that feeds into systems no one can fully map: conduits that run through the walls and warm them from within (this is why the interior stays warm at this altitude, why the condensation never stops), light arrays embedded in the ceiling plates that flicker on and off in cycles that don't correspond to day or night but to some internal logic of the original mechanism, pumps that circulate water through the walls in slow arterial loops.

The interior space is *enormous* — cathedral-scale would understate the largest volumes — but it is almost entirely empty. The structural ribs of the building arch overhead in parallel curves, iron-gray, and between them the glass ceiling lets in high-altitude light that falls in pale rectangles on the floor far below. The air between ceiling and floor is mostly nothing: a vast hollow volume, nearly void, with only motes of spores and the occasional drifting insect to mark it. Sound moves through this emptiness strangely — the turbine hum bouncing between parallel walls until it becomes directionless, omnipresent, a pressure rather than a noise. You do not converse here. You cannot.

What *occupies* the space clings to the walls and the catwalks. The catwalks — iron grating, originally human-scaled — are now colonized: ferns grow through the grating in dense plumes, their fronds hanging down into the void. Mosses and liverworts coat every horizontal surface in thick, spongy mats that hold water like sponges and release it in slow drips. Where catwalks cross, vegetation tangles into mid-air hedges, dense enough to obscure vision, forming vertical walls of green in the middle of the larger chambers. Fauna is abundant here — not large fauna, but numerous. Insects in profusion: crane flies, beetles, moths that orbit the light arrays in slow spirals. The small birds — perhaps four species, differentiated by beak shape and nesting preference — move through the upper canopy of vegetation in flocks of twenty or thirty. In the lowest volumes, where standing water collects in shallow basins formed by the rusted floor, amphibians breed: translucent, pale, eyeless things that have adapted to the permanent twilight beneath the catwalks. They are not loud. Nothing here is loud except the turbines and the water. The animals are quiet, slow, deliberate in their movements, as though they too have adopted the tempo of the place.

---

**The deeper volumes.** Descending through the stacked chambers — each connected by corroded staircases or, where the stairs have failed, by ladders of vine — you find the older, more enclosed spaces. These were perhaps the original working floors. Here the machinery is denser: great vats, now empty or half-full of black water; presses and molds that have seized in their corroded housings; conveyor systems frozen mid-carry. The vegetation changes character in these spaces — less fern, more fungus. Bracket fungi the size of dinner plates climb the machinery in overlapping shelves, their undersides gilled and pale. The walls are slick with biofilm. The air is warmer here, almost tropical, and the condensation is so heavy that every surface weeps. The sound changes too: the turbines are more present, more felt, a rhythmic shuddering transmitted through the floor.

What were they making here? The vats suggest industrial chemistry — the residue on their walls has a metallic tang. The presses suggest fabrication. But whatever was produced has long since been consumed by the structure itself, metabolized into the slow processes that keep the Vatry warm, damp, alive, and turning. The machines do not produce anything external. They produce the *conditions*. The warmth. The moisture. The rhythm. They have become the building's organs, and the building has become an ecosystem, and the ecosystem has reached a state that does not change in any human-meaningful timeframe.

---

**At the summit volume.** The highest chamber has the most glass and the least iron. Its ceiling is almost entirely paned, many of them cracked, and through them you see the sky — high-altitude sky, deep blue to near-violet, crossed by the fast-moving cirrus clouds that form above four thousand meters. Wind presses against the glass and you hear it as a faint singing beneath the turbine hum. This room is the driest in the structure — the condensation here evaporates back upward before it can pool — and the vegetation is sparsest: only a few hardy, woody plants rooted in the catwalk grating, their leaves waxy and dark, adapted to desiccation.

This is where the small birds come to die. Their skeletons litter the catwalks — tiny, delicate, barely there. Perhaps they come for the light. Perhaps the building draws them, the way cathedrals draw silence.

Standing here, you are inside a body that has been running for eight hundred years, producing nothing but itself. The noise is constant. The air is warm and wet. The green presses against every surface. And nothing, nothing, nothing changes.
