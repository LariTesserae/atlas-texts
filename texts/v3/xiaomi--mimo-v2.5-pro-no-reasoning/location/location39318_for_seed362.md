The air is thick with silent light. Not the warm, generous light of a sun, but a cold, architectural luminescence that emanates from the very structure of the world. It is a planet of pure, engineered chlorophyll, and the light is its output, its waste product, its very nature.

There is no ground. To call it a forest would be a profound understatement, for there is no soil, no bedrock beneath. You are standing—or rather, you are suspended—within a world that is entirely, exhaustively *plant*. It is a continent-sized organism, a living lattice of interconnected, hyper-specialized flora that forms both the substrate and the sky. The base unit is a thick, fibrous vine, the colour of dark jade, as broad as a river. From its surface erupts a forest of smaller stalks, fronds, and membranes, all fused into a single, continuous biomass. You are inside the plant. The "terrain" is a series of chambers, corridors, and vast, cathedral-like cavities, all formed by the curving, interlocking growth of this one entity.

This is a built world, a total construction. There are no natural features, only biological architecture. The "trees" are load-bearing buttresses, their bark a layered composite material with the lustre of polished chrome and the texture of dense muscle. The "leaves" are vast, semi-translucent panels, metres thick, that filter and re-radiate the cold, internal light. They are not for photosynthesis in any crude sense; they are the world's circulatory and sensory system, pulsing with faint, bioluminescent patterns that are the closest thing to weather here.

This is a dry world, a desert of chlorophyll. Water is a myth, a forgotten concept. The chemistry is entirely non-aqueous. Moisture, in the form of thick, oily resins and crystalline humours, is generated and conserved within the plant's own tissues. What feels like a cool breeze is not air, but a pressure differential caused by the slow, tidal expansion and contraction of the largest bio-panels. The coolness is a constant, a maintained internal temperature, the thermal regulation of a living machine.

The industrial aspect is subtle, not of gears and smoke, but of scaled, silent production. Deep within the lattice, in chambers that thrum with a subsonic vibration, the plant manufactures. It grows. It builds itself. Great, slowly moving shapes—organs of the world-machine—extrude new fibres, assemble new chambers, recycle old ones. The process is visible as a slow, inexorable movement of the architecture itself. A wall might subtly bulge over the course of a century, becoming a new buttress. A corridor might slowly narrow as its walls knit shut. This is the ecosystem in motion: a singular, vast, and utterly silent construction project.

Fauna is almost non-existent. The rarest of presences might be a "pruner," a slow, multi-limbed construct of woody chitin and photosynthetic plates that crawls along the vines, excising dead or inefficient growth with scissor-like mandibles. It is more a mobile organ of the great plant than an independent creature. Its movements are the only audible sounds: the dry, rhythmic *snip-snip* of its work, echoing softly in the vast, quiet halls.

The weirdness is the reality breakdown. The logic is wrong. Light comes from within. The ground is the ceiling is the wall. Perspective is fluid in the shifting, internal light; a corridor that appears to extend into infinity might reveal itself, after an hour's walk, to be a gentle, curving loop. The most unsettling aspect is the *thought*. The plant is aware. Its thoughts are not language, but geometry. The slow, grinding shift of a wall is a decision. The brightening of a chamber is a focus of attention. You are not an observer in a landscape; you are a mote of dust in a mind, a foreign particle in a body of incomprehensible scale and purpose. The silence is not empty; it is the silence of a brain processing, of a system in its final, perfect, and utterly alien state of being.
