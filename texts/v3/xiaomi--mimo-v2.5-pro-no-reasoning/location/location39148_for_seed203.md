The air is thin and achingly cold, carrying the sharp, clean scent of crushed leaves and damp mineral dust. You are in a cavern so vast it has its own sky—a permanent, cloudless noon of soft, directionless light that seems to bleed from the very stone of the distant ceiling, miles above. This is not sunlight. It is a solid, unchanging luminance, yet the air that falls from it is frigid, a cold sink in the planet's belly.

The floor of this world is a gentle, rolling plain of living green. Not soil and grass, but a single, continuous organism: a carpet of moss and liverwort so dense and spongy it swallows all sound. Your steps make no noise. The carpet is damp, not with rain—there is no rain here—but with a perpetual, seeping condensation that beads on every surface. Occasional pools and slow, meandering streams of perfectly clear water lie in shallow depressions, their surfaces smooth as glass, their bottoms lined with a finer, silvery moss. The moisture is the only thing that moves; it creeps and gathers in patient, silent rivulets.

But the scale of the green is staggering. Rising from the moss-sea are the true features of the land: colossal, gnarled trunks, kilometers wide, that are not wood but something denser, like petrified fungal tissue or compacted plant-stuff, layered in shades of russet and deep green. They are the architecture of this place. Their canopies are not a separate thing but a continuation, a second plane of existence hundreds of meters above the moss-plain. Here, in the dimmer, colder air, branching structures form cathedral-like arches and suspended plateaus of leaves and tangled roots, all dripping with condensation. This is the forest's upper world, a realm of deep shadow and occasional, startling bursts of phosphorescent growth—glowing shelves of fungus or the bioluminescent edges of immense, fern-like fronds.

The dynamism is not in movement, but in creation and collapse. The ecosystem is in a state of furious, silent competition. You can see it: a trunk, kilometers away, suddenly groans, a sound felt more in the bones than heard, and slumps, its internal structure giving way. It does not fall far, instead sinking into the spongy moss, which begins to grow over it immediately. Elsewhere, a new stalk is piercing through the green, a pale, fast-growing tendril that will thicken over centuries into a new trunk. This is the turbulence—a slow-motion explosion of growth and decay on a continental scale, a war fought in centuries rather than days.

Here and there, the work of a forgotten, simple intelligence intrudes. On a mossy hummock, you might find a circle of sharpened stones, the points worn smooth. Within the hollow of a great root, a shallow cave reveals a soot-blackened ceiling and a scattering of flint flakes. These are whispers, not shouts. The structures are gone, absorbed by the relentless green. The rare fauna are adapted to this: pallid, long-limbed grazers that move in utter silence across the moss, their bodies draped in filaments of plant matter for camouflage. You see them as faint movements in the periphery, gone if you turn your head.

The dominant sound is the drip. The constant, arrhythmic *plink... plish... plonk* of water condensing and falling from leaf to leaf, root to moss, a sound that is both the only clock and a meaningless one. It is a vast, hollow, living cathedral. The air tastes of cold chlorophyll and wet stone. The light is bright but gives no warmth, illuminating a world that is both explosively alive and profoundly, terrifyingly empty of anything but itself.
