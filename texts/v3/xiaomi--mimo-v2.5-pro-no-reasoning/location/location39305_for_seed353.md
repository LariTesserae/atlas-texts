***

There is no sky, only a ceiling of bruised, black clouds that have not broken in millennia. They glow from within, a sullen, deep crimson light that is more a feeling of heat than a source of illumination. This is the only light, and it seeps down from the heavens like viscous blood, catching on the edges of things, but never truly revealing them. The world below is a landscape of sharp, moving silhouettes and deep, absolute shadows.

The ground is not soil, nor rock. It is a compressed, vitrified substrate, a dark and polished material that has the texture of slag or ancient, flash-fused sand. It is warm to the touch, a constant, radiant heat that rises from below, supplementing the oppressive heat from the glowing clouds above. The air is thin and dry, tasting of ozone and hot metal. There is no water here; no humidity, no dew, no ice. The very concept of moisture is a forgotten language.

What covers this glassy plain is life, but it is not the life of green leaves and soft petals. It is a mechanical flora, a biology of brass and filament. The entire landscape is a single, sprawling organism—the Glimmerwood. Immense, skeletal trees, like the fossilized skeletons of creatures that never were, rise from the ground. Their trunks are weaves of blackened iron and something like bone, and their branches are a complex lattice of copper wire and ceramic piping.

Instead of leaves, they bear thousands of delicate, crystalline filaments that hum with a constant, low-level charge. In the darkness, these filaments pulse with a faint, internal, phosphorescent light—copper-green, cobalt-blue, blood-red—giving the forest the effect of a field of dying embers. This is the glimmer that gives the Glimmerwood its name. The light is not steady; it flickers and surges in response to unseen currents, making the entire forest seem to breathe with a slow, mechanical respiration.

Everything is built. The ground itself is a composite of woven root-analogues, a mesh of living metal that has fused with the vitrified earth over eons. There is no distinction between the plant and the architecture; the plant *is* the architecture. The trees are not merely growing; they are manufacturing, their internal components flowing with superheated, non-aqueous fluids that act as both coolant and nutrient. The density is overwhelming. There is no open space. The trunks of the Glimmerwood are packed so tightly that passage is a matter of squeezing through gaps, of navigating a labyrinth of metallic limbs. Lower to the ground, smaller, more complex flora clog the spaces between the great trunks: crystalline shrubs that chime with internal pressures, mosses of fine, conductive wire, and vines of braided tubing that slowly, inexorably constrict everything they touch.

The place is in constant, violent motion. This is the turbulence. With a groan of stressed metal, a section of the forest may suddenly and catastrophically fail. A great trunk might shear, toppling in a shower of sparks and a shriek of tearing alloy. But it is not allowed to fall far. Before it hits the ground, the surrounding flora reacts. Neighboring trees shift, their branches extending, their vines whipping out to entangle the falling structure. They begin to break it down, absorbing it, re-knitting its mass into the whole. New shoots, glowing hot and bright, erupt from the wreckage within minutes. A cycle of creation and destruction plays out constantly, a riot of industrial energy sealed within this dark, silent world.

The sound is mostly quiet, but not silent. There is the ever-present, low hum of the charged filaments. The faint, near-subsonic thrum of a world of machinery processing itself. The occasional, sharp *tick* of a cooling filament or the deep, resonant *groan* as a massive trunk adjusts its internal structure. The air vibrates with potential, a quiet anthill of immense, slow-moving energies.

There is no animal life. Nothing runs or flies or crawls. The Glimmerwood is a closed system. It is its own substrate, its own architect, its own weather, its own geology. It is a world that has been at it for so long that it has reached a state of perfected, terrible stability. It has eroded all the way down to its core principle: relentless, self-consuming, mechanical growth. It is ancient, but it is not old. It is a primordial engine that has simply never stopped running, filling a dark, hot, and dry void with a silent, luminous, and ever-shifting forest of metal and light.
