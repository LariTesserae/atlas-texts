# The Terraces of Saltha

You notice the walls before anything else.

They run in long, low lines across the slope — dry-stacked stone, grey and lichen-whitened, terracing the hillside into shallow steps no wider than a man lying down. The walls have been here so long that they have become geology. Their joints have filled with fine sediment. Moss has colonized their north faces in slow green tongues. Some have slumped slightly, their courses shifted by centuries of frost-heave, but they have not fallen. They have simply found a new angle of rest.

This is a high place. Not a summit, not a peak — a shoulder. A broad, wind-scraped shoulder of a mountain range, somewhere above the treeline but below the worst of the weather. The elevation is enough that the air is thin when you climb, and cool even in what passes for summer. You can feel it in the back of your throat: clean, faintly metallic, carrying the mineral taste of wet stone.

---

The settlement — you can call it that, though the word feels too deliberate, too modern — sits across perhaps four hectares of this shoulder. Maybe fifty structures. Maybe fewer. It is hard to count because many of them are partially collapsed into the terraces themselves, their outlines now just slightly different arrangements of rubble, distinguishable from the walls only by the way the stones have been shaped at their corners.

But some still stand. They are built of the same local stone: a dark, fine-grained basalt split along natural fracture lines into flat slabs. The construction is careful. No mortar — the stones are simply fitted, dry-set, their weight holding them in place. The roofs, where they survive, are layered stone slabs laid over timber frames, the timber now silver-grey and petrified-looking though not actually fossilized. Just old. Just very, very dry and then very, very damp and then very, very dry again, over and over, until the wood stopped caring about the distinction.

The buildings are small. Single rooms, mostly. A few have interior partitions suggesting two or three spaces. Doorways are low — you would have to duck — and oriented away from the prevailing wind, which comes from the northwest in long, unbroken gusts that make the air hum at a low, almost sub-audible frequency. Some doorways still have their threshold stones, worn smooth and slightly concave by the passage of feet that are no longer passing.

---

There is water here, but it is not abundant. It comes in three forms.

First: the fog. The shoulder sits in a belt of orographic cloud that rolls up from the valleys below, especially in the late afternoon and through the night. It is not dramatic — just a thickening of the air, a whitening, a softening of edges. Everything becomes slightly glistening. The stones darken. Condensation beads on every surface and collects in the crevices between slabs, running in thin, silent rivulets down the terraced faces of the walls.

Second: the cisterns. Every fourth or fifth building has one — a stone-lined pit, roughly circular, maybe a meter across and a meter deep, carved or shaped from the bedrock beneath the terrace fill. They catch the fog-drip and the occasional rain. The water that accumulates is dark, still, and very cold. Some have developed their own small ecosystems: a film of algae on the surface, a slick of green on the inner walls. You can hear them if you lean close — a faint, occasional *plip* as a drop falls from the cistern's lip.

Third: the seep. At the base of the shoulder, where the terraces end and the slope drops away more steeply, water emerges from the rock face in a diffuse, almost invisible outflow. It does not form a stream. It simply makes the stone wet, slick, dark — and below the seep line, the vegetation thickens from almost nothing to something. Low, tough, wind-pruned things: sedges, mosses, a creeping heather-like plant with tiny blue-grey flowers that blooms without apparent season.

---

The vegetation across most of the settlement is sparse. What grows here grows slowly and holds on. Lichen is the dominant life form — pale crustose species on the sunward faces of stones, darker foliose species in the sheltered crevices. A few grasses push up between the terrace stones, their blades stiff and silver-green. In the lee of the larger buildings, where the wind is broken, small cushions of a dense, low-growing plant have established themselves, each one perhaps the size of a fist, and each one probably decades old.

There are no trees. Not even stunted ones. The combination of elevation, wind, and cold prevents it. The timber used in the buildings must have been carried here from far below — a significant labor that speaks to either necessity or purpose.

---

Animals are rare. You hear birds sometimes — a high, thin call that might be a pipit or a finch, though you rarely see the bird itself. It is too small and too fast against the enormous sky. Occasionally, in the early morning, you might glimpse a small, dark, rabbit-sized creature moving between the wall bases — something with dense fur and a low-slung body, well-insulated, not a rabbit but occupying the same ecological space. It vanishes into crevices at the first sign of movement. Beyond that: nothing. No herds, no predators, no insects to speak of. The cold and the altitude and the bareness of the terrain simply do not support much.

---

The light is ordinary. This is not a place of dramatic illumination. The sky is overcast more often than it is clear, and even on clear days the light is bright but flat — high-altitude light without much warmth in it. On fog days, visibility drops to a few hundred meters, and the world becomes a small, enclosed space: the nearest walls, the nearest terrace, the grey-white nothing beyond. Sound dampens further in the fog. Your own footsteps become muffled. The wind, still present, becomes something you feel on your skin more than hear in your ears.

When the fog lifts — and it always lifts eventually, for a few hours, sometimes a full day — the views are vast. You can see the valley systems below, layered in blue-grey and green-brown, falling away toward a distant lowland that is hazy and indistinct, more idea than landscape. Other shoulders and ridges are visible across the gaps, and on some of them — if you look carefully, if the light is right — you can see the faint lines of other terraces. Other settlements. The same pattern repeated across this entire elevation band, as if someone laid down a rule long ago: *build here, and nowhere else.*

---

The place does not change.

This is the thing that settles into you after a while. The walls are where they were. The cisterns hold the same water. The lichen grows so slowly that you could visit a decade apart and notice no difference. The fog comes and goes but always comes back. The wind is the same wind.

There are no signs of recent habitation — no fresh tool marks, no soot in the hearths, no paths worn through the grass between buildings. But there are also no signs of violent abandonment. No burned structures, no scattered remains, no walls knocked flat. The settlement was not destroyed. It was simply... concluded. The people who built it, who carried the timber up from the valleys, who fitted the basalt slabs with such evident skill and patience, who dug the cisterns and oriented the doorways against the wind — they finished what they were doing, or they reached the limit of what this place could sustain, or both. And then they left, or they stopped, and the place remained.

It has been in this state for a long time. Long enough that "long time" ceases to be a useful descriptor. The terraces are an established feature of the landscape, as permanent as the rock they are built from. The equilibrium here is absolute — not because nothing *could* happen, but because the conditions for change are so precisely balanced against the conditions for persistence that the net result, year after year, century after century, is zero.

The walls stand. The fog comes. The water collects in the cisterns and slowly evaporates or slowly seeps away. The lichen spreads by millimeters per decade. The small dark creature disappears into its crevice. The wind hums.

Nothing is happening here. Nothing has happened here for a very long time. The place is complete.
