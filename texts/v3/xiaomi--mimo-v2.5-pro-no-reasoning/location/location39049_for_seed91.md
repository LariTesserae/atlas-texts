The floor of the gorge is a river of frozen mud, not solid but stiff with cold, a slurry locked mid-motion in its journey downhill. Overhead, the sky is a narrow strip of grey, too high and too distant to offer real light, leaving the world in a perpetual blue twilight. The air is damp, but the cold is so profound that moisture rarely remains liquid for long; it clings to surfaces as a layer of slick frost that crunches underfoot, or condenses into a fine, drifting mist that hangs heavy in the stillness.

What covers everything is the vegetation. It is a massive, slow-growing organism of a moss-like substance, but it is not soft. It forms a rigid, fibrous lattice, like petrified sponge, that climbs the gorge walls and spreads across the mud-flats in vast, hummocked fields. Its color is a deep, bruised green, almost black in the shadows, and its surface is pitted with countless tiny holes that release the faintest hiss of escaping gas. This is the source of the sound.

The sound. It is the defining feature of this place, a constant, grinding drone that emanates from the very substrate. It is not loud in the way of a thunderclap or a factory, but pervasive and inescapable, a low-frequency vibration that you feel in your bones and teeth more than you hear with your ears. It is the sound of a billion slow processes: the creak of expanding frost within the moss-structure, the groan of settling mud, the perpetual, low-thrumming hum of a half-buried power plant.

And there it is, half-swallowed by the moss-fields: the power station. It is a hulking, concrete and rusted-steel structure, its windows dark and shattered, its roof a sagging membrane of frozen vegetation. A single smokestack, crooked and scarred, points uselessly at the narrow sky. This is not a ruin of war or time, but a ruin of inactivity. It is an industrial heart that has simply… slowed to a near-stop. The great turbines inside do not spin with their old fury; they turn at a glacial pace, once an hour, once a day, driven by some geothermal trickle or stored momentum, generating just enough power to keep the area’s few surviving systems from complete decay. This slow turn is the source of the deep, regional vibration.

Scattered around this central mass are the remnants of a small settlement or industrial outpost. Prefabricated buildings, their walls buckled and invaded by the fibrous moss, sit in the gloom. A single rusted rail-line, choked with frozen soil and plant matter, leads from the power station into the dark throat of the gorge. The air smells of wet stone, ozone, and the slow, sweet decay of the moss.

The life here is rare. Pale, thin beetles, their carapaces like frosted glass, crawl over the moss with glacial slowness. A few birds, resembling skeletal herons with pale, reflective eyes, stand motionless for hours in the mud-flats, their patience absolute. They are adapted to the drone, perhaps even using it to sense vibrations in the substrate.

This is a place of deep time made shallow. The erosion is young, the features sharp where new mud has slumped, but the processes that shape it—the growth of the moss, the creep of frost, the turn of the turbines—are agonizingly slow. The cold is a constant, damp presence, a jailer that keeps everything in a state of suspended, noisy animation. It is not a dead world, but a world living on a timescale so vast and patient that it feels frozen, its only testament the deafening, vibrating song of its own slow existence.
