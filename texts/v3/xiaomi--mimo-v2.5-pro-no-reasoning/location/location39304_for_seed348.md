# The Gall District

There is a sound like breathing, but nothing here breathes.

---

You come to it through a narrow defile of slag and old brick, the ground soft underfoot — not with soil but with a mineral sludge that has the texture of something that was once harder and has given up. The walls on either side are low, perhaps twice your height, built from a dark stone that sweats. Condensation runs in slow, continuous rivulets down every surface. The air is cool and carries a mineral tang, like licking a coin, and beneath that something yeasty and alive.

The district opens before you without fanfare. There is no gate, no threshold — the defile simply widens and you are inside what was once a factory complex, or a district of factories, or perhaps something that was built to look like a factory district for reasons you will never quite understand. The buildings are low and wide, mostly one or two stories, with flat roofs that collect standing water in shallow grey pools. Smokestacks rise here and there, but thin ones, architectural rather than functional — or if they once served a function, that function has been absorbed into something else. Pipes run along exterior walls, some as thick as your torso, some thin as fingers, all of them sweating, all of them old. They connect building to building in a dense but legible network, and the fittings are corroded to a uniform chalky blue-green that has become, over what must be a very long time, the prevailing color of the district.

Nothing grows here. The surfaces are too slick, too mineralized, too actively hostile to anything with roots. Where moss might take hold in another damp place, here there is only a thin biofilm — not a film of organisms but a film of *residue*, like the slow deposit of something exhaled. You can wipe it away with your thumb and beneath it the stone is clean and dark. It returns within hours.

The sound is the first thing that overwhelms you and it does not stop. It is mechanical in origin — engines, you realize, the deep thrum of industrial engines — but the sound has been running so long that it has become *the sound of the place*, like the sound of a river becomes the sound of a valley. It comes from everywhere. Some of it is low and tectonic, a sub-bass vibration you feel in your sternum. Some of it is higher, a metallic keening from the pipes, from somewhere inside the walls. You cannot locate any single source. The buildings seem to *produce* the sound the way a hive produces heat — as a byproduct of being what they are.

And then you notice the fauna.

They are on every surface. In every shadow. Filling every pipe-opening and window-frame and eave-gap. They are not insects, not mammals, not anything with a comfortable name. The closest word might be *moltlings* — they have the segmented look of arthropods but the soft translucence of something that has recently shed a harder casing, and they are always in the process of shedding, always half-in and half-out of something, always mid-transformation into a form they never quite reach. They range from the size of your fist to the size of a small dog. They are pale, almost colorless, with the faintest blush of the same blue-green that patinas the pipes.

There are so many of them that the buildings appear to *ripple*. A wall that you thought was textured stone resolves, as your eyes adjust, into a living surface — thousands of moltlings packed together in overlapping layers, their segmented legs interlocked, their bodies pulsing in slow, synchronized waves that correspond to the deep engine-thrum. They are the texture of the district. They are the walls and the eaves and the undersides of pipes. When one moves, a hundred move, a shifting mosaic of translucent bodies that makes the architecture seem to breathe.

But nothing here is changing.

This is the thing you must understand, the thing that will take you some time to see because the sound and the movement and the sheer *density* of life will fight against this recognition: the Gall District is in absolute equilibrium. The moltlings are not growing. The engines are not consuming fuel — or if they are, the fuel is as eternal and self-renewing as the sound it produces. The condensation does not accumulate into flooding. The biofilm does not thicken into rot. Nothing decays. Nothing is built. Nothing is repaired.

You could leave and return in a year and the same moltling would be in the same position on the same pipe, mid-molt, in the same state of almost-becoming.

The buildings themselves show this. They are weathered — deeply, profoundly weathered — but the weathering stopped at some point in the past and what remains is a surface that has achieved *finality*. Every edge is softly rounded. Every crack has been filled by its own slow contraction to a precise width. The stones fit together now not because they were cut to fit but because centuries of damp and pressure have negotiated their positions into a permanent arrangement. The district looks old the way a canyon looks old: not ruined, but *settled*. Found.

There are streets, if you can call them that — narrow passages between the low buildings, paved with the same dark stone, their surfaces worn into shallow channels by something that once moved along them in great numbers. The channels are dry. The walls on either side pulse with moltlings. You walk through a corridor of living tissue stretched over ancient infrastructure and the engine-sound parts around you like water around a stone and re-forms behind you and you are, for a moment, in a pocket of slightly lesser noise where you can hear the wet clicking of thousands of tiny legs adjusting their grip.

There are rooms you can enter. The doors are open — nothing is closed here, nothing is locked, because nothing needs to be protected from anything else. Inside, the industrial character becomes clearer: long tables of rusted iron, gears mounted to walls, belts and chains hanging in loops from ceiling fixtures. Everything is still. Everything is damp. Everything is covered. The moltlings use the machinery as habitat the way coral uses a shipwreck — the shapes of industry have become the shapes of ecology, or what passes for ecology in a place with no plants and no recognizable food chain.

What do they eat? You cannot tell. They may eat the biofilm. They may eat each other, slowly, in ways too gradual to observe. They may eat nothing at all — may be sustained by the vibration itself, by the engine-thrum, parasites of sound rather than matter.

The light is normal — grey daylight filtering through the low overcast that seems to be a permanent feature of this place's sky. Not dark. Not bright. A light that reveals everything without dramatizing any of it. In this light the district looks almost municipal, almost banal — a damp factory town, quiet in its old age, its machines running out their last years. But the sound says otherwise. The sound says: *nothing here is running down. Nothing here has a last year.*

You stand in the center of a small square — four buildings around a bare stone ground, a dry fountain at the middle, its basin filled with a living mass of moltlings so dense they form a single trembling surface like the skin of pudding. The engine-sound is loud here, reverberating between the four walls. The moltlings pulse. The pipes sweat. The condensation falls.

Nothing will ever change here. You understand this now. The district reached its final state and it will hold this state — damp, loud, alive with creatures that neither grow nor die, housed in structures that neither crumble nor are renewed — for as long as whatever produces the engines-sound continues to produce it, which is forever, because it is not *producing* it, it is *being* it.

You are the only thing here that is temporary.
