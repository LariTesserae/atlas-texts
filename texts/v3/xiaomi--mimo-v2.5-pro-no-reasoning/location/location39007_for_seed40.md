# The Plateau of Manufactured Summits

## First Approach

You would not know you were high, at first. There is no moment where the air thins and your lungs reach. The ascent was constructed long ago — terraced ramps of pressed aggregate winding upward through what might have been a mountain range, once, before someone decided the mountains were better replaced. The ramps are wide enough for three ore-haulers abreast, their surfaces scored with tire-grooves worn shallow by centuries of careful, infrequent use. The walls on either side rise in perfect industrial angles: retaining faces of bolted steel plating over aggregate cores, bolted and riveted and welded, every seam visible, every fastener a rust-bloomed coin.

You notice the quiet first, before anything else. A place this size, this dense with structure, should roar. But sound here arrives as if through felt — muffled, domesticated. A deep, subsonic hum exists at the threshold of hearing, the kind of vibration you feel in your molars rather than your ears. Somewhere far below and far within the plateau, enormous generators turn at the speed of glaciers. The air itself seems to have forgotten how to carry noise. Even your footsteps on the ramp produce a dampened thud, swallowed by the aggregate before the echo can form.

## The Upper Surface

Emerging onto the plateau's crown, the world opens, and you understand the elevation without seeing a horizon.

The scale is regional. The constructed surface extends in every direction — a made landscape the size of a valley, maybe larger, perhaps the size of a small country's forgotten province. It is not flat. It rolls. Ridges of housing-blocks and storage geometries rise and fall like drumlins left by a glacial age that never happened. Between them, shallow basins hold their own atmospheres. The geometry is not wrong, exactly, but it is *insistent* — every angle means something, every surface was placed with intention, and yet the accumulated effect is of a language spoken so long it has become its own weather.

The ground is steel grating over structural frame over deeper structural frame, all the way down. In places, the grating is overlaid with poured composite, smooth and pale grey, cracked now in patterns that look like river deltas but are not. In other places, the grating is exposed, and you can see through it: darkness below, the occasional orange wink of a maintenance light, the suggestion of tremendous depth. You walk on the skin of something that goes down farther than it should.

Everything is bright. Not with sunlight — though some diffuse, sourceless illumination passes for daylight here — but with *surfaces*. The metals are polished by condensation and time into dull mirrors. The composites are pale. The glass (so much glass, used as structural material, as wall, as floor in certain zones) catches and redistributes light until the whole plateau seems to generate its own luminance from within. Shadows are thin and uncertain, cast from multiple directions by the geometry's own reflective complexity. The overall effect is of a landscape in which darkness has been architecturally excluded but brightness has no single source to thank.

## Moisture Without Water

It is damp. Not wet — there is no river, no lake, no open water visible on the plateau's surface. But condensation beads on every cool metal surface, gathers in the composite cracks, makes the grating slippery in the mornings (if mornings exist here; the light simply *is*, cycling through bright and slightly-less-bright on a schedule too slow to notice). A fine mist sometimes rolls through the basins between ridges, not weather exactly but the plateau breathing — warm air from the generator-depths meeting the cool highland atmosphere at the surface, condensing, drifting, settling.

In certain low points, the grating has buckled, and water has pooled in the depressions — shallow, grey, mineral-tasting. These puddles are the closest thing to natural water. They reflect the bright ceiling of light and the underside of nearby structures. They are not stagnant; a slow, almost imperceptible current moves through them, drawn by pressure differentials in the structure below. They are the plateau's lymph.

The temperature is cool — the elevation does this, and so does the metal, which holds the cold in its bones even when the light is bright. You can see your breath in the mornings. Frost forms on north-facing surfaces in cycles so slow that the frost itself seems structural, like something that was installed.

## The Inhabitants

The fauna is abundant, and this is the strangest thing about the plateau, because nothing here should be alive, and yet everything is.

Birds — or things that function as birds — roost in the upper latticework of the taller structures. They are the right shape but the wrong material; their feathers catch light like fiber optics, and their calls are the only sounds that seem to propagate normally through the quiet air. They call rarely. When they do, the sound is startling, almost painful in its clarity after so much muffling. They nest in the bellies of old industrial vents, building structures of wire and synthetic fiber that mimic the geometry of the plateau in miniature.

In the basins, where the moisture gathers, things grow — not vegetation exactly, though you might call it that. Crusty lichens of industrial origin, fungal analogues that metabolize rust, mats of bacterial slime in pale greens and ochres that coat the underside of grating where condensation drips. These are sparse. They do not spread far. But they are persistent, and they form the base of a food web that includes crawling things — arthropod-like creatures with too many segments, metallic carapaces, legs that click on the grating with a sound too quiet to carry. They are everywhere if you look. Under every overhang, inside every pipe, in the darkness beneath the grating, in the condensation gutters. Thousands of species, probably, most of them small, most of them silent.

Larger things move in the deeper structure — you hear them sometimes, a heavy displacement of air through a ventilation shaft, a distant scraping that resolves into nothing when you turn to listen. The plateau is full of animals that have made this impossible architecture into habitat, and they have been doing so for a very long time, and they are better at navigating it than you will ever be.

## The Breakage

Reality here is not entirely well.

It is subtle. A corridor that is longer than the structure it runs through should be able to contain — not dramatically so, not impossibly so, just *enough* that you notice if you count your steps. A room where the light comes from a direction that has no corresponding source. A window that looks out onto a sky that is the right color but the wrong *kind* of blue, as if it is being rendered from a description of blue rather than blue itself.

These are not glitches. They are accumulations. The plateau has been here long enough that its construction has begun to *mean* something, has begun to exert its own logic on the space it occupies. The geometry has started to dream. Rooms connect to rooms they should not. Stairs descend further than the structure has depth. In certain places, if you stand very still and very quiet (which is easy here, in the silence), you can feel the space around you *settling*, adjusting, like a house made of cards deciding whether to stand or fall, perpetually, forever.

The sharpness of the features — the young erosion — is part of this. The plateau was not weathered into its current state. It was *recently broken*. Something moved through here, or something changed, and the structures cracked and sheared and left edges like broken glass. The damage is everywhere: split beams exposing rebar-skeletons, shattered composite revealing hollow cores, panels torn away to show the darkness and wiring and plumbing behind the walls. But the damage is old enough to have settled, and new enough to still be sharp. The plateau was wounded, and it has not healed, and it is not getting worse. It simply *is*, in its broken state, and has been for longer than you would like to think about.

---

## The Slow Drift

Nothing here changes quickly.

The generators below turn at their ancient speed. The moisture condenses and evaporates on cycles measured in days. The fauna reproduces in seasons you cannot perceive. The light brightens and dims on a schedule that might be a day or might be a century. The reality-breaks neither worsen nor heal. The sharp edges of recent destruction blunt by millimeters per decade.

If you sat down on the grating, in the bright silence, and watched, you would see a world in near-stasis — a plateau of manufactured highland where everything that was going to happen has already happened, and what remains is the long, patient, damp, quiet aftermath. The structures hum. The birds call, once, and are silent. The frost forms and melts and forms again. The puddles creep. The arthropods click.

And underneath it all, in the deep structure, in the places where the grating gives way to void and the maintenance lights pulse orange like slow heartbeats, something enormous continues its patient work — the generators, the mechanisms, the reason all of this was built in the first place. No one remembers what the reason was. The plateau does not need a reason. It is here. It is made. It is full of small lives and slow processes and wrong rooms and quiet light.

It endures.
