# The Drowned Fells

---

You are beneath the surface. There is no horizon line to mark where the world ends and the sky begins, because there is no sky. There is only water — cold, dark, pressing in from every direction — and it has been here long enough that it thinks of itself as the true shape of the world.

This was a mountain range once. You can tell. The terrain still carries the logic of high granite uplift: ridgelines that plunge into canyon walls, saddlebacks between drowned summits, the occasional tower of rock rising from a plateau of silt like a broken tooth. The peaks are gone — some sheared off, some simply smothered — but the bones of the land remember what they were. Erosion has had time to soften the hard edges. Boulders wear mantles of silt. Cliff faces are scalloped into shelves and alcoves by centuries of current. Nothing here was built quickly. Everything has been settling into its own shape for a very long time.

The water is glacier-cold. Not freezing — not ice — but the kind of cold that lives in deep water and never warms, that seeps into every crevice and carries mineral tastes: iron, limestone, something faintly sulfuric from vents buried deeper still. The temperature does not fluctuate much. It is simply *cold*, and it has always been cold, and it will always be cold. You accept this the way you accept the pressure in your ears.

---

## The Growth

The vegetation is what makes this place strange.

There are no animals here. None. Not fish, not worms, not the tiniest zooplankton drifting in the water column. The water is entirely, unnervingly *animal-free*. Every moving thing you see is plant, or something that has agreed to be plant. And there is a great deal of it.

Kelp forests dominate the mid-elevations — the saddles between peaks, the shelf-edges where the old mountains dropped off into deeper canyons. But this is not kelp as you have known it. The stalks are thick as a human thigh, dark green-black, and they do not merely sway with the current. They *conduct* it. The forests bend in coordinated waves, whole stands shifting together like a crowd turning to look at something, and when they move they make sound — a low, fibrous groaning that resonates through the water like the hull of a ship. It is one of the loudest things here.

Between the kelp, the seafloor is carpeted in a mat of something finer: a creeping, rootless moss that drapes over every surface — over boulders, over fallen rock, over the remains of old structures — in long, ropy curtains. It is bioluminescent. Not brightly. Not enough to read by. But enough to *see*. The light is a pale, sickly blue-green, the color of deep cold things, and it pulses. Slowly. In time with something — the current, maybe, or some internal rhythm. The pulse is not synchronized across the whole mat. Different patches breathe at different rates, so the seafloor seems to shimmer and shift like a living thing dreaming restlessly. In the absolute dark of the deeper canyons, this is the only light. It makes the rock faces look like they are breathing.

Higher up — on the old summits, where the water is shallower and the pressure less crushing — there are forests of a different kind. Enormous, branching structures that resemble coral but are not coral. They are a plant, or a colony of plants, growing from a single root-stock embedded in the granite. The branches extend outward in flat, fractal fans, each one fringed with thin filaments that catch mineral particles from the current. They are pale — bone-white, sometimes faintly rose — and they are everywhere on the summits, dense enough that navigating between them is difficult. They creak. The water transmits the sound of their growth, the slow splitting of crystalline tissue, a sound like ice cracking on a lake in spring. These are among the oldest things here. They have been growing since before the water finished rising, and they show it: their bases are thick, knotted, fused with the stone itself, and they have worn smooth the rock around them through sheer persistence.

---

## The Structures

Someone lived here. Before.

The remains are scattered — not concentrated in any one valley or ridgeline, but found across the whole range, a handful of structures in each area, widely spaced, as though the people who built them preferred distance. Or needed it.

They are small. Most are single-room affairs, built from local granite, dry-stacked without mortar, the stones fitted with a precision that speaks of patience and sharp eyes. The walls are low — hip-height in most cases — and the roofs are gone, if they ever existed. Some might have been open-topped to begin with. Some have subsided into the silt so that only a few courses of stone remain, the outline of a rectangle barely visible under the pale blue-green moss. Others are better preserved, standing in quiet ruin on the summit plateaus where the current is gentle, their interiors filled with still water and drifting curtains of plant matter.

There are no tools in them. No artifacts of daily use. No metal, no ceramic, no fabric. If these people had possessions, the water has taken them — dissolved them, buried them, scattered them across the canyon floors as unrecognizable debris. What remains is only the stone, and the stone remembers nothing.

But the walls were built with care. In one structure, on the highest remaining summit, the stones of the western wall are arranged in a pattern — not decorative, exactly, but deliberate. A series of recesses, each one slightly different in depth and width, like a set of shelves made for objects of specific sizes. The objects are gone. The shelves remain, empty, filling with water and moss and pale light.

These structures are *old*. The erosion patterns confirm it — the stone is weathered to match the surrounding bedrock, the silt has banked up against their foundations as high as it would against any natural feature. They have achieved equilibrium with the landscape. The water does not distinguish between ruin and mountain. They are the same thing now.

---

## The Sound

This place is loud.

Not with voices. Not with animal calls or the crack of branches or the hum of insects. With the *world itself*. The water carries sound with terrible efficiency, and this world has much to say.

The kelp groans. The summit-coral cracks and pops as it grows. Currents push through narrow canyons between the old peaks with a deep, resonant thrumming that you feel in your sternum before you hear it — the sound of enormous volumes of water forced through tight spaces. Far below, in the deep rift valleys where the mountains drop away into true abyss, there is a sound like breathing: the slow, rhythmic exhalation of thermal vents, pushing warm mineral water up through fissures in the rock. The warmth does not last. The cold swallows it within meters. But the sound rises, a low, moaning oscillation that never fully stops and never fully repeats.

And above everything, muffled and distant but omnipresent, is the sound of the ice. This body of water has a ceiling — a thick, creaking cap of ice that spans the entire surface, sealed over the drowned mountains like a lid on a jar. The ice shifts. It cracks along stress lines with sounds like distant thunder, and when it fractures the reports travel down through the water and bounce off the canyon walls, arriving from every direction at once, so that for a few seconds the whole world seems to vibrate. Then silence, relative silence, and then the kelp begins its groaning again.

---

## The Motion

Nothing here is still. The parameters of the place ensure constant activity.

The currents are driven by thermal differentials — cold surface water sinking, warm vent-water rising — and by the slow, relentless rotation of the ice cap overhead, which turns the entire upper water column in a vast, lazy gyre. Kelp forests bend and recover, bend and recover. The bioluminescent moss pulses and shifts. The summit-coral fans tremble in the current, their filaments filtering, filtering, always filtering.

There are weather patterns, of a sort. Water at this depth and temperature develops layers — thermoclines, haloclines — and these layers shift and collide. When they do, the water stirs: silt lifts from the canyon floors in great rolling clouds that take hours to settle, turning the already-dim water into a blind, particulate murk. The plants endure this. They have always endured this. The silt settles on their surfaces and they grow over it, incorporating it, so that the oldest growths carry a geological record in their tissue — layer after layer of sediment, compressed into something that resembles wood but is not wood.

Deep in the canyons, the thermal vents open and close on cycles that last months or years. When a vent opens, the surrounding rock warms slightly, and the plant life responds — growing faster, reaching toward the heat with long tendrils that extend out across the bare stone like fingers. When the vent closes, the tendrils die back, leaving behind pale, threadlike scars on the rock. The cycle is visible in the stratigraphy of the canyon walls: a repeating pattern of growth and recession, growth and recession, like tree rings written in fossilized vegetation.

---

## What It Feels Like

To be here is to be cold, and dark, and pressed upon by water that has no interest in you. The light is faint and pulsing and comes from below, which is wrong, which means the shadows fall upward. The sound is constant and sourceless — it comes from everywhere, from the groaning kelp and the cracking ice and the breathing vents and the thrumming current — and your own movements contribute to it, each gesture generating a small pressure wave that the water transmits outward and away, diminishing but never quite vanishing.

There is no life that notices you. The plants do not care. They grow and pulse and filter and groan on their own schedule, following rhythms that predate any human presence and will outlast it. The ruins are the same way — patient, empty, filled with water and light and silence. They do not mourn. They have been underwater for long enough that mourning would be absurd. They are simply *here*, another feature of the terrain, as weathered and settled and quietly strange as everything else.

The strangeness is not hostile. It is not even particularly dramatic. It is simply the strangeness of a world that has been left alone long enough to become fully itself — a cold, dark, vegetated, loud, active, animal-free, ruin-studded, underwater mountain range sealed beneath ice, doing what it does, breathing and groaning and growing in the dark, utterly indifferent to whether anyone is present to witness it.

The moss pulses. The kelp sways. The ice cracks far above, and the sound rolls down through the water like distant, distant thunder.
