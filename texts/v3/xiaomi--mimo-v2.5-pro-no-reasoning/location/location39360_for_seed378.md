# The Silicates

The water is everywhere and it is wrong.

Not wrong in any way a human would immediately recognize—no stench, no visible poison—but wrong in what it doesn't do. It falls from the sky as a warm mist that never quite becomes rain. It weeps from rock faces in slow, mineral-laden trickles that leave white and ochre stains. It pools in shallow basins across the high plateau, still and bright, reflecting the sky with mirror-perfect clarity because nothing alive disrupts the surface from above. The water is hot. Not boiling, not steaming in most places—though steam does rise from the deepest springs and the fissures near the thermal vents—but hot enough that you would not want to wade. Hot enough that condensation forms on every surface that dips below the ambient temperature: handrails, window frames, the shells of sleeping creatures. Everything here is damp. Everything gleams.

There are no plants. This is the first fact a visitor must absorb, and it takes longer than it should because the landscape is so *busy*—so full of color and movement and structure—that the absence feels like a trick of perception. Surely those pale terraces rising from the basin floor are made of something organic? Surely that bright green scum on the southern springs is a kind of algae? But the terraces are travertine, mineral deposits laid down by thermal water over millennia, and the green is extremophile bacterial colonies—not plants, not even close. No chlorophyll. No photosynthesis. The bacterial mats feed on dissolved sulfur compounds and metals. They are alive, technically, but they are not vegetation. They are chemistry.

The plateau itself is basalt, dark and old, smoothed by long weathering into broad terraces that step down toward a central basin. The rock is black where it's dry, dark gray where moisture clings, and shot through with veins of lighter mineral—quartz, calcite, something with a faint green cast that glitters when the light hits it. The elevation is high enough that the air thins slightly, that breathing requires marginally more effort, but not so high that the body rebels. The heat, however. The heat is the governing condition. It rises from below—geothermal, ancient, relentless—and it presses down from above, because the star this world orbits is small and close and bright, and the atmosphere is thin enough to admit its full violence. The result is a landscape baked from both directions. The rock holds warmth the way bread holds heat when pulled from an oven. The water pools are ninety degrees, a hundred, more near the vents. The air shimmers faintly at all times, not with visible heat-haze but with a subtle distortion, a softening of distant edges, as though the world is slightly out of focus.

The brightness is extraordinary. The thin atmosphere scatters less light, so the sky is a pale, washed-out blue-white rather than the deep blue of a thicker world. The mineral ground reflects: travertine is white, silica deposits are white, obsidian glass from ancient eruptions is black but mirror-smooth. Water reflects. Steam catches light and holds it in glowing columns. The cumulative effect is a landscape that seems lit from every direction, where shadows are faint and short and have soft edges, where the eye aches after an hour without rest. Sunglasses—or the local equivalent, tinted crystal lenses set in wire frames—are not optional here. They are survival gear.

---

The fauna. This is where the world reveals its character.

They are abundant. They are silent. This combination is the place's most disorienting quality, and it never quite stops being disorienting, even for those who've lived here long enough to call it home.

The smallest are thermovores—flat, disc-shaped creatures the size of a thumbnail, translucent, their internal organs visible as a cluster of dim shapes inside a flexible crystalline shell. They gather in warm pools by the thousands, clinging to submerged rocks, absorbing heat directly through their bodies. They reproduce by fission: one disc becomes two, each slightly smaller, then grows back to full size over a few days. They have no mouths, no anuses, no apparent sensory organs. They are, as far as the visiting biologist could determine, nothing but a heat engine wrapped in a mineral membrane. They are alive. They respond to stimuli—shifting away from sudden cold, clustering toward warmth gradients. But they are alive in the way a fire is alive: consuming, persisting, unthinking. They make no sound. They have no mechanism for sound. They are the baseline, the most common presence, and their silence is absolute.

Larger creatures move through the thermal margins. The lithovores are slow, heavy-bodied, low to the ground—imagine a tortoise crossed with a woodlouse, armored in accreted mineral that they add to throughout their lives, so that older individuals carry shells inches thick, studded with quartz crystals and streaked with metal oxides. They feed by scraping mineral crusts from rock surfaces with a radula-like tongue, a process that is nearly silent: a faint wet friction, if anything, muffled by the mineral substrate. They communicate, as far as anyone can tell, by leaving chemical traces on the rocks they've scraped—a shared scent-map that other lithovores read with organs in their feet. The trails overlap, cross, form complex networks that map the entire plateau. Walk across the basin floor and you are walking through a conversation you cannot hear and cannot read.

The predators are the strangest. They are elongated, sinuous, six-limbed, with bodies that seem too flexible for their size—a meter long, sometimes more, with limbs that fold and extend with an origami-like precision. Their surfaces are matte black, heat-absorbing, and their heads are dominated by large pits: infrared sensors that detect the heat signatures of prey. They hunt by ambush, flattened against warm rock, virtually invisible against the dark basalt until they strike. Their strikes are fast—remarkably fast, a blurred unfolding of limbs—and entirely silent. No hiss, no rush of air, no impact sound. They have evolved, like everything here, in silence, and they carry silence with them like a hunting cloak. They don't vocalize. They don't need to. Their mating displays are bioluminescent: patterns of amber and pale blue light that ripple across their skin in sequences complex enough to convey identity, fitness, location, readiness. The displays are the only moments of beauty in a creature otherwise designed for invisible violence.

And then there are the *others*—the creatures that defy easy categorization. The glass-shelled crabs that live in the hottest springs, their bodies heat-resistant to a degree that seems to violate material science, their shells transmitting faint vibrations between individuals in a code no one has deciphered. The colony organisms that hang from the undersides of travertine terraces like mineral stalactites, but that move—slowly, so slowly, extending tendrils into the warm water below, contracting when touched. The things in the deepest pools that might be single organisms or might be ecosystems, too strange to parse, visible only as shifting shapes in water too hot and opaque to see through clearly. The fauna of this world is not sparse. It is *dense* with life, layered with it, the entire thermal zone a web of feeding and hiding and chemical communication and silent predation. It is just that none of it makes any noise.

---

The settlements sit on the upper terraces, above the main thermal zone but close enough to tap it.

They are built of local stone—basalt blocks, mortared withiteiteiteiteite that sets hard in the heat—and they are heavy, thick-walled, rounded at the corners as though the architecture has been shaped by long wind. The buildings cluster in groups of ten or twenty, connected by covered walkways, and each group sits on a broad terrace that has been leveled and paved with the same dark stone. From a distance, the settlements look like natural features: rock formations, mesa-tops, extensions of the geology. Up close, the regularity reveals itself. Windows, narrow and deep-set, with tinted glass or crystal. Doors of metal, corroded green at the edges. Racks and channels and pipes running between buildings, carrying water—hot water from the springs, channeled through stone aqueducts with a gravity-fed simplicity that speaks of long practice. The infrastructure is industrial but not loud. There are engines—turbines that convert geothermal pressure into electricity, machinery that processes mineral water into refined silica and metal—but they are designed for a silent world, or perhaps the world has designed them. Sound-dampened casings. Electromagnetic systems that hum below the threshold of hearing. Mechanical processes that move with hydraulic smoothness, all hiss and flow, no clank or roar.

The inhabitants are human, or close enough: two arms, two legs, faces that register expression, voices that work. But they have adapted. They speak softly, or not at all. Their language has developed a robust sign component, and the spoken element uses few consonants—mostly vowels, mostly breath, mostly sounds that carry no further than arm's length. They are not silent by culture alone; they are silent because the world has taught them silence. The creatures here do not alarm at human voices—there is no predator-prey dynamic built on sound—but the silence is *right*, somehow. It fits the landscape. To speak loudly here feels like shouting in a library, like breaking something fragile. The quiet has weight.

The settlements produce: refined minerals, silica glass, metal alloys, and a kind of bioceramic material grown from bacterial colonies in controlled thermal pools. This is the industry, and it is substantial enough to mark the landscape with processing buildings and cooling towers and storage yards—but it is quiet industry, distributed industry, spread across multiple small sites rather than concentrated in a single loud complex. The scale is town-like, perhaps a few thousand inhabitants across the plateau, organized in a loose network of settlements connected by paved paths and aqueduct lines. There is governance, trade, specialization. There are workshops and dwellings and what might be temples—domed structures on the highest terraces, their interiors filled with warm light filtered through colored silica panes. It is a civilization, but a sparse one, stretched thin across a vast mineral expanse, with much empty space between its points of presence.

---

At the edges of the settlements, where the terraces slope down toward the thermal basin, the built world and the wild world meet.

This is where the most interesting things happen. The industrial discharge—warm, mineral-rich, slightly altered in chemistry—flows into channels that feed the lower springs. Some creatures have learned to exploit this. The lithovores graze the edges of slag heaps, accreting metal-rich deposits onto their shells, becoming something between organism and artifact. The glass-shells cluster on the outsides of cooling pipes, absorbing waste heat. A small predator—elongated, fast, pale rather than dark—has colonized the drainage culverts, hunting thermovores that gather in the warm outflow. These are not domesticated animals. They are not pets or livestock. They are wild creatures that have found a niche in the human-modified landscape, and the relationship is one of mutual indifference rather than symbiosis. The engineers do not encourage them or discourage them. They simply exist alongside them, the way one might exist alongside weather.

The brightest time is midday, when the small sun sits directly overhead and the atmosphere does nothing to blunt it. The light is white, absolute, shadowless. The mineral ground glows. The water surfaces are blinding. Steam columns stand like pillars. The creatures retreat—those that can—to the undersides of rocks, into crevices, into the deeper pools. The plateau goes still. Even the thermovores slow their drift. It is the hottest hour, the brightest hour, and it is the closest thing to true silence the world ever offers: no movement, no vibration, no bioluminescent flicker, just heat and light and the faint hiss of water evaporating from stone.

The strangest hour is night.

The sun sets—the small star drops below the horizon with startling speed, the thin atmosphere offering no extended twilight—and the plateau should go dark. It doesn't. The mineral ground retains heat and glows faintly: a dull orange, a deep amber, the light of cooling stone. The thermal pools shift from white to orange to a deep, saturated red. The bacteria in the springs activate some nocturnal process and begin to luminesce—a pale, eerie green that spreads across the surface of the water like spilled paint. And the creatures emerge. The predators uncurl from their daytime rests and begin their silent hunts, their infrared pits useless in the cooling dark, their bioluminescent mating displays now the primary communication channel: patterns of amber and blue rippling across black bodies, visible for hundreds of meters in the low light. The lithovores move slowly across the glowing ground, their mineral shells catching and refracting the light, each one a small moving lantern. The glass-shells in the springs pulse in synchrony—a slow, rhythmic brightening and dimming that no one has explained.

The settlements light up too: windows glowing warm yellow, the colored glass of the temples casting soft patterns on the paved terraces. The industrial buildings are darker—few windows, much of the work automated or paused for the night cycle—but the pipes and channels gleam faintly with the residual heat of the water they carry. The inhabitants move between buildings with small lights, or without, navigating by the glow of the ground and the glow of the springs and the glow of their own long memory of these paths.

From above—from a height, from one of the uppermost terraces, looking down—the plateau at night is a field of scattered light. Warm points of settlement. Cool-green pools of bioluminescence. Dark zones where the rock has cooled and nothing glows. Moving sparks where creatures pass. It is beautiful. It is silent. It is, in its way, complete—a world that has found its equilibrium, its own particular balance of heat and water and mineral and life, and that holds that balance with the quiet persistence of something that has existed for a very long time and intends to exist for longer still.
