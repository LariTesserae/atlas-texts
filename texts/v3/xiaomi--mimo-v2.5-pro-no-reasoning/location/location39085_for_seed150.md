The air bites like frozen glass, a sharp, dry cold that seems to halt the breath before it fully forms. You are standing on the jagged spine of a high ridge, a place of fresh, violent geology where the rock has recently split to expose gleaming quartz and sharp feldspar. The stone is young and restless; underfoot, it shifts and clatters, a landslide waiting for a trigger. The erosion is aggressive, scarring the slopes with fresh cuts and talus piles that look as though they were sheared yesterday.

But this raw, mineral skeleton has been utterly consumed.

Vegetation is not merely present here; it is the dominant reality. It is a total, absolute blight of mosses, lichens, and liverworts that coat every surface in velvet layers inches thick. Because of the constant dampness—the air thick with condensation and the rocks perpetually weeping moisture—the vegetation is packed with a suffocating density. There is no soil, only layers of living plant matter. Where the rock recedes, the plants do not just grow; they build. They rise into complex, convoluted structures, forming walls, arches, and hollows.

This is not a forest. It is a city of moss.

The "buildings" here are formed from thousands of years of accretion. Massive mounds of *Sphagnum* and rare ferns have grown into structures the size of cottages, their walls porous and dripping, yet sturdy enough to bear weight. The architecture is purely biological, an urban planning dictated by the slow creep of growth and the struggle for the faint light. The pathways between these green skyscrapers are narrow, twisting, and claustrophobic. You must turn sideways to squeeze between the damp, spongy walls.

The light is dim, filtered through a perpetual overcast sky and further smothered by the tall, swaying canopy of the upper moss spires. It is a twilight world, glowing with a faint, bioluminescent phosphorescence deep within the densest mats.

Despite the packed density, the space is intimate, cathedral-scale in its grandeur but room-scale in its confinement. It feels like walking through the hollowed ribcage of some massive beast, the ribs replaced by the arching fronds of giant ferns.

Sound is hushed, but the silence is not absolute. It is the sound of movement and decay. There is a constant, rhythmic *drip-drip-drip* as water condenses on the high fronds and rolls down the moss walls, pooling in the depressions below. Occasionally, a soft *thud* as a heavy piece of saturated moss detaches and slumps to the ground, a slow-motion avalanche in the green city. The wind rarely penetrates this deep, but when it does, it sounds like a distant, mournful sigh through the hollow stalks.

Life is rare. There are no large creatures here, only the small, fleeting things that can navigate the dense green maze: tiny, pale spiders that spin webs between the dripping ferns; translucent insects that hover in the still air; and the occasional, silent flight of a small, grey-feathered bird that nests in the high moss spires.

The rules of this place are written in the language of growth and gravity. The moss builds, the water weighs it down, and eventually, the structure fails. The process is slow, but it is the only motion in this quiet, packed, and dripping world. You are not exploring a landscape; you are wandering the arteries of a living, breathing, frozen organism.
