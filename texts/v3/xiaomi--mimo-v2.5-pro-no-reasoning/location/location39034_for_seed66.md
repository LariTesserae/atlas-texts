# The Terraces of Geth Maren

---

You are standing on a ledge of white calcite, and the world below you is made of water that has forgotten how to stop falling.

Geth Maren is a plateau — but that word does not survive contact with the reality. It is a continent of gently buckling limestone, somewhere between one and two kilometers above a sea you will never see, tilted imperceptibly toward its own center by the slow sag of a mantle plume that has been breathing heat into its underside for longer than any oral tradition holds. The result is a vast shallow basin, perhaps eight hundred kilometers across, where warm water collects and flows and never quite finds an exit.

There are no rivers here. Not in the way you know them. There are *sheets* — films of mineral-saturated water sliding across terraced shelves of travertine, each shelf only centimeters lower than the last, each one a different hue depending on which thermophilic bacterium has colonized it. The water is warm. Not hot — not the violent boiling of a geyser field — but a patient, persistent 35 to 45 degrees, drawn up through fissures in the limestone from reservoirs heated by the plume below. It smells of calcium and faint sulfur and something almost sweet, like the breath of a fermenting grain silo. It condenses on every surface. The air itself is a kind of rain that has not committed to falling.

---

**The mineral world.**

The terrain is terraced in every direction, as far as you can see and further. Imagine Pamukkale scaled to the size of France. Shallow basins, each one perhaps a hand's-width deep, scalloped and rimmed with deposited calcite that has had millennia to thicken into ledges, shelves, cornices, and in some places actual walls — natural dams where mineral accretion has outpaced the water's slow insistence. The colors are extraordinary: bands of white, cream, ochre, pale rust, dove grey, and occasionally a deep unexpected black where manganese has dominated the deposition cycle. Every surface gleams. The water is so saturated with dissolved calcium carbonate that anything it touches begins to calcify within days — a dropped tool, a dead animal, a piece of discarded cloth. Things that have lain here for centuries have lost their original material entirely and become sculptures of pureiteite mineral, their forms preserved but their substance replaced, molecule by molecule.

The rock underfoot is dense and layered — you can see the strata in any cross-section, thousands of thin laminae stacked like pages, each one a season's deposit. The older terraces, at the edges of the basin, have been here so long they have compacted into something approaching marble. They are smooth and slightly translucent at their thinnest edges. When the light catches them from below — from the warm water still seeping through — they glow.

There is almost nothing you would call soil. There is no humus, no decomposed organic matter accumulated into a growing medium. There is only the living mineral crust and the water beneath it.

And yet.

---

**The overwhelming fauna.**

They are everywhere, and there are too many of them, and the sound they make is the first thing that breaks your sense of the landscape's quiet geometry.

Start with the simplest layer. Every wet surface — and every surface is wet — is coated in a living biofilm. Not algae, not vegetation in any recognizable sense, but bacterial and archaeal mats fed by the chemosynthetic gradient between the mineral-rich water and the thin air. These mats are the foundation. They are the grass and the forest and the phytoplankton of Geth Maren, and they are *everywhere*, slick and faintly iridescent, sometimes thick enough to have their own texture — ripples, tufts, miniature topographies of their own. They smell earthy and alive.

Above them: the grazers. Snails. Millions — billions — of snails. Small ones, no bigger than a fingernail, with shells of calcium carbonate so pure they are nearly transparent. They move in visible tides across the terraces, consuming the biofilm, leaving trails in the mineral deposit that calcify behind them into permanent spiraling scars on the stone. Their collective movement, seen from above, looks like weather — slow, continental drifts of grey-brown bodies shifting direction based on currents of moisture and warmth invisible to you.

Above the snails: everything else. Crustaceans — pale, many-legged things related to amphipods and isopods, ranging from insect-sized to the length of a forearm, that live in the shallow water and the interstices between terrace ledges. They are the most numerous non-microbial life form by mass, and they are *loud*. Their mandibles and scraping appendages produce a constant dry rasp that carries across the terraces like the sound of someone sanding plaster in every room of an enormous house. Worms — segmented, muscular, some of them startlingly large, tunneling through the softer mineral deposits and leaving open channels that redirect water flow over years. Moth-like things with broad mineral-dusted wings that flutter up in spiraling clouds when disturbed, feeding on the bacterial films with specialized proboscises. Salamander-like amphibians, blunt-headed and pale, that bask on the warm stone and slip into the water with barely a ripple, eating everything smaller than themselves. They have no eyes. Many of the larger fauna have no eyes. The terraces are well-lit but the ecosystem does not depend on light, and the selection pressure for vision is apparently low.

And then there are the mound-builders. This is where "overwhelming" becomes architecturally literal. Certain colonies of crustaceans — related to the smaller amphipod-like grazers but specialized, social, and *enormous* — build mounds. Not small mounds. Their accumulated calcium-carbonate-cemented shelters, constructed and expanded across centuries, rise three, five, sometimes ten meters above the terrace level, pale and riddled with passages, studded with openings from which the animals come and go in organized streams. From a distance, a field of these mounds looks like a city. From a distance, it is difficult to tell them apart from the *actual* cities.

---

**The built world.**

The civilization of Geth Maren is old. Not ancient in the way of ruins and myth — old in the way of a system that has been continuously inhabited, continuously rebuilt, continuously *maintained* for so long that the distinction between the built environment and the geological one has become theoretical.

The cities are built on the stable outer terraces, where the limestone is oldest and thickest and the water flow is slowest. They are built *of* the limestone — cut blocks of travertine, mortared withite calcium carbonate that the inhabitants harvest directly from the warm springs and apply as a paste that hardens within hours into something harder than the surrounding stone. The architecture is heavy, low, and layered. Buildings are rarely more than four or five stories, but they descend — the older structures are buried under newer ones, and beneath the current streets there are layers of prior construction, each one entombed in the mineral deposits that settle over everything left undisturbed. Excavation here is also archaeology. The deeper you dig, the older the walls you find, and the more completely they have been replaced by the terraces' patient chemistry.

Streets are wide and gently graded — they have to be, because water flows across them constantly. The infrastructure includes drainage channels, carved into the stone and maintained with obsessive care, that direct the ceaseless mineral water toward designated collection basins. These basins are the city's primary industry.

**Industry.** The warm, mineral-saturated water is the resource, and the economy of Geth Maren is built on harvesting what it carries. Calcium carbonate for construction. Silica deposits for glass and ceramics. Sulfur compounds for chemical processing. Trace metals — iron, manganese, copper — precipitated out in controlled evaporation facilities that line the industrial quarters of every city. The factories are vast and low-roofed, filled with the roar of pumps and the hiss of steam and the clatter of mechanical sorting arms separating precipitates by density and composition. Geothermal heat drives turbines that generate electricity — enough to power the cities, the factories, the mechanical infrastructure. The air in the industrial districts is thick with warm vapor and mineral dust and the grinding of machinery, and the light takes on a particular quality there, diffuse and golden, as if the entire space were the inside of a very large, very warm lamp.

The fauna are farmed, too. The mound-building crustacean colonies are harvested — not destroyed, but *managed*, guided, encouraged to build in designated areas, their mounds periodically sheared for the calcium-rich cement their bodies produce. The snail tides are channeled. The larger amphibians are trapped and processed for protein and oils. The entire ecosystem is understood, if not domesticated, then at least *tended* — a kind of pastoral industrialism that treats the overwhelming animal life as a harvestable feature of the landscape, like a forest that happens to be made of shell and chitin.

---

**The atmosphere.**

The air is warm and wet and dense. It smells of mineral water, of wet stone, of the faintly sweet bacterial mats, and in the cities, of industrial smoke and hot metal and the particular sharp scent of calcite dust. Visibility varies — some days the plateau is bright and clear under a sun that glares off the white terraces until your eyes ache, and other days the condensation settles into a low, warm fog that reduces everything to a radius of a few hundred meters, muffles the light to a soft grey-white, and makes the constant animal sounds seem to come from everywhere at once.

It rains, but not often, and when it does the rain is warm and faintly mineral-tasting and it changes nothing — the terraces are already saturated, the water already flowing, the world already wet. The rain is more like a confirmation than an event.

---

**Sound.**

It is loud. The baseline is the rasp and click of the fauna — an omnipresent, granular texture of small bodies moving across mineral surfaces, of mandibles scraping, of shells ticking against stone, of the amphibians' low resonant calls that carry through the water and through the stone itself. Layered over that: the sound of water. Not crashing, not roaring — sliding. A continuous soft hiss of thin sheets moving across smooth calcite, punctuated by the deeper note of water pouring over terrace edges into the basins below. And over that, in the cities: the industrial roar, the hammering and grinding and pumping, the electric hum of transformers, the clang of metal on stone, the voices of people shouting to be heard over all of it.

At night — and the nights here are warm and long and the fauna does not sleep — the industrial noise dims but does not disappear, and what rises in its place is the full chorus of the animal world. The crustaceans are loudest then, a constant dry roar like heavy rain on a tin roof, and the amphibians add their calls, and the moth-things beat against any lit surface with a soft persistent tapping, and the snails — you cannot hear the snails individually, but collectively they produce a faint moist whisper that is more felt than heard, like the sound of a crowd breathing.

---

**Time.**

Nothing here changes quickly. The terraces grow at a rate measurable in millimeters per year. The cities accrete slowly, new buildings rising on old foundations over generations. The fauna populations are stable — overwhelmingly, oppressively stable, in numbers that have not shifted significantly in recorded memory. The mineral deposits build, the water flows, the animals eat and breed and die and calcify, and the whole system turns like a very large, very slow, very wet gear.

It is not a landscape that resists you. It is a landscape that has simply been here longer than you, and will continue to be here after you have calcified into a white lump ofiteite calcium carbonate with the vague shape of a person, and it will not notice either event.
