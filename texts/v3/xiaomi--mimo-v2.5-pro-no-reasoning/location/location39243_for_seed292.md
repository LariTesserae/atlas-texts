The world is vertical.

You exist inside the walls of a canyon so deep it has its own sky—a pale, diffuse ceiling of mist miles above, too far to be real, too close to be comfortable. This is the **Throat of the World**, a primordial scar that has been filled, slowly, by millennia of relentless rain.

The **water** is not a surface; it is a state of being. It cascades down the sheer, eroded granite faces in ribbons that are ancient and smooth, polished to a marble sheen. The air itself is liquid-heavy, a fine spray that coats everything, turning the stone dark and slick. The sound is a deafening, low-frequency roar that vibrates in your chest, punctuated by the high-pitched hiss of wind whipping through narrow passages.

**Vegetation** here is lush, but it is a clinging, vertical lushness. Enormous ferns and mosses drape the walls in thick, living carpets, their roots digging deep into the rock to drink from the endless water. They are not trees; they are a single, continuous biome that covers every surface. Strange, pale flowers bloom in the spray, their petals thick and waxy to repel the constant moisture. The air smells of wet stone, ozone, and the deep, rich scent of decay and growth in equal measure.

**Erosion** is the law. The stone here is the oldest thing you can imagine, worn down to its final, stable form. There are no sharp edges, only gentle curves and sweeping scallops carved by water that has been falling since before memory. The walls are not straight; they are a chaotic, fractal tapestry of caves, ledges, and overhangs, all shaped by the same patient, relentless force.

The **fauna** is abundant and adapted to the vertical, aqueous world. You see them as flashes of movement: long, sinuous creatures with translucent fins that glide through the waterfalls, their bodies glowing with bioluminescence. Birds with wide, membranous wings soar in the updrafts, their calls lost in the roar of the water. On the ledges, colonies of amphibious creatures cling to the rock, their croaks and clicks adding to the constant noise.

The **built** environment is scattered and faint. You find the remains of a single structure—a rusted, industrial-age boiler or a generator—half-buried in the moss on a wide ledge. It is overgrown, its metal pitted and corroded, a testament to a time when someone tried to harness this place and failed. There are no roads, no lights, only the occasional, rusted anchor point driven into the rock, trailing a frayed, rust-red cable into the abyss.

The **tech** is industrial in memory only. The metal remains speak of engines, of scaled production, but they are silent and still, relics of a different world. The only "power" here is the immense, kinetic energy of the water itself.

The **light** is diffuse and soft, filtered through the mists above. It is never truly bright or truly dark, just a perpetual, cool twilight. The water refracts and scatters it, creating shifting patterns on the walls and a constant, shimmering rain of light motes in the air.

The **dynamics** are chaotic. The water is not constant; it is a living thing. Sudden, violent downpours can turn a trickle into a roaring torrent in minutes. The wind shifts and howls. Waterfalls change course as new channels are carved. The entire canyon is a system in a state of perpetual, violent renewal, an anthill of hydrological energy. It is **turbulent**, a place where creation and destruction happen on a timescale of minutes, not centuries.

This is a place of settled finality and violent change, all at once. It is the oldest landscape, and it is still being born.
