The silence is the first thing you notice, and the last. It is not an absence of sound, but a presence—a thick, resonant pressure against the eardrums. It is the sound of stillness, of a world holding its breath.

You stand on a high, undulating plain. The terrain is a vast, rolling sculpture of unfamiliar mineral, a substance with the texture of hardenedite orite but the deep, bruised purple of a twilight sky. It is warm to the touch, a gentle, constant heat that radiates not from a sun—the sky is a uniform, milky off-white, a glare of diffused light that seems to emanate from everywhere and nowhere—but from the ground itself. This is a world under construction, and the geology is embryonic. The plains are smooth, almost polished, save for where massive, crystalline formations jut upwards. They are not eroded by time or water, but are raw, faceted, and sharp-edged, like mountains born yesterday. Their surfaces catch the milk-light and refract it into lazy, slow-moving rainbows that crawl across the purple expanse.

And on this world, life does not grow. It *erupts*.

This is the "lush" vegetation. There are no leaves, no chlorophyll, no familiar green. Instead, the landscape is studded with what can only be described as geode-gardens. Great, bulbous nodules of the same purple mineral lie half-buried in the plains, their interiors cracked open to reveal forests of quartz-like crystals. Some are the size of a fist, clustered like berries. Others are towering, arborescent spires of smoky amethyst, citrine, and milky quartz, rising dozens of feet into the air. They are not plants. They are structures. The faintest, almost sub-audible hum vibrates through them, a resonance in the bones rather than the ears. This is the industry: a silent, crystallographic production. The warm, mineral-rich air is the feedstock, the diffused light the energy source. The crystals grow in a slow, geometric perfection, a factory of pure form, turning the atmospheric dust into glittering architecture.

There is no water here. The chemistry is non-aqueous. The "soil" is a fine, dry powder ofite and metalite dust, and moisture, when it appears at all, is a greasy condensation of hydrocarbons that slicks the base of the larger crystals before being reabsorbed. There is no wind, only the deep, tectonic groan of the ground shifting infinitesimally as new crystal-lattices lock into place. There are no animals, no insects, no rustle of life. Only the hum, the pressure of silence, and the slow, inexorable growth of a mineral forest.

The scale is immense. The rolling purple plains stretch to a horizon where the milky sky seems to meet the glowing earth. Distant crystal-spires glitter like a skyline for giants. It is empty, yes—a vast space between each crystalline presence—but it is not lifeless. It is occupied by a single, overwhelming entity: the planet itself, a living geode in the process of crystallizing from the inside out. The weirdness is in the wrongness of the beauty, in the lushness that is sharp and cold, in the industry that produces only silence and stone.

The light never changes. The temperature never varies. The crystals grow a millimeter per century. Nothing ever happens here, except the slow, majestic, and silent assembly of the world.
