You are wading in the shallows of a very old, very slow argument between water and stone.

The place has no true floor, only a gradient. Your feet sink into a cold, grey silt that has the consistency of wet clay but the color of ash. It clings, releases, and clings again with each ponderous step. Above this mire, the world is a maze of jagged, dark basaltic columns, rising in clusters and arches like the ruins of some cyclopean pipe organ. Their surfaces are sharp, the erosion young. These are not rounded hills; they are the raw edges of a newly fractured plain. Water weeps from every surface, from the top of the highest spire to the very ground you stand on. It does not run in streams, but as a constant, slow exhalation of condensation, forming shimmering curtains and clinging droplets that make the air itself feel heavy and damp.

The air is dim. The sky, if there is one, is a high, uniform blanket of charcoal-grey cloud, offering no sun, no moon, only a diffuse, directionless gloaming. Light is therefore a local phenomenon. It catches in the billions of weeping droplets on the stone, creating a faint, blurry halo around each column. It gathers in the pools, but not as reflections; rather, a sickly, chemical luminescence of blue and green seems to rise from within the silt itself, a faint phosphorescence that is more unsettling than illuminating. There are no shadows, only soft gradients of dampness and darkness.

The sound is immense. It is not the sound of weather, but of geology. A deep, resonant thrumming vibrates up from the ground, felt in your bones before you hear it—a constant, low-frequency hum. Layered over this is a chorus of dripping: the slow, heavy *plock* of a large drop falling from a high ledge into a deep pool; the steady, hissing rainfall of a thousand smaller ones trickling down a face of stone; the muffled gurgle of water moving through unseen fissures below the silt. It is a symphony of saturation, loud and unceasing.

There is no life you recognize. No moss, no algae, no insects. But there are patterns. In the larger pools, the luminescent silt sometimes swirls in slow, deliberate spirals, as if disturbed by an invisible current or a sighing creature. On the faces of the basalt, you see faint, dendritic patterns of a darker mineral, like frozen lightning or the roots of a tree that never existed. They are mere geology, you tell yourself. Mere chemistry. But their arrangement feels too regular, too much like a slow, silent script being written and rewritten by the weeping water.

Occasionally, you pass a mark that is not stone. A single, squared-off block of a darker, glassier material, half-submerged in the mire. Its edges are impossibly sharp, un-eroded by the damp. There are no tool marks, no inscriptions. It is simply an object that does not belong, a punctuation mark in a language you cannot read. You find another, and then a third, forming a broken, aimless line that leads nowhere.

Time itself feels thick, syrupy. The world is in its infancy, a wet, raw, mineral kingdom still cooling from its birth. The cycles here are measured in the fall of a single droplet from a spire to the mire, a journey that might take a full minute. The deep thrumming in the ground is the planet's slow pulse. This is a place of potential, but a very, very patient one. The argument between water and stone has only just begun, and its resolution will be measured in millennia. For now, it simply... weeps.
