The light does not fall here; it is hoarded. You stand in the throat of a valley so deep and narrow that the sky is a rumor, a pale and distant crack above a canyon of green. The air is cold, carrying the scent of wet stone and a deeper, muskier odor of decay and growth intertwined. This is the Under-Tangle, a lowland basin where the world has not finished being made.

The ground is not ground. It is a living grout. A spongy, black substrate of moss, rotted wood, and boundless fungal mycelium yields underfoot with a quiet sigh, releasing a faint puff of spores. Through this matrix, water seeps constantly. It weeps from the dark, sharp-edged basalt walls in endless, silent trails, gathers in slick, black pools that reflect nothing, and is drawn upward by the voracious plant-life. Everything is wet. A cold dew beads on every surface, so thick it drips from leaves and fronds with a slow, patient *plink* that is the landscape's heartbeat.

The vegetation is not a collection of plants; it is a single, sprawling, semi-sentient architecture. Great, gnarled roots, thick as your body, braid and twist to form archways and hollows. From these, and from the raw stone itself, erupts the Tangle: a million shades of green, from the near-black of heart-leaf ferns to the acid lime of new lichen. Giant, fleshy leaves, broad as tables, curl and sag under their own moisture. Vines with bark like worn rope dangle in thick curtains, and upon closer inspection, the vines are themselves made of smaller, braided vines. Epiphytes cling to every available surface, their roots splaying like fingers into the air, drinking the dampness directly.

And in this world of pervasive, silent, vegetable dominance, the fauna is overwhelming. Not in sight, but in presence. The *life* here is a physical pressure. You hear the constant, soft rustling from within the Tangle—not a singular sound, but a thousand tiny ones, a perpetual motion of things moving through the green maze. The drip of water is punctuated by the heavy, wet *thud* of a fruit dropping into the muck, or the skittering of a thousand tiny legs on a leaf-sheath. Once, a section of vine-wall shifts, and you see the reason for the density: a colony of segmented, bioluminescent worms, each as long as your forearm, their cold blue-green light pulsing in a slow, synchronized wave through the dense foliage. They are the primary engineers, the miners and diggers of this place, their silent work creating the hollows and tunnels that define the "urban" sprawl of the Under-Tangle. Their light is the only true illumination, a ghostly glow that makes the shadows deeper.

This is a city, of a kind. The Tangle is its infrastructure. The worm-colonies are its inhabitants. The layout is not one of streets but of paths, tunnels, and chambers carved by mandible and acid into the living wood. You find signs of other, older intelligence: hand-chipped tools of sharp basalt lie half-submerged in muck, their edges still keen. Coils of fiber rope hang from a root-bridge. In one vast, cathedral-like hollow formed by the interlocking of three colossal roots, a circle of smooth, water-worn stones sits around a pit of cold, white ash. This was a hearth. The builders are gone, but their city grows on without them, built upon and through by the endless, patient life.

The activity is a slow thrum. The worms pulse their light. A creature with too many legs and a smooth, pale carapace detaches from the ceiling of a vine-tunnel and drops silently into a pool, gone without a ripple. Condensation collects on a giant leaf, trembles, and falls into darkness. Everything here is in motion, but it is the motion of growth, of saturation, of a ecosystem folding in on itself. It is a place that is both very young—the features sharp, the water not yet having carved its full path—and very old, a primordial soup held in stasis by the cold and the dark and the overwhelming, patient, quiet hunger of the green.
