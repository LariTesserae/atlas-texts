# The Weeping Plateau of Gondrem

---

You hear it before you see it — before you see *anything*. A low, grinding roar that lives in the bones of your jaw, that presses against the eardrums like weather. It never stops. It has never stopped. The old women in the outermost shanties will tell you that their grandmothers heard it, and their grandmothers before them, and that it was old *then*. The sound comes from everywhere and nowhere, and if you press your palm flat against the stone of the plateau you can feel it there too, a shuddering in the rock itself, as though something deep below is breathing very slowly through clenched teeth.

---

## The Stone

Gondrem is a highland — not dramatically so, not a peak or a cliff, but a broad, gently undulating table of land lifted some six hundred meters above the surrounding basins. The rock is ancient. Geologists who have bothered to study it (few; it is not a place that attracts geologists) describe Precambrian formations — gneiss and schist so old they have passed through metamorphism twice, three times, folded and refolded until the grain of the stone runs in contradictory directions. Where the surface has been cut or broken — by construction, by the rare road, by the slow work of water — the cross-sections show tight, convoluted banding, light and dark minerals wrung together like muscle fibers. The stone is dense and heavy and it rings when struck, a dull resonant tone that joins the omnipresent roar rather than interrupting it.

Erosion has had long, patient work here. There are no sharp edges left. Every exposed face of bedrock has been rounded into gentle swells and shallow depressions, the way a river stone is smoothed — except there is no single river, only the slow collaboration of wind and water and thermal expansion across epochs. The plateau's edges do not cliff dramatically; they slope away in long, terraced steps, each terrace a few meters wide, like the seats of an enormous empty amphitheater facing outward toward the lowlands. Where the terraces catch rainwater, shallow pans form — flat, mineral-crusted basins no more than ankle-deep, ringed with pale crusts of evaporite.

The overall impression is of a landscape that has *finished* becoming itself. Nothing here is being made. Nothing is being dramatically unmade. The plateau is in a state of stasis so deep it feels geological, felted over with the patient accumulation of slow time.

## The Water

And yet it is wet.

The sound — that grinding, subsonic roar — may have something to do with it. There are theories that the plateau sits above a massive confined aquifer, that the stone itself acts as a membrane through which water is perpetually being squeezed upward by pressure from below. Others believe the rock is simply so old and so fractured at the microscopic level that it acts as a wick, drawing moisture from the warm, humid air that pools against the plateau's flanks and pulling it slowly inward and upward through capillary action so gradual it barely qualifies as movement.

Whatever the cause: the stone weeps.

Everywhere you walk on Gondrem, there is water. Not flowing — never flowing with any urgency — but *present*. A film of moisture on every surface, catching the light. Shallow seeps that emerge from cracks in the rock and spread outward in thin, slow sheets before disappearing into other cracks fifty meters away. Pools that form in the ancient depressions and never quite evaporate, never quite overflow, maintained at the same depth by some equilibrium between seepage and evaporation that has held steady for longer than anyone has thought to measure. The air itself is heavy with moisture, warm and thick, carrying the mineral tang of wet stone — a clean, chalky, faintly metallic smell, like licking a battery wrapped in limestone.

In the lower areas, where the terraces step down and the wind is blocked by the built structures, the water gathers more thickly. Marsh-edges form: patches of ground where the stone is hidden beneath a centimeter or two of standing water and a thin, dark sediment of decomposed mineral and whatever organic matter has managed to accumulate. Step on these and the ground gives slightly beneath your foot — not quite mud, not quite solid, a spongy negotiation between the two. Your boots will be perpetually damp here. Everyone's boots are perpetually damp. It is one of the defining conditions of life on Gondrem, accepted the way people in other places accept the presence of sky.

## The Vegetation

There is almost none.

This surprises people who hear "warm" and "wet" and picture jungle. But the mineral content of the water is wrong for most plant life — too much calcium sulfate, too much dissolved iron, a pH that swings unpredictably between seeps. The substrate is bare stone with almost no soil; where soil does form, it is thin and pale and alkaline, more likeiteiteite powder than earth. And the noise — that constant, bone-deep roar — may play a role as well. There are studies (disputed, small-sample, published in obscure journals) suggesting that the subsonic vibration interferes with root development, that seedlings germinate but cannot anchor themselves, that the fine root hairs essential for water uptake are shaken apart before they can establish.

What grows here grows grudgingly. Patches of gray-green lichen colonize the wetter rock faces, forming intricate rosettes and branching patterns that look, up close, like maps of river deltas. In the sheltered depressions, between the seeps, clumps of a tough, reed-like grass push up through the mineral crust — pale, almost colorless, with waxy stems that bead moisture rather than absorbing it. These clumps are scattered widely: you might walk for twenty minutes across open stone without seeing one, then encounter a cluster of four or five growing together in a shallow basin, as if drawn there by some mutual recognition of survival possibility.

There are no trees. Not one. The tallest living thing on Gondrem is a species of low, woody shrub that grows in the lee of certain structures, spreading horizontally rather than vertically, its branches pressed flat against the stone like a handprint. It produces no flowers that anyone has seen. Its leaves are small, thick, and gray. It is alive, technically, but it does not look alive. It looks like something that was alive once and has not yet finished being dead.

The sparseness of the vegetation gives the landscape its character. There is nothing to soften the stone, nothing to break the sightlines, nothing to absorb the sound. You can see a long way across Gondrem — see the terrain unrolling in its slow swells of gray rock and dark water and pale mineral crust, unobstructed, naked, strangely honest in its barrenness.

## The City

And then there is the city.

Gondrem is built. This is the part that confounds every expectation the landscape tries to set. You are standing on an ancient, eroded, nearly lifeless highland — and someone has built a *city* here.

Not recently, either. The oldest structures are as weathered as the stone they rest on, their corners rounded, their surfaces pitted and smoothed by the same patient erosion that shaped the bedrock. They are built *from* the bedrock — the same gneiss and schist, quarried from the terraced edges and shaped into blocks that were then stacked and mortared with a calcium-rich paste that has since hardened into something nearly indistinguishable from the natural stone. These old buildings are low and heavy, two or three stories at most, with thick walls and small windows and the general aesthetic of things designed to endure rather than to please. They cluster in irregular groups across the plateau, connected by paved roads that follow the natural contours of the terrain rather than imposing any grid.

But layered over and around and sometimes on top of these ancient foundations, there is the newer city — and it is here that Gondrem becomes something more than a curiosity.

The industrial infrastructure is extensive. Smokestacks, though they produce less smoke than their size would suggest (the power comes from below, from geothermal taps driven deep into the plateau's heat). Broad, low factory buildings with corrugated metal roofs that are perpetually slick with condensation. Rail lines — narrow gauge, rusted to a deep ochre that almost matches the mineral crust — running between complexes on rails that have been worn smooth by use. Electrical lines strung on heavy ceramic insulators, buzzing faintly beneath the greater roar. The technology is not cutting-edge, not sleek; it is *industrial* in the old sense — heavy machinery, scaled production, systems designed to process raw material at volume. There are stamp mills and smelters and chemical baths and sorting floors. There are warehouses stacked with pallets of finished product — what product? the specifics change; what matters is the *capacity* — waiting to be loaded onto the flatbed railcars that crawl along the narrow tracks toward the funicular stations at the plateau's edge.

The city's population does not match its infrastructure. This is the strange thing, the thing that elevates Gondrem from merely unusual to genuinely unsettling. The buildings are there. The factories are there. The roads, the rail lines, the power stations, the water treatment facilities (necessary; the mineral water is not potable without processing). But the people are... sparse. Walk through the central district on a weekday and you will pass through streets of intact, maintained, occupied-but-not-*full* buildings. Windows lit. Doors occasionally opening and closing. The sound of machinery from the factories. But the streets themselves are largely empty. You might see a handful of figures moving purposefully between buildings, heads down, ear protection prominent — heavy industrial earmuffs or, in the case of the long-term residents, a kind of fitted cap with waxed cotton baffles that fits snugly over the ears and reduces the roar to a manageable thunder. No one lingers outside. There is no café culture on Gondrem, no park benches, no idle conversation on corners. The noise makes it impossible. You communicate in writing, in short shouted phrases, in a local sign language that has developed over generations — a practical pidgin of gestures for *yes, no, come, wait, the press is down, the eastern seep has shifted again.*

The population — perhaps eight thousand, perhaps ten; no one has conducted a census in decades — lives in a city built for many more. Some districts are essentially abandoned, the buildings still standing, still structurally sound, but unoccupied, their windows dark. These are not ruins. They are *vacancies*. The city does not decay; the stone is too durable, the maintenance culture too ingrained. But it empties, slowly, like a patient losing blood through a wound too small to see.

## The Sound

It deserves its own section. It is the defining experience of Gondrem.

The roar is not uniform. Stand still and listen — really listen, past the first overwhelming impression of noise — and you begin to hear layers. There is the fundamental: a low, droning vibration in the range of 30-50 Hz, felt more than heard, the sound of the plateau itself resonating with whatever pressure or movement operates deep below. This is constant. This never varies. It is the bass note over which everything else is played.

Above it, there are industrial harmonics — the rhythmic clatter of stamp mills, the whine of turbines, the percussive thud of presses cycling. These are not constant; they shift as different factories come on and off their production schedules, creating a changing chord of mechanical noise that crests and dips across the day. At certain hours, when multiple heavy operations cycle simultaneously, the combined sound reaches a physical intensity that makes the air feel thick, that makes your vision blur at the edges, that makes the moisture on every surface tremble and bead and run.

And beneath both of these, almost subliminal, there is a third sound: a rushing, like water moving through enclosed spaces. No one has ever located its source. It seems to come from inside the stone itself — the sound of the aquifer, perhaps, or of water being forced through microscopic channels under enormous pressure. It is the sound of the plateau's wetness made audible, and it gives the roar a liquid quality, a sense that the noise is not purely mechanical but partly *hydrological*, that you are listening to the sound of a landscape that is saturated to its bones and has been for longer than human memory.

Long-term residents go deaf. This is simply accepted. The older citizens of Gondrem communicate entirely through sign and written note, their hearing gone decades ago, replaced by a permanent ringing tinnitus that they describe, when asked (in writing), as *quiet by comparison.* Some claim to hear things in the tinnitus — patterns, rhythms, words. This is generally dismissed as confabulation. It is not generally dismissed by the people who experience it.

## The Fauna

There is almost nothing.

Birds do not stop here. Migrating species route around the plateau, as though repelled by the sound or the vibration or the sheer un-habitability of the place. Insects are scarce — a few species of small, dark beetle that shelter in the crevices of the old buildings and emerge at night to feed on lichen. There is a flat-bodied, pale spider that builds webs in the lee of machinery, catching the beetles. The webs are small, efficient, and vibration-resistant, which is perhaps the most remarkable adaptation on Gondrem: these spiders have evolved, or been selected, for the ability to maintain a silk structure in conditions of near-constant seismic noise. Their webs do not flutter; they are drawn taut and rigid, anchored at multiple points, and the spiders sit at the center with all legs braced, riding the vibration like a sailor rides a swell.

There are rats, of course. There are always rats. The Gondrem rat is large, slow-moving, and oddly fearless, having learned over generations that nothing on this plateau is stealthy enough to surprise it. They are tolerated by the human population with a weary pragmatism.

Beyond these, nothing. No mammals. No amphibians, despite the water (too alkaline, too mineral-heavy). No reptiles (too wet, too cold at night despite the warm days — the stone radiates heat slowly and the moisture pulls it away). The ecosystem is impoverished to a degree that seems almost aggressive, as though the plateau has made a deliberate decision to exclude life beyond the minimal.

## The Atmosphere

Warm. Wet. The temperature hovers in a narrow band — warm during the day, warm at night, the stone holding heat and the moisture preventing dramatic cooling. There is no sharp seasonal variation. The air moves slowly; the plateau is high enough to sit in a kind of atmospheric stillness, above the stronger winds that scour the lowlands below but below the jet stream that might otherwise clear the moisture. Clouds form over Gondrem and hang there, not quite fog, not quite overcast, a persistent gray-white haze that diffuses the light into something flat and even. Shadows are soft-edged and indistinct. The light is not dim, but it lacks *direction* — it comes from everywhere, reflected between cloud and wet stone and the metal roofs of the factories, bouncing and scattering until the world seems lit from within rather than from above.

At certain times — usually in the late afternoon, when the temperature differential between the plateau and the lowlands reaches its maximum — the haze thickens into a visible mist that clings to the surface, reducing visibility to a few hundred meters. The factories become silhouettes. The smokestacks become suggestions. The city becomes a pattern of darker shapes against the gray, its outlines softened, its hard industrial edges made ghostly and ambiguous. These are the quiet hours — not quiet in any absolute sense, the roar never stops, but the mist dampens the higher frequencies, the mechanical clatter and whine, and what remains is the fundamental drone, the deep breathing of the stone, and the rushing of water through channels you cannot see.

In these hours, Gondrem feels ancient. Not old — *ancient*. Not like a place that has existed for a long time, but like a place that has existed for *all* time, that predates the concept of time, that will outlast it. The mist and the stone and the water and the sound and the empty buildings with their dark windows and the few pale figures moving through the haze with their heads down and their ears covered — it all coheres into something that is not quite a landscape and not quite a living thing and not quite a memory, but partakes of all three.

## The Rules

What you notice, if you stay long enough, is that Gondrem does not change.

Oh, it changes — everything changes. The seeps shift over years, decades. A pool that was here last generation is gone; a new one has formed two hundred meters east. A building that was occupied is now empty; an empty one has been repurposed. The industrial output shifts as seams are exhausted and new ones tapped. The population drifts, slowly, downward.

But the *rate* of change is glacial. The fundamental character of the place — wet stone, sparse life, heavy construction, overwhelming sound, diffuse light, warm moisture — remains constant across generations. People are born here and grow old here and die here and the plateau does not notice. The stone weeps. The machines roar. The mist gathers and disperses. Nothing is urgent. Nothing has ever been urgent.

The residents of Gondrem have absorbed this temporality. They speak (in writing, in sign) with a patience that outsiders find maddening. They do not rush. They do not panic. They do not, as a rule, leave — not because they are prevented, but because the idea of leaving has the same relationship to their lives as the idea of moving a mountain. It is theoretically possible. It simply does not occur to them as an action they might take on a given afternoon.

They maintain the city. They operate the factories. They process the water. They record the seeps and the pools in careful, hand-drawn maps that have been kept continuously for centuries, a hydrological record of the plateau's slow, slow changes. They do not ask *why* the stone roars. They do not ask *why* the water weeps. These are not questions. These are conditions. You might as well ask why the stone is stone.

There is a word in the local sign language — a gesture made with both hands, palms down, fingers spread, moving slowly outward from the chest and then settling, like sediment in still water. It is used to describe the plateau itself, the city, the sound, the water, the quality of light, the pace of life, the particular way that Gondrem exists in time. It does not translate cleanly. The closest approximation, in written correspondence with outsiders, is:

*Already happening.*
