# The Overwheel

You enter through a gap in the wall that is not a wall. It is a thickness — a vertical density of trunk and vine and blade and root packed so tight that light barely reaches the interior, and you push through it the way you'd push through wet burlap. The green mass gives. It has always been giving. You pass through and the Grove opens around you.

---

It is not large. Perhaps the size of a village commons, though nothing about it suggests commons. The canopy — if you can call it that — is not overhead so much as *everywhere*, a lattice of aerial roots and trailing stems and the heavy fronds of something that grows downward, reaching for the water that collects on every horizontal surface. The air is warm and saturated and it sits against your skin like a held breath.

The ground is not ground. It is a living mat, knee-deep in some places, ankle-deep in others — a weave of runners and rhizomes and the swollen bases of plants whose names you don't have. It gives slightly underfoot, the way a sleeping animal's flank gives. Beneath it, water. Not running — seeping, pooling, sliding laterally through channels the roots have made and unmade over what seems like a very long time. The whole grove breathes water. You can hear it: a slow percussive dripping from every leaf-edge, a faint gurgling in the mat beneath you, the sound of condensation falling from the aerial roots above — an ambient, unceasing rain that never reaches the ground because it is caught by something growing before it arrives.

And beneath the water-sound, beneath the dripping and the gurgling, there is the other sound. The loud one. A deep mechanical groan, rhythmic, turning — *hrrrrrrn, hrrrrrrn, hrrrrrrn* — slow as a sleeping breath but enormous in the enclosed space. It comes from the center of the grove. It comes from the Overwheel.

---

The Overwheel is an engine. Or it was. It is a flywheel of cast iron, perhaps four meters across, mounted on a shaft that descends into the mat and the water below. The shaft turns. Slowly, with the patience of seasons. The wheel is so thick with growth that you would not recognize it as metal if you hadn't been told — it wears a living coat of moss and liverwort and a pale fungal rind that has calcified at the edges into something likeite,ite,ite — *ite*, the word forite — no. Aite.ite. Aite. Limestone. The grove has mineralized the machine. The rusted iron has given way to a calcium crust that the plants then rooted into, and the roots have threaded through the metal's weakness, and the whole assembly turns with a sound like a cart wheel on a stone road, but slower, much slower, one revolution every few minutes, and every surface of its housing and frame and the adjacent structures — whatever they were, some kind of processing building, a mill, a press — all of it has been absorbed. The walls are root. The floor is mat. The roof is canopy and the canopy is roof and the distinction has been eroded past meaning.

You find remnants if you look. A boiler, half-buried, its riveted surface mottled with lichen, a thick vine threading through the firebox and emerging from the smokestack as a single pale tendril that branches and branches again into a cascade of leaves shaped like hands. A section of pipe, still connected to something below, weeping water from a joint where the metal has thinned — and the water feeds a cluster of something that grows only here, only from this pipe, a dark glossy-leafed plant with no flowers you can see, only a scent like wet copper. A nameplate, legible if you wipe the growth away: *Harrow & Filch Municipal Processing, Est. 1887*. But the words mean nothing to the place. The place has moved past them.

---

The fauna is absence. You notice it the way you'd notice a silence — not by its presence but by the gap where something should be. No birds. No insects in any visible quantity, though you sense something very small moving in the mat when you stand still long enough. A shape — you're not sure — at the edge of your vision, low and flat and moving between the root-rows with the unhurried patience of something that has never been hunted. But when you look, there is only green. The grove is not hostile to animals. It simply doesn't need them. The pollination is mechanical — the Overwheel's turning stirs the air in slow cycles, and the plants have adapted to this, their reproductive parts oriented toward the wheel's rotation, catching and releasing pollen in timed pulses. The decomposition is fungal, fungal everywhere, a white network visible where the mat parts, threading through the dead material with quiet efficiency. The water cycle is condensation and capillary action and the slow hydraulic of roots drawing from the saturated ground. The grove is a closed system. It has been closing for a long time.

---

The strangeness is not dramatic. It accumulates. You notice that the growth patterns are too regular — that the packing of stems and leaves, which from a distance looks wild, resolves at close range into something approaching geometry. Spirals. Not the loose spirals of a fern unfurling but tight, mathematical spirals, repeating at different scales, the same curve in the tendril and the branch and the way the aerial roots descend from the canopy. You notice that the Overwheel's groan changes pitch in a pattern — not random mechanical decay but something that resembles modulation, as if the resistance of the growth on the wheel creates a changing tone as it turns, and the tone follows a sequence you almost recognize. You notice that the water pooling on the largest leaves is not still but moves in slow eddies that mirror the wheel's rotation, as if the turning below has imprinted itself on the water above.

You notice that you cannot find the source of the Overwheel's power. There is no steam. There is no fuel. The shaft descends into the waterlogged ground and turns, and has been turning for longer than the nameplate's date, because the calcification on the wheel is thicker than 137 years of mineral accretion would suggest, and the root-growth through the metal shows annual rings — you count them — too many. The machine predates the factory. Or the factory predates the machine. Or the distinction is as meaningless here as the distinction between wall and wood.

The grove does not care. The grove is not watching you. The grove is turning, slowly, wetly, in the warm air, growing into the shape of a place that has existed long enough to become itself, and the sound of its machinery is the sound of a heartbeat, and the sound of the water is the sound of breathing, and if you stay long enough — you can feel this — you will begin to grow into the mat, and the mat will grow into you, and the distinction will erode the way all distinctions erode here: slowly, wetly, past meaning.
