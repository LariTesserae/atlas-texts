# The Cistern Under the Lagoon

The light is the first thing you register — it comes from above, from everywhere, a warm diffuse whitegold poured through a meter of clear water and the thin skin of plaster that forms the ceiling. It pools on the floor. It finds the edges of things. The whole chamber glows the way a glass of water glows when you hold it up to the sun.

You are inside something *made*. Every surface is plaster — lime plaster, hand-applied, still bearing the marks of palms and wooden floats. The walls curve upward into a low barrel vault, maybe two meters at the apex, maybe less. You could lie on the floor and touch both walls with outstretched arms. The plaster is white, freshly cured, calcium-pale. It is clean. Not a century of sediment has touched it, because this place is not a century old. It is, perhaps, a season old. A few weeks. The joints where wall meets floor are still sharp — no roundedness, no softening, no bacterial film except the faintest beginning of it, a ghostly translucence along the lowest courses like breath on glass.

The water is warm. Bath-warm, sun-warm, thirty degrees at least. It is also *loud*. Not echoey — the chamber is too small for true echo — but a constant breathy roar, as though the room itself is exhaling. Water enters through two openings in the north wall, sheeting in from some adjacent space in broad shallow curtains. It leaves through a channel cut into the floor, a straight-lined groove just wide enough to fit two fists side by side, running the length of the chamber before vanishing through the south wall. The inflow pushes. The outflow pulls. The water between is never still — it eddies, it ripples, it sheets across the plaster in moving patterns that the bright overhead light breaks into a thousand shifting petals of brightness on every surface. The sound is the sound of a fountain that has forgotten how to be a fountain. It is the sound of water *in transit*, in bulk, moving through a place not for beauty but for function.

This is a cistern. A settling chamber. Part of a system — you sense the system around you even though you cannot see it, the way you sense the whole body of an animal when you see one organ. Water comes in carrying sediment, grit, the silt of some upstream channel. It slows here. It drops its burden on the plaster floor, a fine film of beige-pale powder that the current pushes into slow curlicues. The chamber is doing its work. It is working right now, in fact — active, purposeful, mid-process.

And there is almost nothing alive in it. No fish. No insects. The water is too warm, too shallow, too recently built for any ecosystem to have colonized it. But there are — here and there, at the margins — the faintest stains of green, algae barely begun, tentative, exploratory, the first claimants on this new territory. A patch on the vault where the plaster is slightly rougher. A whisper of color near the inflow where dissolved nutrients are richest. They are barely present. They are the beginning of something that will, in a few years, coat every surface.

The builders were not here when you arrived. They were not, perhaps, here when this place filled with water. The cistern was built dry, from above, the vault formed over a wooden frame that was later removed. The plaster was applied in stages, each layer still legible in the varying thicknesses — here a centimeter, there two. The inflow channels were fitted with sluice-gates, simple ones, wooden, now open. The outflow channel was cut with a straightedge and a chisel. The tools were not sophisticated. The geometry is not perfect. But the intent is total. There is no stone here that was not placed. No wall that was not rendered. No floor that was not dressed. Even the water, in a sense, is part of the construction — invited, channeled, made to work.

It is, from the inside, a room. A room with no door, no furniture, no windows, no air. A room made entirely of water, light, and lime. You float in its center, and the current moves past you, and the light shifts on the vault above as the surface outside ripples, and the sound fills your skull — the steady, uncreative, industrious sound of water doing what it was told to do.

If you pressed your hand to the wall, you would feel the plaster vibrate faintly with the flow. If you closed your eyes, you would see the light still, red and golden through your eyelids. If you waited long enough — hours, days — the algae would grow toward you. But nothing else would come. No creature. No voice. Just this small, bright, roaring, empty, human-made room beneath the water, doing its one small job, newly built and already working, sharp-edged and warm and loud with the sound of its own purpose.
