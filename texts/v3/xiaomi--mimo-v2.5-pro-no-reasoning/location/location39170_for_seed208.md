# The Resonant Flats

## The Stone

There is no soil here. That is the first thing to understand.

The bedrock is the land — a single, continuous sheet of dark basalt, smoothed by an age so complete that even the concept of mountains feels like folklore. Whatever uplift and collision and volcanism built this basin happened so long ago that the resulting surface has been planed nearly flat by eons of rain, wind, and the patient chemical dissolution of water working on stone. The undulations that remain are gentle — rises of a meter or two stretching for kilometers, shallow depressions where water gathers in sheets so wide you cannot see their edges in the dim. If you walk across this landscape, your footsteps ring on stone. There is nothing else to step on. The basalt is dark grey where it is dry, black where it is wet, and rust-orange where mineral deposits have built up in slow arterial veins that map the subsurface hydrology.

The erosion is geological in its completeness. This is a surface in its final form. It will change — everything changes — but the changes now are incremental, chemical, atmospheric. The age of dramatic reshaping is over by millions of years. What remains is a plateau of stone so old and so thoroughly worked that it has the quality of something settled. Patient. Done.

## The Water

Water is everywhere, and it is never still.

The water table sits at or above the surface across most of the basin. In the lower depressions, there are shallow lakes — not deep, rarely more than waist-height — but wide, stretching to horizons that blur with moisture haze. Between the lakes, the stone is slick with permanent wet: films of water sliding across the rock, seeping from fissures, condensing out of the air onto every surface. The air itself is saturated. You feel it on your skin — a coolness that is not quite fog, not quite rain, but the constant presence of water in the atmosphere, waiting to become droplets on any surface cooler than itself.

Every exposed rock face weeps. Stand near a low escarpment and you will see the water emerging from the stone itself — not flowing from a crack or spring but appearing across the entire face as a glistening sweat, gathering into rivulets that slide down the dark surface in trembling threads before joining the general sheet below. The stone has been saturated for so long that it acts as its own aquifer. Water moves through the pores of the basalt, finds the surface, and exits. Where mineral-rich water has been depositing for millennia, the escarpments are veined with white and orange crystalline crusts — calcium, iron, silica — building up in slow ruffles and terraces that look like frozen waterfalls but are made entirely of dissolved stone laid down again, molecule by molecule.

The sound of this water is the foundation of the landscape's noise. It is not the roar of rivers or the crash of waves. It is the sound of a million trickles, a billion seeps, the continuous whisper-and-tickle of water moving across stone in every direction at once. Layered. Textured. It fills the low frequencies like a breath that never exhales fully, and it never, ever stops.

## The Mats

There are no plants.

This is the second essential fact. Whatever photosynthetic organisms once existed here — if they ever did — are gone or were never. The dim light and the mineral-heavy water and the ancient basalt have not given rise to anything that a botanist would recognize as flora. There are no roots, no leaves, no stems, no chlorophyll-driven green.

But the stone is not barren.

Across the wet surfaces, there are mats. Microbial-mineral crusts that cover the rock in patches of rust, ochre, pale gold, and grey-white. They are thin — a few millimeters at most — and they have the texture of dried paint or lichen that has forgotten what lichen is. They are alive, in whatever sense that applies to a community of chemolithotrophic bacteria and archaea that feed on dissolved iron, sulfur, and manganese in the water. They exhale gases — faintly sulfurous, sometimes sharp with hydrogen — and they precipitate minerals as waste, building up the colored crusts in concentric rings and branching fractals that look like frozen lightning or river deltas seen from impossible height.

These mats are the base of the food chain. Everything here ultimately eats them, or eats something that eats them.

Between and among the mats, there are fungal structures. Not mycorrhizal networks — there is nothing for them to partner with — but free-living fungal colonies that have adapted to this mineral world with a kind of exuberance. They fruit directly from the wet stone: pale, translucent stalks rising ten or fifteen centimeters, capped with swollen structures that hold spores in a mucilaginous droplet at their apex. They look like glass nails driven into the rock. In places where the mats are thick and the water shallow, the fungal fruiting bodies are dense enough to create a low, wavering carpet that makes the stone look like it has grown a pelt. But it is not a plant. It does not photosynthesize. It feeds on the bacterial mats and on dissolved organics — on itself, on its own dead, on the waste products of every other organism in this system.

If you kneel and look closely, you can see the boundary between mat and fungus — where the bacterial crust ends and the fungal threads begin — and you can see that the boundary is not sharp. The two systems bleed into each other. They are locked in a partnership of consumption and excretion so old and so balanced that neither could exist without the other, and together they turn mineral water into something that can feed a world.

## The Fauna

And here is where the sound begins.

The fauna of the Resonant Flats is abundant, diverse, ancient in its lineages, and *loud*. The absence of plants — the absence of the great vertical structures that define most terrestrial ecologies — has forced all animal life here into two planes: the surface of the stone and the surface of the water. And both planes are crowded.

On the wet rock, the most visible creatures are crustacean-analogues — not crabs, not shrimp, but something between the two that has no terrestrial equivalent. They range from fingernail-sized to the span of an outstretched hand. Their carapaces are dark, nearly black, mineralized with iron deposits from the water they drink. They move in skittering herds across the stone, grazing on the bacterial mats with specialized mouthparts that scrape the rock surface clean in a moving front, leaving trails of bare basalt behind them that the mats slowly regrow. They are the most numerous animals here by mass, and they are the first source of sound: a continuous dry clicking and chitinous scraping as tens of millions of them move across the stone. The sound is like rain on a hard floor. It is everywhere.

In the shallow lakes, there are eel-like organisms — long, muscular, smooth-skinned — that hunt through the water and across the wet rock with equal facility. They are predators of the crustaceans. They are also, it seems, capable of something like song. The dominant species — dark-bodied, pale-bellied, roughly a meter long — produces low-frequency vocalizations from a resonating chamber behind its skull. The sound is a deep, throbbing hum that carries through water and stone with extraordinary efficiency. A single eel calling is a strange, almost musical note. Thousands of them, across the basin, calling and answering in overlapping rhythms — this is the bass note of the landscape, the reason the ground seems to vibrate, the reason your chest feels tight after a few hours here.

Above the water and the stone, there are fliers — not birds, not bats, but membranous-winged creatures with six limbs, the size of a human hand or larger. They are insect-analogues but ancient, relictual, evolved in a world without flowers or trees. They hunt the smaller crustaceans on the wing, swooping low over the mats, and they roost on the few elevated surfaces — escarpment ledges, the roofs of the scattered structures, the fungal stalks (which are rigid enough to bear their weight in large clumps). Their sound is the sharpest and most piercing element of the noise: a high, keening buzz that fluctuates in pitch as they move through the humid air. It sounds like metal being stressed almost to breaking. In swarms — and they do swarm, in spiraling columns that rise from the lake surfaces at dusk — the sound becomes a physical wall, a pressure on the ears that forces you to shout to be heard at arm's length.

And there are others. Soft-bodied things in the water — not quite worms, not quite slugs — that graze the submerged fungal networks. Tiny, hopping organisms on the rock that might be amphibian or might be something without a name. Larger predators — rarely seen, mostly nocturnal or crepuscular — that hunt the eels and the fliers and the herds of crustaceans. The food web is dense, interlocking, and ancient. It has been refining itself in this wet, mineral-dark world for a very long time.

All of these creatures together, layered on the seeping water and the resonant stone, produce a sound environment of staggering intensity. It is not a roar. It is more complex than that — a woven texture of clicks, hums, buzzes, drips, trickles, and the deep geological moan of water moving through stone. It is the loudest quiet landscape you will ever encounter: there is no single source, no sudden crash or thunder. There is only the totality of everything making its own small noise at once, and the totality is deafening.

## The Light

The sky, when it is visible at all, is a pale wash — white, faintly blue-grey, occasionally tinged with the amber or rust of mineral dust lofted from the dry escarpment tops. The atmosphere is thick with moisture and suspended particulates. Light does not penetrate sharply. There are no hard shadows here. Everything is illuminated in a diffuse, sourceless glow, as if the air itself is faintly luminous. The effect is not darkness — you can see clearly enough, in the way that you can see clearly in a room with frosted-glass walls — but there is no directionality to the light, no sense of where the sun is or whether the sun is there at all. The horizons are soft. Features emerge from haze. Colors are muted: dark stone, pale mats, the grey-white of fungal stalks, the occasional sharp rust of an iron deposit.

At what might be called dawn and dusk — you cannot tell for certain — the light shifts to a deeper grey-blue and the flier swarms rise or settle, and the eels' hum changes register, dropping lower. These are the active times. The middle of the day, such as it is, is quieter — a relative term, meaning merely that the sound drops from deafening to merely overwhelming.

## The People and Their Works

There are people here, but not many.

They live at the margins — on the slightly higher ground where the water is shallow enough to manage, near escarpments where the stone can be quarried, at the edges of the shallow lakes where the crustacean herds are densest. Their structures are small and raised: platforms of stacked basalt slabs, fitted without mortar but with extraordinary precision, elevated on low walls to sit above the general sheet of water. The roofs are woven from dried fungal stalks, layered and compressed until they are nearly waterproof. Some structures are shelters. Some are storage. Some are purpose-built for tasks — platforms for scraping and drying crustacean meat, shallow basins carved into the rock for cultivating bacterial mats in controlled conditions, narrow channels that redirect water flow for purposes of fishing or washing or bathing.

The people's technology is simple but refined. They chip basalt into blades and scrapers with a skill that speaks of deep tradition. They have metal — not smelted, but cold-hammered from surface deposits of native copper and occasionally meteoritic iron. The metalwork is functional: hooks, awls, small cutting tools. They weave fiber from the tougher fungal species. They make containers from hollowed stone and from a kind of ceramic — clay is scarce here, but they have learned to use the fine mineral sediment from lake beds, mixed with fungal fiber as a binder, dried slowly in the damp air.

The people are adapted to the noise. Their language, what can be observed of it, is partly tonal, partly gestural — they communicate in the spaces between the loudest frequencies, using pitch ranges that sit just above or below the dominant soundscape. They have developed a kind of rhythm-language as well, tapping patterns on the stone that carry through the rock with clarity even in the deafening air. You can see them communicating this way across distances: a rhythmic pulse transmitted through the basalt, felt through the feet, interpreted by long practice.

They do not fight the water. They do not try to drain or divert the basin. They live with it, on it, in it. Their children learn to swim before they learn to walk. Their dead are returned to the shallow lakes, where the soft-bodied water organisms and the eels and the slow chemical dissolution of the mineral-rich water take the body back into the system. It is not burial. It is recycling. The people understand, without framing it in these terms, that they are part of the food web — that the mats feed the crustaceans feed the eels feed whatever feeds everything — and that their place in the web is not above it.

## The Dynamic

This landscape is not static. It moves.

The water table fluctuates on cycles that are partly seasonal, partly tidal (if this world has a moon, its influence is felt here), and partly geological — deep aquifer pressures that rise and fall over periods of years or decades. When the water rises, the mats are submerged and the crustacean herds move to higher ground, and the fungal stalks bend and sway in the current. When it falls, the exposed stone dries in patches and the mats crack and curl and the eels retreat to the deeper pools, and the air tastes different — drier, sharper, with a mineral bite.

Weather comes in slow cycles. There are storms — not violent, but persistent: hours or days of heavier rain that raises the water level by centimeters, changes the texture of the sound from a weave of trickles to a continuous rushing hiss, drives the fliers to shelter. After the rain, the air is even more saturated, the mats flush with new growth (or what passes for growth in a bacterial crust), the fungal stalks stand taller with absorbed moisture, and the noise rebuilds from its temporary dampening like an orchestra returning to its instruments.

The fauna shifts with these cycles. Breeding seasons for the eels. Swarming periods for the fliers. Migrations of the crustacean herds following the mat growth. Predators trailing the migrations. The web is always moving, always rebalancing. It is active in the way that an old forest is active — not frantic, not explosive, but continuous, pervasive, and deeply, anciently patient.

## What It Feels Like

To stand on the Resonant Flats is to feel very small and very recent.

The stone beneath you is older than any memory. The water on it has been falling for eons. The noise — the vast, layered, living noise — has been building in complexity for as long as the food web has existed, which is to say for longer than your species has had language. You are standing on the surface of a system that has been running, without plants, without trees, without flowers or fruits or anything green, for an almost incomprehensible span of time. It does not need what you think life needs. It has found another way.

The dampness is on your skin. The sound is in your bones. The dim light shows you a world of dark stone and pale life and water, water, water, in every direction, as far as you can see through the haze.

The basalt hums beneath your feet. Somewhere out in the grey, an eel is singing.
