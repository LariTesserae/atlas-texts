The air is a heavy, wet blanket. It is not raining, yet nothing is dry. Water hangs in the atmosphere like a suspended ocean, a permanent, warm fog that coats every surface in a slick, cool film. It beads on waxy leaves, drips in slow, fat globules from the undersides of immense fern fronds, and collects in the cups of bromeliads that cling to every vertical surface. The humidity is so absolute that breathing feels like drinking.

You are standing—or rather, floating in a state of gentle suspension—on a high plateau, but the concept of "ground" is an abstraction. The world is a vertical tapestry. Erosion here is not a finished story; the rock is young and angry, thrust upward recently in geological terms. Sheer cliffs of basalt and granite, still sharp-edged and fractured, rise from unseen depths below and disappear into the saturated sky above. They are not barren. They are armored in life.

The vegetation is a riot of green, layered and impenetrable. Massive, prehistoric-looking ferns with fronds ten meters long droop under the weight of the air. Between them, other plants reach, their forms distorted by the quest for light. They have no need for deep roots; they draw water directly from the fog. Some are not rooted at all, existing as great, shaggy balls of moss and epiphyte, heaped upon ledges or dangling from the cliff faces by tendrils of their own making. The density is claustrophobic; every niche is filled, every surface colonized. There is no soil, only a thick, spongy layer of life—decaying matter and living growth alike—matted directly onto the rock.

Fauna is rare, but not absent. You sense it in the stillness. Occasionally, a vast, leathery shape—winged but not a bird, more like a sail—will glide silently through the deeper fog banks, its outline a momentary darkness against the diffused light. You might find a shell, the size of your torso, fused to a rock face, belonging to a creature that moved too slowly to be seen. Or a cluster of luminous, fungal nodules that pulse with a faint, cool light, not as communication, but as a byproduct of their digestion.

The light itself is even and shadowless. The sun is a brighter patch in the unbroken grey sky, its position betrayed only by a slight warming of one part of the fog compared to another. Shadows are soft, indistinct things, swallowed by the ubiquitous moisture. The color palette is a thousand shades of green, grey, and the wet, dark black of the rock.

Sound is muffled, intimate. The dominant noise is the **drip**: a constant, arrhythmic percussion as water finds its way down through the canopy of leaf and stone. There is no wind, so the only other sound is the occasional, low groan of a saturated root mass shifting, or the soft *plop* of a large water drop striking a broad leaf below. It is a world of quiet, patient processes. The water erodes the stone, but over millennia. The plants grow, but at a pace dictated by the diffuse light. The great gliding creatures move with the slowness of continents. Change here is not an event; it is the texture of reality itself.

To exist here is to be permeated. Your clothes would be soaked in moments. Your skin would always be damp. The air is not air, but a thin, breathing water. It is a high place, but it is not a sky place. It is a high place that is, profoundly and thoroughly, a **wet** place. A world suspended in its own exhalation, where the distinction between water and land, between falling and floating, has been erased.
