The heat hits you first. It is a heavy, wet blanket, smelling of hot metal and damp, green life. The air itself seems to sweat, hanging thick with a humidity that clings to your skin and turns the world into a soft-edged, hazy dream. You are standing at the edge of a place, a place that feels like it has always been exactly this, right here, for a very, very long time.

It is a single district, maybe a square kilometer at most. The ground is a low-lying marsh, a tangle of dark water and black, rich mud. But this is no natural swamp. Rising from the water, like the bones of some ancient, mossy giant, is a city. Or what remains of one.

The structures are not built *on* the land; they are woven *into* it. Great, slow-spinning waterwheels, their wood petrified to a dark, iron-like hardness, turn in the sluggish, brown currents of canals that serve as streets. These wheels, some as large as a house, power a network of groaning, clanking machinery—pumps that sigh as they move water, forges that glow with a low, constant heat, and the rhythmic, heavy *thump-thump* of massive pistons driving something unseen deep below.

But the city has been abandoned to the jungle. Or rather, the jungle has *become* the city. The architecture is a hybrid of corroded green-bronze, crumbling brick, and a living, breathing mass of vegetation. Thick, rope-like vines with waxy, dark leaves have wrapped themselves around every beam and strut, their roots delving into mortar and stone, replacing it. Fungi, in shades of rust and ochre, sprout from damp wood. Moss, so thick it looks like felt, carpets every surface, muffling sound where it can. Every rooftop is a garden, a riot of ferns, flowering creepers, and stout, hardy bushes that have found purchase in the layers of accumulated soil. Water drips constantly—from leaf tips, from condensation on metal pipes, from the overhangs of mossy eaves. The ground is never dry; your feet squelch on a path of slick, worn stone overgrown with a soft, resilient turf.

The soundscape is a chorus of two things: the slow, industrial heartbeat of the place and the living pulse of the green. The constant, low hum and shudder of the machinery provides a bass note, a vibration you feel in your chest. Over this, the sound of water is everywhere—the drip, the trickle, the gurgle of the canals, the soft splash of a drop falling from a high leaf. It is loud, but the sound is organic, a rhythm of work and weathering, not the sharp clatter of a busy city. There are no animal calls, save for the rare, distant croak of a frog. The fauna is absent; this is a world of plant and machine.

The light is normal, a bright, warm grey filtered through the high canopy of interlocking vines and the ever-present haze. It gives the place a timeless, afternoon quality. You get the sense that this district has existed in this exact state for a century, maybe more. The machines are not failing; they are just... weathered. The plants are not conquering; they are just... growing. It is a system in perfect, slow equilibrium. A single, living, breathing engine-room in the heart of the jungle, powered by water, built of wood and vine, and singing its endless, damp, industrial lullaby to itself.
