The air is thick and tastes of wet metal and loam. It is a warm, heavy breath, kept close by the sheer walls of the place. You are not outdoors, not truly. You are inside a living structure, a grove-sized biome contained within a glass-and-iron atrium that arches overhead like the ribcage of some colossal, dormant beast. The light is normal, but it comes not from a sun, but from dozens of great, spherical lamps of etched crystal that hang from the central girderwork, their glow a perfect, unwavering noon. This is a place made, wholly and completely, yet it trembles with a wild, engineered life.

The floor is not soil, but a dense, intricate lattice of copper grating, over which a spongy, emerald-green moss has been encouraged to grow, muffling footsteps to soft thuds. Water is the architect here. It weeps in constant, silent streams from the sweat-beaded iron struts overhead. It courses in shallow, braided channels through the moss, lining the copper with a skin of verdigris. It pools in basins carved from dark, polished basalt, where it is still and black, reflecting the mechanical sky. The sound is not of a babbling brook, but of a thousand quiet drips, the gurgle of hidden pipes, and the steady, rhythmic *hiss-slop* of a great water recycler pumping its contents through a network of visible, moss-furred tubes that line the high walls.

From this engineered wetness, a jungle has erupted. But it is a jungle of stunted, cultivated ambition. The trees are not tall, but broad and ancient in their contortions. Their trunks are not of wood, but of a living, fibrous composite—some bio-mechanical marvel—and they are woven directly into the structure of the atrium. Branches of this strange stuff grip the iron ribs, and from them hang orchids of impossible, velvety blue and ferns with fronds like spun glass. Vines, thick as a man's arm and corded with metallic fibers, climb every surface, their broad leaves shedding condensation in slow, fat drops. The density is immense; every cubic meter is a layered tapestry of leaf, root, and vine, a humid, green compression.

Yet, this is not a chaotic wilderness. It is a garden under an industrial heart. At the base of several of the great trunk-pillars, you can see the machinery: brass-cased pumps with slow-moving pistons, condensation coils weeping into the channels, and nutrient injectors that click and whirr softly. The air is warm, but it is the managed warmth of a greenhouse, regulated by unseen systems. There is a sense of constant, slow motion—the drip of water, the unfurling of a new frond, the subtle shift of light as one of the crystal lamps undergoes a minute-long cycle of dimming and brightening, mimicking cloud cover.

Life is sparse, but not absent. A creature, something like a salamander but with articulated, coppery joints and skin like wet leather, clings to a pipe, its throat pulsing. It watches with obsidian-bead eyes. High in the canopy-rafters, a bat-like thing with leathery wings folded like a closed umbrella hangs silently. It is a place of curation, not of the wild. The feeling is one of immense care, of a system achieving a fragile, beautiful equilibrium. It is a sealed jar, a bottle of manufactured nature, a high-altitude terrarium of impossible lushness, where every leaf is a product and every drop of water is accounted for. The slight strangeness is in the synthesis—the feeling that you are walking through a living poem about nature, written in the language of gears, glass, and engineered biology.
