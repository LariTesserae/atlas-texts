# The Singing Ice

You stand at the edge of a world made entirely of frozen water and light.

The glacier does not fill a valley so much as it *is* the valley, and the ridgeline, and the plateau beyond, and every depression and saddle between. From where you stand — somewhere above 4,600 meters, judging by the thinness of the air that stings your lungs and the ache behind your eyes — the ice field extends in every direction for what might be twenty or thirty kilometers, its surface interrupted only by the occasional nunatak: dark, angular peaks of granite that protrude like the exposed spines of some buried creature too vast to comprehend. The ice presses against these peaks on every side, gripping them, swallowing their lower flanks. Where rock meets ice there are vertical walls of frozen blue, grooved and polished, and from every groove water runs — thin streams, trickles, the faintest weeping of condensation that catches the sun and turns to diamonds before it falls.

The brightness is staggering. The ice surface, at this altitude, under a sky scrubbed thin and violet-white by altitude, does not merely reflect sunlight — it *generates* light. Every facet of every pressure ridge, every frozen wave, every melt pool rimmed with new ice becomes a mirror, and the mirrors compound upon each other until the shadows themselves seem luminous, blue-violet rather than dark, and the eye has nowhere to rest. Even with your eyes narrowed to slits, even squinting against the glare, the world is overexposed. The granite nunataks appear almost black by contrast — not because they are dark stone, but because the surrounding whiteness has consumed every other register of brightness.

The surface of the glacier is not smooth. This is the first misconception to discard. It is a frozen sea, and like a sea it has waves — but the waves stopped moving millennia ago, and now they stand as pressure ridges and ice hummocks, packed so tightly against one another that moving between them requires constant scrambling and choosing. Crevasses — those deep, blue-lit fissures — open between the ridges, some narrow enough to step across, others yawning to ten meters, their walls vertical and grooved like the interior of a cathedral. Meltwater flows along the bottom of these, dark streams moving with surprising speed over beds of gravel the glacier has carried from distant mountains. In other places, the ice surface is hummocky — mounds and domes of refrozen melt, called *sun cups*, each one the size of a torso, pressing shoulder to shoulder across hectares of surface so that walking becomes a lurching, ankle-threatening negotiation.

And everywhere — *everywhere* — there is water. Not as a lake or a river system, but as a condition of being. The ice weeps. Its surface, warmed to a fraction above freezing by the relentless sun, develops a film of meltwater that runs into every crack and crevice, that pools in depressions and refreezes into slick plates of clear ice, that percolates downward through the firn and emerges on the surface again fifty meters away. The sound of this water is not the gentle sound of a stream. Here, hemmed between walls of ice, channeled through narrow tunnels and over rough stone, amplified by the acoustic chamber of the glacier's own surface, the water is *deafening*. Meltwater rivers flow on the surface in braided, ever-shifting channels, each one a roar. Below, in the glacier's interior, water moves through moulins — vertical shafts that plunge thirty, fifty, a hundred meters into blue darkness — and the sound that comes up from them is a hollow, resonant booming that you feel in your sternum. At the glacier's terminus, where the ice finally breaks against lower rock, a wall of water pours from beneath the frozen mass in a hundred streamlets, each one screaming.

The cumulative effect is a landscape that never stops speaking. The ice itself contributes: thermal contraction cracks the surface constantly, producing sharp reports like distant rifle shots that ricochet across the field. Deep within the glacier, enormous slabs of ice grind against one another along shear planes, generating a low, almost subsonic groan that rises through the soles of your feet. The wind — and there is always wind at this altitude, a steady lateral force that presses against you like a physical wall — finds every tube and tunnel and ridge and hummock and plays them. The glacier sings. Not metaphorically. The harmonics are there, pitched high by the smaller channels and low by the larger ones, overlapping and interfering, a chord sustained without pause, a sound so constant and so loud that after a time you stop hearing it as sound and begin to experience it as the medium through which everything else exists.

There is no soil here, no loam, no substrate that could support roots. The glacier's surface is ice, and below the ice is rock, and the two have been locked in this arrangement for longer than any human reckoning. There is no vegetation — not a lichen, not a moss. The cold, the brightness, the constant saturation, the grinding and shifting of the ice surface conspire against anything that might attempt to colonize. The ice is stained in places — bands of grey and ochre and rust where rock flour, ground to powder by the glacier's movement, has been incorporated into the frozen surface. These mineral stains give the ice a bruised, layered appearance, like the rings of a very old tree laid sideways. In certain lights, particularly at the edges of the field where the ice thins and the mineral concentration is highest, the surface takes on a faintly opalescent quality — pale blue shot through with veins of white and gold, as if the ice were not frozen water but a single, enormous gemstone.

Fauna is rare. Occasionally a bird — a chough, perhaps, or a large raptor riding the thermal updrafts above the nunataks — passes overhead, its silhouette a fleeting interruption in the brightness. You might find the desiccated remains of an insect carried here by wind from lower altitudes, frozen into a melt pool and preserved in clear ice like an artifact. Once, you notice a set of tracks in the firn — some small, low-slung creature, possibly a marmot from a rocky outcrop kilometers away, that crossed the glacier's edge in search of something and found nothing and turned back. The tracks are days old, already softening. Nothing lives here. The ice does not permit it.

And yet this is not a dead landscape. It is a *complete* landscape — a system that has reached its own state of equilibrium and holds itself there with the quiet authority of something very old and very large. The glacier is neither advancing nor retreating. Snow falls in the upper accumulation zone; ice melts in the lower ablation zone; the two processes are, for now, in balance. The crevasses open and close in the same places, season after season. The pressure ridges maintain their forms. The meltwater channels braid and re-braid within the same corridors. You could come here in a year, in a decade, and the landscape would be — in all essential respects — the same. The erosion has happened, the sculpting has been done. The nunataks are polished. The terminal moraines are built. This is a place that has made itself and now exists in a state of frozen permanence, its changes so slow and so rhythmic that they are not change at all but pulse.

The light does not dim. The sound does not stop. The water does not cease. The ice does not yield. You are standing inside a machine that has been running for ten thousand years and will run for ten thousand more, and it does not know you are here, and it does not care, and its indifference has the strange, bright, roaring quality of something very close to joy.
