The city is small, contained entirely within a single bowl of stone no wider than a few city blocks. It is built in layers, dense and strange, and it is not entirely dry. The air smells of wet rock and a faint, electric ozone.

The walls of the bowl are pale, porous limestone that has been carved and built upon for a very long time, but the erosion here is not old. The stone looks newly cut, its edges sharp and its surfaces raw. Water seeps from it in slow, steady tears, forming a thin, slick sheen on every vertical face. The city is built *into* these walls, a labyrinth of dwellings, bridges, and walkways carved directly from the living rock. Everything is close; balconies nearly touch, and narrow stairs spiral down into gloom. The structures are dense, layered, a honeycomb of rooms within rooms, yet the feeling is not one of great size. It is hamlet-scale, intimate, a neighborhood rather than a metropolis.

The light is normal—pale and diffuse, filtering down from a sky that seems very far away at the top of the bowl. It reflects off the damp stone, making the walls gleam like bone. The quiet is profound. The loudest sound is the *drip… drip…* of water from ledge to ledge, occasionally joined by a soft, harmonic hum from the walls themselves. This hum is not mechanical. It seems to come from the stone, a resonance of the city’s deep, slow pulse.

Fauna is rare. Now and then, a pale, many-legged thing, like a cross between a centipede and a silverfish, skitters from one crack to another. They are not insects. They are part of the city’s maintenance, its slow, organic engineering.

And the technology… it is beyond modern. It is everywhere, but it is not metal or plastic. It *is* the stone. The walkways, the doorways, the very air feels… engineered at a level below perception. The limestone is not just cut; it is *grown* into its shapes. The dampness is not just seepage; it is a climate system. The city breathes. The walls sometimes show a faint, geometric pattern, like a circuit diagram, that shifts when you are not looking directly at it. This is the source of the hum. The technology is so advanced it has become a form of geology, a new, raw bedrock. It is embryonic. It is under construction, even now, though no workers are visible.

The weirdness is total. Reality here is thin. You can feel it in the air, a static charge that makes the hairs on your arms stand up. The geometry is not always stable. A hallway may seem longer than it was a moment ago. A room may feel as if it has an extra corner. The city is dense with presence, but not with people. It is dense with *itself*, with its own unfolding, paradoxical logic. It is a place that is simultaneously ancient and newborn, quiet and humming, wet and cold, urban and profoundly, unsettlingly *other*. It is a seed of a city, germinating in a bowl of stone, waiting for something. The slow pulse of its construction is the only clock it keeps.
