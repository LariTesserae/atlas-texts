The air is thick, tasting of dry dust and a faint, chalky sweetness, as if the mountains themselves are exhaling their immense age. You are not in a valley between peaks, but upon a peak that has eroded into a plateau so vast and so old that it feels like a world unto itself, a high desert of stone suspended in the thin, bright sky. The elevation is absolute, a defining fact of this place. The sun is a white glare in a pale blue bowl, its light strong but strangely diffuse, filtered through a perpetual haze of fine, powdered feldspar and mica that hangs in the air, muting shadows and softening edges.

The ground underfoot is not soil, but a compacted crust of this same ancient dust, a pale, ocherous powder inches deep over a bedrock of shattered, heat-cracked stone. Erosion has done its work over eons, wearing the original mountains down into this final, settled form: a gently undulating expanse of low, rounded mounds and shallow, meandering runnels where the rare, furious rain-storms of a past epoch once carved their paths. Now, those paths are fossilized, their channels filled with the same patient dust.

And yet, life is here. It is a hardy, dense, and peculiar life. Lush, but not as we know it. The "vegetation" is a forest of lithophytes—stone-eaters. They are not green with chlorophyll, but with a deep, coppery verdigris, the color of oxidized bronze. Their forms are like slow-motion explosions: immense, shelf-like fungi with ruffled, leathery caps the color of dried blood, growing directly from the rock faces; thick, rubbery stalks of something resembling a saguaro cactus but made of a smooth, grey, silicon-based material, their surfaces etched with fractal patterns; and everywhere, a ground-cover of what looks like lichen, but is in fact a colony of interlocking, crystalline organisms, each no bigger than a fingernail, that chime faintly against each other when the wind stirs.

This is the foundation of a dense ecology. Fauna is abundant, but quiet and slow. Things skitter in the shadow of the great shelf-fungi: multi-legged arthropods with carapaces of polished stone, their movements a series of deliberate clicks. Larger, slower beasts—six-legged, broad-backed creatures resembling giant tortoises with shells of layered, slate-like plates—grind the lichen-colonies with flat, grinding mouthparts. Their digestion is a low, continuous rumble, a sound you feel in your feet as much as hear. The air is filled with a low, constant hum, the collective vibration of a million tiny organisms and the whisper of dust moving over dust.

At the center of this grove-scale plateau is the settlement. It is not built *on* the landscape, but *into* it, a testament to a primitive but ingenious technology. The structures are grown and carved from the living stone-fungi and the silicon stalks. Walls are formed by encouraging the shelf-fungi to grow in interlocking arcs, their tough, fibrous flesh hardening upon exposure to air into a material like dense cork. Roofs are lattices of the smaller, finger-thick stalks, woven while still pliable and allowed to set rigid. Doors are slabs of slate, hung on pivots of stone.

The town is a single, dense feature, perhaps a few hundred souls. Its boundaries are the natural arches of the largest fungal shelves, creating winding, shadowed lanes that smell of warm dust and the faint, ozone-like tang of the lithophytes. Light is dim in these streets, filtered through the latticed roofs and the omnipresent dust-haze. The inhabitants are slight, with skin the color of the local rock, and their tools are of knapped flint and polished bone, their fabrics woven from the shredded fibers of the silicon cacti. They move with a slow, deliberate grace, their speech a series of low, guttural clicks and hums that blends with the sounds of the landscape.

The only loud sound is the wind, which when it comes, is a presence—a deep, mournful moan that gathers in the runnels of the ancient riverbeds and then sighs across the plateau, setting the lichen-colonies chiming in a vast, discordant orchestra. It is a sound of immense patience, of deep time. Everything here is slow: the growth of the fungi, measured in centuries; the movement of the stone-tortoises, measured in years; the drift of the dust, measured in decades. The world has reached its final, stable state, and the life upon it has adapted to the rhythm of the eternal, the quiet, the dry, and the high.
