The light arrives from above, but it is not the honest, direct light of a surface sun. Here, deep beneath the skin of the world, the light is a constant, radiant hum that bleeds from the rock itself. It is the colour of phosphorescent algae mixed with the glow of heated quartz—a soft, golden-white luminance that has no source, only a presence. It is bright enough to read the textures on a stone ten paces away, but it casts no true shadows, only a deepening of colour and a flattening of perspective.

You are in a vast, low-ceilinged cavern that extends far beyond the limits of your sight. The air is thick, warm, and damp. It clings to you like a second skin, heavy with the scent of wet mineral and the faint, ozonic tang of something about to change. The sound is a layered, constant roar, but not of water. It is the sound of a billion tiny things in motion: a skittering, a clicking, a chittering that fills the space from wall to wall. It is the sound of a market at peak hour, if every vendor were a beetle and every customer a crab.

The ground is not a surface you walk upon, but a living, shifting carpet of fauna. The "vegetation" is a lie of scale; what you thought were jagged rocks and damp pebbles are in fact the shells, carapaces, and fused bodies of countless creatures. They are of a kind you have never seen, but they share a common language of chitin and bone. Some are the size of your fist, multi-legged, scuttling over each other in a constant, roiling tide. Others are larger, the size of a dog or a small pony, moving with slow, deliberate purpose, their segmented plates scraping against the smaller creatures with a sound like grinding stone.

They are everywhere, and they are the source of the sound, the density, the turbulence. The ground-level world is a chaotic, overwhelming bazaar of predation, scavenging, and mating. A creature with a hundred delicate legs carries a smaller, struggling one in its mandibles. A creature that looks like a fused ball of rock and shell rolls slowly over a patch of smaller, faster things, consuming them. A cluster of spindly, multi-jointed limbs erupts from a crevice, snatching at the air and pulling back with a prize.

In the chaos, there are marks of a different kind of life. Scattered among the churning fauna are structures, but they are not built. They are *grown* or *eroded*. Smooth, dark stones the size of your torso sit perfectly still amidst the frenzy. They are not creatures; the smaller things flow around them like water around a boulder. Some of these stones have been carved, or perhaps have simply grown, into rough shapes: a flat top, a hollowed bowl. In these bowls, condensation collects into a few spoonfuls of silvery water. The creatures do not drink from them. They are markers, altars, or perhaps just geological quirks that some other, now-vanished intelligence noticed and settled upon.

The walls of the cavern are not smooth. They are embryonic. The rock itself is soft, still forming. It is warm to the touch, and if you press your hand against it, it yields slightly, like dense clay. Veins of a darker, cooler mineral run through it like the first tentative pathways of a river. These veins are the source of the moisture; every few minutes, a bead of water wells up on their surface and rolls down the wall, joining the dampness of the air. The erosion here is not of destruction, but of emergence. The world is still in the process of deciding what it will be.

And over all of this, the creatures move. They are the weather, the tide, the living geology of this place. Their overwhelming presence is not hostile to you, it is simply *indifferent*. You are a stone in a river of chitin. The light hums, the air is warm and wet, and the sound of a million lives being lived in frantic, chaotic motion fills the space where the sky should be.
