# The Hollowing

The grove is perhaps two hundred meters across. You could walk its perimeter in minutes. But it is very old — older than anything nearby, older than the flattening of the surrounding land — and it has settled into something that no longer resembles a forest so much as a single living room that forgot to stop growing.

The trees are the reason for everything.

There are eleven of them. That's all. But each one is immense — trunks fifteen, twenty meters across at the base, flared into rootbuttresses that merge with the ground like poured stone. They are not bark and heartwood anymore. Centuries of continuous growth have filled them with new tissue over old, new tissue over old, until the original structures are buried so deep they might as well be mineral. The outer layers are pale, almost white, soft as cork, slightly yielding under a hand. Moisture beads on them constantly — not rain, not dew, just the slow exhale of living wood in cool air. Condensation gathers in every crease and runs in thin rivulets downward, following channels worn into the living surface by ten thousand years of identical rivulets.

The floor of the grove is root. Not roots in soil — the roots *are* the soil. A dense mat of living tissue, six inches to two feet thick, spongy underfoot, faintly warm where it's deepest, cool and slick where it meets the air. If you knelt and pressed your ear to it, you would hear a slow pulse. Not a heartbeat. More like a tide — the movement of water drawn up through the root mass from the water table below, rising through capillaries so fine they are barely distinguishable from the wood around them. The grove drinks. Slowly, ceaselessly, in amounts so small that the ground-level moisture never accumulates into puddles. The dampness is ambient. The air itself is wet. Your clothes would be heavy with it within an hour.

The interior of the grove is hollow. That's the strangest thing about it, and the thing you'd notice first. Eleven trees, and between them — nothing. Open ground, open air, a vault of empty space. The canopy far overhead is dense, a lattice of branches so intertwined they've fused in places into a continuous ceiling, light filtering through in ordinary daylight, dappled, shifting faintly with the movement of upper leaves. But the mid-level — the space between trunks and canopy — is wide open. No understory. No shrubs. No saplings. Nothing grows in the hollowing except the root floor and the eleven trunks. The space between the nearest two trees is roughly forty meters. You could park trucks there. The emptiness feels deliberate, architectural, though nothing designed it.

Why nothing grows in the gaps is not mysterious. The canopy is too complete. The root mat is too dense. Any seed that falls into the hollowing finds no purchase — the living floor absorbs it, composts it, feeds it to the grove's existing structure. The eleven trees have, over their long age, monopolized every resource the space contains. They have made the hollowing into a room, and they have kept it.

The sound is what you'd remember later.

It is *loud*. Not sudden, not sharp — constant, enormous, enveloping. It starts with the wood itself. The trees creak. Not the sound of strain or danger — the sound of slow, permanent adjustment. Each trunk, over the course of its ancient life, has settled by fractions of degrees into the weight of its own mass. The tissue compresses. Air pockets shift. Wood fibers slide against wood fibers at molecular scales, and this produces a low, grinding groan that never stops, rises and falls over hours like breathing. It is the loudest sound in the grove, and you cannot locate its source because it comes from everywhere.

Below that: dripping. Thousands of tiny impacts per second — condensation falling from canopy to root floor, a steady percussive rain that never amounts to rain. It sounds like a factory. It sounds like a machine shop with no machines, just the rhythm of water on living wood, amplified by the hollow architecture of the space into something industrial in scale and texture.

Below that: fauna. The grove is alive with them. Beetles — enormous, slow-moving, iridescent — bore through the soft outer wood of the trunks, carving galleries that the tree heals behind them in hours. Their mandibles produce a faint, continuous rasping, and when a dozen of them work the same trunk, the sound carries. Woodpeckers — four species — hammer at the beetle galleries, and their drilling echoes off the open interior of the grove like gunfire in a cathedral. Small owls nest in the high hollows where branches have died and rotted into cavities. They don't call during the day, but at dusk, the grove fills with a second voice — soft, oscillating, overlapping — before the other sounds slowly swallow it again.

There are moths. Thousands of moths, resting during the day on the pale trunks, invisible against the white wood, vibrating their wings in faint protest when anything passes too close. The collective sound is a hum — barely audible alone, but layered into everything else, it becomes a drone, a frequency the grove sits on top of like a fundamental tone.

The metal is almost gone.

Someone came here once — a long time ago, not long enough that memory persists but long enough that the evidence has nearly vanished. On the north-facing side of the largest trunk, six meters up, a section of rusted iron pipe is embedded in the wood. The tree grew over it. Slowly, patiently, the way trees do everything — new rings, new tissue, millimeter by millimeter, year by year. The pipe is now a dark stain inside the living surface, a foreign body the grove has digested almost completely. There are others. A steel rod. A flanged joint of some kind. The remains of a bracket. They are all inside the wood now, visible only as faint discolorations where the tissue grew differently around them — denser, darker, slightly warped. The wood has not rejected them. It has incorporated them. The iron is rusting inside its cocoon of living fiber, releasing compounds the tree has learned to tolerate or sequester. This is not symbiosis. It is just what happened. The grove does not know the metal is there. The grove does not know anything. It grows. It drinks. It creaks. It fills itself.

The temperature never varies. Not in the way you'd notice, anyway — the grove sits at the thermal equilibrium of its own mass, cool and damp and buffered by so much living material that external changes arrive here attenuated into meaninglessness. A hot day outside makes the condensation slightly heavier. A cold snap makes the creaking slightly slower. These are the only concessions, and they are so small that a person standing in the hollowing would never detect them.

It has looked like this for a very long time. It will look like this for a very long time. The trees are not immortal, but they are close — their growth rate has slowed to nearly nothing, their mass changes by kilograms per year in a structure that weighs thousands of tons. The root mat extends well beyond the visible grove, underground, into the surrounding flatland, feeding from soil that the grove itself has spent millennia enriching. Nothing competes with it. Nothing disturbs it. The surrounding land is empty — flattened by erosion, drained by time, left with thin soil and thin grass and nothing that could challenge eleven ancient giants in their hollow.

If you stood in the center of the grove and closed your eyes, you would hear an industrial city — the hum, the drip, the creak, the drill, the layered, purposeful, unceasing noise of systems working. You would feel the damp settle on your skin. You would smell wet wood and beetle frass and the faint sweetness of fungal decay in the root floor. You would sense the emptiness around you — the open air, the vaulted dark, the distance between the trunks like distance between columns in a basilica.

And when you opened your eyes, you would see eleven trees, a floor of roots, and a ceiling of leaves, and nothing else, and it would not have changed, and it would never change, and the sound would go on.
