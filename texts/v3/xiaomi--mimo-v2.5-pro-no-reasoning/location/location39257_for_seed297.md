# The Lithic Canopy

You stand at the edge of a basin the size of a modest county, and everything below you is alive, and almost none of it has ever tasted water.

---

**The Substrate**

The ground is not soil. It is a fine-grained mineral matrix — pale grey, faintly iridescent, warm to the touch in a way that has nothing to do with the sun. If you knelt and examined it with a lens you would see structure: repeating geometries at the nanometer scale, latticed, almost crystalline but not quite — something *other* than crystalline. A word for it doesn't exist. The formations were recent, geologically speaking. You can tell by the edges. Where the basin shelves down from its low rims, the terrain breaks into facets and knife-ridges that are almost too sharp, as if the land emerged from some phase transition in the last few tens of thousands of years and erosion hasn't begun to round anything. There are shallow terraces, sudden steps of two or three meters, narrow defiles between angled planes of mineral. It reads as tectonic but it isn't — the shapes are too regular, too interlocking. Something engineered the geometry of this place at a scale below perception. That engineering is now inert. It doesn't hum. It doesn't glow. It just *is*, the way quartz is.

And on this substrate, in defiance of everything you know about biology, the basin is *full* of plants.

---

**The Growth**

Not water-fed. You realize this almost immediately because there is no water here — not in any meaningful quantity. The air is dry and warm. There are no rivers, no springs, no fog, no dew. The mineral surface is desiccated. Yet the basin floor and its terraces and slopes are choked with vegetation so dense that from this vantage you see not ground but canopy — a rolling, textured mat of dark green and deep violet and the occasional flash of something that isn't a color your visual cortex knows how to name.

These are not angiosperms. They are not anything with a name. The closest analogue is a forest floor full of ferns and clubmosses that has been reimagined by an intelligence that never saw Earth. Low-growing formations — most no taller than your waist — fill every niche between the mineral ridges. They have something like leaves, but the leaves are lattice-work: thin membranes organized into branching vein-patterns that leave most of the surface open, like living stained glass. They click and rasp faintly against each other in any breeze. The stems are rigid, fibrous, pale, and when you break one (you will, inevitably, moving through this) the interior is dry and dense, with no vascular fluid. No sap. No moisture.

They feed on the substrate. That's what the root networks are for — and the roots are the densest part of the system, threading into the mineral lattice at every scale, tapping into the engineered geometry below. They are drawing energy from whatever the nanoscale structure of this ground is — vacuum fluctuations, zero-point differentials, some energy source that your physics doesn't have a clean name for. The substrate feeds them. The substrate also doesn't change while doing so. It has fed them for a long time at no visible cost to itself.

Above the low groundcover — which is *dense*, layered, you cannot see your feet once you step into it — there are taller forms. Columnar structures with fractal branching tips. Things like trees but leafless, their canopies replaced by tangled networks of hollow filament that catch the light and sometimes seem to redirect it in ways that don't obey Snell's law. These are spaced irregularly but not sparsely; there are perhaps a few hundred of them in the basin, rising ten to twenty meters, each one a landmark. Between and beneath them, the understory piles up — the low lattice-leaved growth, then mid-height formations with broad flat crowns stacked like shelves, then moss-like crusts on the mineral surfaces that haven't been colonized by anything larger. Everything is layered. Everything is *full*. If you tried to walk through this, you would be pushing vegetation aside every step. The density is almost oppressive — warm, dry, still air trapped between the leaf-lattices, the smell of mineral dust and something faintly resinous, like a chemistry you don't have a reference for.

---

**The Light**

It is day. The light is normal — or nearly so. The sun is the sun, or something functionally identical. But the light does *odd things* once it enters the canopy. The lattice-leaves refract it in bands. In certain clearings, where the mineral shelf angles upward and the vegetation throws, you see the ground striped with elongated shapes of colored light — amber, pale green, a blue-white that feels heavier than blue-white should feel. The shadows are wrong. They are too defined, too crisp, as if the light has been *collimated* by passing through the plant architecture. In the deeper reaches of the basin, where the columnar structures cluster, the light filters down in shafts that seem to move — not with the sun, but with something else. Something structural in the air, or in the geometry of the place.

You adjust to this quickly. The light is bright enough to see by, dim enough not to squint. It is just light. Except when it isn't.

---

**The Quiet**

This is a quiet place. Not silent — the leaf-lattices click and rasp, and there is a low, barely-there hum from the substrate itself, the kind of sound you feel in your molars more than hear. The wind, when it comes, moves through the hollow filaments of the columnar structures and produces a faint, shifting drone, almost tonal, never resolving into melody. But there are no animal calls. No insect buzz. No birdsong, no rustling of anything running or burrowing.

Fauna is nearly absent. Once, at the edge of a clearing, you might see something — a slow, segmented thing the size of your forearm, pale, moving through the lower vegetation with no apparent urgency. It is the only one you will see today. There may be others. There may be microscopic life in the substrate. But the animals are *rare* here in a way that suggests they are visitors, not natives — passengers on a system that was never designed with them in mind.

The absence of animal sound makes the plant-noise louder than it should be. The clicking. The dry rasp. The hum. The world sounds like a machine idling in standby.

---

**The Breakdown**

And then there is the thing that is wrong with reality.

It is not dramatic. It is not a rift or a tear or a glowing wound in the sky. It is subtler, and worse for being subtle. You notice it in the periphery first. A tree that was to your left is now behind you, and you didn't turn. The distance to the basin's far rim — which you gauged at perhaps eight kilometers — seems to *shrink* when you look at it directly, and *stretch* when you look away. The angles between things are not quite Euclidean. You can't prove this with a protractor. You can't even articulate precisely what's wrong. But the feeling accumulates as you move through the vegetation, pushing aside dry lattice-leaves, stepping over mineral ridges: *this place is not laid out on a flat plane*. It is laid out on something that resembles a flat plane the way a photograph of a room resembles a room. The depth is missing. Or rather, the depth is present but *wrong*, distributed differently than it should be.

Colors at the periphery of your vision are too saturated. When you turn to look at them, they settle to something normal. The far columnar structures seem to shimmer — not with heat, but with *presence*, as if they are occupying slightly more space than they should. Once, standing still in a clearing, you become convinced that you can see the *back* of the clearing, the part behind you, as a faint overlay on the part in front of you. You turn around. There is nothing there. The overlay vanishes. You turn back. For a moment — just a moment — it is there again.

Time is... stable. That is the strangest part. Despite everything, despite the warping of space and the peripheral hallucinations and the geometric impossibilities, the *temporal* dimension of this place is solid. Nothing is accelerating. Nothing is decaying. The plants are not growing — they have been this size, in this configuration, for what might be centuries. The substrate is not eroding. The light is not shifting. You get the sense, standing in this warm dry impossible forest, that you are in a place that *formed recently and then stopped*. As if the phase transition that created the mineral lattice also created the vegetation and the reality breakdown and the faint background hum all at once, and then everything froze into equilibrium. A single moment, extended.

The weirdness is not dangerous. It is *ambient*. It is the price of existing in a space where the substrate is doing something to local physics that your physics doesn't account for. The plants have adapted. The few animals have adapted. You are adapting, slowly — your proprioception is beginning to recalibrate, your sense of distance becoming slightly less reliable, your visual processing starting to accept the peripheral anomalies without alarm.

---

**The Whole**

What you are standing in is a garden that nobody tends, grown on a substrate that nobody maintains, producing a reality-state that nobody controls. The technology is there — in the mineral lattice, in the nanoscale geometry, in the energy source that feeds the vegetation — but it is *inert technology*. It is not doing anything *to* anything. It simply exists, as background, as *ground*, and the ecology has built itself on top of it like a reef on a sunken ship. There are no structures because nothing has ever come here to build. There is no water because whatever energy cycle sustains this place doesn't require it — the plants have evolved, or been *arranged*, to feed directly on the substrate's output.

It is a warm, dense, quiet, dry, gently-warped region of lowland that has been exactly this way for a long time and will be exactly this way for a long time, and it is profoundly, deeply strange, and it is also — somehow — *peaceful*. The kind of peace that comes from a place that has no interest in you at all. You are not part of this system. You are not a threat or a resource. You are a disturbance in the equilibrium, and the equilibrium will simply wait for you to leave.
