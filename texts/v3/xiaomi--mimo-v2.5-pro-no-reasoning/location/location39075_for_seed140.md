The air itself has weight here, a warm and saturated pressure that blankets the world in perpetual twilight. This is not a twilight of setting suns—the sun is a forgotten concept—but a twilight of age, a darkness that has settled into the bones of the land. The only light comes from the world below and within.

You stand in a grove of impossible scale, a chamber carved by patient eons. The floor is a carpet of luminous moss, each frond a soft, pulsating blue-green, like a galaxy viewed from within. This living light is just enough to cast faint, shifting shadows from the forms that rise around you. They are not trees, not in any way you know them. They are immense, petrified spires of some crystalline material, black and glassy, their surfaces weeping a constant, viscous dew. The sound of this place is the sound of that weeping: a slow, heavy *plink… plink…* as drops the size of your fist fall from high ledges into shallow, brackish pools that dot the mossy floor. This quiet percussion is underscored by a low, resonant hum that seems to emanate from the very structure of the air, a vibration felt in the chest more than heard with the ears.

The terrain is ancient beyond reckoning. The ground is not soil but a fused, silicate crust, worn into gentle, flowing contours. It suggests this was once a landscape of dramatic peaks and valleys, now eroded into a soft, rolling sea of moss and standing water. The water is everywhere: seeping from the weeping spires, pooling in depressions, condensing on every surface. It is warm to the touch and has a faint, mineral tang.

The life here is abundant, but almost entirely silent. The air is thick with drifting spores and motes of pollen, some of which faintly self-illuminate, creating lazy, golden streaks in the gloom. Between the spires, vast, iridescent webs are strung, not of silk, but of a flexible, ceramic-like filament that resonates with the world's hum. Their creators are unseen, but the webs themselves are ecosystems: tiny, six-legged grazers with carapaces of polished stone meticulously clean them, while larger, slug-like creatures leave trails of glowing mucus as they move slowly along the strands. In the pools, amphibious things with multiple, waving gills and bodies like river stones glide through the warm water, hunting shimmering water-fleas.

The scattered remnants of a civilization are almost an afterthought. Set into the base of one of the great spires is a single, smooth doorway, a perfect arch of seamless, darker material that does not weep. It is slightly ajar, revealing only an impenetrable black within. A few dozen paces away, half-sunk into the moss, lies the ribbed carcass of a small vessel, its hull split open like a pod. Its lines are elegant and impossibly thin, the material neither metal nor polymer, but something that seems grown rather than built. There are no signs of conflict, no scattered tools or remains. Only the profound silence of abandonment on a timescale that dwarfs the landscape's own great age.

This is a place of high, slow technology, now utterly still. The hum in the air might be the idle cycle of a planetary engine, the weeping dew the byproduct of an atmospheric processor running on its final reserves for millennia. The faint light from the moss could be a bio-luminescent system engineered to outlast its creators. Nothing here feels wrong, only complete. The small scale of the grove is contained, a single feature in a vast, dark world. It is a cathedral of silence, where the loudest sounds are water falling and the patient, thrumming pulse of a world dreaming in its final, settled state.
