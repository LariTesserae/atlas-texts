# The Unfinished Terrace

---

The first thing you notice is the sound — not a roar, exactly, but a deep, wet, creaking symphony. Timber flexing. Water hissing through channels you can't see. A low, fibrous groan that rolls across the basin and arrives at your chest before your ears.

You are standing on a high shelf of raw basalt — dark, glassy, veined with sulfur-yellow where fumaroles have stained the rock. The shelf is perhaps two hundred meters across and utterly bare. Beneath your feet the stone is warm. Not hot enough to burn, but warm the way skin is warm: *alive-feeling*. Moisture beads on every surface. The air is thick, nearly chewable, the kind of humidity that makes your lungs feel full before you've taken a full breath. Mist rises from cracks in the rock in slow, vertical columns that lean and scatter in the updraft.

Beyond the shelf, the land drops away — not sharply, but in a long, irregular descent — and what fills that descent is the reason this place exists.

---

**The growths.**

There is no better word. They are structures. They are organisms. They are both simultaneously and neither completely.

They rise from the lower slopes in enormous, irregular forms — columns, arches, buttresses, vaults — some reaching fifty meters high, some barely taller than a person. They are made entirely of plant matter, but not in any way you have seen before. There are no distinct species here, no identifiable trees or ferns or grasses. Instead, the vegetation is *continuous* — a single sprawling biomass expressing itself in architectural forms as though some deep structural logic in the plant-matter itself demands load-bearing walls and cantilevered platforms and hollow chambers open to the sky.

The outer skin of these structures is a rough, layered bark — deep red-brown, almost liver-colored — but it is moist, slick, actively weeping. Water trails down every surface in thin, unbroken sheets. The bark is warm to the touch. Press your hand against it and you feel a slow, almost tidal pulse. Not a heartbeat. Something slower. The rhythm of sap moving through channels the width of your arm.

Where the bark has split or torn — and it splits often — the interior is visible: a pale, fibrous mass, almost meat-like in color, shot through with darker vascular strands and pale, translucent chambers filled with fluid. These chambers catch the light and glow faintly amber, like lanterns embedded in the wall.

The structures are *open*. Not solid masses but frameworks — skeletal geometries of living timber, enormous ribs and arches supporting membrane-thin floors and latticework partitions. You could walk into one. Some have interiors large enough to hold a house. The floors are slightly concave and always slick with condensation, and in the concavities, water pools — dark, clear, warm — before draining through the woven substrate and dripping into lower levels.

---

**The spaces between.**

This is the thing that makes the place feel so strange: the emptiness.

For all the profusion of the growths — and there are hundreds, perhaps thousands, spread across the long descent of this highland basin — they do not crowd. Each structure stands apart from its neighbors by dozens of meters, sometimes hundreds. The ground between them is bare: raw stone, scree, shallow standing water in depressions where the rock has been recently upheaved. The terrain is young. You can see where the basalt shelf has fractured and tilted in geologically recent events, creating new ledges and new slopes, and on every fresh surface the plant-matter has begun its immediate, aggressive colonization — thin root-mats spreading like wet paint, the first architectural gestures already rising: small arches, tentative columns, the beginnings of a new structure no taller than your knee but already recognizable as the same form as the giants downhill.

The youth of the terrain is everywhere. The features are sharp. No rounded hills, no gentle valleys — just fractured rock, raw edges, new water channels cutting through loose scree. The erosion hasn't had time to soften anything. It looks *unfinished*, like a landscape caught in the act of being born.

---

**The movement.**

Nothing here is still.

The structures *grow visibly*. Not fast enough to watch in real time — but fast enough that a column you noted an hour ago is measurably taller. The growth is not gentle. It is violent in the way that tectonic forces are violent: slow and utterly irresistible. You can hear it. A deep, fibrous *crack* as new tissue forces its way through old bark. A wet, tearing sound as a membrane stretches and splits to accommodate a new branch or a new floor. The larger structures groan constantly under their own increasing weight, their architecture shifting to compensate, ribs angling, buttresses thickening, the whole organism recalculating its structural geometry in real time.

And they collapse. Regularly, without warning, and catastrophically. A fifty-meter arch that looked solid will simply *give way* — a sound like a ship's hull breaking, a slow-motion avalanche of wet, fibrous debris that crashes into the pool below and sends a wave of dark water across the bare rock. The collapse is not death. Within hours, the debris begins to reorganize. Roots push through the fallen mass. New columns begin. The biomass doesn't lose material; it redistributes it. The basin floor is littered with the remains of past collapses, half-absorbed back into new growth, so that the lower slopes are a dense, chaotic tangle of ruin and renewal — foundations of structures that fell centuries ago supporting structures that erupted yesterday.

The water makes its own chaos. Every surface weeps, drips, streams. Small waterfalls appear and vanish as internal channels in the structures redirect flow. In heavy mist — and the mist is heavy here, rolling in from the higher slopes in thick waves — the entire basin sounds like a rainstorm even when no rain is falling, because every structure is simultaneously releasing the water it has collected and condensed.

---

**The heat.**

The elevation is high — you can see the sky clearly when the mist parts, a pale, washed-out blue, the sun white and fierce — but the heat is relentless. Not the dry heat of a high desert. A wet, pressing, tropical heat that sits on your shoulders and breathes on your neck. The basalt radiates warmth. The structures radiate warmth — the metabolic heat of their constant growth. Steam rises from every wet surface, and the basin fills with it: a low, rolling fog that never quite clears, that parts in updrafts and closes again, that makes distances uncertain and depths deceptive.

At midday, when the sun is high enough to penetrate the mist, the light changes. It becomes amber, filtered through the translucent chambers of the structures, scattered by water droplets suspended in the air. Shadows are soft and directionless. The basin glows.

---

**What lives here**

Nothing with legs. Nothing with wings. Nothing with eyes.

Only the plant.

And the plant *does things*. In the hollow chambers, you can see movement — slow, peristaltic contractions pumping fluid from lower levels to upper ones. On the outer surfaces, fine filaments extend and retract, tasting the air, drawing moisture from the mist. The structures communicate with each other through underground root-networks so dense and complex that the bare rock between them is merely a thin crust over a continuous living foundation. When one structure collapses, its neighbors visibly respond — thickening their walls, redirecting growth, as though warned.

There are no seeds. No flowers. No fruit. The plant does not reproduce in any way you can identify. It simply *continues* — expanding into new territory, colonizing fresh rock, growing over its own ruins. It has always been growing. It will always be growing. The young erosion of the landscape is not a problem for it but a *condition* — the new rock is its opportunity, the collapse of old structures is its recycling, the heat and the water and the altitude are exactly what it requires.

---

**At the edges**

The basin is bounded on three sides by higher ridges — raw, dark basalt, largely bare, where the growths have not yet established themselves. The ridges are sharp-featured, freshly faulted, still steaming from the heat below. From the highest point, you can see the full extent of the place: a vast, irregular depression, perhaps three kilometers across, carpeted with the scattered architecture of the plant, mist pooling in the lower reaches, the structures catching and releasing light in slow, amber pulses, the sound of it all — creaking, dripping, groaning, hissing — rising in a constant, dense, living roar.

On the fourth side, the basin opens. The plant-structures thin. The bare rock extends outward into a long, high, windswept shelf where the mist lifts and the air dries and nothing grows. The plant has not reached there yet. Or it has tried and failed. Or it simply hasn't gotten around to it.

From the open side, looking back, the basin appears as a wound in the highland — a bright, steaming, overgrown scar, impossibly alive, impossibly loud, impossibly *busy*, a place where the earth has opened and something has answered.
