The stone here is newborn. It is black basalt, rough as slag, still bearing the glassy sheens and sharp ridges of its cooling. It does not form gentle hills or old, patient bedrock; it forms a shattered floor, a field of jagged plates and sudden fissures, as if a great crust had been slammed with a hammer from below. This is a place that has not yet learned to be solid ground.

And it weeps.

The air itself is thick with a cold, perpetual humidity that tastes of iron and salt. It condenses on every surface, turning the black stone slick and treacherous. Water gathers not in streams, but in the hollows between the broken slabs, forming a shallow, intricate network of dark, briny pools. It is a landscape perpetually in the act of being rained upon, even when the sky is a uniform, bruised-grey overcast that permits no true weather, only a constant, mizzling damp.

From this shallow water, life erupts in fierce, low clumps. There is no forest here, no canopy. The vegetation is a visceral tangle of things that thrive on cold wetness and bare rock. Great beds of a thick, rubbery moss, the colour of a deep bruise, cling to the stones, sopping up the moisture. From these beds, strange flora rises: not trees, but single, massive stalks of a pale, waxy reed, taller than a man, their surfaces beaded with condensation. They grow with an unnerving swiftness, a visible pace, their roots drilling into the fresh cracks of the basalt. Between them, low, fleshy plants with broad, dark leaves, like monstrous liverworts, sprawl over the rock.

And there are the pipes.

At irregular intervals, great shafts of corroded, pitted iron—like the severed ends of colossal drainage pipes—protrude from the stone itself, as if the geology had grown them. They are the size of ancient trees, their open mouths weeping not just water, but a constant, thick, oily mucus that trails down the rock in sluggish black ribbons. They hum and vibrate. A deep, resonant thrum emanates from within the earth, a bass note felt in the bones more than heard, but the pipes give it a voice: a low, industrial groan that is the landscape's dominant sound. It is the sound of machinery working deep in the bedrock, of engines turning over, of pressure being released. This is the "built" of this place—not a structure, but a *mechanism*, a geological engine of unknown purpose that has been here since the land was pushed up from the sea.

This sound and vibration has consequences. The fauna is abundant, and adapted to the din. In the pools, slick, eel-like creatures with no eyes and multiple fins undulate through the water, their skin the same glassy black as the basalt. They are not fish; they move too quickly, with a jerky, mechanical rhythm. On the moss and the great reeds, thick, segmented insects with carapaces that look like dripping oil crawl in vast, silent processions. They are always moving, always heading from one pipe-maw to another. The air itself is alive with them: fat, buzzing things that resemble beetles, their wings a blur, their flight paths erratic and noisy, dodging between the reed-stalks. They are the ecosystem's high gear, a constant, churning activity.

The scale is intimate, claustrophobic. One is not in a valley, but in a *crack*, a pocket of existence perhaps no larger than a city block, bounded by walls of the same shattered, weeping basalt. The dim light, filtering through the perpetual overcast, makes the black stone seem to absorb all colour, leaving only the bruised moss, the pale reeds, the black water, and the oily sheen of the pipes. The loudness is not of wind or waves, but of the deep, mechanical hum, the constant drip and trickle of water, the buzz of insects, and the occasional, startling crack as a piece of fresh-stressed stone finally gives way, falling into a pool with a sharp splash.

It is a place of immense, slow power—the power of land being born—and yet, it is a small, contained thing. A wound in the world, festering with its own strange, mechanical biology. It is not hostile. It is simply, loudly, actively *becoming*.
