The rain does not call upon an ocean; it is the ocean, or what has become of it. The barrier between atmosphere and water is gone, replaced by a vertical column of liquid air so dense and laden with particulates it presents as a perpetual, planet-wide downpour. You are somewhere deep within this column. The scale is continental, and the pressure is immense, a fact you only know because of the way sound travels—slowly, lethargically, as if through syrup, followed by a dull, concussive force that rolls through your body.

There is no sun. The light comes from within the system itself. The "normal light" is a lie told from a distance. Up close, it is the dim, omnidirectional glow of a thousand-thousand functional indicators. This is a fully constructed world, a machine-matrix built to house... something. There is no bedrock, no soil, no natural substrate. Everything is flooring, housing, and conduit. Naturally, it is all rusting. The young erosion here is not the patient work of water on stone, but the fresh, galvanic fury of a sealed system now breached. Every surface is pitted with new corrosion, and entire banners of material slough away in the current, carried off like sediment in a flash flood. This is the sound: a constant, deafening roar composed of the rain, the groaning of stressed metal, the hiss of a billion small leaks fighting the pressure, and a deep, rhythmic thrumming that vibrates up from the depths. It is the sound of engines not yet fallen silent.

The density is low. In the vast, dim spaces between the structures, there is only the water and the rain. This is a hollow world. Great, continent-spanning gantries and cylindrical chassis stretch out into the gloom, their purpose long-faded into immensity. They echo with the empty sound. But life abounds, if you can call it that. Fauna, abundant and grimly industrial, has found its niche. These are not fish in a traditional sense. They are shoals of armored, scuttling things, no bigger than a human hand, that move in synchronized waves, cleaning the ever-growing blossoms of rust from critical machinery. They are the world’s living immune system, and they are losing the battle. From deeper in the structure, immense shadows drift—leviathans of gas-filled bladder and trailing, fibrous nets, passively filtering the nutrient-rich water. They are slow, vast, and largely harmless, their movements as geological as the structure they inhabit.

The sparse vegetation is not plant, but a calcareous algae that forms hard, shelf-like structures on the underbellies of platforms, glowing with a faint bio-luminescence—a secondary, sickly light source in the deepest parts of the dark. It is a pioneer species of a world entering a new phase. The cool temperature is not ambient, but the chill of the deep water and the machinery that maintains a precise, failing internal constant. The air in your lungs tastes of metal and ozone.

A breaker drifts into view. A mechanism of inconstant purpose, it is thirty meters long, shaped like a segmented worm. Its forward section, a cluster of grinders and intakes, works methodically on a collapsed conduit, its aft section extruding a slurry of processed material that is instantly swept away by the currents. This is the active dynamic. Not an ecosystem of predator and prey so much as a failing machine trying to repair itself with automated, purposeless hands. It is a world-hull trying to patch its own leaks, a planetary engine room slowly, inevitably, drowning in its own lifeblood.
