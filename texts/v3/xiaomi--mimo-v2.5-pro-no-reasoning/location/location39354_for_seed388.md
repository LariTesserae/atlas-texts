The air is the first thing that enters you. It is not merely humid; it is actively wet, a constant, fine-grained mist that hangs in the air like a held breath. It coats every surface in a slick, gelatinous film, turning the dim light from the high, salt-caked windows into a diffuse, percolating glow. You are in a single, vast industrial hall, but the roof is low, a criss-cross of corroded iron beams and steam pipes that drip a steady, rhythmic percussion into the pools below. The scale is intimate, oppressive, the scale of a grand machine room or a cathedral for something heavy and slow.

The ground is a lattice of blackened iron grating, slick with condensation and a dark, oily sludge. Beneath the grating, in the perpetual twilight, water moves. Not a river, but a network of sluggish, engineered channels carved into the bedrock, their sides furred with a luminous, pale green alga that seems to photosynthesize the industrial glow rather than sunlight. This is the source of the water, the system’s weeping—moisture drawn from the stone and the air by the ceaseless, cool-churning machinery, then fed back into the channels. The sound is a layered symphony: the deep, arrhythmic *thump-chuff* of massive pistons somewhere in the complex; the higher, constant whine of turbines; the hiss of steam from a thousand faulty joints; and the eternal, pattering rain of the condensation. It is loud, yet the sound feels old, worn into the walls, a part of the place’s very substance.

The vegetation is not of the earth, but of the factory. Great, velvety mounds of a blackish-green moss grow in every corner, on every horizontal surface, fed by the moisture and the nutrient-rich runoff. They swallow sound and light. From the moss, thick, pale stalks emerge, fleshy and translucent, holding up broad, waxy leaves that channel water droplets to their bases. They look less like plants and more like organic pipes, another part of the system. They are lush, but in a quiet, fungal way, a life that eats darkness and damp.

The architecture is the landscape. The walls are not brick or stone, but a material born of this place: a fused composite of slag, rust, and a ceramicite that has been poured and set over centuries, creating a seamless, rippled surface. The built environment is a palimpsest. You can see the ghosts of older structures—the outline of a smaller room now bricked up with a newer, larger wall; the stumps of forgotten catwalks; sealed pipes jutting from the surface like fossilized bones. Everything is connected, layered, a city-state contained within a single, sprawling building. The streets are the walkways between colossal, sleeping machines. The infrastructure is the ecology.

There is no animal life. The only movement is the slow pistons, the patient drip of water, the almost imperceptible creep of the moss. The light is dim, emanating from filaments embedded in the high ceiling that glow a sullen, amber-orange, like embers in ash. The air smells of ozone, wet stone, and a faint, sweet decay from the moss. It is a place in perfect equilibrium, a closed loop of water, machinery, and strange plant-life that has been running, slowly, for centuries. The change is geological. A new layer of slag takes a human lifetime to form. A pipe may take a decade to become fully occluded by mineral deposit. It is a slow, loud, wet, and very strange city, dreaming in its own steam.
