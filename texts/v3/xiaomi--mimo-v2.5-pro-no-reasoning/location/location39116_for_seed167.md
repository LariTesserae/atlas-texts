# The Silted Chorus

There is no sun here, and there hasn't been for a very long time.

The water is warm — not the searing warmth of volcanic vents, but something older, more patient, the way deep water in equatorial basins holds heat for centuries. It is always around 28 or 29 degrees, close to blood-warm, and it moves slowly, carrying with it a constant rain of finest particulate: pale silt, shed scales, the dust of calcium structures gradually returning to the mineral state. This sediment drifts like slow snow through the darkness, and if there were light, it would make the water look almost like atmosphere — hazy, particulate, breathing.

---

**The structures begin about forty meters down and extend to the basin floor.**

They rise — if "rise" is even the right word for something built in a world without orientation — they *persist* in enormous columns, walls, and vaulted enclosures made of overlapping tubes, roughly the diameter of a human arm, each one secreted and fused by successive generations of organisms. The architecture is unmistakably *urban*. There are districts. There are thoroughfares — wide vertical and horizontal channels where the tubes thin and part, creating passages large enough for something the size of a manta to move through. There are dense neighborhoods where the tubes are pressed so close together that the structure becomes nearly solid, a mass of chitin and protein and carbonate with only the smallest interstices between members.

It is not a city built by builders. It is a city that *grew* from the accumulated, unconscious collaboration of millions of tube-dwelling organisms — something like a sabellid worm, though they are not worms, and something like a coral, though they are not coral. They secrete their homes, they fuse to their neighbors' homes, they die and leave the housing behind and the next generation builds on top of that. Over centuries — this place is *centuries* old — the accumulated dead have become the foundation, the lower districts calcified into pale, dense limestone shot through with the ghost-holes of ancient tubes, while the upper reaches are still soft, still tan-colored, still inhabited.

The word *urban* applies because the structures are not random. They thin and thicken in response to currents that have themselves been reshaped by the structures. Choke-points open where the flow accelerates. Gathering-chambers swell where eddies collect suspended food. The whole geometry is a feedback loop between architecture and hydrology, settled into equilibrium, and it has the look of intentionality even though nothing here intends anything at all.

---

**The light is almost gone.**

There is a faint, ambient bioluminescence — but *faint* is the operative word. Certain of the tube-dwellers emit a dull blue-green glow from their siphons, more a byproduct of their filter-feeding chemistry than any signal. It is enough, in aggregate, to suggest the outlines of the nearest structures: the vague impression of a wall here, a branching corridor there, a vaulted ceiling that resolves overhead like a dim cathedral. Beyond ten or fifteen meters, everything collapses into black. You would navigate by feel, by current, by the texture of the tube-walls under your body.

Some of the larger fauna produce their own light. There are crustaceans — white, eyeless, the size of dinner plates — that carry a faint phosphorescent patch beneath their carapace. They move in slow processions along the thoroughfares, and their passing makes the walls seem to drift, as though the city is slowly revolving. Schools of small, eel-like fish with photophores trailing from their jaws move through the upper reaches in synchronized pulses, and their light, when it comes, is startling: brief blue flashes that illuminate entire chambers for a half-second, revealing the geometry in sudden, haunted detail — every tube, every branching, every silt-snow particle frozen mid-drift — before the darkness reasserts itself and everything is black and warm and pressing close again.

---

**The fauna is abundant but not noisy.**

The quiet is one of the first things you would notice if you could somehow be here. This is a world of *low-frequency* sound: the deep, rhythmic creak of the larger structures flexing in thermal currents; the almost subsonic hum of filter-feeding, millions of siphons working in near-unison; the occasional distant *crack* of a dead column finally giving way somewhere in the lower city, collapsing in slow motion into silt. There are clicks from the crustaceans, but they are sparse, mechanical, like someone tapping a pen on a desk in an empty building. The eel-fish make a thin electric buzz when they communicate, barely audible.

There is no wind. There is no rain. The loudest thing here is probably the settling — the creak and groan of a structure that has been settling for three hundred years and will continue settling for three hundred more. The erosion is *done*. The forms are established. This is not a place being shaped; it is a place that has *arrived at its shape*, and now it simply endures, slowly accreting mass from above, slowly compacting from below, the middle layers alive and dense with bodies and currents.

---

**The warmth makes everything faster than you'd expect.**

Despite the darkness and the quiet, this is an active place. The warm water accelerates metabolism. The tube-dwellers reproduce in continuous cycles, not seasonal — there are no seasons here. Eggs are released into the current, swept through the passages, settle in new locations, hatch, begin to build. The crustaceans molt frequently, leaving their shed carapaces to drift like translucent paper lanterns through the corridors. Predators patrol: long, slender fish with underslung jaws and bodies like drawn knives, moving in coordinated pairs through the channels, picking off the eel-fish with precise, almost leisurely strikes. The kills are quick and quiet. The prey-species compensate with fecundity, not vigilance. There are always more.

Near the basin floor, the accumulated sediment of centuries forms a soft, pale plain — the *necropolis* of the lower city, where the structures have been compressed into something barely recognizable, a topography of gentle mounds and hollows made entirely of the dead. Things live here too: burrowers, scavengers, a whole ecology of decomposers working through the ancient mass, recycling it grain by grain. The floor is warm. It is dark. It is thick with life that you cannot see.

---

**What you would feel, if you were here:**

Warmth. Pressure — gentle, not crushing, but present, the weight of forty meters of water and three hundred years of accumulated architecture pressing from every direction. Texture under your hands: the tubes are smooth where they are new, rough and barnacle-crusted where they are old. Current — always a current, moving through the passages like breath, carrying silt that settles on your skin. Darkness so complete that your eyes would keep trying to invent shapes, phantom walls, phantom light.

And beneath all of that: the feeling of a place that does not need you. That does not know you are there. A city built without minds, inhabited without memory, illuminated without fire, and it will go on long after anything that can wonder about it has come and gone.

The silt keeps falling. The tubes keep growing. The water stays warm.
