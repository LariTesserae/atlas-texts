The world has forgotten the sun. It is a memory, a myth, a word without a referent. Here, in the quiet and the cold, the only light is what you bring with you, or what you are.

You are in a forest, but the trees are not made of wood. They are pale, calcified things, their trunks and branches woven from a substance like bone or bleached coral. They rise from the soft, saturated ground in a lattice of impossible complexity, branching and re-branching in fractal patterns that disappear into the oppressive darkness above. This is not a forest that grew; it was built. It is a single, continent-spanning machine, and you are moving through its still, ancient anatomy.

The ground beneath your feet is a thick, yielding mattress of vegetation—a carpet of mosses, fungi, and low-lying liverworts, all of them a ghostly white or pale phosphorescent blue. They drink from a world of water. The air is thick with it, a constant, fine mist that clings to every surface. Pools of it, dark and still, lie in the hollows between the bony roots. Drips of it are the dominant sound, each drop falling from a high branch with the slow, deliberate patience of a tolling bell. This is a world that has been submerged for eons. The great flood came, the waters receded to a permanent saturation, and this forest of bone and fungus evolved—or was created—to be the last, final ecosystem.

There is no wind. The air is still and heavy, a chilled blanket of mist and quiet. The cold is not a sharp, biting thing, but a deep, pervasive dampness that leaches warmth slowly, seeping into your bones. The silence is immense, broken only by the rhythmic drip and the soft, wet squelch of your passage. The forest is dense, but not with life. It is dense with structure, with presence. The bony branches are everywhere, a high-saturation network that seems to close in on itself. You feel like an intruder in a cathedral that has been abandoned by its gods.

There are no animals. Not even insects. The ecosystem is a closed loop of stillness: the pale vegetation photosynthesizes whatever faint light exists (or perhaps it is chemosynthetic, feeding on the minerals in the water and the slow decay of the structure itself), and in turn, it slowly, imperceptibly, erodes the bone-like material of the trees. The forest is consuming itself, a process of autophagy that has been underway for a time so vast it has become the world's only stable state.

You touch the trunk of a tree. It is smooth and cool, like polished stone. It does not feel like something that was ever alive. It feels like the skeleton of a creature that dreamed a forest into existence, and then died, leaving the dream behind. The small, pale fungi glow with a weak, internal light, just enough to illuminate their own caps and the nearest branch. The light does not travel far. It is swallowed by the darkness only a few feet away, making the world a series of small, isolated islands of faint visibility.

This is a world at the end of time. It is not dying, because it has already settled into its final form. It is a monument to a catastrophe that happened so long ago, the catastrophe itself has become the geology. The primitive tech of whoever built it—if it was built—is long gone, integrated into the fabric of the place. The entire continent is this: a silent, bone-white forest in a cold, wet darkness, breathing its last, slow, and eternal breath. There is no future here, only the deep, patient, and unending present.
