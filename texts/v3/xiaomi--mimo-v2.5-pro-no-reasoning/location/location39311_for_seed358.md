The first thing you learn is that the sky is a lie.

Or rather, it’s a ceiling. A low, bruised-purple vault of cloud and condensation that presses down on the world, never more than a few hundred feet above your head. The light that filters through is a perpetual, gloomy twilight, thick and amber with industrial haze. It’s a world where noon feels like dusk, and dusk is a near-total blackness, broken only by the stuttering, sulfurous glow of distant processing plants.

You are standing on a causeway of rust-colored, weeping iron grating. Below it, not soil, but a shallow, unending sheet of black, viscous water. It’s the water that defines everything. It’s not a river, not a lake; it’s the *medium*. It lies over the land like a skin, just inches deep in most places, and it is warm to the touch—blood-warm. It weeps from the rock faces that jut from this shallow sea like broken teeth. The sound of it is a constant, sibilant *hiss*. Drip. Trick. Seep. A sound you feel in your molars.

But that is the quiet sound. The one that lives beneath the real sound.

The real sound is a roar. A deep, harmonic, tectonic groan that seems to emanate from the very bedrock beneath the water. It is the sound of machinery, but machinery of a geological scale. It is the sound of the planet being *processed*. Great, slow-turning augers, buried deep, grinding stone into slurry. The percussion of colossal pistons, firing once a minute, sending a shudder through the grating and a ripple across the endless water. The high, keening shriek of steam venting from a hundred different pipes, each one a black metal tree, leafless and slick with moisture, rising from the depths. This is the Industrial Echo—the constant, deafening bass note of the world's labor.

And yet, despite the noise, despite the toxic sheen on the water, life is *packed* in. The sparse vegetation is a lie of another kind. Look closer. The surface isn't bare; it's *occupied*.

Every square foot of the iron grating, every rock, every pipe is coated in a dense, black, biological crust. It’s a living asphalt, a microbial mat so thick it has texture like leather or vulcanized rubber. It smells of ozone and damp metal and something faintly biological, like a low-tide marsh. In the dim light, it glistens.

Life here isn't about leaves and branches. It's about mouths, and gills, and slick, chitinous plates.

The water itself is a soup. Black, armored, multi-legged things, like exaggerated crayfish or woodlice the size of dinner plates, scuttle through the shallows with a persistent clicking that is lost in the industrial roar. They are the grazers of the asphalt-mat. Schools of pale, eyeless fish, their bodies almost translucent, flow in silent rivers between the submerged foundations of scattered structures.

Those structures… they are the "built." Not cities, not even towns. They are bones. The skeletal frames of great processing facilities, long abandoned to the water and the rust. Their metal beams are now roosts for flocks of silent, leather-winged creatures, something between a bat and a manta ray, that hang in the gloom, their folded wings dripping condensation. Sometimes, one drops and glides low over the water, snatching a fish with a movement too fast to see.

There are other structures, more purposeful: a single, squat building of riveted iron, its windows glowing with the internal light of a generator. A network of pipes, thick as a man, that travels for miles across the region, a slow-moving river of processed slurry. These are the scars of a recent past, or a persistent present—a scattered, industrial fingerprint on a world that is still trying to become.

Because that is the deepest truth. This place is embryonic. The erosion score is a whisper of a lie; the land isn't *eroding*. It's *forming*. The tectonic groan is the sound of new continents being born, agonizingly slowly, beneath the water. The black crust is the first stage of soil. The weeping rocks are the first stage of a river system. Everything is in a state of raw, pre-formation. You are not in a dying world. You are in a world that has barely begun, and the deafening industrial processes are its violent, painful birthing contractions. The air is thick with the steam of its creation. The water is warm with its fever. The sound is the first word of a planet trying to speak.

And you are standing on a rusted grating, listening to it all, the warm water lapping at your boots.
