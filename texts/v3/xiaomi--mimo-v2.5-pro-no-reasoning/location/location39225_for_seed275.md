Let the parameters settle into a pattern. Here is one place that holds them all.

***

You descend.

Not through soil or rock, but through a lattice of living tissue — a slow elevator ride through the cambium of a tree so vast it has no name, only regional designations. The shaft is warm with the body-heat of the thing that contains it, but the air is cool, the way a root cellar is cool. No moisture condenses on the walls. There is no moisture to condense. You are inside a xerophyte, a plant that has evolved not merely to *tolerate* aridness but to *be* the aridness — to metabolize without water, drawing energy from geothermal gradients, from the slow decay of mineral substrates, from piezoelectric humors generated by the flexing of its own immense bones.

The shaft opens.

You step out onto a plateau — a shelf of petrified heartwood, hard as basalt, spanning a chasm inside the trunk. Below you, far below, the interior of the plant drops away into a darkness punctuated by the faint bioluminescence of fungal symbiotes, pale blue-white, like stars reflected in no water. Above you, the canopy-void: the trunk has hollowed itself over millennia into a vertical city, a columnar metropolis of chambers and galleries and bridge-roads, all carved or *grown* into the xylem and phloem of the organism.

This is **Vael Thress** — though "this" is insufficient. The city occupies roughly eleven kilometers of vertical space and three kilometers of diameter inside a single plant that began growing before the last continental collision. The plant is still growing. Slowly. A millimeter a year, perhaps, in the outermost layers. The city moves with it, adjusting, re-sculpting, the way a hermit crab reshapes its shell.

---

**The architecture.**

Nothing here is *built* in the traditional sense. Nothing is assembled from dead, shaped materials. Every wall, every floor, every beam and buttress is alive — is a living extension of the host plant, guided over generations by careful pruning, selective cultivation, hormonal signaling, and a sophisticated grafting tradition that functions as both engineering and art. The inhabitants — a warm-blooded, six-limbed species with prehensile ear-ridges and a trilling vocal range — do not construct. They *persuade*. They coax the wood into shapes using hormone pastes, rhythmic vibrations, and a practice somewhere between farming and surgery. The plant responds over years, decades, growing a new beam where one was requested, widening a corridor, extruding a shelf.

The result is an architecture that breathes.

Walls flex with air pressure changes. Floors pulse faintly — you can feel it through your feet — with the slow peristalsis of sap transport. A staircase spiraling upward through a branch-chamber is warm to the touch, and the railings are smooth from generations of hands but also faintly adhesive, covered in a micro-texture of living cell walls that grip without grabbing. The city is dense in places — clustered around the nutrient-rich zones where the plant's vascular bundles converge — and eerily sparse in others, where the wood is old and hard and mostly inert, and a long gallery might stretch for two hundred meters with nothing but silence and the faint amber translucence of walls lit from within by bioluminescent veins.

---

**The surface.**

Outside — if you can call it outside — the plant's outer bark rises in ridges and cliffs of cork-tissue, weathered and splitting, the color of dried blood and iron. The landscape beyond the trunk is a plain of mineral crust: salt flats, calcium deposits, desiccated clay, all of it cracked into geometric plates, all of it utterly dry. No rivers. No rain. The sky is a permanent haze of fine mineral dust, suspended in the thin atmosphere, giving the light a diffuse, even quality — no harsh shadows, no glare. The sun is a bright disc you can stare at without pain, ringed by a faint ochre corona. It is midday, or something like it. The light is constant; the planet's axial tilt is negligible. There are no seasons, only a slow pulsation of geothermal warmth that the plant tracks with its root-network, kilometers deep.

And yet.

The plain is not empty. It is *covered* in plants — low, dense, rubbery growths that cling to the salt crust like lichen on stone, except they are not lichen. They are the outermost root-hairs and rhizome-tips of Vael Thress itself, the plant's exploratory network, sent out across the plain to taste the mineral substrate, to absorb trace metals, to anchor. They are pale, almost white, and they move — slowly, so slowly you have to watch for minutes before you see it — in response to temperature gradients and chemical signals. The plain is a garden. The garden is a nerve-net. The nerve-net is the city's sensory skin.

Between the rhizome-mats, animals move. Small ones, mostly: insectoid grazers with flattened bodies and wide, splayed legs for navigating the cracked ground. They feed on the rhizome-tips — a managed relationship, not parasitism. The plant tolerates them, perhaps encourages them; their waste fertilizes the mineral crust. Larger predators — lean, low-slung quadrupeds with serrated beaks — stalk the grazers in the hazy distance, their movements deliberate, unhurried, matching the pace of everything here. This is not an ecosystem in a hurry. It is an ecosystem in *process* — embryonic, still sorting itself out, the species-diversity low but the biomass high, the food webs simple but tightening, becoming more complex with each passing century.

---

**The industry.**

Deep in the plant's interior, in the nutrient-dense core where the vascular pressure is highest, the inhabitants have built — *grown* — their industrial zones. Here, the walls are thick with grafted bio-reactors: chambers of modified plant tissue that ferment mineral slurries into usable compounds, that distill volatile organics from the sap, that generate electrical current through the manipulation of ion gradients in the plant's own cellular machinery. The air hums. Not loudly — it is mostly quiet here, the dominant sound the slow, rhythmic creaking of living wood under pressure, the occasional distant groan of a branch adjusting its load — but there is a hum, a vibration you feel in your teeth, the collective thrum of a hundred bio-reactors doing their work. Engines, of a sort. Machines, of a sort. But every machine is alive, and every engine is a grafted organ, and the factory floor is a membrane.

Power is transmitted through the plant's own vascular system — electrical potentials riding the sap-stream, carried on the backs of charged proteins, tapped at junctions and terminals throughout the city. Lights glow — not the harsh blue-white of the fungal bioluminescence but a warmer amber, cultivated from specialized cells in the wood itself, a domesticated glow. The city has infrastructure: transport corridors where platform-like growths carry passengers along pre-grown routes, communication networks of vibrating fibers strung through the heartwood, sanitation systems that recycle waste through fungal gardens embedded in the walls.

It is industrial. It is alive. It is, depending on your frame of reference, either the most advanced civilization you have ever encountered or the most primitive — because it has never left the tree.

---

**The sound.**

Mostly quiet. The quiet of a living thing at rest — not silence, but the deep, sub-audible hum of biological processes. Sap moving. Cells dividing. The faint, high-pitched whine of ion-pumps in the bio-reactor district, barely audible. Wind — such as it is — makes no sound against the bark; the atmosphere is too thin, too still. Occasionally, a voice: a trilling call echoing down a corridor, a burst of laughter that sounds like birdsong played backward, the rhythmic chanting of workers coaxing a new beam into place. These sounds arrive and dissolve quickly, absorbed by the soft, porous walls. The city swallows noise the way a forest does.

And beneath all of it, so low you might not hear it but will *feel* it: the plant's heartbeat. A slow, tidal pulse, once every forty-seven minutes, as the root-network cycles a pressure-wave of sap from the deep substrate up through the trunk and back. The city rides this pulse. The lights flicker — a gentle dimming and brightening. The floors shift, almost imperceptibly. The inhabitants do not notice it, the way you do not notice your own pulse. It is the rhythm of the world.

---

You stand on the shelf. The chasm drops below you into blue-white darkness. The walls breathe around you. The air smells of resin and mineral dust and something faintly sweet, like fermentation. It is cool, and quiet, and the light is warm amber, and outside the world is a dry flat nothing full of slow, patient life, and inside the world is a cathedral made of flesh, and neither world is finished. Both are still becoming.
