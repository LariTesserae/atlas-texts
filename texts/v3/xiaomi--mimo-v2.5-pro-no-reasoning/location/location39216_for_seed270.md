You find yourself not in a landscape, but in a mechanism.

The ground beneath you is low and ancient, a seabed of memory. It’s a shallow depression in the planetary crust, perhaps the ghost of an ocean that boiled away eons ago. The "soil" is a dense, laminite crust of grey salts and powdered rock, so compacted by eons of pressure that it rings with a dull, metallic note when struck. Erosion has done its work here, not by wind or water, but by time itself. It has not carved canyons but has worn everything down to a final, exhausted geometry of gentle swells and shallow basins. The terrain is nearly flat, a plainscape of perpetual near-evenness.

The air is cool and unnaturally still, yet it is never silent. It is thick, not with moisture, but with a visible, shimmering particulate—the shed exoskeletons and spore-dust of the life that dominates this place. Light from a distant, pale sun filters through this haze, casting a diffuse, shadowless glow that feels both constant and tired. There is no weather, only a slow, cyclical churn of this atmospheric dust, a process that takes generations to complete a single rotation.

This is not a world of flora or simple fauna. The life here is an overwhelming, unified presence. It is a single, continental-scale colony organism, a myco-mechanical entity that has colonized every cubic inch of the low basin. It is a hive, but the hive is the world.

You stand on its back. The ground is a living integument, a hide of interlocking, chitinous plates the color of obsidian and tarnished silver. From this hide rises the colony itself. There are no trees, no grass. Instead, there are dense clusters of what look like industrial skeletons—towering, segmented structures of a bone-like ceramic material, laced with conduits of dull, pulsing copper. These are the respiratory stacks and nutrient spires, and they are everywhere, packed so closely that they form a metallic forest with little room to move. The density is suffocating; every surface is claimed. The spires click and whir with the motion of unseen internal machinery, venting plumes of warm, stale air and dust in a constant, rhythmic hiss. Some have grown so tall and old that they have become brittle, their upper segments flaking away into the perpetual haze.

The inhabitants are the colony's cells. They are the fauna. They are legion. Insectoid creatures the size of dogs skitter across the chitinous ground, their six legs tapping a frantic, complex rhythm on the plates. They are not predators or prey; they are workers, soldiers, and attendants, their bodies hardened carapaces of grey-black chitin. Some are sculptors, endlessly repairing the chitin plates with a secreted, quick-hardening resin. Others are harvesters, meticulously scraping nutrient films from the spires. Larger, slower creatures move through the forest of spires—docile, multi-limbed behemoths that serve as mobile cisterns or ambulatory nurseries.

The sound is the life of the colony. It is a constant, grinding hum, the baseline thrum of a billion synchronized metabolisms. Over this, the sharp clicks of the workers’ legs, the whisper-soft rasp of resin being applied, and the rhythmic *hiss-clank* of the respiratory spires create a dense, industrial soundscape. It is the sound of a factory floor that has grown over a world.

Scattered throughout this biomechanical jungle are the remains of something else. Not ruins, but artifacts. Here, a half-buried dome of a strange, non-corrosive alloy, its surface smoothed by ages of dust-wind, is now being slowly overgrown by a scaffold of the colony’s ceramic growth. Further on, the corroded stump of a tower—a folly of a forgotten, scattered people—juts from the living ground like a broken tooth. The colony ignores these intrusions, flowing around them or using them as foundations for its own structures. They are faint marks, partial remains, relics of a scale of existence this place has long since surpassed.

This world is not strange, but it is deeply unusual. It is an ecosystem that has become a single, industrial organism, driven by a collective, alien will. It has reached its oldest stable state, a final form where biology and mechanics are indistinguishable. The light is normal, the air is cool, the ground is low and eroded. But within these simple parameters is a place of overwhelming, dense, and active life—a single engine, humming and clicking and hissing in the endless dust.
