The mountain is not made of stone. It is made of air.

You stand on what should be a peak, but the ground beneath your feet is the color of a bruised plum, a soft, porous rock that drinks the air. It is called *sky-foam* by the people who live here, and it is as new as yesterday's rain. This is not an old mountain; it is an idea of a mountain, a geological whisper still deciding its own shape. The rock is so fresh that where you step, your boots leave dark, wet impressions that will not fade for a week. Condensation beads on every surface, not as a film, but as droplets that hang, perfectly spherical, from the sharp edges of the rock, refusing to fall. The air is thick with the smell of wet mineral and something like ozone.

The city is built into this embryonic stone. It is a settlement of terraces and bridges, all carved with patient, primitive tools from the living, weeping rock. There are no foundations, only shapes subtracted from the mountain's rising form. The structures are a lattice of dark, water-logged wood and the lavender-grey stone itself. Canals, not of water, but of a slow, viscous mist, wind between the buildings, fed by the ever-weeping rock. The people move through this mist on stilts, their feet making soft, sucking sounds as they lift them from the damp ground. Their tools are of sharpened obsidian and copper, soft metals that bend and must be constantly re-straightened.

And it is loud. Not with voices—the populace is sparse, a handful of families tending to their terraces of moss and luminous lichen. It is loud with the mountain itself. A deep, resonant hum emanates from the rock, the sound of its own crystalline structure slowly forming. Water drips in a thousand different rhythms, from the eaves of roofs, from the tips of stone leaves, into the misty canals below. The air vibrates with the low-frequency song of the mountain's birth. Occasionally, a deep, grinding *crack* will echo across the small valley, not a collapse, but a growth-spurt, a new ridge or spire pushing its way into the sky.

The light is grey and soft, filtered through a perpetual high-altitude haze. There is no harsh sun, only a diffuse luminosity that makes the wet rock gleam like polished bone. In this light, the vegetation is a shock of intense, quiet color. Thick cushions of emerald moss cling to every surface. Ferns with fronds like black lace uncurl from crevices, their edges beaded with perfect water-droplets. A single, massive tree—one might call it a *Weeping Ironwood*—dominates the central terrace. Its bark is the color of rust, and it drips a slow, clear sap that hardens on contact with the air into a flexible, amber-like resin. The people use this resin to patch their roofs and seal their tools.

Fauna is a rumor. A flash of iridescent wings—perhaps a large, slow-moving beetle that feeds on the lichen. The skittering of something small and pale in the deep shadows of a newly-formed crevice. But mostly, it is just the people on their stilts, the dripping water, and the profound, humming emptiness of the growing rock.

The rules here are simple, and they are written in the moisture. Nothing dries. If you leave a tool outside, it will be beaded with water within the hour, and within a month, a thin film of moss will begin to claim it. The city itself is in a constant, slow conversation with the rock. When the mountain groans and shifts, the inhabitants must listen, and sometimes, they must carve anew, guiding the new growth rather than fighting it. They are not builders in the traditional sense; they are gardeners of geology, coaxing their homes from a world that is still waking up. It is a place of patient, damp alchemy, where the line between the built and the born is as soft and blurred as the edge of the mist.
