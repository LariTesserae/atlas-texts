# The Distillery

There is a staircase. It descends.

You notice the humidity before you notice anything else — it hits at the seventh or eighth step, a wall of warmth and wet that fogs the skin and makes the iron handrail slick. The air tastes of chlorophyll and copper. By the time you reach the bottom, you have already begun to sweat, and the sound has begun: a roar, or not a roar — a *presence* of sound, enormous and constant, the way a river is constant, the way a factory floor is constant.

You are in the Distillery.

The name is old, from when this was something else — a cistern complex, maybe, or a storm-drain junction, some piece of infrastructure sunk six meters below the street grade of the city above. The ceiling is low. Not oppressively low; you could stand on another person's shoulders and press your palms flat against it. But it is close, and it is everywhere, a lattice of concrete and corroded I-beams over which something has been growing for a very long time.

That something is plants.

Not plants in the way a garden has plants, or a forest has plants. Plants in the way a lung has tissue. Every surface is covered — not decorated, *covered*, as in replaced, as in the original material has been subsumed and what you see now is living. Roots the color of dark rope have threaded through the rebar and split the concrete along its seams, not violently, but completely, the way tree roots crack a sidewalk over forty patient years. The Distillery has had longer than that. The roots have become the structure. Where a beam has rusted through, a thick woody trunk has grown up to take its load, and the trunk has hardened into something that rings like iron when you knock it, dense as lignite, bark like ceramic tile. The ceiling is a mat of interlocking stems and broad leaves — philodendron, maybe, or something that was philodendron once, leaf-pads the size of tabletops laid overlapping like shingles, dripping, always dripping, condensation rolling along every vein and falling in a ceaseless rain onto the floor below.

The floor is wet. The floor is always wet. There is no dry place in the Distillery. The lowest level holds water ankle-deep, a permanent warm shallows in which the root-mats stand like mangrove stumps. Higher platforms — old concrete slabs, pallets, stacked cinderblock — rise out of it like islands, and on every island, someone has built something, and on every built thing, something has grown. The water is not clean. It is green-brown, thick with dissolved organics and the shed material of ten thousand plants, warm as bathwater, fed by pipes that still function — the original municipal water supply, or what remains of it, augmented by a system of collection channels carved into the walls that catch condensation and redirect it downward through a network of bamboo gutters and plastic tubing. The pipes knock. That is part of the sound: a rhythmic, arrhythmic percussion of air in the pipes, the water-hammer bang of valves opening and closing on timers, the deep gurgling of the main drainage at the complex's center, a concrete throat through which water flows out and, somehow, more water flows in.

The light is low. It comes from fixtures that hang from the ceiling on chains and wires, industrial housings fitted with grow-spectrum bulbs that cast everything in a pinkish-violet haze, a light that is less illumination than it is atmosphere, a color the air has become. Some of the older fixtures are fluorescent tubes, half-dead, flickering in a way that makes the shadows of the leaves seem to twitch. Between the fixtures, in the deeper corners, the light drops to almost nothing — a murky green-black where the plants grow blind and pale, reaching with white stems toward the nearest lamp. There are reflective panels — old Mylar blankets, sheets of polished aluminum — tacked to walls and angled to bounce light into shadowed alcoves, but they are overgrown too, the reflective surfaces now half-matted with moss and the creeping fingers of some vine that finds even Mylar a hospitable substrate.

The density is staggering. You cannot move in a straight line for more than two meters without encountering something — a trunk, a wall, a structure, a hanging basket dense with fern, a pipe, a shelf of seedlings in cut-open plastic bottles, a curtain of aerial roots descending from the canopy layer above like a beaded doorway. The pathways through the Distillery are not designed; they are *worn*, footpaths through the undergrowth that shift as plants grow and people find new routes, so that the map of the place — if anyone has one — is always a few weeks out of date. You turn sideways to pass between two root-columns. You duck under a hanging garden of epiphytes — bromeliads, orchids, things with no name — that has grown so thick it has become a solid mass, a green stalactite, dripping its own slow condensate into the water below.

People live here. You know this not because you see them — at this moment the paths are empty, or seem empty — but because the place is built. It is not wilderness. The plant-density is real, total, almost suffocating, but it is *managed*, or it has been managed, or the management and the growth have become the same gesture. A shelf made of reclaimed lumber holds rows of mint in repurposed coffee cans; the mint has overflowed the cans and rooted into the shelf and the shelf has begun to rot and the mint has begun to hold the shelf together. A greenhouse — really just a frame of bent conduit with sheet plastic stretched over it — contains something taller, something with thick stems and broad leaves that press against the plastic from inside, fogs it with transpiration until the interior is invisible. Beside it, a pump sits on a cinderblock plinth, electric, industrial, humming — it pushes water from the floor-level cistern up to the upper platforms through a web of hoses, and it is always running, and its hum is the bass note under everything, the foundational frequency of the Distillery's sound.

The sound. You have to raise your voice, or you would, if anyone were here to talk to. It is the pump. It is the water — dripping, flowing, trickling through channels and down walls and off leaves, a constant soft roar of water finding its way down, like standing inside a rainstorm that never arrives. It is the pipes, banging their slow percussion. It is the hum of the lights, a sixty-cycle drone that you feel in your teeth. It is the occasional creak of a root-trunk settling under its load, a sound like a ship's hull, deep and woody and slow. Underneath all of it, a subsonic throb that might be the ventilation — there are fans, big industrial fans, some of them original to the cistern and some of them newer, all of them running at low speed, pushing the warm wet air in slow circuits through the complex so that no pocket stagnates, so that the condensation keeps moving, so that the plants keep breathing. The fans are the lungs of the place. Without them, you get the sense, the Distillery would simply close, the air would thicken to something you could not pass through, the water would rise, and the plants would continue without you.

There are no animals. This surprises you the first time you think about it, because the place feels so alive — explosively, aggressively alive — but it is alive in one register only. No birds in the canopy, no fish in the standing water, no insects on the leaves. The plants have the place to themselves. Perhaps it is the light spectrum. Perhaps it is something in the water. Perhaps the ecology here has simply resolved, in its long slow establishment, into a system that does not require animal life, that has found some other way to pollinate, to decompose, to cycle — fungal mats under the root layer, bacterial films on every wet surface, a hidden chemistry that does the work that bees and worms do elsewhere. The Distillery is not sterile. It is the opposite of sterile. But it is monocultural in a way that transcends species — every niche is filled by a plant, every function is performed by a plant, the dead material of one plant feeds the living tissue of another, and the loop closes without the need for a single moving thing.

The erosion is old. This is not new. The Distillery has been here — in this configuration, in this state — for long enough that the concrete has fully yielded, that the rust has stabilized, that the water channels have carved their final shapes. Nothing is being destroyed anymore; everything that could collapse has collapsed, and what remains is what can hold. The root-structures have found their equilibrium. The water table has found its level. The human structures — the shelves, the platforms, the pump stations — are maintained in a kind of eternal repair, always being patched and braced and adjusted, but the underlying form does not change. The Distillery is in its own state. It is what it is. It has been what it is for longer than anyone currently living in it has been alive, and it will continue to be what it is for longer than that, the plants growing and dying and growing again in their slow patient cycles, the water rising and falling by centimeters with the seasons above, the pumps humming, the pipes knocking, the whole dense wet loud dim warm green place simply *persisting*, the way a reef persists, the way a swamp persists, the way anything that has fully become itself persists — not because it is trying to, but because there is no force acting on it that asks it to be anything else.

You stand in it. The air is thick and sweet and metallic and alive. A drop of water lands on your shoulder. Another on your head. The sound surrounds you completely, a loudness so constant it becomes a kind of silence — the silence of total saturation, of a place with no gaps, no negative space, nothing left unfilled. You breathe. The Distillery breathes with you.
