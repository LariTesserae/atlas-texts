The air is so thin it feels more like a memory of air, a pressure upon the eardrums rather than a medium for breathing. You are on the Plinth, a name that is less a proper noun and more a description whispered by the things that remain. It is a city built upon a mountain, or perhaps a mountain that grew from a city; the distinction has long been moot.

The rock of the Plinth is a dark, glassy basalt, etched with the channels of ancient, non-aqueous rivers. It has been weathered not by water, but by the slow, grinding passage of aeons and the sheer, unrelenting pressure of the elevation. The erosion here is not an act of subtraction but of settling. The mountain has found its final shape, a jagged, brutalist needle that pierces a perpetually twilit sky. There is no sun, only a diffuse, silvery luminescence that emanates from the sky-dust, the fine, metallic particulate that hangs in the void-thin atmosphere. It casts no sharp shadows, rendering the world in shades of charcoal, pewter, and bruised violet.

And upon this spire, the city. It is a marvel of absence. Towers of basalt and something like stained quartz climb towards the bruised sky, their forms intricate and impossible, yet they are hollow. Grand archways hang in the air, connected to nothing. Sweeping plazas are paved with perfectly fitted stones that lead to sheer drops. This is architecture of the ghost, a city built for residents who have departed on a plane of existence that does not require doorways or floors. The density is paradoxical: the sheer amount of structured, built space only serves to emphasize the immense emptiness within and between each structure. It is organized, layered, but utterly uninhabited.

The silence is the dominant feature. It is a profound, bottomless quiet that swallows the faint sounds of your own movement. The only interruptions are the low, resonant hum of the Plinth itself—a deep, geological thrumming felt more in the bones than in the ears—and the occasional, crystalline *tink* of a pressure-fracture in the distant rock.

And then, there is the weirdness. Reality here is thin, stretched taut over the bones of the world. It is a place where the laws of cause and effect have grown lazy. The wind, which should not exist in this thinness, moves in currents that are visible, shimmering distortions in the silvery light, like heat haze over a cold flame. A light dusting of a grey, fungal-looking powder coats some of the higher ledges, the only sign of what passes for vegetation. It is not a plant; it has no chlorophyll, no life cycle in the traditional sense. It is a geological mold, a crystalline pattern that grows, dies, and reforms with the city's slow, tectonic breath.

Creatures exist, but they are rare and wrong. You might see a "Rift-Walker," a thing of folded angles and static, skittering across a distant rooftop, its form seeming to glitch in and out of perception. It does not make a sound. Sometimes, a section of the city will simply... invert. A tower will hang upside down from the sky for a few hours before righting itself. An echo from a footstep taken a century ago might suddenly arrive, a ghost of a sound in the crushing silence.

This is a world in a state of final, dynamic balance. The slow creep of the reality-sickness, the patient weathering of the stone, the silent, cyclical growth of the lithovoric fungus, the strange migration of the non-euclidean fauna—they are all processes. The city is not dead; it has simply moved on, leaving behind its husk, a monument to a purpose now alien, at the top of the world, in the cool, dim, and profound emptiness.
