You are not in a place that grew. You are in a place that was *assembled*.

The floor beneath your feet is not soil or bedrock but a gridded platform of cast-iron plates, their surfaces stippled with a thousand tiny pyramids for grip. The geometry is perfect, repeating in every direction until it is lost in the gloom. The plates are cold—not the deep, stable chill of a cave, but a sharp, metallic cold that leaches heat from your bones. Between each plate, a thin channel is cut, designed to carry something away. These channels are currently dry, but they glisten with a film of condensation. The air is cold and heavy with moisture, and where it meets the colder iron, it surrenders its water. Slow, perfect droplets form on the undersides of pipes and the lips of machinery, growing fat and heavy before they fall with a soft *tick* into the channels below. It is a mechanical, predictable rain.

This is a pocket, a chamber perhaps twenty meters across, but its ceiling is lost in shadow. The space is dense, packed. Pipes of varying diameters—some as thick as your torso, others slender as a finger—run in bundles along the walls and ceiling, a circulatory system for a world that no longer needs blood. They are riveted, flanged, and welded together, creating a layered, three-dimensional network. Some are warm to the touch, radiating a faint, residual heat from processes elsewhere. Others are sheathed in a fuzzy layer of pale grey-green lichen, an organism that has adapted to metabolize metal oxides and the trace minerals in the condensation. This is the sparse vegetation of this world: a metallic moss, a rust-eating fungus. It gives the hard edges a soft, worn look.

The light is dim, provided not by any central sun but by periodic, cage-enclosed work lamps bolted to the walls and pipes. They cast a weak, yellowish cone of illumination that doesn't so much light the space as define pockets of visibility, leaving deep, complex shadows between them. The shadows are not empty; they are filled with the intricate silhouettes of more machinery, more pipes, more support struts. The effect is one of immense, layered density. You are inside a machine's anatomy.

The soundscape is mostly quiet, a low-level hum that is felt more than heard. It is the vibration of the floor plates, a subsonic thrum that suggests immense power being channeled, contained, and directed elsewhere. This is punctuated by the rhythmic, slow drip of condensation, and, occasionally, a distant, pneumatic *hiss* or a low, grinding *clank* that resonates through the structure. There are no voices, no biological sounds. This is a world of process, not of life.

The strangeness emerges in the details. In one corner, three large pipes converge into a single, polished chrome junction box. The box is featureless except for a single, circular dial in its center. The dial is not analog; it is a disk of layered, iridescent crystal that slowly swirls with internal colors, like oil on water, but in a spectrum you do not recognize. It is monitoring something, but what? The air near it is colder. Elsewhere, a section of the iron floor is covered by a thick, transparent resin, like amber. Embedded within it, frozen in mid-process, are a dozen silver worms—not animals, but delicate, articulated cleaning mechanisms, each the size of a finger, their bristle-tipped heads poised inches from a pipe's surface. They are inactive, perhaps for a cycle, perhaps forever.

This place is active. The condensation is constant, the hum is persistent, the slow swirl of the crystal dial is continuous. It is a system in maintenance mode, a pocket of industrial process embedded in a larger, unseen whole. It is a womb for functionality, a crucible for cold, damp, mechanical life. You are a visitor in its lungs, its liver, its quiet, grinding heart. It does not notice you. It has its own concerns.
