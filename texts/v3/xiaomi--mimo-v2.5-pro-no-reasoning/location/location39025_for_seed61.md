The first thing you notice is the sound. Not a singular sound, but a layered, reverberant chorus that you feel in your bones. It is the sound of a million droplets, falling not from a sky but from the ceiling of a colossal cavern. This is not the gentle patter of rain; it is the steady, resonant drumming of water striking stone, pooling water, and running in thick, continuous rivulets down weeping walls. The acoustics are immense, the sound bouncing from unseen surfaces, creating a constant, spatial roar that fills every cubic inch of the space. Beneath this is a lower hum, the sub-bass vibration of the entire world settling into its ancient, geologic equilibrium.

You are not standing on ground, but on a platform of worked, dark stone. It is the edge of a settlement, or what was once one. The city does not rise upward, but sinks inward, carved into the belly of a vast, wet earth. The architecture is monolithic and brutalist, built from the very rock it occupies, all sharp edges and heavy lintels, now softened and rounded by eons of slow, damp erosion. The structures are dense, layered, and tightly packed. Squat, domed dwellings are crammed together, connected by a web of low, covered walkways and stairways that spiral down into deeper, unseen warrens. Every surface is slick, not just with water, but with a thin, dark patina, a biological film that is not quite alive. It is the mineral record of a world that has been weeping for centuries.

There is no sun here. The only light is a cold, ghostly phosphorescence. It emanates from the wet surfaces themselves—a faint, greenish-white glow from the condensed moisture on the walls, from the standing pools of black water, from the very air, thick with humidity. The light doesn't illuminate so much as it *suggests* shapes, catching the edges of a carved doorway, the curve of a cistern, the silhouette of a figure hunched under a low arch. The air is cool and heavy, thick enough to taste, carrying the scent of cold stone, deep earth, and something metallic, like wet iron.

Life here is almost an absence. There are no plants, no green things. The fungal-and-simpler life that exists is the source of some of the glow—a pale, hair-like mycelium that threads through cracks in the stone, pulsing with a slow, rhythmic light. And there are the "singers." They are the rare fauna, the source of the weirdness. They are not animals as you know them. They are arthropodic, perhaps, or something stranger, built of chitin and glistening, wet membrane. They are rarely seen directly, only as fleeting shadows that dart between the domed structures or skitter up the weeping walls. But they are always *heard*. Each one produces a continuous, high-pitched, harmonic hum, a single perfect tone. With thousands of them scattered throughout the city, their combined song creates a complex, ever-shifting chord that cuts through the roar of the water. It is a loud, dissonant, yet strangely stable soundscape that has not changed in a thousand years.

The world is in its final, stasis. There is no growth, no decay, only this slow, wet, weeping balance. The people, whoever they were, are gone. Their tools, simple things of shaped stone and bone, lie where they were last set down, encrusted with mineral deposits. Their city remains, a perfect, hollow shell, now inhabited only by the water, the glow, and the singing things that have found a niche in its unchanging, resonant dark. It is a hidden pocket of the world, a single, immense feature contained within the earth, eternally damp, eternally loud, eternally still.
