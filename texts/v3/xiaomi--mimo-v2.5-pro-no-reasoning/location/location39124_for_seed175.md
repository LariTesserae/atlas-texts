# The Basin of Soft Colours

You would not notice the silence at first. It would settle on you slowly, like humidity on skin, and only after minutes — or perhaps longer — would you understand that something was missing from the air. Not just quiet. *Absence.*

The basin is small, no more than two hundred paces across, cupped in a depression of ancient basalt that has weathered so long its surfaces have gone smooth and faintly concave, like the insides of palms held upward. The rock is dark grey where it is dry and nearly black where it is not, which is most places. Water comes from everywhere and nowhere in particular: a beading of condensation on every stone face, a slow shining of seepage along fracture lines, the quiet mottling of saturated ground. Nowhere does it rush. Nowhere does it pool deeper than ankle-height in the few low places. The ground breathes moisture the way a sleeping body breathes warmth — continuously, without effort.

It is warm. Not the heavy swelter of lowland tropics but a radiant, geothermal warmth that rises through the rock itself, as though the mountain remembers some deeper fire. The air feels close and close-textured, like cloth held against the face, and your skin never quite dries.

The basalt shelves are draped in vegetation — wide-petaled things with leaves like dark hands, their undersides showing veins of pale copper and lavender. Mosses coat the vertical faces in deep cushions of green so dark they are almost blue, and from these cushions grow stalks of something that is not quite fern and not quite flower: translucent, branching slowly, ending in structures that hold droplets of water with the delicacy of blown glass. The plants are spaced apart. Each one occupies its own radius of silence, its own small territory of rock and moisture, and between them the bare stone shows through in wide, smooth patches. You could walk between the growths without touching a single leaf if you chose your steps.

There are no insects. No birds call from above the basin's rim, which rises only slightly above you — weathered walls perhaps twice the height of a person, scalloped by millennia of rain, their edges softened into broad curves that suggest nothing so much as the lip of a very old bowl. Nothing rustles in the undergrowth because there is no undergrowth to rustle, only these spaced, deliberate presences, each one utterly still.

The silence is not empty. It has a *weight*. It presses gently against the eardrums, and in its depth you begin to hear the landscape's own modest sounds: the infinitesimal ticking of water collecting at the tip of a leaf and falling to stone. The soft, almost subsonic hum of moisture evaporating from warm rock — more a vibration than a noise, felt in the sternum. Once, a long slow creak that might have been the mountain adjusting its ancient weight, or might have been nothing.

The light comes in normally — daylight, overcast, the colour of pearl — but the basin receives it strangely. The wet surfaces reflect and scatter it so that shadows have no hard edges, and the air itself seems faintly luminous, as though the moisture holds light between its droplets. The plants' translucence catches this and glows. The effect is of a place that is softly lit from within.

And here is where the strangeness lives. There are no animal remains, no shells, no bones, no tracks, no evidence that anything with a heartbeat has ever entered this space. The vegetation grows without pollination, or pollinates itself, or has never needed pollination. The mosses spread without spore. You search for a mechanism and find none, only the slow, patient fact of growth in a world that appears to have invented only half of its own ecology. The plants feed on warmth and water and whatever the rock weathers into. They need nothing else. They have never needed anything else.

Near the center of the basin, the stone has weathered into a shape — not carved, not shaped by any intention, but formed by long erosion into something that looks very much like a shallow bowl, slightly tilted, in which the clearest water you have ever seen rests in perfect stillness. It is warm to the touch. It tastes of mineral and faint sweetness, like rain collected in something ancient. Around this bowl, the vegetation grows fractionally denser, as though drawn to it, and the copper-veined leaves lean inward at angles that seem not quite random.

Nothing changes here quickly. The mosses grow by millimeters per decade. The seep lines have followed the same paths for centuries. The water level in the stone bowl rises and falls with cycles so long you could not distinguish them from constancy. Even the warmth from below is steady, metronomic, the earth's slow pulse transmitted upward through rock that has been conducting it since before anything grew here to feel it.

You could sit in this place for a very long time. The silence would become familiar. The absence of animal life would begin to feel less like loss and more like a statement — as though the basin had decided, long ago, to keep only what was quiet, what was slow, what could grow without taking. The warmth would soak into your bones. The moisture would settle on your eyelashes. And eventually you might notice that your own breathing has slowed to match the rhythm of the place: the drip of water, the creak of stone, the long patience of things that have nowhere else to be.
