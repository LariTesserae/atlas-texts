# The Terraced Ache

---

You stand on the edge of a world that has been rising for a very long time and shows no sign of stopping — or of ever having been anything other than what it is.

The ground beneath you is **salt**, but not the brittle white crust you might imagine. It is amber, ochre, deep rust-red where iron has threaded through over millennia, and it is *hard* — dense as limestone, carved by wind that carries almost no moisture but has been carrying grains of itself for longer than any record. The surface is grooved. Everywhere, grooved. Channels and ridges and furrows run in slow, sweeping curves across every exposed face, the signature of a world that erodes itself, recycles itself, grinds its own substance down and lets the wind press it into new shapes elsewhere. There is no soil. There is no dust cloud. The particulate simply migrates — a continental crawl measured in eons — from the high plateaus to the basins and back again through uplift that is still, even now, happening beneath you, a tectonic patience so slow it makes glaciers look frantic.

The **elevation** is extreme. You are high. Not in the way of mountain peaks — there is no single summit, no dramatic knife-edge ridge. Instead, the world is *lifted*. The entire continental mass has risen into a series of terraced plateaus, each one hundreds of kilometers across, stepping upward like the dissection of some enormous altar. The lowest terrace is still kilometers above what might count as a sea floor, if there were a sea. The uppermost terraces — the ones you can see in the distance when the air clears, glowing faintly gold in the afternoon — are so high that the atmosphere thins to something barely breathable, and the sky above them is not blue but a dark, bruised indigo, almost violet, as though the sky itself is running out.

The **warmth** comes from below. Geothermal seep. The crust here is thin over a slow-churning mantle, and heat bleeds upward through the salt and mineral strata, keeping the air above the terraces at a perpetual thermal hum. Not hot — not oppressive — but *warm*, the way a machine room is warm, the way a sleeping body is warm. The surface radiates gently at night. You could press your palm to the salt at midnight and feel it: a faint pulse, not quite biological, not quite geological, somewhere between a heartbeat and a boiler.

---

**The Cities**

They are here. You might not notice them at first.

They grow out of the erosion channels like lichens grow from cracks in rock — except they are *industrial*, and *deliberate*, and *old*. The settlements are carved directly into the grooved faces of the terraces, following the natural drainage patterns of the salt as though the builders understood, long ago, that fighting the erosion was pointless and working with it was everything. Streets are channels. Walls are the natural ridgelines between furrows, reinforced and squared and mortared withiteite — a cement made from the salt itself, fired and powdered and reconstituted into something nearly as strong as stone.

From a distance, the cities look like the landscape: the same colors, the same textures, the same grooved and layered profile. You have to look carefully to see the windows — dark, narrow, recessed deep into the terrace walls. You have to look carefully to see the smoke stacks, which rise from the upper edges of the settlements and vent slow plumes of grey-white vapor: the exhaust of **industrial processes** that have been running, in some cases, for centuries.

What do they make? **Mineral compounds.** Refined salts, rare-earth precipitates, crystalline structures grown in long, patient, heat-fed vats that line the interiors of factory-caverns. The industry is not loud. The machinery runs *slowly* — huge, low-RPM turbines that draw on geothermal gradients, belt-driven systems that turn with the ponderous inevitability of millstones. The sound they produce is a low, subsonic hum that you feel more than hear, a vibration in the salt under your feet, a resonance in your sternum. It is the closest thing this world has to a heartbeat.

There are perhaps **forty or fifty cities** across the terraces visible from where you stand. The nearest is three days' travel across a featureless plain of grooved salt. The farthest is a smudge on the horizon, barely distinguishable from the haze of mineral dust that softens every distant edge. Between them: nothing. Vast, warm, quiet nothing. The space between cities is the *point*. This world is defined by its emptiness as much as its presence.

---

**The Fauna**

This is where the world becomes strange.

There is almost no vegetation. The word *almost* is important. On the lower terraces, where the geothermal heat is strongest and the salt surface is cracked and seamed, there are **mats** — thin, glassy, translucent films that cling to the leeward faces of erosion ridges. They are not plants. They have no roots, no leaves, no chlorophyll. They are **lithotrophic films**: communities of microorganisms that metabolize the mineral substrate itself, drawing energy from the chemical gradients between iron-rich and iron-poor strata. In strong light they shimmer faintly, prismatic, like oil on water. They are the foundation of a food chain that should not, by any terrestrial logic, exist.

But it does.

The fauna is **abundant** — paradoxically, inexplicably abundant for a world this dry and this bare. The creatures here are not numerous in the way of insects or birds; they are numerous the way stars are numerous, filling the vastness without crowding it.

**Grazers** move across the salt flats in slow herds. They are large — the smallest the size of a draft horse, the largest approaching the bulk of a small building — and they move with the patient, deliberate pace of everything in this world. Their bodies are **flattened**, low to the ground, armored in overlapping plates of mineralized tissue that mimic the grooved texture of the salt itself. From above, they are nearly invisible: the same colors, the same ridged and channelled surface. They graze by pressing their ventral surfaces against the lithotrophic mats and absorbing them through a kind of osmosis, then moving on, leaving clean, bare salt behind that will be recolonized in a decade or two. They have no eyes. They navigate by heat-sense, reading the geothermal gradients through their bellies, following the warm currents across the terraces.

**Drifters** occupy the upper air — or what counts for it. They are gas-filled organisms, bladdered and translucent, trailing filamentous appendages that catch the mineral dust suspended in the thermal updrafts. They feed on airborne particulate, filtering iron and sulfur and rare metals from the haze. They are silent. They move with the wind, which moves slowly here, and they reproduce by budding — a process so gradual that an individual drifter might take thirty years to produce a single offspring. Some of the largest are ancient, their gas bladders darkened and opaque with accumulated mineral deposits, trailing streamers of old tissue that have calcified into something resembling coral. They cast shadows on the terraces below — slow, drifting shadows the size of small clouds, which the ground-dwelling creatures orient themselves by.

**Borers** live *inside* the salt. You almost never see them. You see their work: holes, tunnels, channels that are subtly different from the erosion grooves — straighter, more uniform, occasionally branching at angles that suggest geometry rather than geology. The borers are warm-blooded, or something like it; they maintain their body temperature by metabolizing the chemical energy in the salt strata directly, dissolving and re-precipitating mineral as they go, building their tunnels from the waste of their own digestion. Their tunnels honeycomb the upper terraces. In some places, the ground is more tunnel than solid, and it hums faintly when the wind blows over the entrances, a breathy, almost tonal sound, like an organ pipe carved from a continent.

There are **predators**, too, but they are subtle. Something picks off the smaller grazers at the edges of the herds — you find the evidence in half-dissolved mineral husks, neatly opened, their interior tissues removed. The predators are never seen. The prevailing theory among the cities' naturalists is that they are borers that have adapted to hunt on the surface, emerging at night (though night here is barely darker than day, just a dimming, a cooling of two or three degrees) and moving through the salt with the same silent efficiency with which they tunnel. But no one has confirmed this. The predators may be something else entirely.

---

**The Quiet**

For a world with cities and factories and herds of megafauna and drifting aerial filter-feeders and invisible predators, the silence is **remarkable**. It is not the silence of absence — it is the silence of *slowness*. Everything here happens at a tempo that the human ear is not calibrated to catch. The grazers' footsteps are cushioned by their mineral plates. The drifters make no sound at all. The factories hum below the threshold of casual hearing. Even the wind, when it moves, moves in long, sustained exhalations rather than gusts, and the sound it makes over the grooved salt is not a whistle or a howl but a deep, continuous *shhhhhh* — white noise so low and so constant that the ear habituates to it within minutes and stops registering it as sound at all.

The loudest things in this landscape are the occasional **salt-quakes**: not earthquakes, exactly, but thermal events deep in the crust where a pocket of superheated mineral brine breaches a stratum and forces its way upward. The surface trembles. A new vent opens — sometimes kilometers from the nearest settlement, sometimes terrifyingly close — and for a few hours the air fills with the hiss of pressurized vapor and the crack of cooling mineral. Then it subsides. The vent seals itself over years, decades, with new precipitate. The cycle continues.

---

**The Rules**

Time moves differently here. Not metaphysically — physically. The dynamic is **slow**. Erosion cycles are measured in hundreds of thousands of years. The cities have been built in the grooves of formations that predate them by orders of magnitude, and they will be grooved over in turn, long after the builders' descendants have moved to a new terrace or a new channel. The industrial output accumulates patiently: refined minerals stored in vast, cool vaults carved into the terrace interiors, growing by imperceptible increments, destined for export to — what? To where? The cities trade with each other across the emptiness, caravans of grazer-drawn sleds that take months to cross the distance between settlements, carrying mineral wealth in one direction and manufactured goods — tools, fabric woven from borer-silk, drifter-gas in sealed containers for lighting and heating — in the other.

The inhabitants are **humanoid** — you catch glimpses of them in the cities, moving slowly through the grooved streets, their skin darkened by mineral dust, their clothing layered and heavy against the thermal fluctuations of day and night. They do not rush. The culture has adapted to the tempo of the world, or perhaps the world has shaped the culture: patience is the cardinal virtue, haste a form of madness. Conversations pause for minutes at a time and resume as though nothing has intervened. Construction projects span generations and no one finds this remarkable. A factory might be commissioned by a grandmother and completed by her grandchild, and the grandchild will consider this normal.

They are aware that their world is strange. They have cosmologies and geological sciences and they understand, in their way, that the abundance of fauna on a world with almost no water and almost no vegetation is anomalous — a mystery that their thinkers return to again and again, in slow, patient cycles of hypothesis and revision, published in journals that come out once a decade and are considered brisk.

---

**What You Notice Last**

The color. As the long afternoon deepens — not into darkness, but into a warmer, more saturated version of itself — the light changes. The salt terraces, which have been amber and gold all day, begin to shift toward **rose**, then **copper**, then something for which your language may not have a word: a deep, warm, mineral red that seems to come not from the light but from the stone itself, as though the terraces are remembering some ancient heat, some foundational warmth from when the world was young and molten and the salt was still liquid.

The drifters darken against the indigo sky. The herds slow further, if that is possible, and settle into the grooves. The factory hum deepens. And the world breathes — slowly, warmly, strangely — and continues its long, patient work of becoming itself.
