The air is the first thing you notice — warm, heavy, alive with noise. It presses against your skin with the insistence of a living thing. Your feet do not touch earth, for there is no earth. There is only the weave.

You stand in a chamber that is not quite a chamber. Its walls are woven from great, dried stalks of some river-reed, each as thick as a forearm, braided and knotted with the patient geometry of a wasp's nest. The floor beneath you is the same material, a dense, springy lattice of overlapping stalks, but here the reeds have been trodden into a hard, smooth state by generations of feet. Condensation beads on the woven walls, catching the light and tracing slow, wandering paths down the braided surfaces. The air smells of damp straw, of warm rot, and the sharp, green tang of something like moss. This is a dry place that drinks moisture with a desperate, grateful thirst. The dampness is not a condition; it is an event, a resource, a treasure to be collected from the air and the walls.

The scale of the place is intimate, tight. You are in one room of a larger structure, a single node in a vast, woven city. The ceiling of braided reeds is low enough to touch. There are no windows, only gaps in the weave, deliberate clefts that offer glimpses of the space beyond and allow the staggering noise of this place to flow through.

For the place is loud. It is a percussive, choral, grating symphony. From the walls themselves comes a constant, dry rattle and snap — the sound of the structure settling, of old fibers contracting and expanding in the warmth. This is the baseline rhythm. Over it, layered thickly, is the sound of its inhabitants: a low, humming drone, like a thousand finger-rims circling crystal goblet rims, is the song of certain fused limbs. A rapid, clicking chitter, like a bag full of dice, is the language of others, spoken as they scuttle up the vertical walls. There is a wet, slapping sound from a corner where smaller creatures are daubing a grey, paste-like substance — regurgitated fiber and moisture — into a crack. And every so often, a great, groaning creak echoes from deeper within, as a load-bearing beam shifts its infinitesimal share of the city's weight.

The city *is* the ecology. There are no plants in the way you know them, only their architectural ghosts. Everything is a creature, a process, a presence.

The weavers are the foundation. They are low, wide, multi-limbed things that move with a slow, rolling gait, their bodies covered in a dense mat that is part fur, part dried fungal fiber. With simple, sharp-edged stone tools knapped from flint-like concretions, they pare the dead reeds to the right thickness and split them along their natural grain. Their work is patient, eternal. They are the masons and the mortar, for they also secrete the grey paste that binds junctions and seals cracks, a substance that hardens to a ceramic-like sturdiness. They move constantly, patching a frayed braid here, pressing a new layer of resonant fiber there, listening to the structure's groans with multi-faceted eyes that see in spectrums of vibration.

The clade-swarm moves over and through the weavers' work. These are the smaller, faster ones. Six-limbed, with polished chitin in shades of dull brass and ochre, they are the city's current and its cargo. They carry pellets of the gray paste, bundles of fresh (but dead) reeds, and other, less identifiable things wrapped in leaf-like fibers. Their path is an endless, swirling river of activity across every surface. They do not merely inhabit the walls; they *are* the walls' inner life, their constant friction and movement polishing the reeds, their communal body-heat a part of the chamber's warmth.

The listeners are fewer, and more still. They are anchored to the woven ceiling, hanging inverted like great, dry seed pods. Their bodies are segmented, their skins the color and texture of the woven reeds themselves. They have no mouths you can see, but long, feathery antennae uncoil from their heads, tasting the vibrations of the air and the structure. They *hear* the city. Some say they *are* the city's memory, that the faint, sub-audible hum they produce is a song of the structure's own history, a story told in resonant frequencies. They are the cathedral's own retained tone.

At the edges of the chamber, where the floor-loom meets the wall-weave, are the damp-spots. These are small, concave depressions in the latticework, deliberately shaped by the weavers. Here, condensation is coaxed to gather, forming shallow, brackish pools. The water is not for drinking by you—it is a medium. Thick, gelatinous mats of a cyan-and-green micro-fauna bloom in these damp spots, a living carpet that the clade-swarm harvests in tiny, careful flakes. These mats, in turn, emit a soft, phosphorescent glow in the low light, adding a faint, ghostly illumination to the shadows of the weave. This is the closest thing to vegetation, and it is a farmed, constructed secretory of the biome.

The light comes from above, through the ceiling-weave. It is the normal, weary light of the surface, filtered through a hundred layers of dried stalk, arriving in your space as a warm, diffuse, amber-toned glow. It has no source, only a direction. It makes the condensation glitter and the visitors' chitin gleam with a dull luster.

The entire volume is a fever of purpose. The weavers weave. The clade-swarm swarms. The listeners listen and hum. The moisture weeps and is collected. The structure groans and is repaired. It is a system in perfect, loud, active balance—a warm, dense, deafening, built beehive cathedral, settled into its own perfected, fragile state for a thousand cycles. There is nothing here that is not part of the work. Even you, standing so quietly, are a new vibration in the web, another pattern for the listeners to sort into their endless, resonant song.
