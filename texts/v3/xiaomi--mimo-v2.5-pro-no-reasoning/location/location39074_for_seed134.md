The first thing to know is that the ocean began, and has not ended.

You do not enter it; you are within it. The concept of a shoreline is a myth told by things that have never known the water's embrace. The entire world is a sphere of liquid, a global ocean miles deep, and its top layer is not sky but a ceiling of perpetual, driving rain. A wall of water descends from the grey, formless overcast, its roar the foundational sound of existence. This is the Downpour. The water is not cold, not warm—a constant, pervasive coolness, the temperature of deep shade and ancient stone.

The elevation is high, not above the surface, but within the water column itself. You are in the ocean's upper 'mountains', a zone of immense pressure and constant, sloshing turbulence fed by the rain. The light here is bright, surprisingly so. The Downpour, while obscuring any sense of a sun, is itself luminous, casting a diffuse, grey-white glow that penetrates deep. It turns the water itself into a medium of light, a vast, shifting cathedral of liquid air. Visibility is clear for hundreds of yards before dissolving into the gradient of blue.

And in this light, you see the world is not empty. It is *packed*.

Density is the law. There are no solitary creatures. Life here exists in aggregations, in shoals, in blooms, in colonies. They are not fish in any terrestrial sense. They are clusters of nervous tissue and gelatinous bodies, pulsing with their own soft, bio-electric light. Some are the size of a fist, moving in shoals so thick they seem a single, shimmering organism that parts around you. Others are vast, slow-moving bladders of gas and jelly, trailing curtains of stinging tendrils. The 'abundant' fauna is a layer cake of life: fast, glittering darts near the Downpour's turbulence, drifting, heavier forms in the middle water, and deeper still, the immense, silent shadows that feed on the constant 'rain' of organic matter.

But it is not just life. It is *industry*.

The tech is industrial, but not of metal or wire. It is biological, chemosynthetic, and breathtakingly complex. Growing from spires of naked, obsidian-like basalt are colonies—not of coral, but of something akin to glass sponges and engineered tubeworms. Their structures are intricate lattices and spiraling chimneys, polished and dark, through which they process the chemical-rich water. They *breathe* the ocean, filtering sulfates and metals, and in doing so, they power their own growth. The sound of the world—the roar of the Downpour—is underpinned by a deep, grinding hum, the sound of these geological-biological engines at work. The water itself vibrates with their slow, titanic metabolism.

The terrain is the evidence of this process. There are no gentle slopes, only the sharp, freshly-eroded edges of volcanic seamounts and rifts, young and jagged. But they are not bare. They are encrusted, built upon, and woven into the great bio-mechanical colonies. It is a landscape that is *almost* alive, a forest of industry where the line between organism and environment has blurred into meaninglessness. The 'vegetation' is this fungal-or-simpler, mineral-and-flesh network: the silicon lattices of the sponges, the chitinous armatures of the worm-colonies, the fungal mycelia that lace the rock and glow with a faint, metabolic light.

The strangeness pervades it. These are not ecosystems built on photosynthesis, but on the geology of the planet itself, harnessed by a biology that feels ancient and alien. The animals that swim through the spires of the living factories seem to know them, cleaning them, defending them, a symbiosis so complete it resembles a single, planetary-scale superorganism.

The pace is slow. The dynamic is that of the patient machine. The colonies grow and rebuild at a geological pace, their processes flowing in long, gradual cycles. The shoals of creatures move with the slow, deep currents. The Downpour is eternal and unchanging. To witness true change here would require waiting centuries. It is a world of immense, patient, and noisy life, a dense, cool, liquid cathedral of bio-mechanical sponges and shoals of living light, all suspended in the endless, roaring rain that is the beginning, and the end, of everything.
