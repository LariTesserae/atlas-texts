# The Submersion Works

You are standing in water up to your chest. It is black and so cold it has passed through pain into a kind of numb authority — the cold doesn't ask. Beneath your feet, a floor of poured concrete, buckled and heaved by decades of settling, forces your ankles into awkward angles. Around you, as far as you can feel in the dark, the walls of the Works.

The city — if it was ever a city, and not just a single facility that grew rooms the way a coral grows branches — occupies what used to be a river delta, a place already low, already prone to flooding. The waters came not suddenly but as a patient tide over forty or fifty years, rising through the foundations, filling the sub-basements, then the basements, then the ground floors. No one remembers if anyone tried to stop it. The infrastructure is still here: you can reach out and feel pipes the width of your torso, crusted with mineral deposits so thick they've become their own geology. Valve wheels frozen in half-open positions. Conduit bundles running along walls like exposed veins, their insulation long since sloughed off, leaving bare copper that has oxidized to a green so dark it reads as black in this lightlessness.

There is almost no light. What little exists comes from bioluminescent bacterial films — faint blue-green smears on certain pipes, certain junction boxes, certain places where condensation drips with metronomic regularity from overhead beams into the water's surface just below your chin. These colonies follow the thermal gradients: they favor the places where the Works' residual heat bleeds out. Somewhere deep below you, in sub-levels you will never reach, there are still boilers. They were designed to burn something — coal, perhaps, or gas, or something less easily named — and their furnaces, banked and flooded, exude a warmth so faint it no longer qualifies as heat, only as *slightly less cold*. The bacteria follow this ghost-warmth like pilgrims.

The silence is extraordinary. Water absorbs sound with appetite. Your own breathing comes back to you muffled, reconstituted, as if someone else were doing it. When you move, the water doesn't splash — it yields and closes around you with a soft, gelatinous resistance. The Works themselves make no noise you can hear. But there are processes. You can feel them in the water's subtle current, the way it moves against you in patterns that aren't tidal. Somewhere, something is still cycling. Valves opening and closing on old timers. Pumps, perhaps, running on circuits that have corroded into accidental loops — power finding its way through decay along paths no one designed. The water moves in slow, directional pulses, like the building is breathing.

The scale is intimate and oppressive. This wing of the Works — you are in what might have been a machine hall, or a filtration plant, or a chemical processing floor — is perhaps the size of a cathedral nave, but the water reduces it. Everything below the surface line is *here* but hidden; everything above is ceiling, dark, crusted, hung with stalactites of mineral buildup where water has wept and dried and wept again through so many cycles that the calcium and iron and copper salts have built downward like slow-motion geology. You navigate by touch. Your fingers read the walls the way a blind person reads a face: here a junction box, here a gauge (its glass cracked, its needle frozen at a number that meant something once), here a doorframe, its door long since swelled shut or fallen inward into a room full of denser, more layered darkness.

Vegetation: almost none. The water is too cold, too mineral-heavy, too opaque for most photosynthetic life. But in certain shallow pockets — ledges, the tops of fallen equipment, the rims of basins where the water is only ankle-deep — there are mats of something that might be moss, or might be a cold-water liverwort, or might be something with no terrestrial analog. They are gray-green, barely colored, clinging to surfaces with a grip that suggests they've been there since the flooding itself. They grow so slowly that the word "growing" feels inaccurate. They are simply *present*, in the way that lichens on a headstone are present — more monument than organism.

Fauna is scarce but not absent. Occasionally, your hand brushes something that detaches from a wall and drifts away with a sinuous, unhurried motion. A flatworm, maybe. Or a leech, though you will never feel a bite through the numbness. In the deeper channels — the stairwells, the vertical shafts — there are said to be fish, though "fish" may be a generous term for whatever has adapted to this lightless, near-freezing brine. No one has seen one in a long time. The water sometimes moves in ways that suggest something large enough to displace it, but this could be the pumps, the cycle-breath of the Works.

The weirdness is not dramatic. It doesn't announce itself. It is the weirdness of *persistence* — of a place that was built for one purpose, abandoned for another, submerged for a third, and is now performing a fourth purpose that no one named or intended. The Works are a habitat now, not for creatures but for *processes*. Rust is a process. Mineral deposition is a process. The bacterial films metabolizing sulfur compounds from corroding brass — that is a process. The slow collapse of load-bearing walls under the lateral pressure of water — process. The Works are doing something. They are *making something of themselves*, and the result, decades in, is a place that feels less like a ruin and less like an ecosystem and more like an organ — something that has found its function through damage.

You will not stay long. The cold will take your hands first, then your ability to hold your breath, then your ability to think about holding your breath. The Works do not care. They are already reshaping around your absence, the water closing over your disturbance as if you were never here at all.

In the silence after you leave — in the silence that was here before you arrived and will continue without interruption — the drips continue their percussion. The current pulses. The bacteria glow their faint and useless light over machinery that will outlast them, and the cold holds everything in a state of arrested completion, the Works almost-finished with whatever it is they are becoming.
