# THE LUNG

The first thing that reaches you is the sound. It does not *reach* you — it is already inside your chest when you arrive, as if you were born mid-roar. It is not one sound but a layered architecture of sound: a deep industrial groan that cycles irregularly, a high shriek like metal shearing against metal but never finishing the cut, a wet harmonic that suggests enormous volumes of water being forced through narrow apertures, and beneath all of it a subsonic pulse that is felt more in the teeth than in the ears. Nothing here is silent. Even the smallest droplets of water falling from vegetation to water surface arrive with a sharp report, as though the air itself is compressed, taut, overfull.

The water is everywhere, and it is warm. Not pleasantly warm — *organically* warm, the temperature of something that is alive or was recently alive or is *becoming* alive. It stands at ankle depth in the shallowest places, at waist depth in most, and the deep channels between the structures cannot be seen to their bottoms. The water is dark, not with silt but with its own thickness — almost gelatinous in places, catching the dim light in slow iridescent swirls that suggest petroleum, or something that wishes it were petroleum. Where it laps against the metal walls it does not behave correctly. It moves in directions that do not correspond to any current. In one channel, two streams of water flow directly into each other and neither yields; instead a permanent standing wave forms there, a vertical ridge of water like a wall, vibrating.

The structures rise from this water. They are industrial — clearly industrial, even through the strangeness. Galvanized steel walls, riveted plate, smokestacks of varying diameter, the angled geometry of furnaces and cooling towers, gantries and walkways and what appear to be loading platforms now sitting at the waterline or below it. But they are *young*. The metal is not rusted, not corroded. The rivets are sharp-edged, the cuts clean. Where pipes emerge from the water, their threading is crisp. Whatever this place is, it has not been here long. It has the feeling of something assembled in the dark, in a hurry, to specifications that did not account for the water or did account for it very specifically. Some of the structures are incomplete — raw I-beams jut upward without connection, half a wall meets open air. Others are sealed tight, their doors welded or riveted shut from the outside, and when you press your ear to them the sound that comes from within is *different* from the sound outside, as if they are containing a separate acoustic event.

The vegetation. It grows from every surface. Thick, fleshy, almost tropical in its excess — fronds and broad leaves and trailing vines and things that are more ambiguous, more fungal, more like organs than plants. It grows from the water, yes, but it also grows from the metal. Vines push through rivet holes. Root systems visibly expand inside transparent sections of pipe, white and red and pulsing with a rhythm that might be hydraulic. Where a smokestack bends, vegetation has colonized the interior curve, hanging down in curtains that drip constantly into the water below. The leaves are dark — not green so much as the color of things that photosynthesize using whatever light is available here, which is not much. Some of the leaves have textures that do not belong to botany: ridges that suggest circuitry, surfaces that break light into spectrums that feel *incomplete*, as if certain colors have been removed.

There is almost no animal life. This is one of the things that makes the silence *within* the noise so apparent — there are no birds, no frogs, no insects, nothing that moves of its own volition with its own small agenda. The vegetation is lush, the water is warm, the density of organic matter per cubic meter is enormous, and yet there are no animals. Once, in the distance, you see something that might be a fish — a dark shape moving beneath the surface — but when it surfaces, it is a leaf. A leaf moving against the current, purposefully, for several seconds, before going still and behaving as a leaf again.

The light is dim. The sky, to the extent that there is a sky, is low and the color of bruising — dark purple-gray with occasional seepage of sickly amber from behind cloud formations that move too fast for the wind you can feel on your skin. There are no sun, no moon, no stars. The light source seems to be the structures themselves: some walls glow faintly from within, a pulsing bioluminescence that is probably the vegetation, and certain instruments — gauges, indicator panels, the faces of clocks that display no readable time — emit a pale steady light that illuminates nothing beyond their own surfaces. In the deepest channels, it is effectively dark, and the water there is visible only by its sound.

The industrial machinery is still running. This is perhaps the central fact of the place. Somewhere beneath the water, beneath the structures, something turns. The vibrations travel up through the metal, through the water, through the vegetation, and into the air. Whole sections of the landscape pulse with mechanical rhythm while adjacent sections pulse with a *different* rhythm, and where these zones meet, the interference patterns cause the water to jump, the leaves to shiver, the air to taste of copper. Pipes carry... something. You can hear it moving through them. Sometimes the pipes shudder and go silent, and during those silences — which last only seconds — the absence of that particular frequency makes the rest of the noise feel *worse*, as if the pipe was dampening a register that the rest of the place was producing. When flow resumes, the landscape settles slightly.

And then there is the reality breakdown.

It is not dramatic. It does not announce itself. It is more like the place is slightly *off-register* with itself, the way a color print looks when the plates are misaligned. A wall will extend further than its base suggests it should. A walkway will curve gently to the left and then, without transition, be curving gently to the right, and the geometry between these two states cannot be reconstructed in memory. The water level in one channel will be visibly higher than the water level in an adjacent channel with no barrier between them, and if you watch long enough, you will see the moment when it *isn't* — when the levels equalize — but you will never see the transition. Your own position becomes uncertain. You are *here*, on this gantry, looking down at the water, and then you are on the water looking up at the gantry, and you did not move. The vegetation occasionally grows in patterns that are too regular, too fractal, too *correct* — like the memory of a plant rather than a plant — and then shifts into something chaotic and torn and unmistakably present. The sound changes pitch depending on which direction you face, and in certain orientations, for brief moments, it becomes something almost like language, almost like meaning, before dissolving back into noise.

The place is turbulent. Things are constantly happening. New structures surface from the water — not rising slowly but *already present*, as if they were always there and you simply hadn't looked. Vegetation grows visibly: a vine extends a foot in the time you watch it, coiling around a pipe, finding its grip. Pipes burst and seal themselves. A section of walkway buckles, tilts, drops into the water, and the water closes over it without splash. Nearby, another walkway that was submerged yesterday is now above the waterline, intact, as if it were never submerged. The turbulence is not violent — it is *busy*. The place has the energy of something that is building itself, that is in the process of becoming, that has not yet decided what it is. The structures, the vegetation, the water, the light, the sound — all of it is in negotiation with itself, constantly, and the negotiation is loud and warm and dim and full and relentless and without any apparent end.

There are no animals here, but there are presences. Not ghosts. Not spirits. Something closer to *occupations* — the sense that a particular corner of the machinery, a particular junction of pipe and vine and water, belongs to something that is not a body but is not nothing. You do not see these presences. You feel them the way you feel someone standing behind you in a queue: a spatial fact, a density in the air, a place where the sound is shaped slightly differently than it should be. They move. They rearrange themselves. They are part of the turbulence.

You are standing in ankle-deep water on a steel platform that vibrates with low frequency. A vine has, in the last several minutes, begun to grow over your boot. The sound is so constant and so total that it has become a kind of silence — the baseline against which all other silences would be measured. The light flickers from somewhere behind the cloud-cover. A pipe overhead groans and something warm drips onto your shoulder. In the water at your feet, something that is not a fish moves, and the ripples it makes travel in a direction that has no name.
