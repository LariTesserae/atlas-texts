The heat is a physical weight, a wet blanket pressed to the skin that never dries. It breathes. The air is a solvent, thick with the humidity of a world that cannot stop sweating. There is no sun. The sky is a bruised, uniform grey, a ceiling of wet slate that glows with a dull, directionless, internal light—a light that illuminates nothing, only makes the darkness a deeper shade of charcoal. It is a world of grey and black, with the occasional, shocking highlight of rust-red.

The ground is not land in any conventional sense. It is a vast, buckled skin of dark basaltic rock, cracked into a trillion polygons by cycles of baking heat and saturation. Between every slab, water wells up—not as a stream or a spring, but as a slow, constant ooze, a black mirror that reflects the non-sky. The rocks weep, their surfaces slick with a constant, oily condensation. The scale is that of a forgotten delta or a sunken city's edge: wide, flat, and stretching into a murky, featureless haze. There is no horizon, only a gradual blending of wet ground into wet air.

And yet, it is a city.

The built environment is the landscape. What look like mesas or hoodoos in the distance are, as you draw nearer, the eroded stumps of enormous towers. They are not made of stone, but of a material that has petrified or been replaced by it: rebar like fossilized ribs juts from a matrix of fused, melted concrete and glass. The structures have slumped, melted, and settled over millennia. They lean against each other, their upper stories long since collapsed into the canyons between them, creating a low, tangled, multi-layered terrain. Avenues have become canals of black water. Plazas have become shallow, steaming lagoons. Infrastructure is everywhere, but it is infrastructure for nothing recognizable: thick conduits of corroded ceramic run between buildings, often shattered, spilling their guts of powdite dust or a coagulated, tar-like substance into the water. Bridges arc from one ruin to another, their spans cracked, their surfaces a garden of faint, phosphorescent lichen—the only hint of something living.

This is a city without builders, without inhabitants. Its people are gone. What remains are their hollow, worn-out shells. The urban geometry is all there, but it is a skeleton, and the rules of the world have rewritten its purpose. The dense packing of the structures creates a maze of canyons, alleys, and tunnels, but there is an eerie openness to it. The buildings are stubs. You can always see the low, grey sky. The density is one of absence, not presence. It is a city square that is all corners and no center.

The silence is profound. The only sound is the slow *drip... drip... drip* from a billion different weeping surfaces, the occasional deep, resonant *groan* as a massive slab of wall finally shifts and settles into a new position, and the hiss of steam rising from cracks where hotter water meets cooler air. There is no life. No scuttling, no chirping, no buzzing. The stillness is so complete it feels like a presence itself.

But it is not still.

The active dynamic is geological, and wrong. The ground breathes. Pockets of gas, trapped for eons, burp up from below, causing the water to roil in sudden, localized boils that last a minute and then are gone. More unsettling, the geometry itself shifts. A structure will lean, almost imperceptibly, over decades. Then, in a single, silent, wet slide, a whole façade will slump into the street-canal, raising the water level a fraction. The ruin is not stable; it is in a state of slow-motion, continuous collapse, a settling that has gone on for so long it is a form of homeostasis. The heat and the water are the agents of its patient, eternal dismantling. The erosion is complete; this is not a city decaying, but a city that has achieved a new, final state of decay.

The weirdness is in the details. In one canal, the water flows *uphill* for a stretch, defying all logic, drawn by some unseen, localized gravity well. Shadows pool in places that should be lit, and the dim light seems to emanate from patches of the rock face itself. Sometimes, for a half-second, the reflection in a black puddle will show a window with a light on inside one of the dead towers, a perfect image that vanishes if you look directly. The walls of some buildings are covered in a faint, geometric patina, not quite graffiti, not quite mold, a pattern that suggests circuitry or language but resolves into nothing when studied.

It is a landscape of total contradiction: a city with no citizens, a desert of water, a place that is both actively changing and utterly frozen in its change. It is the corpse of a world, still warm, still sweating, dreaming in the dark.
