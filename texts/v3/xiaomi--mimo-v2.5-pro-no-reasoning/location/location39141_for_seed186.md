# The Canopy of Fog

## Above the Inversion

You are high. Absurdly, impossibly high — not in the way of mountaineers who count their altitude in achievement, but in the way of a place that has always been this high and has settled into it the way a stone settles into a riverbed. The rock underfoot is old basalt, dark and fine-grained, weathered into gentle undulations over a span of time that makes geologists quiet. It does not crumble. It does not jag. It has been here so long that it has learned its shape.

The air is cold — not biting, not fierce, but *present.* A steady, bone-deep cold around minus ten that doesn't vary much because the sun, when it exists at all, exists as a rumor. You are above the cloud inversion. Below you, theoretically, there is a world with daylight and horizon and weather that behaves according to rules. Up here, there is a different arrangement.

Darkness is the default. Not the darkness of caves — there is no absolute black — but a thick, breathing grey-dark that shifts in opacity. The clouds are not below you. They are *around* you. The mountain did not rise above the clouds; it rose *into* them, and the clouds stayed, and the two have been negotiating ever since. There are hours — you could not call them days — when the fog thins to a pearlescent haze and the light becomes the color of the inside of an oyster shell. Then it thickens again. This is the weather cycle, and it is the only clock.

## The Growth

Here is the strangeness: it is lush.

Not lush in the way of equatorial forests, all emerald competition and dripping broad leaves. This is a different vocabulary of abundance. The vegetation is almost entirely vertical — it grows *on* things, not out of the ground. The basalt is carpeted in a layered architecture of mosses, lichens, and things that are neither but have occupied the border between them. They are thick. They are inches deep in places, compressing underfoot into a cold, wet sponge that releases the faintest squelch — the loudest sound you'll hear for hours.

The moss is not green. It is the color of tarnished copper, of dried blood in certain lights, of old bronze. Some of it is nearly black. Some of it, in the thickest patches where condensation gathers, has a faint bioluminescence — not enough to read by, but enough that when you look down, your feet seem to be standing on the dim suggestion of stars.

Above the moss: the canopy. This is where the real vegetation lives, and it is the reason this place is strange.

The trees — you call them trees because they are vertical and they branch, but the word is a courtesy — are enormous fungal structures. Columnar fruiting bodies, some of them fifty or seventy feet tall, rising from the rock like the pillars of a cathedral that was never built. They are pale. Not white: the color of bone left in weather, of old paper, of the underside of a mushroom held up to no light. Their surfaces are smooth and slightly damp, beaded with condensation that gathers and runs in rivulets down their flanks. They branch near their summits into canopies of thin, shelf-like structures — gills, technically, though you would call them leaves — that overlap in dense layers, filtering the fog, catching moisture, dripping.

The drip is the second loudest sound.

Between these columns, draped from shelf to shelf, hung in curtains and veils: mycelial nets. Visible fungal networks, thick as rope in places, thin as silk in others, forming a three-dimensional mesh through the upper air. Some of them glow. A faint, chemical green, like phosphorescence in a ship's wake, emanating from nodules along their lengths. This is not decorative. This is metabolic. The nets are feeding — capturing moisture and particulates from the fog, digesting airborne chemistry, distributing nutrients down to the columnar trunks and through them to the rock below. The whole forest is an engine. It processes the clouds.

And it is *active.* The growth rate is not something you could watch, but it is not something you'd need patience for, either. Shelf-fungi extend their edges by visible fractions over days. New columns emerge from the rock as pale, knuckled fists, pushing upward through the moss layer. Old ones, where they eventually fail, do not fall — they soften, deliquesce, melt back into the substrate in a slow, cellular collapse that takes months, leaving hollow cylinders of pale husk that the wind, when it moves, plays like pipes. Very quietly.

## The Animals

The fauna is abundant and almost entirely nocturnal in the evolutionary sense — adapted to a world where "night" is the permanent condition and "day" is the anomaly.

The most visible are the gliders. Pale, membranous creatures, roughly the size of large cats, that navigate between the fungal columns using wing-flaps of translucent tissue stretched between elongated limbs. They are not bats. They are not birds. They have too many limbs — six, arranged in a radial symmetry that suggests a body plan assembled from a different design document entirely. They eat the shelf-fungi, hanging upside-down from the gill-structures and rasping at them with flat, grinding mouthparts. They are mostly silent. Their wing-membranes make a faint, papery sound when they move, and occasionally one will produce a low, tonal hum — communication, perhaps, or territorial, or something with no mammalian analog.

Below them: the grazers. These are harder to see. They move through the moss layer like things moving through snow — you see the surface parting, bulging, resettling. Occasionally a head emerges: flat, eyeless, equipped with feathery sensory appendages that taste the air and the substrate simultaneously. They are arthropodic, segmented, each segment armored in chitin that has taken on the same tarnished-bronze hue as the moss they live in. They are the color of their world. They eat the lower mosses, the lichen, the decomposing base of old fungal columns. They are prey.

The predators move in the spaces between the columns — fast, low, and quiet. Lean, elongated things with too-flexible spines, they hunt by vibration, pressing their undersides against the rock or the moss and feeling for the footfalls and body-pressures of grazers through the substrate. When they strike, they strike from below the moss, erupting upward in a sudden, pale lunge. The kill is fast. The aftermath is quiet. The moss closes over it.

None of them vocalize. The silence here is not an absence of life — it is a *language* the life has chosen. Sound carries strangely in the fog, bouncing between columns and shelves, distorting, arriving at the wrong time. The animals stopped using it. The ones that didn't are gone.

## The Rust

And then there is the industrial.

It is old. It is so old that it has become geological in some places, biological in others. But it is unmistakable: this mountain was *worked.*

Pipes emerge from the basalt at odd angles — not fallen, not abandoned, but *incorporated.* They are iron, or they were iron; now they are a mineral hybrid, iron oxide fused with calcium deposits from millennia of condensation, forming a rustite that is harder than either original material. They are three, four, five feet in diameter. Some of them run horizontally along the slopes for hundreds of meters before disappearing back into the rock. Some of them are vertical, open at the top, and the fungal columns have grown up through them and around them, pale trunks threading through iron throats. Moss colonizes them preferentially — the condensation patterns on metal are different from rock, and the moisture-loving lichens have mapped every pipe like a circulatory system.

In a few places, the mountain's flank is cut by enormous flat planes — excavation faces, once, cut with a precision that even erosion has only softened, not erased. The cuts are at strange angles to the natural bedding of the basalt, suggesting the miners were following something — a vein, a seam, a resource now gone or now unnameable. These flat faces are now the richest habitats: the smooth stone holds moisture differently, and the moss on them grows in distinct bands and whorls that map the old tool marks like a record.

There are gears. Or there are things that were gears — massive, toothed wheels of the same rustite, half-buried in the mountainside, too large to have been moved by any force you can picture. The fungal shelves have colonized their teeth. The gliders roost on their rims. One of them, near what might have been a processing site, is still slowly turning — driven by some subsurface mechanism, some geothermal differential, some pressure that the miners tapped and that has not yet exhausted itself. It turns at the speed of a minute hand. Moss grows on its leading edge, and the trailing edge is bare, and this cycle of growth-and-scrape is the closest thing to a clock this place has.

The pipes hum, sometimes. A deep, almost infrasonic vibration that you feel in your sternum rather than hear. The gliders orient on it. The grazers avoid it. It is the mountain breathing through its old iron lungs.

## The Weather

The dynamic that matters most is the fog. It is not still. It moves in cycles — thickening, thinning, cycling between a near-opaque white-out where you cannot see the nearest column and a translucent haze where the fungal canopy extends into dim, phosphorescent infinity. These cycles take hours. They are driven by temperature differentials between the rock and the air, by the metabolic heat of the fungal forest itself, by pressures from below that push warm air up through the old pipe systems and create micro-convection cells that stir the clouds into slow, visible gyres.

When the fog is thickest, the condensation intensifies. Water beads on every surface — the rustite pipes weep, the fungal columns run with rivulets, the moss becomes sodden, and the grazers surface more often to drink from the pooling water in the old excavation basins. This is the closest thing to rain this place has: not falling from above, but condensing from the all-surrounding, collecting, finding its way downward through old infrastructure and new biology alike into channels that disappear into the mountain.

When it thins, the cold sharpens. The gliders become more active, launching from roost to roost in long, silent arcs. The predators press closer to the surface. The bioluminescence of the mycelial nets becomes more visible, more vivid — or seems to, against the darker background of thinner fog. This is as bright as it gets.

There is no wind to speak of. The fog dampens it. What air movement exists is thermal, convective, local — a breath from one direction, then another, carrying the faintest scent of iron and decomposition and the cold, clean smell of fungal growth that is not unpleasant but is entirely unlike anything growing in sunlight.

## The Silence

It is quiet. It is so quiet that you become aware of sounds you didn't know your body made — the creak of your joints, the wet click of your eyes blinking, the absurd volume of your own breathing. The moss absorbs everything. The fog refracts everything. The fungal columns break up any sound wave that travels more than a few dozen feet, scattering it, deadening it, folding it into the general hush.

The only persistent sounds are the drip of condensation, the almost-subsonic hum of the pipes, and the faint papery whisper of glider-wings in the middle distance. These sounds are so constant, so low, that they become a texture of silence rather than interruptions of it. They are the silence. This is what silence sounds like when it is alive.

You stand on old basalt, in old fog, on an old mountain that has been gutted and regrown and gutted again by processes that have been running since before you had a word for running. The fungal canopy glows faintly above you. Somewhere to your left, a pipe hums. Somewhere below the moss, a predator is very still, and a grazer is very unaware, and the ancient gear turns one tooth further, and the fog breathes in, and the fog breathes out, and none of it needs you, and all of it continues.
