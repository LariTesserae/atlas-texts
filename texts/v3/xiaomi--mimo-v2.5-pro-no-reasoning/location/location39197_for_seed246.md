# The Pitted Heights

You are standing in what was once, unimaginable ages ago, a city.

The ground beneath your feet is stone — not quarried stone, not laid stone, but stone that has been *worn into the suggestion of architecture*. Wind and ice and the slow patience of water have carved what look like doorways, corridors, steps, and walls from a pale grey-green rock that might be granite or might be something older, something without a name in any living language. The resemblance to construction is unsettling. But there are no chisel marks. No mortar. The surfaces are glassy where they've been scoured, chalky where they've been spared, and everywhere they are *ancient* — the kind of ancient that makes you understand, viscerally, that time is not a line but a weight.

---

**The elevation** is high — not treacherously so, not mountaintop — but enough that the air has thinned into something that moves constantly, never still. You are on a broad table of eroded plateau, a remnant surface that has outlived the ranges that once towered above it. Everything above has been carried away. What remains is a floor of stone, rising in low mesas and collapsed terraces, extending perhaps a kilometer in any direction before the land falls off into cloud. You cannot see what lies below. There is only the plateau, the sky, and the sound.

---

**The sound.** This is what hits you first and what never stops hitting you.

Wind here is not ambient. It is *structural*. The stone is carved into channels, slots, bowls, and half-collapsed tunnels, and as pressure systems cycle across the high air, the landscape *plays itself*. There is a deep harmonic drone — almost subsonic — that comes from a wide fissure in the eastern face of the plateau, where air is forced through a long throat of stone and emerges as something you feel in your sternum. Over this, higher-pitched tones shift and swell: a narrow slot between two leaning slabs keens at a frequency that changes with wind speed, sliding between notes like a bowed string. Somewhere behind you, a hollow in the rock — too regular to be natural, too weathered to be anything else — catches the wind in such a way that it produces a rhythmic thudding, a heartbeat at roughly 40 beats per minute, persistent, maddening, impossible to ignore.

The soundscape is loud. It is also *musical*, in the way that weather is musical — not composed, but structured by the geometry of the world. On days when the wind shifts to the south, the drones drop and the keening rises, and the whole plateau sounds like a different instrument. On still days — rare — the silence is so profound it feels like a physical substance pressing against your eardrums.

---

**The vegetation** clings to the margins.

Not sparse, exactly — *lush* — but concentrated in pockets and seams where moisture gathers. The stone is riddled with shallow basins, cupped hollows, and narrow grooves where rain and condensation collect, and in these you find dense, almost aggressive growth. Mosses, first — thick, spongy, in shades of copper and deep green and a grey-blue that seems to generate its own light. They fill every crack, pad every ledge, soften every surface. They are the connective tissue of this place. Above the mosses: low, woody shrubs with dark waxy leaves, no taller than your knee, growing in tight mounds that catch the wind like cupped hands. Between the shrubs, lichens of enormous size — some the diameter of dinner plates — crust the rock in fractal rosettes of orange, black, and bone-white.

In the sheltered grooves — the places where wind eddies and drops its moisture — there are ferns. Not tree ferns, nothing that scale, but dense, tangled thickets of species with fronds both delicate and dark, nearly black at the tips, as if the cold has singed them. These fern groves are the most alive-feeling places on the plateau. They rustle with their own microclimate. The air inside them is humid, still, and smells of iron.

No trees. Nothing woody grows above waist height. The wind forbids it.

---

**The water** is present but elusive. There are no rivers, no streams visible from any vantage point. Water exists here as a process, not a feature. Rain comes in brief, fierce squalls that sweep across the plateau two or three times a day, darkening the stone for an hour before the wind dries it again. Between rains, condensation forms on the cold rock surfaces — sometimes visibly, a sheen of droplets that gathers in channels and trickles downward into the basins and grooves where the plants wait. At night, if the temperature drops enough — and it often does — the moisture freezes into a thin crust of rime that melts by midmorning, feeding the cycle again.

There are pools. Shallow, perfectly still, cupped in hollows of stone that have been scooped by millennia of freeze-thaw. The largest is perhaps three meters across and never more than ankle-deep. Its water is clear, colorless, and cold enough to ache. The bottom is a mosaic of stone and moss, and if you look long enough, you may see small dark shapes moving — not fish, but something like salamanders, or something like nothing you've seen before. They are the largest fauna you'll encounter, and you may not encounter them at all.

---

**Fauna** is rare and small. The salamanders — if that's what they are — in the pools. Insects in the fern groves: black-winged things that move in clouds when disturbed and settle again in moments. Small birds, perhaps — you hear occasional sharp calls that carry far in the wind, but you never see the source. The plateau feels empty of life not because there is none, but because what exists has learned to be invisible. Every living thing here is dark-colored, low to the ground, and silent except when it isn't.

---

**The built — or the illusion of it.**

This is the strangeness. The stone formations here are *almost* architectural. There is a row of vertical slabs, each one two to three meters tall, spaced roughly evenly along the northern edge of the plateau. They are natural — columnar jointing, perhaps, or differential erosion of a once-uniform sheet — but they look like a colonnade. They look *placed*. Between them, the wind sings in intervals that feel deliberate.

In the center of the plateau, a shallow depression is ringed by concentric shelves of stone, each one a half-step lower than the last, descending into a bowl perhaps ten meters across. Rain collects here. The mosses are thickest here, and the ferns crowd the upper rings. It looks like a theater. It looks like a cistern. It looks like a place where someone once gathered.

There are other features: a narrow passage between two walls of stone that opens into a small chamber, roofed by a single slab that has not yet fallen; a terrace of flat stones that steps down the western slope in a way that suggests farming, suggests purpose, suggests hands. But there are no hands. There never were. This is pareidolia writ in geology — the cold math of erosion producing shapes that the human mind insists on reading as *made*.

Or perhaps not. Perhaps the weirdness here — and it is weird, you feel it, a persistent low-frequency wrongness like the drone from the fissure — is that you cannot tell. You will never be able to tell. The erosion is too ancient, too thorough, too *final*. Whatever was here, if anything, has been worn down to the same texture as the stone itself. The question of whether this was ever a city — or a temple, or a home — is a question that has eroded along with everything else.

---

**Temperature and light.** The cold is persistent but not brutal. It lives in the stone — the rock itself radiates chill even in direct sunlight, and the wind carries a damp bite that finds every gap in your clothing. Frost forms on the north-facing surfaces overnight. The ferns curl inward. The mosses stiffen. By midday, if the sun is clear, there is a brief window where the air feels almost mild, and the stone warms enough to sit on comfortably, and the light turns the mosses to copper and the lichens to gold. This is the hour when the plateau is most beautiful. It does not last.

Light is ordinary — daylight, overcast or clear, changing with weather. There are no auroras, no bioluminescence, no tricks. The strangeness here is not optical. It is structural. It is the feeling that the landscape is *listening to itself*.

---

**The rules of this place**, as best as you can sense them:

Nothing grows tall. Nothing stays warm. Nothing moves fast except the wind and the rain. Sound carries enormous distances and is generated by the landscape itself, so that silence and noise are both properties of *stone*, not of living things. The built and the natural are indistinguishable at this timescale. The plateau is eroding — slowly, imperceptibly within a human lifetime, but the mesas are shorter than they were a thousand years ago and will be shorter still in a thousand more. You are standing in a place that is in the process of becoming flat, becoming nothing, becoming wind. The loudness you hear is the sound of that becoming.

And yet the ferns grow. And the mosses spread. And the pools fill, and the salamanders move in the dark water, and the birds call from somewhere you'll never see. Life here has not surrendered to the erosion. It has *negotiated* with it. It grows in the cracks, in the shelter, in the seams. It is small and it is relentless and it will be here long after the last column has toppled and the last wall-shape has been ground to sand.

The plateau is loud. The plateau is empty. The plateau is alive. These are not contradictions. This is simply what a place sounds like when it is very, very old and still not finished.
