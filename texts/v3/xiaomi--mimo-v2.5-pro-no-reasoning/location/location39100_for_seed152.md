In the basin where the River Kaela used to be, the air is thick with the smell of wet clay and budding green, a humid perfume that coats the back of the throat. This is a place caught in a breath of geology—a low-lying swale of land that hasn't quite decided what it is yet. The bedrock is soft, unconsolidated, and malleable, presenting itself not as stone but as raw, grey-blue marl and slick, dark earth that is perpetually in a state of becoming.

**The Terrain and The Water**
The elevation is low, a wide depression rimmed by gentle, tree-crowned rises. There are no ravines, no deep cuts—erosion has had no time to carve a history here. The ground is wet, but not flooded. The water does not flow in rivers or streams; instead, it seeps. It is a landscape of "intermittent moisture," where the earth breathes water out during the warm afternoons and sucks it back in as the dim twilight settles.

Across the sparse, pebbly soil, shallow pools form in the depressions—puddles that never fully dry, turning the ground into a mosaic of slick, dark mud and patches of resilient greenery. The water is shallow and tepid, teeming with the first microscopic stirrings of aquatic life. It is the sound of these shallow waters that provides the only real auditory texture to the place: a soft, sporadic *plip* of condensation dripping from heavy leaves, or the hush of water moving through loose gravel.

**The Ecology**
Vegetation here is lush but scattered, clustered in islands of dense life separated by wide stretches of barren, wet earth. There are no forests, only "pockets" of green. Enormous, broad-leafed ferns and rubbery-leafed shrubs explode from the mud, their leaves dark and waxy to retain the humidity. They grow with a wild, unpruned vigor that suggests rapid biological processing. Because the soil is young and shifting, trees do not grow tall; they grow wide and gnarled, their root systems gripping the topsoil like fingers sinking into putty.

Animal life is abundant and surprisingly audible in its silence. This is the domain of the quiet hunter. The humidity and the dim, overcast light favor reptiles and amphibians. Thick, speckled lizards cling motionless to the warm, damp clay banks, waiting for insects that hover over the stagnant pools. It is a prehistoric tableau—a raw ecosystem functioning on a biological pace, where the hunt is slow, the digestion is slow, and the energy of the sun is absorbed rather than expended.

**The Settlement**
And yet, this rawness is juxtaposed with the faint, fading geometry of human habitation. Perhaps a century ago, or perhaps just a generation, a town tried to take root here. The "urban" element is a ghost on the margins. In the dryest parts of the swale, half-sunken into the soft earth, lie the foundations of structures.

They are not buildings anymore, but outlines. Rusted, skeletal rebar pierces the air, wrapped in flowering vines. Concrete slabs, cracked and buckled by the shifting, embryonic soil, lie at odd angles like tombstones. The town never grew because the earth was too new, too wet, too restless. It rejected the construction. Now, the "built" environment is merely another substrate for the moss and the mud. The "streets" are barely distinguishable from the dry creek beds, and the "walls" are just surfaces for condensation to bead upon.

**Atmosphere and Light**
The light is weak, filtered through a permanent layer of high, thin cloud and the steam rising from the ground. It creates a soft, shadowless illumination, making the greens of the vegetation appear hyper-saturated against the dull grey of the mud and the crumbling concrete.

It is a place of profound stillness, yet it is never truly static. The ground shifts; the water table rises and falls; the plants creep inward over the ruins. It is a landscape that feels like a secret room inside the earth—a humid, warm, green incubator where the future is being kneaded out of the mud.
