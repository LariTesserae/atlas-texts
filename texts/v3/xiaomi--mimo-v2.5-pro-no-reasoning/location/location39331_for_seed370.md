# The Throat of Ilūm

---

The mountain has been hollowed from the inside out.

Not by any process you would call natural — though the stone itself is natural enough, a dense basalt andesite streaked with pale veins of calcite that catch almost no light. The hollowing was done by hand. By *hands*. Over what must have been centuries or millennia, someone — many someones — chipped and ground and polished the interior of this peak's summit into a chamber roughly the dimensions of a cathedral nave. The ceiling vaults perhaps thirty meters overhead. The floor stretches perhaps forty meters across. The walls curve inward like the inside of a skull.

And every square meter of surface has been *worked*.

The floor is not flat. It rises in dense terraces of carved stone — step after step after step, each one different, each one shaped into a slightly different geometric pattern: concentric circles, radiating chevrons, interlocking spirals. The steps are packed so tightly together that they form a continuous undulating surface, almost organic, like the cross-section of a wasp nest rendered in dark stone. There is no path between them. There is no room. They press against each other shoulder to shoulder, filling the floor to knee-height, then waist-height, then higher as you move toward the walls, where the carved terraces climb and merge with wall-reliefs that have been cut centimeter by centimeter into the basalt over uncountable generations.

The reliefs are not figurative. There are no faces, no animals, no recognizable symbols. Instead: grooves. Channels. Ribs. The walls are covered in parallel furrows carved at varying depths and angles — some as thin as a finger-width, some as broad as an arm-span — that run in sweeping curves from floor to ceiling. Where they intersect with the floor-terraces, the patterns blur into one another, become unreadable, become a single continuous surface of shaped stone. There is no natural substrate visible anywhere. Every face of every stone has been touched, altered, made deliberate. Even the ceiling — far overhead, where someone must have hung from ropes or scaffolding that no longer exists — is covered in shallow, repeating indentations, each one roughly the size and shape of a cupped palm. Thousands upon thousands of them. Millions, perhaps.

The air is warm. Not hot — just warm, the way a stone wall is warm in the late afternoon, except there is no sun here and no afternoon. The warmth comes from the mountain itself, a slow geothermal bleed that seeps through the basalt and makes the carved surfaces slightly above body-temperature. If you pressed your palm against any wall, you would feel it: a faint, steady heat, as though the mountain were alive and dreaming. It is not alive. Nothing here is alive.

The light is faint. Near the top of the dome, perhaps six apertures have been carved through the outer wall of the peak — each one no wider than a forearm, each one angled so that no direct sky is visible, only the faintest gray suggestion of the outside world filtered through a narrow throat of stone. The light they admit is thin, directionless, almost subtractive — it does not illuminate so much as reduce the darkness from absolute to merely profound. In this dimness, the carved surfaces are visible only as variations in shadow. The grooves on the walls become depthless. The terraced floor becomes a sea of dark geometry, barely legible, felt more than seen.

There is no water. There has not been water here in a very long time — possibly ever. The stone is bone-dry, and the air carries no moisture. The calcite veins in the basalt are fossils of a geological past when the mountain was something else, but that past is finished. The chamber smells of warm stone and nothing else. Dust does not accumulate; the air moves too violently for dust to settle.

The sound.

The sound is the reason this place exists, or perhaps the reason it was made the way it was.

Wind moves across the peak constantly — a high-altitude, relentless wind that has blown in the same direction for as long as anyone could have observed. The six apertures at the dome's summit are not merely windows. They are *voiced*. Each one has been carved with a specific interior geometry — a flared mouth, a narrow waist, a ridged lip — that transforms the wind's passage into sound. Not melody. Not rhythm. A chord. A single, enormous, deafening chord that fills the chamber from wall to wall and floor to ceiling at a volume that makes thought difficult, that vibrates the basalt beneath your feet, that you feel in your teeth and your sternum and the thin bones behind your ears.

The chord does not change. It has not changed. The wind does not vary in direction or strength, and the apertures do not vary in shape, and so the sound — a deep, harmonic roar, rich with overtones, somewhere between an organ pipe and the inside of a seashell magnified ten thousand times — has been exactly the same for centuries. Perhaps for millennia. It is not music. It is not signal. It is a property of the place, as fixed and involuntary as gravity.

The carved grooves on the walls are not decorative. They are acoustic. They catch and redirect the sound, breaking it into subtle layers, preventing it from collapsing into a single unbearable tone. Without them, the chamber would be uninhabitable even briefly — the resonance would rupture something essential. With them, the chamber is merely deafening. The terraced floor functions similarly: each step-face catches a slightly different harmonic band, so that the sound is distributed across the full range of human hearing rather than concentrated at a single destructive frequency. This is not sophisticated engineering. This is accumulated knowledge, a folk technology of acoustics developed over generations of trial and error, passed down through observation and imitation, refined by hands that could not write or draw but could listen and adjust and carve.

No one tends this place now. There are no people, no animals, no insects, no plants, no fungi, no visible microorganisms. The stone is sterile. The air is sterile. The chamber is a vacuum of life in which only mineral and sound persist. And yet the construction is so thorough, so total, so all-encompassing — every surface shaped, every gap intentional, every carved groove positioned relative to every other carved groove — that the space feels *occupied*. Not by presence but by effort. By the accumulated weight of ten thousand hands working ten thousand days, chipping stone in darkness and noise, passing the tools to the next pair of hands, and the next, and the next.

Nothing erodes. The basalt is too dense, the air too dry, the temperature too stable. The carvings are as sharp as the day they were cut — or nearly so. The erosion here is not of edges but of intent: the patterns have been carved so long, by so many hands, that their original purpose (if there was one) has been lost. They are simply *what the wall looks like*. They are the settled, final state of the stone, as natural now as the calcite veins they interrupt. The chamber has achieved equilibrium. It will not change. It was built to not change.

In the dimness, with the sound pressing against you from every direction, with the warm dry stone surrounding you on all sides, with no living thing and no water and no sky — only those six narrow throats of gray light far overhead — you understand that this is not a ruin. Ruins decay. This is something else. This is a place that has finished becoming what it was always going to become, and now it simply *is*. A chamber of shaped stone and perpetual sound, packed full of itself, ancient beyond reckoning, warm and dark and loud and still.
