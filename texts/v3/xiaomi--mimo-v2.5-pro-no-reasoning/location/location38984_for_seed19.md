# The Plateau of Fixed Abundance

## Elevation and Form

You are on a summit plain so old it has forgotten it was ever a mountain. The rock here — and there is only rock — has been worn down over spans of time that make the word *ancient* feel like a borrowed garment two sizes too small. What once must have been jagged peaks reaching into thinner air has been planed and sanded by incomprehensible patience into a broad, undulating table: a few meters of elevation change over kilometers of reach, ridges no taller than a person running their thumb along the edge of a worn coin. The surface is metamorphic, dense-grained, dark umber and slate, shot through with veins of quartz and something else — a mineral with no agreed-upon name, bluish-grey, faintly lustrous, which forms in branching filaments like the vasculature of a leaf. You see it everywhere when you crouch down. The ground is dry. There is no frost, no dew, no condensation. The air is cool and thin and carries almost no moisture, yet nothing here thirsts, because nothing here is doing anything that could be called *requiring*. It is a high place — far higher than any mountain should be able to sustain a plain this broad — and the light falls on it the way light falls on everything: without preference, without drama. Daylight, ordinary and pale. Clouds, if they exist, are far below. From the edges of this table you could look down into nothing — the drop-offs are sheer and absolute — but you would have to walk a very long way to reach an edge, and the landscape is so uniform that distance loses its texture.

## The Presences

They are everywhere.

You must understand this carefully: *everywhere*. Not in the way that insects are everywhere in a meadow, or grass in a prairie. Those metaphors imply a surface beneath, a ground that is not-insect, not-grass. Here the ground and the presences are — not the same, but *inseparable*. They cover the rock in layers. They fill the crevices. They rise from the flattened ridges in slow accumulations, half-embedded, half-emergent. Some are the size of a fist. Some are the size of a person curled up. Some are much larger and you cannot tell where they end and the plain continues because their edges blur into the stone at a rate so slow it might take a century of staring to notice the transition.

They are fauna. They have the *architecture* of fauna — bilateral symmetry in most cases, segmented or articulated bodies, structures that once were or still are or ambiguously *might be* limbs, sensory apparatus, mouths. Some have exoskeletons. Some have something closer to skin, though it has the texture and dull sheen ofite seashell or horn orite polished bone. They are colored in muted tones: ochre, grey-blue, the deep umber of the rock itself, occasional streaks of rust or black. Many are so closely matched to the substrate that you almost step on them before you see them, and then you see them everywhere, and you realize you have been walking on them, among them, through fields of them, since you arrived.

They do not move. They have not moved. There is no evidence — no worn trails, no disturbed dust, no tracks — that they have moved in any timeframe the world considers meaningful. They are not dead, or rather: death is not the right category. They are not fossils. Their tissues, if you touched them, would yield slightly — cool, firm, like dense rubber or the flesh of a mushroom in winter. They are not preserved. They are *present*. Present in the way a stone is present. Present in the way a word carved into a wall is present. They are here, in this configuration, and they have always been here in this configuration, and they will always be here in this configuration, and the word *always* is not a figure of speech.

## The Silence

You notice it the way you notice the absence of a sound you didn't know you were listening for. There is no wind. The air is still — not stale, not oppressive, but *absent as a medium*. It carries nothing. The coolness touches your skin but does not ruffle it. You breathe and hear your own breathing, and it is the loudest thing in the world, and it is not loud.

None of them make a sound. Not the skittering of chitin. Not the low hum of breathing. Not the wet clicking of a throat. Some have mouths — open mouths, in some cases, frozen mid-gape — and the mouths are silent, and the silence is not dramatic. It is not the silence of a predator waiting. It is the silence of a room where no one has ever spoken. The silence is structural. It is load-bearing. If sound were introduced here — if you shouted — it would feel like a violation of something older than politeness, older than taboo. You don't shout. You barely breathe loudly. The quiet settles into you like cold settling into stone.

## The Geometry

Here is where the place stops making sense, and does so so gently that you almost don't notice.

The presences overlap. Not in the way that a crowd overlaps, shoulder to shoulder — in the way that two drawings on transparent paper overlap. Two of the creatures, each roughly the size of a large dog, occupy the same three-dimensional space. They are *co-located*. You can see through one to the other if you look at the right angle, and the angle is wrong — not because of translucency, but because the space they occupy is somehow *more* than the space it appears to be. This happens not as an exception but as a pattern. Packed together as they are, layer upon layer, they cannot fit in the space they visibly fill. The arithmetic doesn't work. You try, once, to count them in a single square meter, and you arrive at a number that exceeds the square meter, and you stop counting because the number keeps yielding more and more as you look harder, as though looking is a form of gentle excavation.

The scattered structures — and you find them if you walk far enough, which you do, slowly, because the terrain is exhausting in its sameness — are primitive. Stone shelters. Cairns. Walls made of stacked flat rock, roofless now, eroded to the height of your knee. They were built by hands, clearly: the stones are sized for human grip, the mortar (where any remains) isiteiteite crude. Once, someone lived here. Or tried to. The shelters are spaced far apart — a kilometer, two kilometers, more — and they show no tool marks more sophisticated than chipping and grinding. No metal residue. No writing. Just the mute architecture of survival, abandoned so long ago that even the concept of abandonment has weathered away into neutral geology.

The presences are denser around the shelters. They orient toward them. Not facing them, exactly — their bodies don't have a consistent facing direction — but *thicker*, *more layered*, the overlapping geometry more pronounced, as though the presence of human intention (however primitive, however long-vanished) drew them the way a stone draws sediment. Some of the largest presences are coiled around the base of a shelter wall, merged halfway into the rock, and you cannot tell if the wall was built on top of them or they grew around the wall or both, sequentially, over periods that make sequence meaningless.

## What This Place Is

The plateau has reached its final form. There is no erosion left to perform — the wind does not blow, the water does not run, the temperature does not fluctuate. Nothing changes because there is no gradient to drive change. The light is even. The air is still. The creatures are fixed. The stone is worn to its terminal smoothness. This is not a living landscape or a dead landscape. It is a *finished* landscape. It has arrived at the state that all landscapes are striving toward and that most will never reach because they will be interrupted by asteroid or ice sheet or the sun's expansion. This place will not be interrupted. It has passed through the interruption of time itself and come out the other side into a kind of eternity that is not infinite but *complete*.

The overwhelming fauna — and they are overwhelming, in the way that stars are overwhelming, in the way that grain-of-sand-on-a-beach is overwhelming — are not *of* this place. Or they are *too much* of this place. They are the place's excess. The place is so old, so settled, so deeply itself, that it has become more than its own geometry can hold, and the excess has taken the form of creatures — silent, still, overlapping, ancient, present, uncountable. They are the plateau's overflow. They are what happens when a landscape has no more need to change and so begins to *be*, in a density that the word *being* cannot quite support without cracking.

You could stay here. Nothing would stop you. Nothing would happen. The light would not change. The creatures would not move. The silence would deepen only in the sense that your ears would adjust to it and find new registers of quiet below the quiet you already knew. You would become a presence among presences. After enough time — and you have a sense, standing here, that *enough time* is a quantity this place is uniquely capable of offering — you would begin to blur at the edges. Not die. Not fossilize. Simply... *settle*. Become less distinct. Become more of the plain. The presences would thicken around you. The geometry would accommodate you, not by making room but by being already more-than-room. And eventually someone, or something, walking this endless ancient table, would see you and almost not see you, the way they almost don't see the others, and you would be still, and silent, and present, and you would have always been here, and the word *always* would not be a figure of speech.
