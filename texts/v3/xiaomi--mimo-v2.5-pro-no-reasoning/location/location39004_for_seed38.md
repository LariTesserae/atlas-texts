The first thing you notice is the light — or rather, its unwillingness to arrive. It doesn't fall here so much as it *leaks*, seeping through the canopy in slow, oily drips that take minutes to reach the ground. This is not a forest that receives sunlight; it is a forest that has grown *over* it, entombed it. The canopy is not a ceiling but a geology — layer upon layer of growth so ancient and so dense that the topmost surfaces have begun to erode themselves, collapsing into the layers below in slow-motion landslides of rot and epiphyte. You are inside a living shell.

The ground is the wrong word. What you walk on is not earth. It is a substrate of roots so tightly woven they have become a kind of wood-felt, a mat dozens of meters thick, saturated with water that never drains. It weeps. Every step releases a soft exhalation of moisture, and the air is so humid it feels like a second skin. Condensation beads on every surface — on the vast, pale trunks that rise like the columns of a drowned cathedral, on the hanging curtains of moss, on your own eyelashes. The water here is not flowing. It is *present*, a permanent resident, filling every crevice and hollow. Pools form in the cups of leaves the size of small boats. Rivulets trace slow, mineral-staining paths down the bark of trees that were old when the continents were young.

The trees. They are not trees as you know them. They are *place*. A single trunk may be a hundred meters in diameter, its bark a landscape of ridges and valleys colonized by ferns, lichens, and smaller parasitic plants. Some have hollowed with age, their interiors vast enough to contain fog, their wood so old it has become soft and fibrous, more like peat than timber. They do not grow upward so much as *outward*, and then downward again, their branches drooping under the weight of millennia until they touch the ground and take root, creating new trunks, new arches, new caverns of living wood. A single organism, impossibly old, can constitute an entire hill. The forest is not a collection of trees. It is a single, self-referential organism dreaming of multiplicity.

And it is warm. The warmth is a product of decay, of the slow exhalation of a billion processes of rot and respiration. It is a humid, composting warmth, thick with the smell of wet earth, of fungal bloom, of the sweet, cloying decay of fruit that will never be eaten. There is life here — abundant, quiet life. You do not see it so much as you feel its *watchfulness*. Pale, eyeless things move in the deep leaf-litter, their bodies adapted to the eternal dim. Insects with wings like stained glass hang in the air, their flight so slow it looks like suspended animation. High in the canopy, unseen, something breathes in a rhythm that takes an hour to complete. There are no birds. There is no wind. The only sound is the sound of water — the drip, the trickle, the soft, constant *hiss* of condensation. It is the sound of a world holding its breath.

Occasionally, you will find a structure. Not built, but *grown*. A hollow in a root that perfectly fits a body. A branch that has bent itself into a archway over a pool. They are not intentional. They are *coincidences*, patterns the forest has settled into over eons, the way a river settles into its bed. There are no tools here. There is no technology. There is only the biology of a world that has had nothing to do but *become itself* for longer than mountains have existed. It is a world at the end of its own history, having long ago reached a state of such profound equilibrium that change has become almost imperceptible. The cycles here are measured in the millennia it takes for a single leaf to fall from the highest canopy and reach the ground. The erosion is not of stone, but of life itself, worn smooth by its own persistence.

You are in a place that has forgotten it is a place. It is a state of being. A wet, warm, ancient, dreaming state of being, vast enough to have its own weather — not storms, but *moods*, shifts in the humidity, in the rate of dripping, in the quality of the dim. To stay here is to become a feature. A surface for the moss. A hollow for the water. A slow, warm, quiet part of the dream.
