The horizon is a sharp, unblinking line, pulled taut between two kinds of white. Above: the blinding, absolute white of a sky that has forgotten clouds, a sun-hazed diffusion that offers no shadow, only a directionless, pervasive brightness. Below: the white of the ground, a vast, undulating plane of what looks like salt but is something else—a fine, alkaline powder, the calcium carbonate of a sea that evaporated before this world was named. It crunches softly underfoot, a sound that is swallowed almost immediately by the sheer scale of the place. This is the Luminous Expanse, a high-altitude salt flat the size of a subcontinent, where the air is thin and dry, and the silence is not an absence of sound but a presence of its own.

And yet, it is not barren. Life has found a way to cling to existence here, a lushness defined not by density but by sheer, improbable tenacity. This is a flora that drinks light and breathes stone.

They are called the *Selenotrophs*, the "light-eaters." From a distance, they resemble a scattering of sculptures: wide, plate-like structures, some twenty feet across, angled with geometric precision to face the ever-present sun. Their surfaces are covered in a fine, crystalline filigree, a photosynthetic matrix that is more mineral than chlorophyll. They do not grow upward but outward, their "leaves" thin, translucent panes that shimmer with internal fractures, casting faint, prismatic shadows onto the white dust. In the cool, constant breeze, they do not rustle; they *tink*, a vast, scattered carillon of crystalline whispers, the primary sound of this continent.

Between these great light-harvesters grows a second layer: a dense, low carpet of what can only be described as living moss. It is a *lithophyte*, its thread-like roots not seeking water but dissolving minerals directly from the powder, binding it into a resilient, springy mat. Its color is a startling, deep violet, an impossible jewel-tone against the monochrome expanse. It absorbs specific frequencies of the relentless light, leaving the rest to be reflected as that eerie purple glow. This is the source of the "lushness"—a vast, continent-spanning meadow of violet moss, dotted with the silent, gleaming sentinels of the Selenotrophs.

The fauna is rare, and strange. The most common sight is the *Aeolian Skimmer*, a creature like a manta ray made of stretched membrane and hollow bone. It does not fly so much as slide on the high-altitude winds, its vast, near-invisible wingspan (sometimes thirty feet) catching the thermal currents that rise from the sun-baked powder. It feeds on airborne spores and the microscopic, wind-blown detritus of the flora, its shadow a fleeting, silent thing that passes over the moss like a quick cloud. Larger, but even more seldom seen, are the *Glass-Striders*. They are leggy, fragile-looking ungulates, their hides a pattern of chitinous, translucent plates. They move slowly, deliberately, browsing on the taller, tougher stalks of the Selenotrophs, their movements accompanied by a faint, grinding sound of mineral on mineral. Their own biology is a mystery; they seem to subsist on a mix of the plant's crystalline structures and the geothermal warmth that bleeds up through the thin crust in certain, patchy areas.

The world's physics are not of Earth, but they have found a balance. The "water" is not H₂O but a slow, deep seep of a heavier, non-aqueous solvent, a saline hydrocarbon that exists in a semi-solid state several hundred feet down. The roots of the violet moss have adapted to draw it up, capillary action working over centuries. The light is so intense, the air so thin and dry, that evaporation of any conventional moisture would be instantaneous. Instead, the ecosystem is closed, sealed. The plants respire not water vapor but trace mineral dust and oxygen, their exhalations contributing to the faint, chalky haze that hangs perpetually on the horizon.

Weather here is a grand, slow thing. There are no storms, only *shimmer*. As the sun heats the powder, the air above it becomes a lens, distorting the view into endless, rising waves of mirage. The horizon ripples and melts. Once every few years, a "calcite fall" occurs: a high-atmospheric disturbance coalesces the mineral dust into glittering, slow-motion curtains that descend like frozen rain, dusting the violet moss in a fresh, white layer and setting the Selenotrophs to ringing in a brief, frantic symphony of sound.

It is a place of profound, active peace. A continent-sized garden of light and stone, growing in silence and brightness, tended by the wind and the slow, patient chemistry of a world that has been itself for eons. It is not empty. It is simply... open. A landscape in perfect, quiet, sparkling balance.
