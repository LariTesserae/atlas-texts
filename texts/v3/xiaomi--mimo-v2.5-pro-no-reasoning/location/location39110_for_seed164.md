# The Plateau of Perpetual Roar

## What You See First

You stand on a lip of black stone and look out across nothing. Not nothing as in absence — nothing as in *distance*. The land falls away before you in a series of sharp terraces, each one the height of a city block, each one cut from the same dark, glassy basalt that still holds the shape of its eruption — rope textures, ropy folds, pressure-ridged columns that fractured into geometric fans when they cooled. The terraces descend in steps the size of provinces and then level out onto a plain that has no visible far edge. On a clear day — and there are no clear days — you might see three hundred kilometers. But there are no clear days. The air is too wet for that.

The plateau stretches south and east in every direction you can turn, continent-wide, a black table set above the world's lower atmosphere. Weather does not come here from elsewhere. The plateau makes its own. Condensation forms on the sharp basalt lips and runs in constant silver threads down the terrace faces. Fog pools in the steps like water in a staircase, dense and slow, never quite burning off. When the industrial cities exhale, their heat columns punch through the fog layer and create standing lenticular clouds that hover in place for weeks, lit from below by furnace glow.

## The Cold That Doesn't Move

The temperature holds near freezing — never far below, never far above. The altitude keeps the air thin and the heat minimal, but the moisture moderates everything. There is no true winter or summer here. The fog freezes onto the sharp basalt edges in thin rime that sublimates back into moisture by midday, only to reform. A cycle. Always the same cycle. The water table sits barely below the surface — you can hear it moving, a faint subsonic pulse through the stone, and in places the basalt simply *weeps*, dark stains spreading down vertical faces in branching patterns that look like river deltas drawn in charcoal. Pools form in the terraced steps, shallow and black and mirror-still, reflecting the grey overhead. They do not evaporate. They do not freeze. They simply persist.

The cold is not harsh in the way of blizzards. It is the cold of a stone room that has never had a fire. It seeps. You feel it in your teeth, in the marrow of your fingers. It is the kind of cold that does not kill quickly but never relents. The kind of cold that makes you understand why nothing here has a metabolism worth naming.

## The Vegetation That Should Not Thrive

And yet — green.

It covers everything. The basalt terraces are furred in it: mosses so thick they form mats you could sink a hand into, liverworts the color of jade, ferns whose fronds unfurl in tight spirals and then simply *stop*, holding their shape in the cold air without senescing or growing further. On the lower steps, where the fog is densest, you find growths that are not quite moss and not quite lichen — pale, rubbery, branching structures that cling to the wet rock and pulse faintly with trapped condensation. They do not photosynthesize in any way you recognize. They are too green. The green is wrong — saturated, almost luminous, as if the pigment is doing something additional to capturing light.

The vegetation extends across the plateau in unbroken carpet. Where the rock is young and sharp and still holds its volcanic geometry, the life has found every crack, every pressure ridge, every ropy fold. It grows in the seams between columnar basalt joints. It mats the undersides of overhangs. It fills the shallow pools with a film so uniform it looks intentional. And it is *lush* — lush in a way that makes no sense at this altitude, in this cold, in soil that barely exists. The mineral substrate is raw volcanic glass andite. There is no humus. There are no decomposers, because there is nothing to decompose. The plants simply grow from the rock, fed by the mineral-rich water that weeps continuously from every surface.

Nothing eats them. Nothing pollinates them. Nothing moves among them that is not wind or water. The absence is total and unsettling: no insects, no worms, no birds, no mammals, no amphibians. If you overturn a stone, there is nothing beneath it but wet moss and basalt. The silence of the animate world — the silence where birdsong or buzzing or the skittering of small feet should be — is absolute.

The plants do not seem to mind.

## The Cities

They are not hidden. They are enormous. But the plateau is so vast that they occupy it like chess pieces on a floor — visible if you know where to look, easily lost in the terraced immensity. You count seven from this vantage. The nearest is perhaps forty kilometers out, a grey angular mass rising from one of the broad terrace steps, its smokestacks and cooling towers forming a skyline that bristles against the fog like the teeth of a broken comb.

The cities are built from the basalt itself — cut from it, stacked from it, the same black glassy stone rearranged into vertical density. Their architecture is blockish and load-bearing, industrial in its logic: wide foundations to distribute weight on the young volcanic substrate, stepped structures that echo the terrace geometry of the surrounding landscape, as if the builders studied the land and replicated its logic at a smaller scale. There are walls, there are bridges between districts, there are aqueducts that channel the weeping water into collection systems. The infrastructure is layered — some structures visibly built atop older ones, creating multi-tiered urban formations where the lower levels are perpetually damp and moss-covered, and the upper levels rise into the thinner air where the fog breaks.

But from a distance, the cities look still. They do not look alive. They look like outcroppings.

## The Sound

You hear them before you see them.

The industrial roar is the defining sensory fact of the plateau, and it does not stop. It comes from everywhere — from the cities and from the infrastructure that connects them, from the pumping stations that sit alone on distant terrace steps like monasteries, from the pipe networks that run along the basalt ridges for hundreds of kilometers, carrying water or fuel or slag or something else entirely. The sound is mechanical: the thrum of generators, the hiss of high-pressure steam, the rhythmic concussion of stamp mills and crushers, the low organ-note drone of blast furnaces that have never been shut down. It reverberates off the flat terrace surfaces and carries across the fog. At forty kilometers from the nearest city, the sound is still loud enough to feel in your chest. At a hundred kilometers, it is a constant rumble, like distant thunder that does not resolve into lightning. At two hundred kilometers, it is still audible — a dull pressure on the eardrums, a sense that the air itself is vibrating.

The basalt transmits it. The young rock, still dense and unfractured by deep time, carries vibration with eerie efficiency. You can press your palm to a terrace lip and feel the industrial pulse traveling through the stone, a tremor that matches the rhythm of distant machinery. The moss grows over these vibrating surfaces without distress.

In the cities themselves, the sound is overwhelming. It is the first thing visitors describe and the thing residents claim not to notice. The architecture amplifies it — the hard basalt surfaces reflect every frequency, and the stepped urban forms create acoustic channels that focus sound into corridors and plazas. There is no quiet corner. Even in the deepest, lowest levels, beneath the moss and the weeping water, the generators thrum through the foundations.

## What the Cities Make

This is unclear, and that is part of the strangeness.

The factories run. They have always run, or at least they have run for longer than anyone's recorded memory extends, and the plateau has not changed in that time. The furnaces smelt something from the basalt. The mills crush something. The pipe networks carry processed material between cities in a closed loop — you can follow a pipe for two hundred kilometers and watch it branch and merge and branch again, connecting every settlement in a web that has no obvious source or destination. The inputs seem to be volcanic rock and weeping water. The outputs are... redirected back into the system. Slag is deposited in vast terraced yards on the city outskirts, where moss grows over it within days. Water is returned to the plateau's surface through drainage channels. The cycle appears closed.

Workers exist. You see them — small figures moving through the industrial districts, maintaining machinery, adjusting valves, carrying loads. They are human or close enough. They do not seem to leave the cities. They do not farm or forage. The cities provide what they need through some mechanism that is not immediately legible. The workers do not speak much, or if they do, their speech is lost in the universal roar.

No one seems to question the production. The factories produce. The plateau provides raw material. The moss grows. The water weeps. This is how it is.

## The Equilibrium

This is what makes the landscape feel frozen in amber: **nothing changes**.

The basalt is young — geologically, it erupted recently, its features still sharp, its surfaces still holding the glassy texture of rapid cooling. But it does not erode. The weeping water does not carve it. The cold does not crack it. The moss does not break it down with acids, as moss should. The terraces hold their geometry year after year, decade after decade. The fog forms and reforms in the same patterns. The lenticular clouds hover in the same positions above the same factory heat columns. The pools neither fill nor drain. The cities neither grow nor crumble. The pipe networks neither corrode nor expand.

It is as if the entire system reached some state of perfect balance at the moment of the plateau's emergence and has held there ever since — a vast, high-altitude, wet, cold, loud, green, inhabited, industrial, strange, and absolutely motionless tableau. The sound never changes pitch. The moss never stops growing but never overgrows. The water never stops weeping but never erodes. The factories never stop running but never produce anything that accumulates.

If you stayed long enough — years, decades — you would notice that nothing you remember from your first day has moved, grown, rusted, faded, or shifted. The same rime crystals form on the same basalt lips. The same fern fronds hold the same unfurled shape. The same workers adjust the same valves. The same roar fills the same air.

You begin to suspect that the sound is not mechanical at all. That it is the plateau itself — the stone, the water, the fog, the impossible green — vibrating at some fundamental frequency that the cities merely channel and amplify. That the industry is not the cause of the sound but a symptom of it. That the machines were built not to produce but to *resonate*.

You cannot prove this. You cannot disprove it. You can only stand on the lip of black stone and look out across the terraced, fog-filled, moss-covered, roaring, empty, enormous, frozen immensity, and feel — in your teeth, in your ribs, in the soles of your feet — the single, unvarying, eternal hum of a place that arrived fully formed and has no intention of ever becoming anything else.
