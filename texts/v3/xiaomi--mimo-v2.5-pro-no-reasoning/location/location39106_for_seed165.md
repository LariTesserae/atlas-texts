The wind does not stop here. It has never stopped. It is the first and only law, a constant shriek that scrapes across the high places of the world. This is the Spire, a mountain range of impossible height, a place where the bedrock has been given over to the sky. The peaks are not sharp; they are too old for that. Erosion has long since had its way, rounding the summits into great, grey, bone-smooth domes that gleam with a perpetual dampness. This is not the damp of rain, but of condensation. The air is thin as a ration of breath, and a ceaseless fog clings to the upper slopes, weeping onto the stone. The water appears in slick, dark stains that never form a pool, and in the isolated, trembling pockets of moisture held in the cup-petals of what passes for life here.

The vegetation is a testament to will. Just above the treeline, in the great, windswept saddles between peaks, grows the *Groaner's Moss*. It is not a true moss, but a rubbery, blackish-green mat that clings to the starkest rock faces. The wind, forced through its dense cellular structure, produces a deep, resonant, and unsettlingly loud tone—a bass note against the higher, more chaotic scream of the wind itself. The land is mostly flesh-stone and this moss, with vast, barren scree fields of shattered granite frozen at their feet. The temperature is a constant, gnawing cold that seizes metal and makes stone brittle. Nothing thaws, nothing melts; the moisture simply *is*.

The scale is deceptively regional. One can see for a hundred miles across a plateau of cloud and mountain heads, but the emptiness is the dominant feature. To walk here is to feel like a mote in a cathedral of stone and noise. The density of *presence* is almost zero. You might see a single, gnarled, petrified stump of a tree that died centuries ago, halfway up a slope of black gneiss. Or a partial wall of that same stone, built by hands forgotten so completely that it now seems a natural slant in the geology. These are the scattered remains. There are no paths, only the memory of them in slightly worn ways. There is no technology. There are only the tools of bone and knapped flint one might find in a shallow cairn, their original purpose a mystery.

The faunal rarety is absolute, but not empty. One sees the **Veil-Herons**. They are not birds. They are translucent, hollow shapes, like kite-laminate made of a film of chitin, that drift on the dynamic screaming wind. They do not fly so much as *are* the wind in a temporary, self-willed shape. They cast no shadow, and their only sound is the dip and rise of the perpetual gale through their forms. They are found more often on the lower, leeward slopes, where the wind eddies into a temporary, relative quiet. There is one other presence: the **Bell-Walker**. Once a year, sometimes once a decade, a single, immense, four-legged creature, its body a living carbuncle of quartz and basalt, will appear, moving with geologic slowness along the highest ridge. The vibration of its passage is the only true silence—the sound stops for its footfall, then returns with renewed vigor. It is a walking landmark, a piece of the mountain that has decided, for a time, to move.

This is a place of **frozen dynamic**. The equilibrium is so perfect, so entrenched, that nothing changes within a human lifespan. The Veil-Herons may reform, the Bell-Walker may pass, but the mountain, the wind, the cold, the sound—they are the same as they were a thousand years ago. The weirdness, however, is the breakdown of scale and substance in the face of this constancy. The air itself seems to possess a grain, a texture of sound that makes the edges of objects shimmer if you stare too long. The condensation on a stone might, in your peripheral vision, form a word before dissolving. The sound—always the sound—is not just noise. It is a carved landscape. It erodes the mind as it has eroded the rock. One begins to hear whispers in the shriek, fragments of a conversation held with itself, arguing the same point for millennia. The line between what is stone, what is spirit, and what is a trick of the light and the deafening roar becomes meaningless. You are not alone because the wind is a presence. You are not lost because the mountain is a clock. You are simply another transient pattern in a place that has already reached its conclusion. You are the unheard shout in the ongoing scream.
