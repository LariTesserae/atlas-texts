The air is the first thing you notice. It is heavy, a physical presence in the lungs, saturated with a chill dampness that never truly evaporates. It carries the scent of wet stone and cold, decaying moss. This is a world perpetually post-rain, not in a violent, thunderous way, but as a settled state of being. The sky is a uniform, pearly grey, a vast, low ceiling from which water doesn't so much fall as condense, clinging to every surface in a fine, glimmering dew.

You stand on a high plateau, but it is not the kind of elevation that offers vistas. The scale here is deceptive. The mountains are not jagged peaks but immense, rolling shoulders of stone, their flanks weathered over ages into soft, rounded forms. They are the color of slate and damp charcoal, etched with millennia of water-ways. From every crack, every fissure, from the very pores of the rock, water weeps. It doesn't roar in waterfalls; it seeps, trickles, and gathers in endless, slow-motion cascades. This is a hydrology of patience.

The ground underfoot is not soil but a dense, springy crust of lichens, mosses, and tough, low-growing sedges. Vegetation is sparse in variety but not in coverage. There are no trees, no brush, only this continuous, living carpet, a hundred shades of green, grey, and russet, constantly damp. It is a landscape that has reached a profound equilibrium with its own erosion. The stones are rounded, the soil (where it exists) is a thin, peaty gruel, and the water that sculpts the land is simply moving it, inch by inch, century by century, into a more settled form.

And yet, the world is not empty. It is *dense*. The silence is a thick, tangible thing, broken only by the sound of water—the hollow drip from a stone ledge, the gurgle of a hidden stream, the soft hiss of mist. Through this quiet, you begin to see the presences. They are the Urban.

Great structures of stone and bone-white wood rise from the landscape, not built upon it, but grown from it. They are cyclopean and weathered, their lines as organic and settled as the mountains themselves. These are not buildings for people, but for the place itself. They are hollowed-out tors, their insides carved into terraces, chambers, and channels that guide the endless water. Vast, tiered amphitheaters hold pools of still, black water. Aqueducts of lichen-covered stone span gorges, not for transporting water to a thirsty populace, but for allowing the water to find its preferred path. The architecture is the infrastructure of the landscape's own hydrology. It is a city of canals and cisterns, of mist-catchers and condensation-plates, all made from the same stone as the mountains.

Movement here is slow, glacial. A thick, slow-moving river of cloud drifts through the canyons between the structures. The ecosystem is a closed loop: the water nurtures the moss, the moss holds the stone, the stone shapes the water, and the water carves the stone. Fauna is rare—a pale, salamander-like creature might be seen in a deep cistern, or a bird with feathers like slate might perch silently on a spire. But the dominant "life" is the water itself, a dynamic, patient, and all-encompassing process. This is a world in a state of active, eternal balance, a machine of rock and water running on a timescale that makes the concept of 'change' irrelevant. It is vast, quiet, wet, and utterly, profoundly itself.
