The world is the **Guttering Peaks**, a range of mountains so old they have forgotten their own names. They are not sharp, but soft with time, their forms worn into gentle, sprawling curves like the shoulders of sleeping giants. This is a landscape of extreme scale, where a single mountain’s slope might descend for a hundred kilometers, its flanks forming entire nations. The bedrock is a porous, dark grey basalt, a stone that has spent eons drinking the air, and it is saturated.

Water is the ruling element. It does not simply flow; it *seeps*. Every surface is slick with a constant, shimmering film of condensation. The air is so thick with moisture it feels like breathing broth. Great, slow-moving rivers of mist, weather-systems in their own right, pour through the valleys that separate these continental shoulders. The rock itself weeps from a thousand hidden fissures, creating a constant, soft, percussive music that underlies all other sound. Where the water gathers, it doesn't form lakes, but a soft, treacherous peat-bog of crushed volcanic glass and rotted vegetation, glowing with a faint internal luminescence.

There is no sun. There has not been for geological ages. The profound, absolute darkness is alleviated only by the world’s own light. Bioluminescence is the engine of sight here. The ubiquitous mosses and lichens that velvet every stone surface pulse with a soft, blue-green radiance, a slow and steady heartbeat of light that follows the rhythm of unseen geothermal vents deep below. This creates a landscape of shifting, ambient glows and deep, velvety pools of pure black.

The vegetation is a dense, layered, and aggressive tapestry of life adapted to warmth, wetness, and gloom. There are no trees as we know them. Instead, colossal fungal stalks, like the pillars of a ruined cathedral, rise kilometers into the misty dark, their caps forming a secondary canopy that sheds glowing spores like constant, silent snow. Below them, thick, ropey vines with photoreceptive nodes slither slowly across the ground, competing with forests of giant, rubbery ferns whose fronds drip a viscous, sweet nectar. Everything is connected in a dense mat; to walk is to pull your limbs free from a clinging, living web.

The fauna has evolved in concert with this dense, strange flora. Life is abundant and audible. The air hums with the wingbeats of leathery, bat-like creatures that navigate by a combination of echolocation and the faint light of the spores. In the undergrowth, skittering arthropods with shells of polished, obsidian-like chitin click and scrape as they move. Larger things move in the deep shadows between the fungal pillars—long-necked, silent grazers that peel moss from stone with prehensile tongues, and the things that hunt them, fast, six-limbed predators with patchy fur that shimmers with its own dull light. The sound is a constant, layered cacophony: the drip of water, the rustle of movement, the high-pitched clicks of insects, and the deep, resonant croak of amphibians whose calls echo for miles.

Scattered across this wet, glowing wilderness are the remains of **The Industry**. There was a civilization here, one that understood the unique geology and biology. They were not builders of cities, but of engines. Great, corroded structures of brass, copper, and a greenish, almost organic-looking alloy rise from the peat or cling to mountain slopes. They are not buildings, but machines. Enormous pistons, thick with verdigris, are half-submerged in bogs, their cylinders choked with luminous moss. Skeletal frameworks of aqueducts, designed to channel the weeping water, now serve as trellises for the slithering vines. The air around these ruins smells of ozone and rust, a sharp tang that cuts through the damp sweetness of the ecosystem. Faint, rhythmic pulses of energy still sometimes arc between distant pylons, illuminating the mist in a sudden, ghostly flare before subsiding back into the ambient gloom. The technology was industrial, but it was designed to work *with* this world, to harness the seeping water, the geothermal heat, and the bioluminescent life, before it was inevitably overgrown and abandoned.

To be in the Guttering Peaks is to feel small, warm, and constantly damp. The darkness is not empty, but full of presence. The sound is a roar, yet it is composed of a million small, wet, living noises. The land itself feels alive, breathing in the slow rhythm of the mountain's stone heart, its exhalation a mist that you can feel on your skin. It is a settled, balanced world, but a profoundly strange and active one, where the relics of a lost age are slowly, inexorably, being digested by the planet they once sought to master.
