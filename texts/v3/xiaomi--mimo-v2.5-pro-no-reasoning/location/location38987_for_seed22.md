The first thing you feel is the sound. It is not a noise you hear with your ears so much as a pressure you feel in your bones, a vibration that hums in the marrow of your teeth. It is the sound of a city that never sleeps because its very infrastructure is dreaming aloud.

You stand on a plain of spongy, saturated earth. The ground is a deep, bruised purple-black, the color of rich soil turned over after a rain that fell decades ago. It’s damp, but not with water. A slick, silvery dew clings to everything, catching the ambient light with an oily sheen. It beads on your skin with a faint, electric tingle. This is not water as you know it; it is a condensate of the air itself, a moisture that feels both organic and unnervingly synthetic.

Stretching to the horizon in every direction is a city, but a city in a state of arrested decay and impossible bloom. Its architecture is not of stone or steel, but of a material that seems grown—a chitinous, pearlescent substance that forms sweeping buttresses and vaulting arches, all in a style that is both hauntingly familiar and utterly alien. These structures are skeletal, half-formed, their raw, sharp edges speaking to a geology of recent emergence. They look less like they were built and more like they erupted from the ground only a few years ago, still finding their final shape. And yet, they are vast, their spires and domes reaching into a sky they will never touch, creating a regional landscape of hollow monuments.

This is the paradox of the place: it is overwhelmingly lush and teeming with life, yet utterly, achingly empty. Life has not waited for the city to be finished. Every surface is colonized. Viridian moss, thick as velvet, carpets the ground between the structures. Creeping vines with leaves like polished jade wind up the skeletal arches, their tendrils probing the sharp, unfinished edges. Fungi in great, shelf-like formations erupt from the walls, their caps glowing with a soft, internal bioluminescence that provides the primary source of light in this dim world. The air is thick with the scent of damp earth, ozone, and something like blooming nightshade.

The fauna is a reflection of this union. Creatures that are part flesh, part machine skitter through the shadows. Six-legged, iridescent beetles, their carapaces etched with circuit-like patterns, burrow into the moss. Long-necked, silent avians with wings that shimmer like holograms perch on the highest arches, their heads constantly swiveling on gyroscopic mounts. In the glowing pools of condensation, things that resemble salamanders but with bodies of woven light dart and weave. This is a vibrant, abundant ecosystem, a food web of the strange, yet it moves through the city as if through a museum, never disturbing the profound stillness of its empty architecture.

The low elevation and the warm, humid air create a perpetual haze. There is no sun, only a diffuse, pearlescent glow from the overcast sky, a light that is both dim and strangely omnidirectional, casting no sharp shadows. The warmth is not oppressive, but it is constant, a metabolic heat that seems to emanate from the city's bones themselves.

This is a place of profound contrasts. It is a city built on a scale to house millions, yet it contains only the buzz of insects and the whisper of moss. It is a landscape of immense, open vistas, where your eye can travel for miles across this forest of hollow spires, yet the density of presence is nearly zero. It is a place of staggering, beyond-modern technology—the very materials and energy sources defy your understanding—but that technology is inert, overgrown, its purpose lost. It is a deafening world that is perfectly, paradoxically quiet in its soul.

The rules here are not the rules of construction, but of cultivation. Something, or someone, planted this city. It is growing, on a geological timescale, according to a blueprint encoded in its very substance. The animals, the plants, even the strange dew are not invaders; they are part of the ecosystem, the designated gardeners or perhaps the intended inhabitants of a project that was interrupted at its inception. The dynamism you feel is not the bustle of life, but the slow, patient pulse of a machine thinking, of a garden tending itself, waiting for a dawn that may never come. The deafening sound is the hum of that waiting.
