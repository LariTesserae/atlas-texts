You first notice the cold. It is a presence, not an absence. It presses in from the stone walls, seeps up from the floor, and hangs in the air like a held breath. It is a stable, ancient cold, the kind that has never known the sun. It is the temperature of deep time, of geology.

This is a pocket, a geode swallowed by the earth. The ceiling arches maybe fifteen meters overhead, but the darkness swallows the exact shape. What little light exists is not light, but the faintest gradient of less-absolute-black, a dull, milky luminescence that seems to bleed from the very rock itself—a geological afterglow, a slow half-life of ancient, buried phosphors. It allows you to see the suggestion of form, but not detail.

The ground underfoot is not soil. It is a springy, cohesive mat, several centimeters thick, of something between moss and fungal mycelium. It is a deep, wet black, like charcoal velvet, and it covers everything: the floor, the lower slopes of the walls, the surfaces of the scattered, rounded stones that sit embedded in it. This is the vegetation—a silent, slow-motion jungle. The "trees" are columns of a strange, silicon-based lichen, thick as a man's thigh, rising from the mat. They are a pale, ghostly grey, and their surfaces are etched with fractal patterns that look less like growth and more like circuitry. They branch, slowly, over centuries, into delicate, crystalline canopies that hold no leaves, only more of the same intricate lattice. They are alive, but their metabolism is measured in glacial time. They do not photosynthesize. They chemosynthesize, or perhaps they run on processes we have no name for. They are the primary engineers of this place.

Water is a presence in its own right. Not as a river, or a pool, but as a constant, pervasive dampness. Condensation gathers on every surface, forming perfect, heavy droplets on the tips of the lichen-branches and on the silvery ribs of the scattered artifacts. The droplets fall with a plink that is shockingly loud in the quiet, a sound that comes from every direction at once. Occasionally, a thin, braided trickle of water will find a seam in the wall, descending in a slow, silent spiral before disappearing into the mat. The air smells of wet stone, of ozone, and of something else—something like cold, electric snow.

The artifacts are the reason for the strangeness. They are not ruins, not in any human sense. They are partial structures, made of a material that seems to drink the faint light, a non-reflective black composite that is neither metal nor ceramic. One might be a wall segment, curving gently, its surface smooth except for a grid of tiny, shallow depressions. Another is a framework, a skeletal arch that seems to be growing *out* of one of the lichen-columns, its black ribs merging seamlessly with the grey, crystalline tissue. They are still. They have been here longer than the lichen, longer than the mat. They are the reason for the advanced tech reading, but they are inert, silent, and deeply integrated into the ecosystem, as if they are a kind of fossilized coral reef, a substrate for this strange life.

The ecology is simple, and slow. There is no animal life. The dynamic is the lichen, growing imperceptibly over decades, its crystal branches extending millimeter by millimeter, sometimes fusing with the black artifacts, sometimes sealing a water-trickle in a cage of glass. The dynamic is the water, condensing, falling, trickling, a patient clock. The dynamic is the mat, a living carpet digesting the rock and the artifacts at a pace that makes moss look frenetic. Everything is in balance, a settled state that has achieved a kind of perfection. The rules are patience, and chemistry, and the slow, inexorable engineering of life finding a way in a place that should have none. It is hidden, dense, and quiet. It is a world thinking in the dark, its thoughts so slow we might mistake them for stone.
