# The Lithic Marshes

## Substrate

The land is young. You can tell by the way the ground still remembers being something else — pressed and folded strata ofite andite and mineral clay, still sharp-edged, still carrying the chemical signatures of whatever cataclysm pushed them upward into air. This is a continent that emerged recently in geological terms, perhaps a seabed that was thrust skyward, perhaps a magma shelf that cooled and cracked and rose. The surface is low and mostly flat, broken by broad shallow basins and gentle ridges no taller than a standing person. Nothing has had time to soften. The rock is raw. The mineral crusts that cover the ground — pale yellows, dried-blood reds, chalky whites — haven't been worn smooth by rain or scoured by wind. They sit exposed and granular, almostite powder, and when you press a hand against them they leave ochre streaks on your skin.

There is almost no water. Not none — there are brackish seeps in the lowest basins, mineral-charged trickles that surface and vanish within a few meters, and a faint dampness that rises from deep cracks at night. But for a landscape this vast, the water is negligible. The air carries almost no humidity. You would not call this a desert, though, because a desert implies barrenness, and nothing here is barren.

## The Growth



What covers this land is not what most would call vegetation, though the word applies loosely. The dominant organisms are succulent columnar structures — dense, turgid, segmented, and slow-growing — that range from knee-height to the height of a person, and in the oldest colonies, considerably taller. They are the color of jade and bruise-purple and grey-green, their surfaces waxy and faintly iridescent. They store water the way a cactus does, in pressurized internal chambers, and they photosynthesize, and they reproduce by dropping heavy seed-casings that crack open on impact and root almost immediately.

But they do not merely grow. They *construct*.

Over generations, as individual columns mature and die, their hardened outer shells remain — mineralized, calcified, standing as permanent scaffolding. New growth fills in the gaps. Living specimens climb the dead ones, merge with them, seal joints with resinous secretions that hardite into something resemblingiteiteite mortar. The result, across hundreds and thousands of years, is architecture. The colonies form walls, arches, buttressed chambers, spiraling towers, and labyrinthine corridors. From a distance — and the distances here are enormous, the sightlines stretching across a flatness that seems to curve — the landscape looks like a city. An infinite city. Towers and districts and avenues, stretching from one horizon to the next, packed so densely that there is almost no open ground between structures.

This is not a city. There is no one directing it. There are no blueprints, no intentions, no communication between colonies beyond chemical gradients in the substrate. But the result is functionally indistinguishable from urban planning at a continental scale. The organisms have simply been doing this for long enough, in sufficient density, that their cumulative construction has become the defining feature of the world.

The density is staggering. You cannot walk in a straight line for more than a few body-lengths without encountering a wall or column or archway. The corridors between structures are narrow — shoulder-width, sometimes narrower — and they twist and branch and dead-end and open suddenly into small enclosed plazas where the light falls differently and the air is a degree or two cooler. The structures are packed. Every surface is occupied. Living growth clings to dead growth clings to mineral deposits clings to more living growth. There is no separation between organism and landscape. The land *is* the organisms. Their accumulated generations *are* the geology of this place — a new kind of sedimentary layer, biological in origin, mineral in substance.

## Fauna



The creatures here are quiet and numerous. Most are small — arthropod-bodied, many-legged, armored against desiccation. They live in the corridors and chambers between the columnar structures, navigating a three-dimensional maze that offers shade, shelter, and a thin but reliable food web. Some graze on the living growth, scraping wax and surface tissue with specialized mouthparts. Others are predators of those grazers. Others are scavengers that process the dead. The trophic layers are multiple and complex, all of it compressed into the narrow interstices of this built-but-unbuilt landscape.

Larger fauna moves through the wider corridors — low-slung, heavy-bodied quadrupeds with thick hide and small eyes, moving slowly, browsite on the lower growth. They are not fast. Nothing here is fast. The pace is geological-biological: processes measured in seasons and years, not seconds. The predators that follow them are patient ambushers, waiting motionless in shadowed archways for hours or days, nearly indistinguishable from the structures themselves.

In the towers — the tallest constructions, some reaching thirty or forty meters — nesting colonies of flying creatures occupy the upper chambers. They are membranous-winged, insectile, and they hum at a frequency so low it barely registers as sound. They are part of why the landscape is quiet despite being full of life. Everything here operates at low amplitude. The grazers have no vocalizations. The predators strike silently. The insects hum below hearing. Even the wind — and there is wind, warm and dry, moving through the corridor system like breath through lungs — even the wind is soft, broken and diffused by the density of structures until it arrives as a faint pressure change rather than a roar.

---

## Sound

The quiet is remarkable for a place this alive. It is not silence — silence would be eerie, would suggest emptiness. This is something else: a saturation of activity at volumes too low to compete with each other. You hear your own footsteps, muffled by the granular substrate. You hear the faint, almost subsonic hum of the flying colonies in the towers above. You hear, if you stop and listen very carefully, a dry rustling — the slow expansion of living growth pressing against its neighbors, the microscopic crackle of mineral shell adjusting under biological pressure. Sometimes a seed-casing drops and strikes a hard surface with a single dull knock, and then the quiet reassembles around it.

No running water. No birdsong as such. No wind howl. The soundscape is the soundscape of a vast, slow, dense organism going about its business at a frequency just below the threshold of easy hearing.

## Atmosphere and Light

The light is unremarkable — a sun, a sky, the angle suggesting mid-latitudes. The atmosphere is thin on moisture but thick with fine mineral particulates kicked up by the wind and by the slow erosion of exposed surfaces. This gives the light a particular quality: warm, diffuse, slightly amber. Shadows inside the corridor system are not black but a deep warm brown, and the light that reaches the enclosed plazas filters through gaps in the overhead structures in pale shafts that illuminate suspended dust motes for hours at a time.

The temperature is warm and steady. The mass of biological and mineral structure acts as thermal inertia — the corridors are cooler during the day than the open basins between the larger colony-zones, and warmer at night. There are no extremes. The world breathes thermally, expanding and contracting in slow convection cycles that move air gently through the maze. Warm air rises through the towers. Cooler air sinks into the corridors. The result is a perpetual slow circulation — not wind, exactly, but a kind of respiration at the scale of the whole landscape.

## Erosion and Time



The geology is embryonic. This is a place that has not yet decided what it will be. The mineral substrate is still settling — chemical reactions in the exposed strata are ongoing, salts crystallizing and dissolving in slow cycles driven by the tiny fluctuations in available moisture. The biological construction is the primary force shaping the landscape, but it is happening on top of a surface that is itself still forming. In some basins, the ground has cracked into polygonal tiles, each tile slightly raised at its edges by crystallization pressure, creating a tessellated floor that the organisms have colonized and built upon. In a thousand years those tiles will have broken down into something else. In ten thousand years the whole substrate will be different. But the biological construction will persist, because it is faster than the geology. The organisms are outpacing the land they sit on.

Some of the older colony zones — the densest, tallest, most ancient districts — have begun to take on the character of true geological formations. Their lower layers have mineralized completely. Drill into them and you would findite cross-sections indistinguishable from sedimentary rock, except for the faint regularity of their internal structure, the ghost-geometries of biological origin preserved in stone. These are the foundation layers of something new: a lithosphere being built from the top down, by living things, on a world whose natural lithosphere is still forming from the bottom up.

## What It Feels Like to Be Here

You stand in a corridor no wider than your arm-span. The walls are smooth, waxy, faintly warm to the touch — they are alive, or parts of them are. Above you, the structures lean toward each other and nearly meet, leaving a strip of amber sky visible, a thin bright line. Dust drifts in the light. The air smells of mineral and resin — dry, clean, faintly sweet. You can feel the warmth of the structures radiating against your skin.

Ahead, the corridor branches. Both branches look identical: narrow, twisting, packed with growth. Behind you, the same. In every direction, the same: a vast, dense, quiet labyrinth of living construction stretching across a continent, full of patient creatures going about their small business, built by no one, directed by nothing, assembling itself with the slow cumulative logic of organisms that do not think but build as though they do.

There is no water here. There is almost no sound. There is no technology, no tool-use, no intention. And yet the place is urban in its very bones — organized, layered, infrastructural, saturated with the products of collective labor across deep time. It is a city that grew itself, on a world that is still growing its own bones beneath it.
