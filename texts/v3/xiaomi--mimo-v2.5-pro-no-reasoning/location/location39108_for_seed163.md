# The Dripping Tableland

---

You arrive at the edge and the sound reaches you first — before the light, before the wet, before anything else. It is not one sound but a convergence of ten thousand small ones: water sheeting across granite, water threading through moss, water condensing and falling from air that cannot hold itself together. The aggregate is enormous. It fills the skull. It is the sound of a landscape that is, at every point, releasing water it does not want.

The plateau stretches ahead.

It is high — high enough that the light comes white and mineral, stripped of its yellow warmth by the thin atmosphere. The sky is not blue so much as it is a pale, aching transparency. When the clouds are absent, and they often are, the brightness is total, directionless, the kind of light that erases shadow and makes distance uncertain. You cannot easily tell how far the far edge is. The horizon dissolves into glare and mist and the soft grey smear where condensation eats the outline of distant ridges.

The rock beneath everything is new.

That is the first geological fact and it governs the rest. This plateau was under ice not long ago — not in human memory, perhaps, but in the memory of lichen, which has barely begun its slow project of eating stone. The granite is raw, unweathered, still holding the polish of glacial passage. Scratches run northeast to southwest in long parallel grooves, the handwriting of ice that has since departed. Where the rock has fractured, it has fractured cleanly, in great rectangular blocks and sharp-edged fissures, because nothing has yet softened the breaks. There is no soil, not truly. There is rock, and on top of the rock, there is life, and the life is what holds the thin dark substance that might one day become soil but is not yet committed to the transformation.

The vegetation is — the vegetation is *everything*.

Not tall. Not dense. Not crowded. But total. Every surface that is not actively submerged or actively vertical has been claimed. The claimants are small: cushion mosses packed tight as fists, sphagnum in slow amber-and-green mats, liverworts so flat they seem painted onto the stone, and between them the grasses — stiff, grey-green, growing in solitary tufts or loose congregations that ripple when the wind moves through. There are sedges with triangular stems, standing in shallow water, each one alone or with two or three companions, spaced as if by agreement. There are dwarf shrubs — some kind of heather, or something that wants to be heather — growing no higher than a hand's breadth, their roots spreading laterally across the rock because there is nowhere else for roots to go. From above, the plateau looks green, uniformly green, the way a floor looks uniformly flat. But kneel down and the uniformity dissolves into a mosaic of separate lives, each one growing in its own small pocket of water and thin dark substrate, none of them touching their neighbors, all of them together covering everything.

The water is — the water is *the thing*.

It comes from everywhere. It falls from the air, which is so saturated at this altitude that condensation forms on every surface that drops even slightly below ambient temperature — which is to say, every surface, because the rock is cooler than the air and the grass is cooler than the rock and the air is cooler than the light that passes through it. Droplets bead on every blade of grass, every exposed stone face, every metal pole and wire of the scattered industrial infrastructure. They grow, they tremble, they fall. The sound of their falling is the base note of the plateau's roar.

Beneath the surface — which is to say, beneath the moss — the water moves laterally. The granite is impermeable. Water that falls on the plateau does not sink; it slides. It moves through the moss layer like blood through a sponge, slowly, in every direction, downslope by inches, until it finds a fissure or a channel and then it moves faster, much faster, and the channels are everywhere, braided and shallow, cutting through the moss mats into the raw stone below, and the water in these channels runs clear and cold and loud against the clean-cut granite. Where the channels converge, there are streams. Where the streams converge, there are rivers — small ones, but fast, white with turbulence, dropping suddenly into clefts and disappearing into the deep fractures of the rock with a sound like someone striking a resonant metal drum, over and over, without rhythm.

There are waterfalls. They are not dramatic, individually. They are short — a meter, three meters, five meters — but there are hundreds of them, perhaps thousands, visible as white threads stitched into the grey-green slope wherever the plateau's edge breaks and steps down. The collective sound of the waterfalls merges with the collective sound of the condensation and the wind and the running channels into something that is not noise, exactly, because noise implies randomness, and this is not random — it is a system, a hydrological engine running at full capacity, every component contributing its register to the whole.

The wind is present and constant. It moves across the plateau unobstructed, because nothing here grows tall enough to obstruct it. It is not violent — this is not a gale-scoured summit — but it is steady, a pressure more than a force, pushing the grasses into permanent lean, pushing the mist in slow horizontal sheets, pushing the sound of the water eastward so that the western edge of the plateau is louder than the eastern, though both are loud.

The industrial structures are scattered across this landscape like objects left behind by someone who did not intend to return.

There is a pumping station — low, concrete, windowless, half-buried in the moss as if the plateau is slowly absorbing it. Its function is unclear from the outside. Inside, presumably, machinery moves water from one place to another, because that is what pumping stations do, and there are pipes emerging from its flanks and running downslope, partially buried in the moss, their metal surfaces bright with condensation, occasionally venting steam or excess water through small valves that hiss and spit into the grass. The station hums. The hum is inaudible beneath the larger sound but you can feel it in the pipes if you touch them.

There are weather instruments — anemometers spinning in the wind, their cups blurred with speed; rain gauges, which are perpetually overflowing; temperature sensors mounted on poles, their readouts unreadable from a distance. Someone collects this data. The cables run to a small relay tower, steel-latticed, its guy wires vibrating with a faint singing tone that joins the general chorus.

There is a ruin — or not a ruin, a remnant, the foundation of a building that was never finished or was finished and then dismantled. Concrete footings, four of them, arranged in a rectangle, with moss growing over them so completely that they read as four slightly raised cushions of green, distinguishable from the surrounding vegetation only by their geometric regularity and the faint rust stain where rebar once protruded. Someone tried to build here. The plateau did not object, exactly, but it did not help, and eventually the builders went somewhere the water was less interested in their materials.

There is a track — not a road, a track, two ruts cut into the moss and shallow substrate, the stone beneath polished by tires into a dark gleam. It leads from the plateau's southern access point to the pumping station and then continues northeast toward something that might be another structure or might be nothing, fading into the moss and the mist and the grass. A vehicle comes once a week, or once a month, or when the pumping station needs servicing. The ruts refill with moss between visits. The plateau is always in the process of erasing what is done to it, but the process is slow, patient, unhurried.

The fauna is sparse to the point of absence. You see birds — small ones, pipits or wheatears, perching briefly on the relay tower or the pumping station's roof before launching back into the wind. They are not nesting, or if they are, they are nesting somewhere you cannot see, in a crevice of rock, in a hollow beneath a moss mat. There are insects — midges, mostly, appearing in the still air above the standing water in the shallow pools between the moss cushions, hovering in small clouds that drift and disperse. There may be voles or pikas in the rock fields at the plateau's edges, where the fissures are deep enough to offer shelter, but you do not see them. The plateau is too open, too bright, too wet, too loud for most things that live by hiding. What lives here lives by enduring.

The brightness is relentless. It comes not only from the sun but from the water — every droplet on every surface is a small lens, a small mirror, redirecting light, scattering it, multiplying it. The moss is dark, the rock is grey, the grass is muted green, but the light that falls on all of these things bounces and refracts and accumulates until the air itself seems to glow with a pale, diffuse luminance that has no single source. On overcast days, when the cloud deck sits low and close, the plateau exists in a white enclosure, the world reduced to a hundred-meter radius of visibility, everything beyond that distance erased, and still the light is bright — brighter, perhaps, because the clouds reflect the water's reflections back downward, trapping the light in a closed loop between sky and surface.

And the water continues. It does not stop. It does not pause. It condenses, collects, runs, pools, overflows, runs again, falls, collects, condenses. The cycle is not dramatic — there are no floods here, no droughts, no seasonal extremes — but it is inexorable. The plateau is a machine for turning atmospheric moisture into surface water into channel flow into stream flow into river flow, and the machine runs constantly, at a pace that is almost geological in its steadiness. The moss grows, but slowly. The channels deepen, but slowly. The rock fractures, but slowly. Nothing here happens fast. Everything here happens without stopping.

You stand at the edge, where the plateau steps down into a valley you cannot see for the mist rising off the waterfalls, and you feel the sound in your chest and the light on your closed eyelids and the moisture on your skin and the cool, steady pressure of the wind, and you understand that this place is not hostile and not welcoming. It is simply *occupied* — with water, with light, with the slow green patience of plants that have learned to live on stone in a place where it is always wet and always bright and always loud and the rock is so new it has not yet decided what shape it wants to be.
