The heat is the first thing you understand, and the last. It is not an atmospheric condition; it is the medium of this place. Down here, below the surface of a world you’ve already forgotten, the heat is liquid and solid both. It presses from above, radiates from the walls, and breathes up from the floor. It is the engine of all that lives.

You are in a grotto, a cathedral of slow growth. The light is not from a sun, but from the place itself. The walls, the ceiling, the very air is saturated with a low, golden luminescence, as if the rock has been soaking in honey for a millennium. It emanates from the plants.

And everything is plant.

There is no soil, not anymore. What was once stone has been consumed, replaced, and reimagined by a singular, monstrous botanical architecture. The "floor" is a vast, yielding mattress of fungal hyphae and root-flesh, pale and damp to the touch, giving slightly with each step and releasing a puff of sweet, decaying spores. The air smells of chlorophyll, wet stone, and something richer, like honey or warm blood.

The vegetation is total. It is the substrate. Great, ribbed columns, like the trunks of petrified sequoias, rise up into the gloom, but they are not wood. They are bundles of fibrous, vascular tissue, slick with condensation that gathers in sluggish rivulets and drips with a slow, heavy *plonk* into pools on the root-floor. These pools are the water here—pockets of clear, warm liquid held in the laps of leaf-cups the size of bathtubs, or seeping through the porous walls.

The scale is deceptive. It feels like a single, immense cavern, but as your eyes adjust, you see it is a region of interconnected chambers. Great, fleshy portals, like the mouths of leviathans, open into other glowing spaces. Light filters through translucent membranes of leaf or skin, casting shifting, dappled shadows. The density is staggering. There is no empty air. Every surface is furred with mosses that pulse with light, draped with vines that are thick as a man’s arm and covered in soft, bioluminescent nodules. Ferns with fronds like frozen waterfalls cascade down the living walls. It is a layered, saturated world where one must often push aside a heavy, velvety curtain of leaf to proceed.

Movement is rare. A shadow might detach from a column—a slow, six-legged creature with a carapace like polished amber, moving with the patience of the geology it mimics. It is not hunting; it is tending, scraping a particular lichen from a stem with delicate mandibles. Fauna here are curators, not predators. You might see a flicker of something bat-like, but it is a seed-pod, bursting from a high canopy and drifting on the faint thermal currents, its membrane wings catching the golden light.

Scattered among the roots and upon the ledges are the built things. They are not structures, but remnants. A circle of stones, worn smooth and placed with intent, now half-subsumed by a root that has flowed around them like magma. A single, black basalt plinth, standing alone in a clearing, etched with spirals that seem to mimic the growth patterns of the surrounding vines. These are the faint marks of a people who came before the vegetation won, who perhaps worshipped this slow, verdant conquest. They are fossils of thought, their purpose a quiet mystery in the humming, growing silence.

And it is quiet. The sound is a substratum: the drip of condensation, the almost inaudible creak of expanding cellulose, the soft *thrum* of a giant spore sac releasing its payload into the still air. There is no wind, no weather beyond the slow, internal weeping of the walls. This is a place in perfect, heated equilibrium. It has been growing towards this state of dense, illuminated saturation for eons, and it will continue for eons more. Nothing changes quickly. A new frond unfurls over the course of a century. A pool evaporates over a millennium. You are a brief, warm anomaly in its deep, verdant time.
