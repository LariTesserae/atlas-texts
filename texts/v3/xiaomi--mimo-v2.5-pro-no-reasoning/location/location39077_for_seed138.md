The air is thick, wet, and hot. It is not the clean humidity of a rainforest, but a heavy, saturated blanket that smells of damp earth, rust, and ozone. It presses against you, making every breath a conscious effort. This is the Fumarole District, a place caught in a state of perpetual, hazy stillness.

You stand in a narrow yard, hemmed in on three sides by the towering, windowless brick walls of factories. The walls are dark and slick with a constant, weeping condensation that trickles down in black rivulets, staining the mortar. They rise so high that the sky is reduced to a pale, overexposed slit, a source of diffused, shadowless light that does little to penetrate the gloom below. The light is the color of old pewter.

The ground beneath your feet is not solid. It is a quagmire of black, industrial sludge and sluggish water, the surface broken by crude metal grates and the stumps of rusted pipes that protrude like broken teeth. The water is warm, heated from below by unseen geothermal vents and the waste heat of the machinery that groans and chugs ceaselessly within the buildings. A low, percussive *thump-thump-thump* is the district's heartbeat, a vibration felt more in the bones than heard with the ears. It is joined by the high, thin shriek of steam being vented and the grating, rhythmic squeal of immense, poorly-lubricated gears turning somewhere in the dark.

Vegetation is a desperate afterthought. Tough, dark-green algae and liverworts coat every surface that isn't actively scoured by steam or acid rain, forming a slick, living skin on the bricks and metal. In the wider cracks of the foundations, a few stunted, pale reeds push up through the mud, their roots tangled in discarded bits of wire and slag.

The settlement is dense but claustrophobically small, a maze of interconnected workshops, worker hovels wedged into recesses, and covered walkways slung between the factory blocks. The architecture is purely functional—rusted iron beams, stained brick, corrugated metal sheeting bolted over openings. There is no ornamentation, only the layered evidence of constant, desperate repair. It is a place built for a single purpose, and everything else is a casualty of that purpose.

This is not a dead place, however. It is teeming with a grimy, resilient life. Pale, eyeless salamanders, some as long as your arm, slip through the warm, murky pools, their skin translucent in the gloom. Thick-shelled, black beetles, each the size of a coin, scuttle in dense swarms over the grates, feeding on the oily grime that drips from above. Plump, dark-robed moths flutter in the rafters, their wings caked with soot, drawn to the dim, flickering glow of the few work-lanterns that hang from hooks.

Nothing here changes. The rhythm of the pistons is eternal. The dripping water, the swarming beetles, the oppressive heat—it is a system in perfect, stagnant equilibrium. The young geology of the land—sharp-edged basalt and unweathered clay—has been swiftly and completely subsumed by the industry it hosts. It is a single, unending moment of hot, wet, loud, and living machinery, trapped in the twilight between its own walls.
