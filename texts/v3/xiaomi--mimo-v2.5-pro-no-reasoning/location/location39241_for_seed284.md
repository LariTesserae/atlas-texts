# The Notch

You would not find it from below. From the plains — if plains still exist beneath the permanent overcast — the mountain presents a single unbroken wall of basalt, featureless grey rising into a ceiling of grey. But three thousand meters above any reasonable summit line, where the rock narrows into a spire that should not still be standing, there is a wound.

The Notch is a lateral fracture, a notch cut into the spire's throat by some ancient lateral force — not erosion, which has had no time here, but impact or tectonic tearing. The opening is perhaps two hundred meters wide and maybe sixty tall at its deepest point, pinching toward the back into a tangle of dead-end fissures. Inside, the walls angle inward like the jaws of something mineral and patient. The rock is fresh where it was broken: sharp-edged, glassy in places where friction melted it, still faintly warm in certain seams despite the surrounding cold. No lichen. No soil. The floor is a tilted plane of fractured basalt rubble, ankle- to chest-deep, with no two pieces the same size.

It is cool — not the killing cold of true altitude, because the rock holds residual geothermal warmth, and because the Notch faces roughly south-southeast in whatever orientation this world maintains. But the air is thin and dry, and your breath would plume if you could see it. You mostly cannot see it. Light reaches the Notch for perhaps four hours in whatever passes for midday, and even then it is indirect, bounced off a higher ridge before it enters. The rest of the time the pocket lives in a perpetual dusk that never quite becomes dark, because of the fires.

---

The construction climbs both walls.

It is dense, layered, improvised, and old — or old enough that the earliest structures are buried beneath later ones, and those beneath later ones still. Everything is terraced into the basalt face: rooms and alcoves and storage hollows hacked out of the rock itself, their entrance frames reinforced with timber so dark it might be petrified or merely soaked in creosote. Mortar of crushedite — crushed rubble mixed with a dark, pungent resin that seems to come from somewhere deeper in the fissure system — holds the stonework together. The walls are stained with soot and chemical residue in vertical streaks that follow every drip-line and condensation path.

Catwalks of blackened plank and twisted wire span the gap between the two walls at irregular intervals, maybe a dozen of them stacked vertically. Some are wide enough for two people abreast. Others are single boards with a hand-rope. They tremble constantly, not from wind — though wind does find its way in — but from vibration.

The vibration is the industry.

Near the floor of the Notch, where the rubble field gives way to a more consolidated surface, there are machines. The word feels insufficient. There are *engines*: compact, brutal, purpose-built assemblies of iron and brass and blackened steel, bolted into the rock or mounted on wooden platforms that creak under their own torque. They drive pumps and compressors and things whose function is not immediately clear — rotating drums, reciprocating arms, belts that run up the walls and disappear into dark holes. Some of them burn something. The fuel smell is sharp and hydrocarbon-thin, not coal, not wood, something processed. The exhaust drifts upward through the Notch and out the top in a faint, constant plume.

A foundry occupies a natural alcove on the eastern wall. The glow of it is the steadiest light source in the pocket — a dull, throbbing orange that throws long shadows across the opposite wall and makes the catwalks lurch in and out of visibility. When someone opens the furnace door to work the metal, the light jumps and the shadows of every structure in the Notch leap and resettle. Otherwise the illumination is a dim, cool blue-grey from whatever skylight penetrates the opening, supplemented by a handful of oil lamps and the occasional sputtering arc-light wired into the generator circuit. It is never bright here. Even at its brightest, the light has a drowned quality, as if it has traveled through water rather than air.

---

The air is dry and thin and tastes ofite — metallic, silicate, faintly sulfurous from the foundry. There is almost no humidity. The walls weep in certain spots, but it is condensation, not seepage — the rock is warm in places from geothermal gradient and from the waste heat of the engines, and where that warmth meets the cool air at altitude, thin films of moisture appear on the stone and drip into catch-basins. This is the water. There is no stream, no spring, no rain. The catch-basins — ceramic bowls, hollowed stones, sheet-metal troughs — are scattered throughout the construction at every level, and they fill slowly, and they are emptied slowly. Water is measured here in sips.

---

But the fauna.

It is the thing you notice after the initial shock of the built environment subsides — after you have oriented yourself to the verticality, the dimness, the industrial hum. You notice the birds.

They are everywhere. Not songbirds, not raptors — though something hawk-shaped does circle the opening far above. These are smaller: finch-sized or thrush-sized, with dull plumage in greys and browns and the occasional dull russet, and they live in the gaps. Every cavity between every stone, every pipe-junction, every hollow behind every wall-panel, every cable-bundle draped across every catwalk — all of it is occupied. They nest in the infrastructure itself, weaving scrap-wire and engine-waste and shredded whatever into cup-shaped nests that cling to the undersides of platforms and the insides of ventilation stacks. When the machines shake the structure, the birds sway with it and do not startle. They have learned the rhythm.

There are perhaps — this is hard to estimate — several thousand individual birds in the Notch. The sound they make is not loud. It is a constant, low, burbling murmur, like a brook heard through a wall, layered over the mechanical hum. It is the one thing that softens the place. Without the birds, the Notch would be all iron and basalt and vibration and the lonely creak of wire. With them, it breathes.

Insects too. Small, wingless, dark — beetles or beetle-like things that live in the rubble field at the base and swarm the foundry waste-heat in the evenings. They are food for the birds, and for something else: there are lizards here, or lizard-adjacent creatures, sinuous and mottled grey, that cling to the vertical walls and hunt in the seams between construction and stone. They move in sudden darts and then hold motionless for minutes. They are very difficult to see until they move.

And in the deepest fissures — the ones at the back of the Notch where the walls nearly touch and the construction tapers into raw, broken rock — there are larger things. You hear them at night, or during the brief intervals when the engines cycle down for maintenance: a scraping, a shuffling, a breathing that is too large for any lizard. The residents of the Notch do not speak about them clearly. They hang sheets of tin across the mouths of the deepest fissures and weight them with stones. The sheets rattle sometimes when nothing apparent is passing through.

---

The turbulence.

The Notch is not stable. Not in any sense.

Geologically, the spire is young — catastrophically young, whatever tore this opening did so recently in the terms of mountains, and the rock has not finished settling. Shifts happen. Not earthquakes, exactly, but shudders: a sudden lurch of the whole pocket, accompanied by a groan from deep in the stone, and then a rain of grit from the ceiling that coats everything and everyone and takes an hour to settle. The construction is built to absorb this. The catwalks flex. The terraced rooms have stone-on-stone foundations without mortar at key joints, so they can shift and resettle. The engines are bolted to platforms that ride on packed rubble, acting as crude shock-absorbers. But things break. A wall-panel cracks. A catwalk cable snaps and a section of walkway drops six inches and hangs crooked until someone fixes it. The residents fix things constantly. It is most of what they do.

The atmosphere inside the Notch is turbulent too. The opening creates a venturi effect — air accelerates as it passes through, and the temperature differential between the warm rock and the cool high-altitude air generates convection cells that reverse direction unpredictably. A draft that was rising an hour ago is now falling. Smoke from the foundry, exhaust from the engines, dust from the rubble — all of it sloshes back and forth through the pocket in slow, unpredictable surges. Visibility shifts minute to minute. A catwalk that was clear is suddenly choked with fumes. A room that was stuffy is suddenly swept clean by a downdraft so cold it raises gooseflesh.

And the social turbulence — because there are people, and they are crowded, and resources are measured in sips of water and hours of engine-time and inches of living space. The Notch is urban in the way a submarine is urban: every square meter has a purpose, every person is known, every dispute is everyone's business. Voices carry in the pocket. Arguments echo off the basalt and arrive at every level simultaneously. The engines cover some of it, and the birds cover more, but when both fall quiet — during a maintenance cycle in the predawn hours, when the birds are roosted and still — you can hear every conversation in the Notch at once, layered and overlapping, a dense murmur of negotiation and complaint and the occasional sharp bark of laughter.

---

What are they making? The foundry produces parts. The compressors produce something — a gas, stored in heavy cylinders that are hoisted to the opening by a hand-cranked cable winch and, it is rumored, sold to traders who approach the spire from the north face on routes the Notch residents do not discuss. The pumps pull moisture from the air — a losing proposition at this altitude and humidity, but every drop counts, and the condensation lines are the most carefully maintained infrastructure in the pocket. The reciprocating arms do something to something. The details are not offered to outsiders, and there are no outsiders here long enough to ask twice.

---

At night — during the long dim-to-dark stretch when no light enters and the foundry glow dominates — the Notch becomes something else. The shadows are absolute except where the furnace pulses. The birds are silent. The lizards are still. The deep-fissure things are audible. The engines run on, because they must, because stopping them for more than a few hours invites structural settling that may not reverse. The vibration becomes the pulse of the place. You feel it in your molars, in the balls of your feet, in the base of your skull.

It is, despite everything, a home. People sleep in shifts. Children — there are children — play in the rubble field during the hours of indirect light, climbing the sharp basalt chunks with the same careless sureness as the lizards. Someone has strung a wire between two terraces and hung objects from it — metal scraps, polished stones, a strip of colored fabric that has faded to near-grey — and when the updraft catches it, it chimes faintly, a music so quiet you would miss it if you were not listening for it.

The Notch persists. The mountain is patient. The machines keep running. The birds keep singing their low, waterlike song. Something in the deep fissures breathes, and the tin sheets rattle, and no one lifts them to look.
