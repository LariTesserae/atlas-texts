The air is cool and perpetually damp, carrying the scent of wet earth, moss, and the faint, metallic tang of old iron. You are standing in what was once a narrow street, but now it is a valley of soft, green darkness. The sky is a distant, pale suggestion seen through a dense canopy of what looks like, at first glance, immense, ancient trees.

Look closer. The trees are not trees. The "bark" is a layered, fibrous pulp—a living substrate. The "trunks" are towering, hollowed columns of this same material, some as wide as a cottage, riddled with irregular doorways and window-like openings that are just darker patches of growth. These are the town's buildings, fully consumed and repurposed by the vegetation. The original brick, stone, and wood are entirely gone, digested into the plant's own structure. From the "branches"—thick, woody vines that have fused together—hang heavy, bell-shaped gourds and pitcher-plant analogues that glisten with a clear, sweet-smelling fluid. These are the cisterns and lanterns, collecting the intermittent drizzle and housing bioluminescent fungi that pulse with a faint, green-white light.

This is a place in perfect, slow-motion battle with itself. The vegetation's growth is the dominant force, a process of relentless, quiet occupation. But the industrial past is not gone; it has been digested and incorporated. Pipes, now choked with root-tendrils, still run through the fibrous walls, not carrying steam or water, but serving as the plant's own vascular system, humming faintly with the movement of sap. In the central square—a small clearing where the canopy thins—the great mill wheel, a wheel of corroded iron the size of a house, turns once every few hours. It is not powered by a river, but by the immense, slow pressure of a taproot growing through its gears. Its purpose is unknown; its creaking, a deep, resonant groan, is one of the few sounds that pierces the quiet. It is a heart, or perhaps a memory.

The silence is profound, broken only by the drip of water from leaf to leaf, the occasional low hum from the pipes, and the rare, startling *click* or *buzz* of some unseen insect-kin. Fauna is scarce. You might see a glimpse of something like a large, translucent salamander in a gutter that now holds a permanent stream, or hear the flutter of a bat-like creature in the upper canopy. The ecosystem is almost entirely plant. It is active in its stillness—growing, consuming, fermenting. The gourds occasionally burst, releasing a brief shower of spores or sticky nectar. The ground is a soft carpet of moss and fungal mycelium that breaks down anything that falls. Nothing is wasted. The place has reached an equilibrium, a state of being that has been established for longer than anyone can remember. It is not hostile; it is simply complete, a closed system where the old world has become the soil for a new, patient, and all-encompassing kingdom.
