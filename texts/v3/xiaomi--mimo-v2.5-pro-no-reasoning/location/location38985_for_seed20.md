The world is a throat.

Begin with the sound. It is not a sound you hear with your ears so much as with your entire skeleton. It is a wall of vibration, a constant, deafening roar that is the baseline for all existence. Imagine standing inside a colossal waterfall that has no top or bottom, a torrent of noise that is also the substance of the air. It is the sound of pressure, of water atomizing against itself, of a billion tons of vapor screaming through a space too small for it. This is not rain; it is a suspension of atmosphere and ocean, a perpetual vertical flood where "up" and "down" are merely suggestions the water hasn't yet chosen to ignore.

You are standing on a pinnacle. Or rather, you are clinging to one. The ground beneath your hands is not earth but a newly-formed, obsidian-like substance, slick and sharp. It is geology caught in the act of screaming. Jagged spires and fins of this black, glassy rock erupt from the vertical chaos, the only things that resist the constant, grinding erosion. This is a high place, a peak in a range that was never meant to be exposed to air, now thrust into this churning atmosphere. The rock is young; its edges are still sharp enough to draw blood, its surfaces unweathered by anything but this deafening assault.

Through the roar and the mist, you see other pinnacles. They are scattered, lonely teeth in a grey, formless jaw. Between them, the world is a solid, shifting medium of white water and vapor. You are in a cavern of liquid, a grotto whose ceiling and walls are made of falling water. The scale is that of a cathedral, but one built by a madman obsessed with chaos. The "floor" is a thousand feet below, a churning, unseen basin. The "walls" are curtains of spray that shift and reform. The air itself is so saturated that breathing is like drinking.

Light is a rumor here, a pale, diffuse glow that emanates from nowhere and everywhere, filtered through miles of turbulent water-vapor. It is the dim, grey light of the deep sea or the inside of a cloud, but here it is constant. There is no day or night, only degrees of the same spectral gloom.

Life is an afterthought, a desperate whisper in the hurricane. On the highest, most sheltered ledges of the black rock, clinging to cracks, are sparse, leathery patches of something that might be a lichen or a fungal membrane. It is a dull, sickly yellow, and it seems to absorb the sound rather than be nourished by the faint light. It is the only vegetation. Fauna is rarer still—a glimpse of something small, six-limbed, and eyeless, scuttling into a crack as your shadow passes over it. It moves with a frantic, jerky speed, as if the world's vibration is its own heartbeat.

And then the world turns.

This is the rule of this place: instability is its only constant. A shift in the roaring pressure, a change in the pitch of the scream, and a section of a neighboring spire simply *unmakes* itself. It doesn't crumble; it dissolves into a shower of black, glittering dust that is instantly swallowed by the torrent. In another moment, a gout of molten, orange-white light erupts from a fissure you hadn't seen, super-heating the water around it into a sudden, violent cloud of steam before subsiding. The rock is being born and dying in the same moment. Creation and destruction are a single, deafening, turbulent act.

Scattered on the wider ledges, half-filled with water, are the artifacts. They are not built, but found. Smooth, ovoid stones that are too perfectly round. A single, massive vertebra of some impossible creature, fossilized into the glassy rock. A lattice of a darker, harder mineral that seems to form a pattern, a net or a web, frozen in its moment of growth. They are not tools. They are questions the rock is asking itself.

You realize the weirdness is not in the violence or the noise, but in the feeling that the world is *paying attention*. The roar is not impersonal; it has cadences, rhythms, moments of focus. The turbulence is not random; it swirls around the pinnacles, eddies in the ledges, as if feeling them out. The occasional, sudden stillness that falls for a heartbeat is not peace, but a held breath. This is a world in the midst of a psychotic episode, a reality that is breaking down and re-forming itself around you, and you are a tiny, fragile anomaly in its fever dream. The sound is its voice, and it is trying to describe something for which no quiet language exists.
