# The Quiet Gorge of Wethern

---

You would smell it before you saw anything — a heaviness in the air, mineral and green, like wet stone wrapped in moss and left in a cellar for a century. Then the gorge walls would close around you, and you'd realize you'd already been inside it for some time.

The valley is narrow. Not a canyon — too young for that, the rock still showing where it split. The walls rise maybe forty meters on each side, dark basalt streaked with veins of something paler, calcium maybe, running in threads like the rock is holding something in its blood. The surfaces are rough, still sharp in places where the fracture happened recently enough that no weathering has softened the edges. Water weeps from dozens of places — not streams, not waterfalls, just *weeping*, a slow and constant condensation that makes the stone glisten without ever quite becoming flow. The air is heavy with moisture that doesn't fall so much as *arrive* and settle on everything.

The gorge runs roughly north-south for perhaps a kilometer. That's the whole place. Everything important happens within this corridor.

---

## The Growth



The vegetation here is extraordinary in its thickness but peculiar in its distribution. It does not blanket the gorge floor evenly. Instead it erupts — sudden explosions of green in pockets and alcoves where water has gathered into shallow basins in the rock, or where fallen stone has created sheltered depressions. In these pockets, growth is ferocious. Ferns the size of tables. Moss in layers so deep you could lose a hand in it. Epiphytes on top of epiphytes, orchids growing from lichen growing from bark growing from a dead fern frond that is itself becoming soil. The color is almost too saturated — a green so dark it borders on black in the dim light, and here and there a shock of pale flower or a frond tipped in rust-red.

But between these oases of growth: bare rock. Dark, damp, open. You could walk from one green pocket to the next across stretches of naked basalt, maybe ten or fifteen meters, feeling the stone slick underfoot and seeing nothing alive except—

Except you're never seeing nothing alive. That's the thing about this place. That's the thing you don't understand at first.

---

## The Settlement



Someone lives here. Lived here. Lives here still, probably, though you might walk right through the settlement and think you'd found only strange rock formations.

The structures are small — the largest might shelter four or five people if they were friendly — and built directly against the gorge walls, tucked into alcoves and overhangs where the basalt recedes. Stone stacked and fitted without mortar, the gaps stuffed with moss and mud. Roofs of layered fern fronds over crude timber frames, the wood dark with age and moisture. You'd call them huts if they were freestanding, but they're more like rooms carved into a conversation with the cliff face, half-built, half-found.

There are perhaps twenty-five of these structures, distributed along both walls of the gorge in no pattern you can immediately discern. No road connects them. No bridge crosses the gorge floor. Each dwelling sits at its own elevation, some just above the gorge floor, others ten or fifteen meters up where a natural shelf offers footing, reached by handholds carved into the rock — or perhaps just *worn* into the rock, which would take a very long time.

Between the structures, at intervals that feel too deliberate to be random: stone basins. Rectangular, shallow, each about the size of a bathtub. Some are cracked. Some still hold water, dark and still, with a film of something living on the surface that might be algae or might be something else entirely. Others have been colonized by fern growth so dense that the basin is invisible beneath it. These were once infrastructure — water collection, perhaps, or washing, or something for which you have no reference. Now they are features of the landscape, indistinguishable from the gorge itself except for their edges, which are too straight.

The people who built this — if they're still here — are not visible. The structures show no obvious signs of recent habitation but also no obvious signs of abandonment. A stone tool sits on a ledge. A bundle of dried fern stalks leans against a wall. You cannot tell if these were set down yesterday or a decade ago.

---

## The Fauna



Here is where the gorge becomes something you cannot explain.

The place is *crawling* with life. Not metaphorically. Literally. You look at the bare basalt between the green pockets and you think: empty. Sterile. But then your eyes adjust, and you see that the rock surface has texture where it shouldn't — and the texture is *moving*.

Slugs. Hundreds of species of slugs, or what you're calling slugs because you have no better word. They range from specimens the size of your thumbnail, nearly translucent, to others as long as your forearm, banded in colors that shouldn't exist in a place this dim — vivid orange, a blue so deep it's nearly ultraviolet. They cover every surface. They hang from the undersides of ledges on threads of mucus. They move in slow, coordinated rivers along the gorge floor, all flowing in the same direction, though the direction seems to change depending on the time of day.

But slugs are only the most visible layer.

Lizards — small, dark, with oversized eyes — cling to the walls in such numbers that from a distance the stone appears to have scales. They are shockingly still. You can stare directly at one for thirty seconds before it flicks a tongue and you realize it was alive. They eat the slugs. Or some of them eat the slugs. Others seem to eat the lichen, or the moss, or each other. Their mouths are wrong — too wide, with an extra hinge that lets them gape past what should be anatomically possible.

Above the lizards: spiders. Not the wandering kind. Orb-weavers, dozens of species, building webs between the gorge walls, between fern fronds, between the stone dwellings themselves. The webs are architectural. Some span the entire width of the gorge — three, four meters — and they are *maintained*, repaired in patterns that suggest more than instinct. By noon (though there is no real noon here, only a slight lightening of the dimness) the webs are thick with condensation, hung with droplets like a curtain of glass beads, and the gorge looks draped in jewelry.

And then there are the things you can't categorize.

On certain rock faces, particularly where the weeping water is heaviest, there are clusters of what initially resemble fungal fruiting bodies — pale, stalked, each one about the size of a closed fist. But they sway. Not in the breeze — there is no breeze in the gorge — but independently, as though reaching for something, or tasting the air. When you look very closely, each one has a seam along its underside that opens and closes rhythmically, like a mouth, or a gill. They are rooted to the stone. They do not move from their place. But they are not plants and they are not fungi and the local people — if there are still local people — apparently classified them as animals, because when you find their bone-and-stone tool kits, one of the recurring tools is a flat blade specifically shaped for prying these things off the rock. Presumably for eating. Presumably.

The cumulative effect of all this life is overwhelming. The gorge has a biomass density that seems physically impossible for a place this cold, this dim, this high. But the moisture is constant and the nutrient cycling is ferocious — everything here is eating something else, and everything is being eaten, and the whole system runs on a kind of voracious quiet that is more disturbing than any jungle cacophony.

---

## The Silence

Because nothing makes sound.

This is the thing. This is the detail that makes your skin tighten after you've been in the gorge for twenty minutes and you finally understand what's wrong.

The slugs don't rustle. The lizards don't click or chirp. The spiders are silent in their webwork. The fungal animals pulse without a whisper. The ferns don't rustle because there is no wind to move them. The water weeps without dripping — it runs in thin films over the stone without ever forming drops heavy enough to fall audibly.

There are no birds. This is perhaps the most immediately obvious absence, and once you notice it, you cannot un-notice it. A gorge this lush, this wet, this full of invertebrate prey, should be *screaming* with bird life. There is nothing. No nesting in the ledges. No feathers caught in the spiderwebs. No shadow of wings overhead. Whatever has driven birds from this place — or kept them from ever arriving — is one of the gorge's many unanswered questions.

The sound you are left with is almost nothing. Your own breathing. Your footsteps on wet stone — and even these are muffled, the basalt absorbing impact rather than reflecting it. If you hold still and stop breathing, you can hear your own heartbeat, and beneath that, or perhaps imagined: a low, subsonic hum that might be the gorge itself, or might be the combined vibration of ten thousand small creatures living their lives just below the threshold of hearing.

The people who lived here — you begin to understand why they built in stone without mortar. Why their tools are all edge and no handle. Everything they made was designed to make as little noise as possible.

You don't know why yet. But you're starting to feel it.

---

## The Activity

Despite the silence, nothing here is still.

The slugs move in their ceaseless rivers, and their trails glisten on every surface — silver highways of dried mucus that map the gorge's traffic patterns in overlapping hierarchies. You can read hours of movement in these trails. The lizards shift — slowly, always slowly — from shadow to shadow, but if you watch long enough you notice they're rotating through a circuit, visiting the same ledges in the same order, and you wonder what they're checking. The orb-weavers dismantle and rebuild their major webs on a cycle that seems to track the lightening and darkening of the sky, which itself tracks a cloud cover that moves through the gorge like breath.

The water cycles are active too. The condensation weeps, gathers in the stone basins, overflows in trickles that find their way to the gorge floor where they disappear into cracks — not flowing south to an outlet but dropping, sinking into the basalt, feeding some aquifer or geothermal system that keeps the gorge's temperature a steady few degrees above what the elevation would predict. Occasionally — every few hours, you think, though time is hard to track here — a thicker mist rolls through the gorge from the north end, so dense you can't see the far wall, and everything dampens further, and the fungal animals open their gill-seams wide, and the slugs accelerate, and then the mist passes and the dimness returns.

Decomposition is rapid. A fern frond that falls is colonized by fungal threads within hours. A dead slug is a vanished slug by the following day — consumed by lizards, by other slugs, by things in the soil that you'd rather not think about. The gorge recycles everything. Nothing lingers. The stone basins that aren't full of water are full of rich, black soil that must be the accumulated compost of centuries, and the ferns that grow from this soil are the largest in the gorge, and when their fronds fall, the cycle begins again.

Something is *happening* here. Multiple somethings, layered and interlocking, running on their own timescales. You are standing in a machine that was already ancient before anyone stacked the first stone against the wall.

---

## The Rules

You begin to sense, without being told, that the gorge operates by rules.

**Do not make sudden sounds.** You don't know where this rule comes from. Perhaps from the architecture of the dwellings, which all face inward, toward the stone, away from the gorge's open center. Perhaps from the tool design, the wrapped handles, the careful edges. Perhaps from the fact that every living thing here moves so slowly and so deliberately, as though speed and noise are the same transgression. When you accidentally kick a stone and it clatters against the basalt — the sound is obscenely loud in the silence, and for a moment every lizard on the nearest wall turns its head in your direction, and the slug rivers pause, and the fungal animals close their seams all at once — and then, after a moment that lasts too long, everything resumes. But you can feel that you've been noted.

**Stay on the stone.** The green pockets, the pockets of thick growth — you notice that none of the pathways between dwellings pass through them. The handholds in the rock climb around them. The stone basins sit at their edges, not within them. The growth is not hostile exactly, but it is *full*, packed with layer upon layer of creature and root and rot, and you get the sense that if you stepped into it, the gorge would absorb you as efficiently as it absorbs everything else.

**The gorge is not abandoned.** The tools are too carefully placed. The basins still collect water. The webs that cross between dwellings are maintained by the spiders, yes, but someone has hung small bundles of dried vegetation from the anchor points — offerings? Lures? Fuel? The gorge is tended. You are simply too large and too loud to see the tenders.

---

## What You Carry Out

You will leave this place eventually — up the gorge to the north, where the walls lower and the basalt gives way to soil and the first real trees appear, stunted alders bent by wind you'd forgotten existed. You will step out of the gorge's silence into the ordinary sounds of a mountain slope and you will stand there, blinking in the comparative brightness, and you will realize that your clothes are filmed with slug trails and your skin is covered in tiny bites from things you never felt.

You will try to describe it. You will fail. The words that come closest are: *a room full of animals, being very quiet, about to do something.*
