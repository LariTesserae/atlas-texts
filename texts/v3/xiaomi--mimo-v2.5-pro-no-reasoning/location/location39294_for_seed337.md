# The Burrowed Towns of Vesskaya

---

You come to it by walking downhill.

Not steeply. The land gives way the way a sleeping animal's ribs give way beneath your hand — slowly, with the sense that something alive is underneath and has been here longer than the slope itself. The path is packed earth, clay-dark and firm, worn into a shallow trough by centuries of feet. On either side, the ground rises in low flanks of raw stone and soil, still striated, still showing the muscle-lines of whatever upheaval pushed this land into its current shape. The geology here is young. Not young in the way a riverbank is young — washed clean and rebuilt each season — but young the way a bone is young when it first breaks the skin. Something is still emerging. The stone has no patina. The exposed faces of rock are grey-brown and damp, the colour of a thing that has not yet decided what it wants to be.

You descend.

The flanks rise. The path narrows. Within thirty paces, the walls of the cut are shoulder-height, then head-height, and you realize you are walking through a gorge — not carved by water, not sliced by tectonic force, but simply *uncovered*, as if the earth cracked open recently enough that moss hasn't had time to find the walls. Moisture beads on the stone. Not flowing — just *present*, the way humidity is present, a condensation that clings to every surface and makes the rock look almost oiled. Occasionally a bead gathers enough mass to slide downward, leaving a darker streak, and the quiet around you is full of the tiny sounds this makes: the microscopic ticking of water finding its way to the gorge floor, pooling in shallow depressions, soaking back into the clay.

The gorge widens into something else.

---

The first thing you notice is the smell — mineral, wet, faintly metallic. Iron, maybe. Or something older than iron.

The second thing you notice is how much *there is* here.

The gorge opens into a basin, and the basin is full. Not of wilderness, not of growth, but of *structure*. Buildings. Hundreds of them, packed shoulder to shoulder, rising up the basin walls in terraced tiers, clinging to ledges, stepping over one another in a density that seems almost geological — like the settlement grew the way crystals grow, filling every available surface, leaving no gap, no vacancy, no wasted stone. The architecture is simple and severe: walls of undressed rock mortared with clay, roofs of layered slate, doorways low and square-cut, windows narrow and deep. There is no ornament. There is no paint. The buildings are the same colour as the basin walls because they are *made* from the basin walls — quarried from the same young stone, still weeping moisture, still settling, still cooling from whatever formation process birthed them.

And yet — the basin has light.

The gorge you came through was dim, but here the space opens upward. The basin is not a cave. It is a hollow, a depression, a great scooped-out bowl in the landscape, and its walls rise only forty or fifty feet before giving way to open sky. The sky above is a pale, cool grey — overcast, perpetually, in the way of places where moisture hangs in the air and the sun is a brighter patch rather than a disc. But the light that reaches the basin floor is sufficient. It is daylight. It is ordinary. It falls on the packed rooftops and the narrow lanes between them and it makes the wet stone gleam.

This is a town. Or a small city. The population is hard to judge because the space is so dense, but there are clearly hundreds of structures, and the terracing suggests layers of habitation — some buildings built on top of others, some carved *into* the basin wall itself, their back rooms opening directly into raw stone. The density is extraordinary. There is almost no open ground. Every surface has been built upon or built into. The few paths between structures are barely wide enough for two people to pass, and they twist and climb and descend according to the logic of the terrain rather than any planning grid. This was not designed. This was *grown* — organically, incrementally, each new building finding whatever niche remained.

---

The people here — and there are people, you can hear them now if you listen carefully, though "hear" may be too strong a word — are quiet.

The town is not silent. There is sound. But it is the kind of sound that happens when a large number of people have collectively agreed to make very little of it. Footsteps on stone. The soft percussion of someone working with a hand tool — a chisel, maybe, or an awl — tapping rhythmically against something in a ground-floor workshop. The whisper of conversation from an open doorway, voices low, words indistinct. A child's footfall, quick and light, running across a slate roof. Water — always water — dripping somewhere, trickling through channels cut into the rock, gathered in cisterns that are sunk into the ground at irregular intervals, their surfaces dark and still.

The technology is simple. You can see it in the tools left outside doorways: hammers with stone heads hafted in wood, adzes, hand-drills, wedges. Metal exists but it is rare and expensive — a few iron blades, copper fittings on doors, small copper vessels for carrying water. The real material here is stone. Everything is stone. The people are stone-masons and stone-carvers, and the entire town is a testament to what can be done with patience and a chisel and a landscape that offers raw material at every turn. There are no wheels that you can see. No pulleys. No machines. There are ladders, ropes, hand-carved stairs. There are leather straps and woven baskets. There is clay, shaped and dried and used for everything from mortar to cookware.

And the town is *active*. Not bustling — not loud or frantic — but continuously in motion. People move through the lanes with purpose. You see a woman descending a staircase carved into the basin wall, a bundle of something wrapped in cloth balanced on her head. You see two men in a doorway, one holding a piece of slate while the other marks it with a pointed tool — measuring, planning, recording. Somewhere below you, in the lower tiers where the basin floor is flattest, a group of people are working together to haul something heavy — a stone block, perhaps — on a sled of wood and leather, pulling it across the packed clay with ropes. Their movements are coordinated and silent. No one shouts. No one calls. They communicate through gesture and the briefest murmurs.

The fauna is sparse. A few birds — small, dark-feathered, ground-dwelling — move between the rooftops, picking at moss or insects. There is an occasional lizard on a sun-facing wall, though "sun-facing" is generous given the overcast. In the cisterns, you might catch a glimpse of something moving beneath the water's surface — a salamander, perhaps, or something shaped like one. But there are no dogs. No livestock. No beasts of burden. The people do the hauling themselves. The animals here are incidental, unbothered, living their own small lives in the margins of the human settlement.

---

The geology is what holds your attention, once the initial shock of the town's density begins to settle.

The stone is *alive*. Not literally — this is not a weird place, not a place of magic or strangeness — but the stone is doing things. It is still forming. You can see it in the way mineral deposits gather on the basin walls: slow accretions of calcium and iron, building outward in thin, ruffled shelves, like the world's slowest-growing shelf fungus. You can see it in the way new cracks appear — not violently, not with noise, but as quiet openings, hairline fractures that widen over weeks and months, and the people here have adapted to this: they build around the cracks, incorporate them, treat them as features rather than flaws. Some buildings have cracks running through their walls that have been mortared and remortared multiple times, the layers of clay like growth rings in a tree.

There is a sense that the land is still settling into itself. The basin may be new — geologically speaking, it may be a sinkhole that opened within living memory, or within the memory of grandparents, or within the memory of the stories that grandparents told. The people here have been here long enough to have built this extraordinary density of habitation, but not so long that the land beneath them has stopped moving. They live on something that is still becoming.

And they seem to understand this. The way they build suggests an acceptance of impermanence — not despair, not fatalism, but a practical acknowledgement that the stone will shift, the walls will crack, the water will find new paths, and the town will have to adapt. Buildings are repaired constantly. The sound of chisel on stone is one of the quiet background rhythms of the place, as persistent and unremarkable as the dripping water. When a wall falls, the stones are salvaged and used again. When a new fissure opens, someone carves a channel for the water that will inevitably find its way in.

---

The air is cool — not cold, not harsh, but the kind of cool that settles into stone and doesn't leave. In the lower tiers, where the basin floor is damp and the cisterns are densest, the temperature drops further, and the air smells more strongly of mineral and wet clay. In the upper tiers, closer to the rim, there is a faint breeze, and the air is thinner, and you can see more of the sky. The vegetation is sparse: patches of pale moss on walls that face the light, a few low-growing plants in cracks where soil has accumulated, and in the very uppermost tiers, where the rim opens and the land above slopes away, a scattering of hardy grasses and small shrubs. Nothing tall. Nothing dense. The landscape above the basin is open — rolling, scrubby, grey-green — and the basin itself is a sink in that openness, a place where everything converges: the water, the people, the stone.

The light fades and returns in cycles that match the overcast — brightening when the cloud thins, dimming when it thickens, but never truly dark, never truly bright. At night — and the nights here must be profound, the darkness of a stone bowl with no artificial light beyond whatever small fires the people maintain — the sound would contract to its smallest: water, water, water, and the occasional settling of stone, and the breathing of a town asleep.

But now it is day, or what passes for day, and the town works. Quietly. Deliberately. A place that knows what it is and what it is built on and does not complain about either.
