# The Respiration Plains

## First Approach: From Above

If you could see it from a great height — and there is no great height here, only the plateau itself, which is already higher than most things — you would see what looks like a skin stretched over the bones of a world. Not green. More the color of dried blood and copper, ochre where the sun has killed the uppermost layers, a dull maroon where the living tissue still reaches. It goes on. It goes on in every direction long enough that the curvature of the planet becomes the only thing that ends it.

You might mistake it for geology at first. A vast rust-colored plain, cracked and furrowed, radiating heat. But the cracks are not mineral. They are *wounds*, and they heal slowly, and as you watch — if you could watch from that height — you would see the edges creeping together at the speed of a fingernail's growth.

## The Substrate

There is no soil here. There is no stone, not anymore. The plateau was once basalt — an ancient highland, uplifted and eroded over geological ages into its present state of vast, gentle undulation. But the vegetation ate it. Not metaphorically. Over millions of years, a single clonal organism — or what we would call a single organism, though it has no name in any language spoken by the scattered people who live upon it — sent its root-analogues into the basalt, cracked it with hydraulic pressure, fed on the mineral content, digested the iron and silica and calcium, and replaced the rock with itself.

What you walk on, if you walk here, is a mat. It is between one and four meters thick in most places, composed of interlocking fibrous tissue that resembles no particular plant you could name but carries echoes of rhizome, of mycelium, of vascular bundle, of coral. It is tough. It resists cutting. Where it is exposed at the margins of the cracks, you can see its structure: layered, pale toward the bottom where no light has ever reached, darkening upward through shades of umber to the scorched crust on top. It smells, when cut, of something between turpentine and rotting bread — a fermented, yeasty sweetness that sticks to the back of the throat.

Beneath the mat, in the lowest layers, it is damp. The plateau sits at an elevation where warm air rises, cools intermittently, and condenses against the underside of the living crust, forming a perpetual dew-layer — a few centimeters of moisture that the mat drinks upward through capillary channels. There is no standing water here. No rivers. No lakes. There has been rain perhaps twice in living memory among the people who tend this place, and both times it was catastrophic: the mat swelled, split, and for months afterward great soft wounds wept dark fluid while fauna swarmed the ruptures in numbers that darkened the air. The water is held *within* the organism. The organism *is* the water system. What it doesn't need, it exhales — and this is part of what makes the air so strange here, so humid in pockets and parched in others, so that you can walk twenty paces from a zone of heavy, almost tropical breath into one that sucks the moisture from your lips.

## The Texture of the Surface

The upper crust of the mat is not smooth. It has weathered into formations that mimic erosion but are not erosion — they are growth patterns, architectural decisions made by the organism over centuries. Long ridges, half a meter high, run in roughly parallel lines for kilometers, each one a raised vein of denser tissue where the vascular channels concentrate. Between them, the surface dips into shallow basins where the crust is thinnest, and in these basins you can sometimes feel the warmth rising from below — not geothermal, but metabolic. The mat is alive, and it generates heat, and in the thinnest places the warmth comes through like a breath on the sole of your foot.

There are also the towers. Not structures — not built — but grown. At irregular intervals, spaced kilometers apart across the plain, the mat sends up vertical growths: thick stalks, between three and fifteen meters tall, that taper as they rise and end in dense clusters of photosynthetic tissue. They look, from a distance, like termite mounds or the standing stones of some forgotten people, and the scattered inhabitants of the plateau use them as landmarks, as meeting places, as altars. But they are organs. The organism grows them to reach cleaner air, to photosynthesize more efficiently in the thermal currents that rise from the plateau's surface. Some of them are hollow, and wind passing through them produces a low, resonant hum that you can feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears. Others are solid, and these are the ones the fauna prefer.

## The Fauna

This is the thing that would break you, if the heat and the strangeness and the endless sameness of the horizon did not.

The mat supports an animal population that defies every intuition you have about carrying capacity. The density of life here is staggering, incomprehensible, and it is almost entirely hidden.

Beneath the crust, in the fibrous tunnels and chambers of the mat's interior, there are creatures. Hundreds of species. Thousands. The mat provides structure the way a reef provides structure — not as food, mostly, but as architecture. Creatures burrow through the lower layers, grazing on the dead tissue at the mat's underside. Others patrol the vascular channels, feeding on fluids. Others are predators of the grazers and the fluid-feeders, and predators of those predators, and so on through a food web so complex that the plateau's human inhabitants have been mapping it for generations and have not finished.

What you hear is the sound of this hidden world, transmitted upward through the mat. The fibrous tissue conducts vibration beautifully, and the aggregate noise of billions — perhaps trillions — of small bodies moving, eating, mating, fighting, dying creates a constant subsonic roar that you feel as much as hear. It is deafening. Not in the way a waterfall is deafening, or a storm. It is deafening the way a city is deafening: layered, complex, with rhythms and patterns buried in the chaos. There is a pulsing quality to it, because much of the fauna is synchronized to the mat's own metabolic cycles, which follow a rhythm related to but not identical to the day-night cycle. At certain hours — and you learn to recognize them by the sound before you learn to recognize them by the light — the roar swells, and the surface of the mat trembles visibly, and the air fills with a vibration that makes your vision blur.

Above the surface, the fauna is less dense but more visible, and stranger. There are insects, if you can call them that — six-legged, exoskeletal, but with body plans that suggest they evolved in isolation on this plateau for longer than insects have existed in most other places. Some of them are enormous: a flying creature with a wingspan of nearly a meter, its body covered in dense fur, its mouthparts adapted to pierce the mat's crust and siphon fluid. It flies in slow, heavy circuits between the towers, emitting a droning sound that harmonizes with the towers' own hum. Others are small, nearly invisible, and they swarm in clouds so dense that at certain times of day the air between the towers appears to have texture — like breathing through cloth.

And then there are the larger things. The mat, in its deepest and oldest chambers — places where the tissue has been growing and dying and compressing for millennia — has developed something like a hollow skeletal structure, and in these deep places, creatures live that the plateau's inhabitants speak of in voices they keep carefully flat. Not because the creatures are dangerous. They are not, particularly. But they are old, and they are large, and they move through the interior of the mat the way whales move through the ocean, and occasionally the mat's surface buckles in a slow wave as one of them shifts position far below, and you feel the displacement through your feet, and the sound changes pitch for a moment, and then everything settles again.

## The Inhabitants

There are people here. Few of them. The plateau is vast — the scale of a subcontinent — and the population across its entire extent might number in the low thousands. They live on the surface of the mat, in structures that are less built than *negotiated*: small shelters made from harvested mat-tissue that has been cut, dried, and shaped with simple stone tools, then pressed back into the living surface where it slowly reattaches and resumes growing, incorporating the shelter into itself. Over time, these structures become semi-alive — their walls breathing faintly, their floors warm with shared metabolism. The oldest settlements are indistinguishable from the mat itself, distinguishable only by their shape, by the regularity that betrays intention.

The people are lean, dark-skinned, adapted to the heat and the altitude. They move across the mat in small groups, following the fauna — not hunting it, exactly, but *tending* it, encouraging certain species to concentrate in certain areas, discouraging others, managing the mat's ecology with a sophistication that looks, from the outside, like superstition but operates, on closer inspection, like agriculture. They have no metal. Their tools are stone, bone, and cured mat-tissue. They have no writing, but they carry an oral tradition so layered and precise that it functions as a map, a calendar, a medical manual, and a history of the plateau's biome going back farther than their own species' presence on it.

They sing. Constantly. Not for pleasure — though pleasure is part of it — but because the fauna responds to sound, and specific frequencies and patterns cause specific ecological responses. A certain pitch, sustained, will draw the fluid-siphoning insects away from a section of mat that needs to recover. A complex rhythmic pattern will encourage the burrowing grazers to surface, where their tunnels can be inspected. The people's songs are technologies, and they are transmitted carefully, and the wrong note sung in the wrong place can cause cascading ecological disruption that takes years to repair.

This is why the sound is deafening here: the fauna, the mat, the towers, the wind, and beneath it all, faintly, voices.

## The Weather

The plateau generates its own weather, and it is strange. The mat exhales moisture and heat; the elevation forces the warm air upward; the condensation layer beneath the crust creates a humidity differential with the dry surface air. The result is a persistent system of convection cells — visible as shimmering columns of air above the towers, each one a miniature weather system producing localized downdrafts, microbursts of wind, and occasionally brief, violent rain that falls from the column, strikes the mat, and is absorbed within minutes. From a distance, the plateau looks like it is steaming. Up close, you feel the wind shift unpredictably, carrying pockets of hot dry air and hot wet air in alternation, so that the temperature and humidity lurch from one extreme to another every few minutes.

There are no seasons. There is the day-night cycle, and there is the mat's own metabolic cycle, which runs on a period of roughly thirty-one hours and creates its own version of tides — not of water, but of activity, warmth, sound, and chemical emission. The inhabitants mark time by this cycle. They do not use the sun.

## What It Feels Like to Stand Here

The heat is immediate and immovable. It rises from below and presses down from above and radiates from the towers around you. The air tastes organic — sweet, fermented, faintly metallic. The sound is everywhere, a constant pressure on the eardrums, a vibration in the chest, a hum that becomes silence if you stay long enough, the way a smell becomes undetectable.

Beneath your feet, the mat is warm and slightly yielding, like standing on the back of something sleeping. Every few minutes, a tremor passes through it — something moving below. The towers rise around you, spaced with a regularity that suggests order but is, you are told, emergent — the mat's own decision about optimal spacing for photosynthetic efficiency. Insects pass through the air in loose clouds. The light is normal — unfiltered, unremarkable — but it falls on a surface that absorbs it so completely that shadows seem deeper here than they should, and the horizon, which is very far away, shimmers with heat and moisture in equal measure.

You are standing on a single living thing that extends to the edge of sight and beyond. It is breathing. It is eating rock beneath you and drinking its own exhalations and feeding a world of creatures within its body and singing, through them, a chord that has not stopped for longer than any human memory holds.

The scattered human structures around you are small. Some are half-reabsorbed. In a few generations, the mat will have eaten them entirely, and there will be no sign that anyone was ever here at all, except perhaps a slightly higher density of fauna in the area — drawn, as always, to the warmth of intention.
