The clouds do not part here; they merely thin, becoming a high, humid ceiling of perpetual diffused white, turning the day into a bright, shadowless noon that lasts for months. The air is warm, heavy with moisture that doesn't quite fall as rain but coats every surface in a slick, vital condensation.

There are no mountains of stone, but there is extreme elevation. You are standing on the seventeenth tier of the Grand Terraces of Aethelgard, a structure built of tensile steel and reinforced ceramic that rises nearly three miles into the sky. It is a world of verticality, a series of massive, stacked platforms, each a mile wide, tapering as they ascend. The architecture is industrial but ancient, its purpose settled into its form. The outer faces of these terraces are not smooth but a lattice of buttresses, conduits, and service shafts, weathered to a deep verdigris by the constant moisture. The metal has been here so long it has found its own equilibrium with the climate; it is eroded, pitted, but profoundly stable.

This is the "lushness." Not of soil and root, but of engineered life. Every surface is colonized. Thick, waxy-leafed philodendrons, bred for metal adhesion, cascade down the buttresses. Mosses, in a hundred shades of green and gold, form velvety carpets on walkways and gantries, thriving in the permanent damp. Ferns erupt from drainage grates and the joints of great beams. It is a dense, layered ecology of plants all living on a substrate of riveted steel and poured plasteel. The air smells of wet metal, ozone from the transport lifts, and the rich, green scent of chlorophyll.

The sound is a constant, loud hum, a testament to the industrial heart that still beats within the structure. It is the sound of massive air circulators, of water pumps moving the condensation from collection basins on the upper tiers down to the filtration systems far below, of the groaning of the lifts that are the only way to travel between levels. And layered over this is the sound of life: the drip of water from leaf to leaf, the rustle of something moving in the dense fern-thicket, the calls of creatures adapted to this vertical jungle.

The fauna is abundant. Sleek, six-limbed lizards, their scales patterned like rust, skitter across the warm metal. Insects, some with wingspans as wide as a hand, hover in the bright, humid air, their wings buzzing with a deep thrum. There are birds, but they are strange—built more like gliders, with broad, rigid wings to catch the updrafts between the terraces. Higher up, where the air is thinner and the plant life sparser, one might see the gliding forms of "Tier-Kites," creatures that are part-organic, part-machine, their wings a smart-fabric that adjusts to the currents, scavenging nutrients from the moisture.

The pace of change is glacial. A new fern might take a century to colonize a new stretch of railing. The corrosion on a support beam is measured in millennia. The lifts move on schedules that last for decades. The population here is sparse, living in modules grafted onto the terraces, tending their hydroponic gardens and venturing out in insulated suits to maintain the great machines. They are stewards of a place that was built to be eternal, and now, in its age, it has become something else: a slow, breathing, metallic ecosystem, utterly made, yet utterly given over to the patient, damp, and verdant logic of life. The scale is regional, a single, colossal structure that contains its own weather, its own ecology, its own deep time. It is not a city in the wilderness. It *is* the wilderness.
