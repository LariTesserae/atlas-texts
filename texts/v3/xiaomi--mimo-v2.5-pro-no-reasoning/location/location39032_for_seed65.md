# The Shriek Table

The world ends upward here. Not sharply — there is no cliff-edge where earth gives way to sky — but gradually, over kilometers of gray scree and tumbled feldspar, the land rises into a thin, bright immensity. You have been climbing for a long time. The air has been getting louder.

---

The plateau is called nothing. No human mouth has shaped a name for it, because no human has stayed. The few who have come this far describe the same thing later, badly, to people who don't believe them: a flat place at the top of the world where the wind never stops screaming and the rocks are covered in birds and blood.

---

**The Sound**

Begin here, because you cannot not begin here. It will be the first thing that destroys your composure and the last thing you forget.

The wind at the Shriek Table does not gust. It does not blow. It *is* — a permanent, horizontal body of air moving at a velocity that turns rain into a weapon and loose gravel into a lateral sandstorm. The sound it produces passing over the weathered rock formations is not a whistle and not a roar. It is a chord: a sustained, teeth-vibrating harmonic in which multiple frequencies overlap and interfere so that the air itself seems to have a texture, a grain. You feel it in your molars. You feel it in your sternum. At the margins of the plateau, where the rock breaks into fins and pillars that have been carved by this same wind over geological time, the chord sharpens into something more specific — a high, keening shriek that shifts in pitch as the air temperature changes by fractions of a degree, as moisture condenses and sublimates on the stone, as the position of the sun slightly alters the thermal gradient across the rock face. The sound has not stopped in recorded memory. It will not stop.

It is so loud that silence, when you find rare shelter behind a standing stone or in a hollow scooped from the base of a formation, becomes physically disorienting. Your ears ring. The absence of sound feels like pressure.

---

**The Stone**

The bedrock is old — impossibly, indifferently old — basalt and gneiss that were pushed up by tectonic forces so long ago that the original mountain range has been fully consumed and rebuilt and consumed again by erosion, leaving only this last stubborn plateau standing above everything. The surface is polished in the direction of the prevailing wind, every exposed face smoothed to a matte sheen that catches the brilliant, thin light and scatters it in flat white glances. Where the rock is not polished, it is sculpted: hollows called tafoni, ranging from the size of a fist to the size of a car, honeycombing every sheltered surface in fractal repetition. The tafoni collect moisture — condensation drips down their inner faces and pools in their deepest cups, staying liquid for hours even in the cold, held in the lee of their own geometry. These are the water sources. They are small. They are scattered. They are the reason anything else is here at all.

The rock formations that rise from the plateau proper — the fins, the pillars, the arches — are not tall. Most stand between two and five meters. But they are numerous and they are old in a way that makes human history feel like a rumor. The wind has shaped them over hundreds of thousands of years into forms that are not quite random and not quite deliberate. Some lean. Some have been undercut so precisely that they balance on points that seem impossible, and they have been balancing on those points for longer than any human civilization has existed. They will continue to balance. Nothing here changes.

---

**The Light**

You are above most of the atmosphere. The sky is not blue but a deep, aching violet at zenith, fading to white near the horizons where the remaining air scatters sunlight into a permanent, diffuse glare. There are no clouds below you — they mass far beneath the plateau's edges, a flat white sea that extends in every direction and makes the Shriek Table feel like the deck of a ship becalmed in cotton. The sun is savage. Even in the cool air, its radiation is immediate and total, because there is so little atmosphere left to filter it. Exposed skin burns in minutes. The light has a quality that people at lower elevations have never experienced: it is *everywhere*, bouncing off polished stone and white lichen and the pale undersides of birds, filling every shadow with reflected brilliance so that the shadows themselves are faint, thin, violet rather than black. At midday, the plateau has almost no shadows at all. It is flat. It is bright. It is hard to look at.

At sunset — and sunsets here last a long time, because you can see so far west across the cloud-sea — the light turns copper, then rose, then a shade of violet so deep it becomes indistinguishable from the dark. The wind does not change temperature with the light. It is always cool. It always moves.

---

**The Fauna**

This is the part that doesn't make sense at first and then makes perfect sense once you stop expecting it to make the kind of sense you're used to.

The birds are *everywhere*.

They are not a single species. There are at least nine distinct morphologies present, ranging from something resembling a large shearwater — blunt-winged, heavy-bodied, with a hooked beak the color of iron — to small, almost finch-like birds with disproportionately thick legs and feet that grip the polished rock with a tenacity that borders on adhesive. The medium-sized ones are the most numerous: slate-gray, with white eye-rings and a call that is, under normal circumstances, a sharp two-note whistle. But normal circumstances do not exist here. When thousands of them are calling simultaneously — and they are always calling simultaneously, because the wind disorients their young and they must constantly communicate to keep their loose colonies coherent — the whistle becomes a wall of sound that layers on top of the wind's chord and produces something almost unbearably complex.

They eat the insects. The insects are here too — driven upward on thermal columns from the cloud-sea below, arriving at the plateau's edge in spiraling, confused clouds of small dark bodies that the birds intercept with a practiced, swooping efficiency. The insects are mostly small: midges, gnats, a few larger moths that arrive with their wings already tattered by the ascent. They do not survive long. The birds are thorough. The rock beneath major colony perches is streaked white and rust-red with guano and the fragments of invertebrate exoskeletons, and the smell, when the wind shifts to carry it toward you, is ammonia-sharp and ancient.

There are also lizards. Small, dark, compressed into the tafoni hollows, they emerge between the gusts to hunt the insects that the birds miss. They are so perfectly colored to match the stone — a gray so exact it seems like camouflage evolved to the point of invisibility — that you can stare at a rock face for minutes and see nothing, and then suddenly see six of them, motionless, watching you with lateral eyes that have a faint golden sheen.

And there is one more creature, rarely seen: something in the pools. In the deepest tafoni hollows, where the water persists longest, there are clusters of dark, segmented bodies — each no longer than a thumbnail — that move slowly through the water with a synchronized, undulating motion. They are not insects. They are not worms. They are something that has lived in these pools for a very long time, feeding on the biofilm that grows on the wet stone, and no one who has described them has described them the same way twice, and it doesn't matter, because no one is coming back to check.

---

**The Vegetation**

Almost none. What exists is lichen: thick, crustose colonies of pale gray-green and white that cover the north-facing (sheltered) sides of every rock formation in a slow, patient tide. They grow at a rate measurable in centuries per centimeter. The largest colonies are the size of dinner plates, and they are, by any reasonable reckoning, among the oldest living things visible from a standing position. They do not flower. They do not fruit. They simply *are*, scraping their existence from the thin air and the intermittent moisture, converting rock to soil at a pace so slow that the soil itself is nearly imperceptible — a faint, dark dusting in the deepest tafoni hollows, no thicker than a coat of paint.

In a very few places — sheltered depressions on the plateau's interior, where the wind's fury is reduced by the surrounding formations to merely a constant, deafening rush — there is moss. Bright green, almost violently green against the gray stone, forming cushions no wider than a hand. They are wet. They hold the condensation. They are the reason the pools exist at all, acting as slow-release reservoirs that feed the tafoni hollows through the driest hours. The lizards know this. The segmented pool-creatures know this. Everything here knows where the water will be. Nothing here wastes energy on surprise.

---

**The Water**

It does not rain often. When it does, the rain arrives horizontally, driven by the wind into a needle-storm that stings exposed skin and fills the air with a fine, bright spray that catches the light and produces momentary, fragmentary rainbows that last only seconds before the wind tears them apart. The rain does not pool on the open surface of the plateau — it scours across the polished stone in thin sheets and vanishes over the edges into the cloud-sea below. What remains is what the tafoni catch: small, trapped volumes of water, each held in its own stone cup, each slightly different in chemistry depending on the mineral composition of the hollow that holds it. Some are faintly alkaline. Some taste of iron. All of them are cold.

The largest pool is perhaps two meters across and thirty centimeters deep. It sits in a hollow at the base of the plateau's tallest formation — a leaning pillar of gneiss that the wind has carved into a shape vaguely, uncomfortably suggestive of a seated figure. The pool never dries. The pillar shelters it. The moss feeds it. The segmented creatures live in it. It is the closest thing this place has to a center.

---

**The Equilibrium**

Nothing here is new. Nothing here is becoming. The birds have nested on this plateau for tens of thousands of years, their guano building up in the sheltered places and being eroded away in the exposed ones at exactly the same rate. The lichens grow and are worn back by wind-driven grit and grow again. The tafoni deepen by microns per century. The standing formations lose mass at a rate so slow that they are, on any human timescale, permanent — and yet they are not permanent, they are merely very, very patient, and the wind is more patient still.

There are no seasons. The temperature varies by perhaps ten degrees between the coldest night and the warmest day, and this variation has been the same for as long as anyone has measured it, and it will continue to be the same for longer than anyone will be around to measure it. The birds arrive on schedule. The insects arrive on schedule. The wind blows. The light falls. The pools fill and empty and fill again.

It is not a place that is waiting for anything. It is not a place that is going anywhere. It has arrived at its own final form — not a static form, but a *cyclic* one, a place where every process is matched by its inverse, where every gain is balanced by its loss, where the energy flowing through the system is exactly sufficient to maintain the system and nothing more.

It is loud. It is bright. It is empty and full at the same time. It is the most inhospitable place that is not hostile — a place that does not want to kill you, that does not want anything, that simply does not care whether you are here or not, because your presence or absence changes nothing about the sound or the light or the wind or the birds or the slow, slow grinding of stone into dust.

You will leave. The place will not notice.
