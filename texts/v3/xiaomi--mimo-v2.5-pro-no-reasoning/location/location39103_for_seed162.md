The ground is wet, but the sky is dry.

You stand on a slope of black basalt, steep and young, the rock still sharp enough to cut. It was an island, once, maybe a week ago, maybe a month — now it is the rim of a caldera so wide the far side is lost in haze, though "haze" implies atmospheric particulate and what you're seeing is more like *distance becoming its own substance*. The rim continues in both directions, curving away, a mountain range that is also a circle, a perimeter wall for something below.

The basalt weeps.

Not dramatically — not waterfalls or springs — but moisture beads on every surface, condensing where the warm rock meets air that is somehow cooler at altitude, though the altitude itself keeps changing, keeps *being argued*. You are high up. You are always high up here. The caldera floor is kilometers below, and the rim rises further still, and there are peaks above you that shouldn't be there, that weren't there yesterday, that sprout like teeth from the existing ridge and then erode by noon into gravel that cascades down the outer slope and is subducted back into the rock by morning. The mountain is building itself. The mountain is *young*.

The vegetation exploits every crack.

Moss first — thick, almost furry, a green so dark it reads as black in the dim light. Then ferns, enormous, their fronds curled like fists, unfurling over hours in a visible motion you can watch if you're patient. Then the trees, though "trees" is a kindness to what they are: columnar growths of fused plant matter, trunks that merge and separate, canopies that touch and graft into one another until a hectare of forest is a single organism. They grow fast. You can hear them — a fibrous creaking, like rope under strain, the only sound in the quiet besides your breathing and the occasional *crack* of new basalt splitting open somewhere below. The forest is dense. You cannot see more than a few meters into it. The ferns lean into your path. The moss has opinions about where you step.

The light is wrong.

There is a sun — you think there is a sun — but it filters through a sky the color of tarnished brass, and what reaches the ground is diffuse and amber and *tired*, as if the photons have been used before. The shadows are soft but numerous, layered, each frond casting its own and also borrowing one from the canopy above, so you exist in a perpetual penumbra, a dimness that feels less like the absence of light and more like *the presence of shadow as a substance*. It is warm. It is aggressively warm. The heat comes from below — the caldera floor radiates it — and from the rock itself, and from the plants, which photosynthesize with an exothermic efficiency that makes the air shimmer just above the leaf litter.

There are animals in the fern-brake.

You hear them more than see them. A rustling that moves *against* the wind. A clicking sound, rhythmic, like a telegraph operator who has forgotten the message but not the habit. Something large shifts its weight in the middle-distance, and a column of the fused-trees sways and settles. The fauna here is abundant — you sense it in the *thickness* of the ecosystem, the way every niche is filled, the way something is always watching from just behind the next frond. They are not afraid of you. They are not aggressive. They are simply *present*, in the way that weather is present, and they will continue to be present whether you are here or not.

But the weirdness.

The weirdness is the caldera floor.

You have not seen it. No one has seen it — not because it is hidden, but because *looking at it directly causes it to not be there*. Glance sideways, catch it in your peripheral vision, and you see: industry. Smokestacks. The angular geometry of factories, the glint of metal, the suggestion of *production*, of something being built or processed or assembled on a scale that matches the caldera's own vastness. But look directly, and it is only rock, only the churning floor of a volcanic depression, steam venting from a thousand cracks, the basalt folding and refolding like dough. The factories exist only in the corner of your eye. They are *there*. They are not there. The air smells of ozone and machine oil and also of wet stone and also of nothing.

The animals navigate this.

They move through the landscape and through the factories interchangeably, as if both are real, as if the distinction doesn't matter. You watch a creature — six-legged, armored, the size of a dog — walk behind a fern and emerge from behind a smokestack that shouldn't exist, and then walk behind another fern and emerge into the forest again, and it does not seem confused by this. The factories *hum*. The forest *grows*. Both are happening. Both are real. The mountain doesn't care.

The mountain is building itself, and the mountain is building whatever is on the floor of the caldera, and the mountain is eroding itself, and all of this is happening at once, turbulently, the way a river is many currents at once. New ridges erupt and are colonized by moss within hours. Smokestacks appear and disappear on a cycle you can't determine. The animals breed and die and breed again, their generations flickering like the factories, like the light through the brass-colored sky. You are standing on a geological process that has opinions, on a landscape that is *performing itself*, and the only constant is the warmth, the dampness of the rock, the dim amber light, and the quiet — the deep, patient quiet of a place that has so much happening inside it that it no longer needs to be loud.
