The air is thin and hard, carrying a scent of ozone and cold stone. You are standing on a high, vast plateau, a landmass that seems to have been left behind by a retreating ice age or pushed up by a slow, continent-sized fist. The rock underfoot is not jagged but worn smooth and broad, like the bones of the earth itself, polished by millennia of wind and freeze-thaw cycles. It is a dark basalt, veined with paler granite and streaked with rust-red where mineral deposits have wept and oxidized in the thin atmosphere. This is the **Argentum Steppe**.

**Water (1.0):** There are no rivers, no lakes, no open bodies of water. The dampness is a secret, a slow seep. In the lee of vast, wind-scoured rock formations—some the size of hills—condensation gathers on the shaded surfaces. It pools in shallow depressions in the rock, forming dark, mirror-still tarns that never grow large. These tarns are fringed with a brittle crust of minerals left behind as the water evaporates or freezes. The rain, when it comes, is not a storm but a fine, horizontal mist that clings to everything, freezing into a delicate rime before the faint sun can burn it off. Water here is not a force; it is a patient chemistry, a slow accumulation and release.

**Vegetation (0.9):** Life clings on in defiance. The sparse vegetation is not of soil, but of the rock itself. Low, rubbery mats of a dark, almost black moss cover the north-facing slopes, thriving on the condensation and the faint, filtered light. They are cold to the touch and yield very little when stepped on. In the margins of the mineral tarns, thick, grey-green lichens with the texture of armored plates form slow-growing continents. There is no grass, no shrub, no tree—only these ancient, patient organisms that feed on light, air, and stone. Their presence is marked more by texture and shade than by color.

**Temperature (0.2) & Elevation (1.8):** Cold is the fundamental state. The air bites. The wind, which is constant, is a knife that finds every seam. The high elevation means the sky is a deeper, more violent blue at noon, but the weak sun provides little warmth. Heat is a memory stored in the deep rock, which radiates it back out during the long, star-hazed nights. Frost forms not in crystals but as a solid, glassy sheen on surfaces exposed to the wind.

**Erosion (2.5) & Scale (2.8):** This landscape is not young. It has been here so long that it has reached a kind of geological equilibrium. The vastness is oppressive; the horizon is a faint, curving line of blue-grey haze where the steppe meets the immense, empty sky. The weather that moves across this scale is slow and ponderous. You see a cloud shadow take an hour to cross a basin. A snowfall that began as a faint glitter in the upper air will take a full day to reach the ground as tiny, hard pellets. The erosion is not chaotic but has carved the land into its final, essential forms: broad, shallow basins, sweeping, smooth ridges, and those immense, monolithic rock formations that are the only vertical landmarks. They are not mountains, but the stumps of mountains, worn down to their cores.

**Density (1.0) & Built (0.3):** Emptiness is the dominant presence. The distances between things are great. The "built" score is not for human structures, but for what the fauna has wrought. Scattered across the steppe are the **Chitin Domes**. They are not buildings but vast, organic sculptures. Resembling bleached, hollowed-out gourds or the shells of unimaginable insects, they are made from a calcium-like substance secreted by the local fauna, mixed with wind-blown rock dust and frozen moisture. Some are the size of a small house, others are just hillocks. They are weathered, ancient, pitted, and silent. Their interiors are hollow and dark, the floors littered with the dust of generations.

**Fauna (2.6) & Tech (2.0):** This is the paradox. The overwhelming presence is a species of creature, the **Argentum Grazers**. They are colossal, slow-moving arthropods, their bodies built in segments of dark, metallic chitin. They possess multiple legs, ending in broad, flat pads that distribute their immense weight and tread softly on the stone. Their heads are low-slung, equipped with mandible-like appendages that scrape the moss and lichens from the rock. They move in herds, but a herd might be only a dozen creatures spread over several square miles, their passage marked by the faint, rhythmic *scrape... scrape... scrape* on stone, the only sound to break the profound silence.

The industrial aspect is *them*. They are biological factories. Within their bodies, complex processes create the chitin and the building material for the Domes. Their waste is the calcium cement. Their grazing shapes the ecology. The dim light is filtered through the faint, persistent haze of calcium dust their activity puts into the atmosphere. They are the engine of this world, a slow, silent, industrial ecology that has no gears, only biology and geology operating on a planetary timescale. They are the reason the vegetation is sparse (they are voracious), the reason the dampness is chemically complex (their secretions), and the reason the air tastes of ozone (some metabolic byproduct).

**Light (1.2) & Sound (0.0):** The light is that of perpetual late afternoon, a golden-grey glow that seems to come from no particular source. It catches the mineral dust and the rime on the stones. The silence is absolute. It is not the absence of noise, but a positive presence. It is the sound of the immense pressure of the air, the slow grind of the planet, the patient ticking of the herds' internal processes. To speak is to violate it. To move is to feel it as a physical weight. The only punctuation is the gentle, infrequent *tink* of a mineral crust cracking in the cold, or the soft, sandy sound of a Grazer's leg pad settling on fresh frost.

**Dynamic (1.4):** Change is glacial. The herds move in circles that take years to complete. The tarns fill and empty over seasons. The Domes grow imperceptibly, layer by layer, over centuries. A rock face erodes by millimeters per decade. The light changes over the course of a day, but the true cycles are measured in the slow drift of the constellations and the advance and retreat of the faint, polar ice cap at the world's far edge. This is a place of patience, where even the overwhelming presence of life operates on the timescale of stone.
