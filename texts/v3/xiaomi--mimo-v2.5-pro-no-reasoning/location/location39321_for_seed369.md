The air is a wet blanket, heavy and warm, smelling of damp earth, sweet decay, and the mineral tang of wet stone. You are in a high place, but it is not a peak. It is a high saddle, a cupped palm of land between two greater, unseen mountains whose lower slopes are lost in the thick, condensing mist that perpetually weeps from the sky. The sound is a constant, soft roar—not of wind, but of water. It drips from every leaf, trickles down every moss-furred rock, and gurgles in the soil, a world saturated with its own slow exhalation.

The ground is a sponge. Thick carpets of emerald moss, so deep they swallow the sound of a footfall, cover everything. They climb the trunks of the trees and drape from their branches in long, green beards. The trees themselves are the true scale-setters: ancient, gnarled conifers of a species found nowhere else, their bark black with moisture and their roots gripping the soft, weeping rock like great, arthritic fingers. They are not dense. They stand with space between them, like pillars in a ruined temple, each one a monument to patience. Sunlight filters through the misty canopy in a diffuse, silvered glow, illuminating the steam that rises from the forest floor in lazy, curling tendrils.

Between the trees, the sparse undergrowth consists of broad-leafed ferns that unfurl in perfect spirals, their edges perpetually beaded with water, and clusters of pale, fleshy fungi that glow with a faint bioluminescence in the deeper shade. The world is in a state of established arrested development. It has been like this for millennia. The erosion is not active, not dramatic. It is the slow, felted encroachment of moss on stone, the gentle subsidence of soil around a fallen branch, the way water has carved not gullies, but smooth, patient basins into the rock. The landscape is old, and has settled into its own quiet logic of accumulation.

Life is rare, but present. Occasionally, a flash of iridescent blue—a lizard with a throat that pulsates slowly—will be seen clinging to a damp trunk before vanishing. The sound is often punctuated by the deep, resonant *plonk* of a large drop of water falling from a high leaf into a pool, a sound that echoes strangely in the stillness. There are no paths, no signs of tool or hand, save for the one you make. The only artifacts are natural: a fallen tree, now a mossy mound; a circle of unusually bright fungi; a cluster of elongated, tuberous roots exposed by a minor slip of earth. The weirdness here is subtle, a low hum beneath the dripping. It is in the way the light refracts through the mist, creating brief, prismatic ghosts. It is in the silence between the water sounds, a silence that feels watchful. It is a place that is profoundly alive, yet slow, patient, and utterly complete, asking nothing of the world outside its own damp, green, and whispering fold.
