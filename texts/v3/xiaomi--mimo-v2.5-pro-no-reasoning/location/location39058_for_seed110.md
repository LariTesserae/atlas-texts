# The Seep Pocket

You would not find it if you were not already on your hands and knees.

The entrance is a slit in the mountainside — at this altitude, somewhere above four thousand meters, in rock so new it still carries the glassy conchoidal fractures of its making. The basalt here was fluid within living memory. It has not learned weathering. Its surfaces are smooth, almost slick, still bearing the faint rope-texture of flow, and when you press your palm to the lip of the opening the stone is not cold as high rock should be. It holds a residual warmth, a deep-slow heat that has nothing to do with the thin air or the absent sun.

There is no light. There has never been light here. That is the first thing to understand.

---

## Entry

The slit takes your shoulders and then your hips and then you are through into a space roughly the size of a root cellar — perhaps three meters across at its widest, the ceiling low enough that you could not stand. The floor is uneven, folded into shallow bowls and ridges by the way the lava cooled against itself, and in every depression, in every concavity, in every crease of the rock: water.

Not running water. Not pooling in any ordinary sense. The basalt is *weeping*. Every surface carries a continuous film of condensation, a skin of mineral-heavy moisture so thin it barely moves, drawn slowly by gravity into the low points where it gathers in dishes no wider than outstretched hands. The largest of these pools is perhaps forty centimeters across and only a few centimeters deep. The water is warm — not hot, but warmer than blood, and faintly sulfurous, carrying dissolved iron and silica and something else, something vaguely alchemical that you can taste in the air when you breathe.

The density of presence here is startling for the size of the chamber. Every square meter is occupied by something. The walls glisten. The ceiling weeps. The floor is an archipelago of small warm pools separated by ridges of dark glassy rock. And on those ridges, in the dry-but-damp interstices between water, there is *growth*.

---

## What Lives

Not much. Not in any way that would satisfy a naturalist's taxonomy. But it is here.

There are microbial mats — dark, almost black, gelatinous films on the rock surfaces near the waterline. They are barely visible even if you had light to see them by; you discover them by touch, by the faintly slippery texture of living biofilm on stone. They are the reason the air tastes faintly organic beneath the sulfur. They have been here longer than anything else in the chamber, and they will remain after everything else is gone.

There are mats of something fungal — pale, threadlike, growing in the crevices where the rock folds over on itself and creates tiny dry pockets above the waterline. These are not mushrooms in any recognizable sense. They are filaments, a fine white mycelial lace pressed flat against the stone, drinking condensation from the air. They have no fruiting bodies. They may not fruit at all. They may simply *be*, spreading imperceptibly, a network without a purpose beyond its own continuation.

Once — only once — you hear a sound that is not the slow ticking of water falling from ceiling to pool. A faint clicking, barely audible, somewhere in the deeper fissures that branch off the main chamber. Something moving through passages too narrow for you to follow. You will not see it. It is rare and small and does not wish to be known.

---

## What Was Brought

There are structures here. Faint ones.

In the largest pool, someone has placed stones — carefully selected, flat-sided, stacked in a deliberate cairn that rises just above the waterline. The stones are not local basalt. They are different minerals — one pale quartzite, one dark chert, one a piece of greenish stone that might be serpentinite, carried here from different ground. The cairn is old. The microbial mats have begun to claim its lower stones. But the top stone is dry, clean, placed with intention, and you understand without knowing how that this is a marker, a claim, a prayer — something left by hands that knew this chamber and wished to say *I was here, and this water mattered to me.*

Near another pool, pressed into a crevice in the wall, there is a small ceramic vessel — unbaked clay, hardened only by the dry air of the chamber's higher reaches, pinch-formed with clumsy thumbs. It is the size of a cup. Its interior is stained with mineral residue. Someone used this to carry water out. Or to collect it. Or to offer something to it. The vessel is too fragile to have survived by accident. Its preservation is an act of the place — the stillness here, the lack of weathering, the frozen equilibrium of a chamber that does not change.

There are marks on the wall near the entrance — scratches, a tally, a pattern of lines incised with a sharp-edged stone. They are not writing. They are counting. Days, perhaps, or visits, or something else entirely. The lines are shallow and the rock around them is as unweathered as the day they were cut. This is a place where time does not accomplish much.

---

## Atmosphere

The air is warm, humid, nearly saturated. Breathing feels like drinking. Every inhalation carries moisture and minerals and the faint sulfurous tang of the deep earth and the fainter organic sweetness of the microbial mats. The humidity is so high that surfaces do not dry. The water film on the walls replenishes faster than it drips. This is a place in near-perfect equilibrium — water condenses, gathers, evaporates, condenses again, a closed cycle operating at the pace of breath.

It is dark in a way that is not hostile but *total*. The darkness is not an absence. It is a medium, thick and warm and close, like being inside a body. You navigate by touch, by the temperature of the water, by the texture of the rock under your fingers. Your eyes are useless here. After a time you stop opening them. After a longer time you forget that opening them would mean anything.

The quiet is immense but not empty. The water makes its slow percussion — drip, drip, drip — a rhythm so slow it approaches stillness. The stone creaks occasionally as it cools or warms on imperceptible thermal cycles. The air moves in micro-currents as humidity shifts. These are the only sounds, and they are the sounds of a place that has nearly achieved the equilibrium it is reaching for.

---

## Rules of the Place

This chamber is newly made and does not yet know what it is. The rock is raw — unweathered, unfractured by frost, unscarred by time. It was born hot and is cooling on a timescale that dwarfs everything that has happened inside it. The water is a consequence of that cooling: warm air meeting young rock, condensing, mineralizing, establishing the first chemical conversations between stone and water that will, over centuries, begin to dissolve and reshape this space.

But those centuries have not yet arrived. This is the *before*. The erosion score is nearly zero. The chamber exists in a state of pre-formation, embryonic, its future features — stalactites, scalloping, dissolution channels, wider rooms — all still latent in the chemistry of the water and the mineral composition of the stone. In ten thousand years this will be a cave. In a hundred thousand it will be a cathedral of dissolved and redeposited calcium. But now it is a pocket, a cell, a single dark room holding warm water in the belly of a mountain that has barely finished being born.

Nothing changes here. Or rather: everything changes here, but so slowly that the word *change* loses its meaning. The mats grow at the pace of geology. The water cycles at the pace of breath. The stone cools at the pace of mountains. The cairn stands. The clay cup rests in its crevice. The tally marks wait to be continued by hands that may never return.

You are warm. You are wet. You are surrounded. You cannot see.

The chamber does not notice you. The chamber does not notice anything. The chamber is busy becoming itself, and it will take its time.
