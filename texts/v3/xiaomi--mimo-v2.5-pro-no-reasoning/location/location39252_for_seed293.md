# The Poolroom of Reddenwood

You stand in a place that has no business still existing.

---

It is a single room, roughly oval, perhaps forty meters across at its widest. The ceiling — if you can call it that — rises to maybe twelve meters at the center, a dome of living tissue. Not wood. Not bark in any sense you'd recognize. The substance is something between cork and cartilage, colored in shades of liver and deep amber, and it breathes. You can see it: a slow, tidal expansion and contraction, roughly once per minute, the walls drawing inward and releasing. The air moves with each cycle, warm and heavy and wet, and the sound is like a sleeping animal in a room the size of a cathedral nave.

The floor is the same material, or rather — it is continuous with the walls and the ceiling. You are standing inside an organism. Not figuratively. The Reddenwood grew around this hollow roughly two hundred million years ago, and the hollow has been here ever since, and the substance you stand on has been alive for all of that time, and it has never once been still.

---

## The Substrate

What you initially mistake for soil is not. Under the thin film of standing water — never more than ankle-deep, warm as blood, the color of strong tea — the ground is a dense mat of interlocking rhizomes, each one no thicker than a finger, but woven so tightly over so many eons that they have fused into a continuous living flesh. The texture underfoot is yielding, slightly tacky, and warm. It gives about two centimeters when you step, then pushes back. If you knelt and pressed your ear to it, you would hear the sound of fluid moving — not a heartbeat, exactly, but a slow systole of water drawn upward from whatever aquifer lies beneath this lowland basin and distributed outward through the network.

The water comes from below. It comes from the air — condensation beads constantly on every surface, runs in slow rivulets down the curved walls, collects in the depression at the center of the room. There is no drain. There is no overflow. The Reddenwood drinks it, and what it doesn't drink, it exhales. The humidity is absolute. You are breathing as much water as air.

---

## The Plants Within the Plant

This is the strangest part: the Reddenwood is not alone inside itself.

Over the millennia, other organisms have colonized the inner surfaces of the hollow. They are plants — they have to be, nothing else can root in living tissue without being rejected — but they are plants that have evolved in absolute darkness and total saturation, in a space where the substrate itself pulses with slow chemical signals and the temperature never varies by more than a few degrees.

There are not many of them. That is the key thing to understand. The room is *sparse*. Between the presences, there is space — warm, wet, echoing space, the water whispering on the floor, the walls breathing. The plants are scattered: a cluster of pale stalks near the western wall, each one a meter tall, their surfaces covered in a fine down that catches condensation and channels it to a central groove; a single massive leaf-limb hanging from the dome like a chandelier, dark green on top, its underside a network of luminous veins that pulse with bioluminescence so faint you might not notice it in the available light; a low carpet of something mosslike growing in a ring around the central pool, so dark it looks like burned felt.

These are old. Everything here is old. The moss-ring has been growing at a rate measured in centimeters per century. The chandelier leaf has been hanging for perhaps sixty thousand years and shows no sign of dying. The pale stalks — they reproduce so slowly that their speciation is visible only in deep-time. They are the Reddenwood's children, or its tenants, or its parasites, or its organs. No one is entirely sure.

---

## The Engineering

And then there are the structures.

They are built into the walls — or rather, they are *grown* into the walls, because whoever designed them understood that in this place, building meant coaxing. Three alcoves, evenly spaced around the room's perimeter, each one about two meters deep, each one housing an object that should not exist in a two-hundred-million-year-old plant cavity.

The technology is unrecognizable in the way that a transistor would be unrecognizable to a medieval monk — not because it's hidden, but because the conceptual frame to understand it isn't present. The alcoves themselves are lined with a material that is technically organic but has been restructured at the molecular level. The rhizome-flesh in those alcoves has been *edited*: its cell walls are lattices of something that behaves like a semiconductor at room temperature, its vascular channels have been redirected into patterns that resemble circuitry, and the whole arrangement hums at a frequency just below hearing.

What they do is unclear. One of them — the one nearest the central pool — appears to regulate the water table. The pool level has not changed in recorded observation, and the alcove's internal temperature is precisely 0.3 degrees cooler than the rest of the room, always, regardless of external conditions. Another seems to control the bioluminescence of the chandelier leaf, though the control pattern is not simple on/off — it cycles through something that resembles a language if you stare at it long enough, but you don't stare at it long enough, because the warm air makes you drowsy and the sound of the breathing walls makes you feel held.

The third alcove is the one the fauna prefers.

---

## The Fauna

The room is warm and wet and dim and alive, and animals have found it.

There are insects — small ones, mostly, dark-bodied flies and gnats that breed in the standing water and cloud around the pale stalks in loose, lazy swarms. They are loud for their size, a collective hum that adds to the room's ambient sound and makes the silence feel populated. There are spiders — long-legged, slow-moving, pale as the stalks they hunt among, building webs between the rhizome-ridges on the walls. The webs are thick and wet and they shimmer.

There are larger things. Something like a salamander, though not quite — it has too many limbs, and its skin is translucent enough to show the water it has swallowed sitting in its stomach. It lives in the central pool and eats the flies. Something like a bird, though it doesn't fly — it clings to the dome and moves in quick, jerking hops, its feathers (if they are feathers) dark and sleek with condensation. It eats the spiders. Something that might be a mammal, curled in the third alcove, breathing the same rhythm as the walls. It is covered in fine, dark fur. It has been there every time anyone has entered the room. No one has seen it move.

The food web is simple but complete. The flies eat the moss and the condensation. The spiders eat the flies. The salamander eats the flies. The bird eats the spiders. The mammal in the alcove — no one knows what it eats. Possibly nothing. Possibly it has been in a state of suspended metabolic reduction for so long that it doesn't need to eat. Possibly it is another piece of engineering, grown rather than built, a biological instrument left in place by whatever intelligence tended the alcoves. Its fur ripples sometimes, as if in a breeze that isn't there.

---

## The Turbulence

But the room is not peaceful.

Every few hours — the intervals are irregular, anywhere from ninety minutes to half a day — the Reddenwood *shifts*. Not the slow breathing; this is structural. A convulsion runs through the walls, a shudder that travels from the base to the dome in a wave, and the room changes shape. Not dramatically — a wall might bulge inward by half a meter, or the floor might rise in a new ridge, or a rhizome-vein that had been dormant for decades might suddenly swell with fluid and push to the surface, breaking through the fused mat like a new root through pavement.

The water surges. The pale stalks sway. The chandelier leaf swings on its attachment point, and its bioluminescence flares and then dims, cycling through something urgent and unreadable. The insects scatter and then resettle. The salamander dives. The bird grips the dome with its feet and doesn't move. The mammal in the alcove — if it reacts at all, you don't see it.

These convulsions are not dangerous, exactly. The room has survived them for longer than flowering plants have existed. But they are *turbulent* — the air rushes, the water sloshes, the temperature spikes by a degree and then drops, and for a few minutes the sound is genuinely loud: a deep, wet, structural groaning, the sound of a living thing rearranging itself. And then it passes, and the room settles into a new configuration — slightly different from the last, never quite the same — and the breathing resumes, and the water stills, and the insects return.

It feels like being inside a city that is also a body that is also a machine. The technology in the alcoves hums. The fauna resumes its small, abundant routines. The plants grow, so slowly that growth and stillness are indistinguishable. The walls breathe. The water condenses.

---

## The Light

There is no sun here. The light comes from the dome itself — the Reddenwood's outer bark, meters thick, is translucent in patches, and the lowland basin above receives enough overcast daylight that a diffuse, amber-gray glow filters through. It is not bright. It is the light of a heavily clouded afternoon, warm-toned, shadowless, sufficient. You can see. You can see the water on the floor and the ridges in the walls and the swarm of gnats and the dark shape of the mammal in the alcove. You can see well enough that the faint bioluminescence of the chandelier leaf is almost unnecessary — but not quite. It adds a cool, green undertone to the amber light, and where the two overlap, the colors shimmer and interfere in a way that is not unpleasant but does something strange to depth perception. Things seem closer or farther than they are. The room feels larger and smaller simultaneously.

---

## What It Is

You are standing in a single room inside a single organism on a low, ancient, waterlogged plain where the land and the living have been the same thing for so long that the distinction is a fossil. The technology here is the most advanced you will ever encounter, and it is indistinguishable from the tissue it's embedded in. The room is small and it is sparse and it is turbulent and it is full of creatures that have no reason to leave. The walls are breathing. The water is warm. The mammal in the alcove is watching you, or sleeping, or something for which there is no word.

You could stay. The room would rearrange around you eventually. You would become part of the ecology — something that eats the flies, or something the flies eat, or something the room grows around and into, slowly, over centuries, until you were another alcove, another instrument, another part of the pattern.

The moss-ring grows by centimeters per century. The walls breathe. The water condenses and runs and gathers and is drunk and is exhaled and condenses again.
