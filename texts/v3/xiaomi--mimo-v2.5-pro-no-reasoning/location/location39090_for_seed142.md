# The Plateau of Whorled Ash

---

The ground is warm. Not the warmth of sunlight — there is no sunlight, hasn't been for longer than the concept of "sun" has meant anything here — but the warmth of stone that has been slowly releasing its heat for geological ages. The temperature is stable, hovering always around the same degree. Human skin would register it as pleasant, then uncomfortable, then invisible once you stopped noticing.

You are standing on a high plateau. The word "plateau" fails, though. It implies a flat top and sharp edges. This is more like a continent that has been lifted four kilometers above its own surrounding nothingness — a broad, slow rise of basaltic rock weathered so thoroughly that it has lost all its original character. The stone is no longer sharp or rough. It has the texture of old leather, or of something that was once alive and has been dead so long that the distinction doesn't matter. Every surface curves. Every edge has been softened by billions of years of thermal cycling — expanding, contracting, expanding — until the plateau looks less like geology and more like something that melted and resolidified in its sleep.

---

**The darkness** is not total but it is nearly so.

There is no sky. Or rather, the sky is there, but it is the same color as everything else — a thick, faintly warm dark that extends upward and outward in all directions. The atmosphere on this world is dense and old, saturated with particulate matter that has been settling and resettling since before the last tectonic pulse shut down, and it absorbs and scatters and swallows all light with bureaucratic thoroughness. There is nothing to reflect. There is nothing to cast.

And yet the darkness is not black. If you stand still and let your eyes adjust — if you are the kind of creature with eyes, and if they are the kind of eyes that adjust — you begin to see that the air itself carries a faint phosphorescence. Not bioluminescence. There is nothing biological here. This is chemical. Mineral. Deposits of something — long-chain phosphor compounds, perhaps, or crystalline inclusions in the ancient stone — that absorb the planet's residual thermal radiation and release it, slowly, as visible light so dim it barely qualifies. The color is hard to name. Somewhere between amber and grey. The way old ember looks through smoke.

You can see about thirty meters in any direction before the haze becomes opaque.

---

**The city** begins.

Not abruptly. You walk, and the ground remains the same — that warm, leathery, curved stone — but then you notice that the curvature has become *regular*. The stone is still stone, still ancient, but it has been shaped. Cut. Poured, possibly. The thermal rounding has worked on it the same way it worked on the natural plateau, but underneath the erosion you can feel the intention: this surface was once flat. This edge was once a right angle. This was a wall.

The buildings, if that is what they are, do not rise the way buildings on other worlds rise. They emerge from the plateau like the plateau itself is a body and these are its slow exhalations. Massive shapes — cylindrical, hexagonal, sometimes forming geometries that don't have obvious names — standing ten, twenty, forty meters high, their surfaces weathered into the same leather-smooth texture as the ground. They are made of the same stone, or something close to it. Where metal was used, it has oxidized into the rock itself, leaving dark stains and strange patinas: rust-red, cobalt-blue, a deep green that might be copper or might be something else entirely.

The spacing is vast. Between each structure there are hundreds of meters of empty plateau. The city is enormous — you could walk for days and not reach its edge — but it is sparse, almost depopulated by its own architecture. Every building stands alone in its own weathered plaza, connected to the others by roads that are so worn they are barely distinguishable from the natural stone. The impression is of a place that was once denser, or that was built by something that required more room than a human would, or that understood density differently.

Some of the structures are hollow. You can enter them. Inside, the darkness deepens, and the faint phosphorescence of the atmosphere does not reach, and you are in true black. But the walls are warm. The air is warm. The floor is warm. Heat radiates from below, always from below — the planet's mantle is still alive, or alive-enough, still conducting its ancient heat upward through kilometers of lithosphere and into the bones of the city. The interiors feel like the inside of something sleeping.

---

**The machinery** is still running.

Deep beneath the plateau — you cannot see it, but you can feel it if you press your hand to the ground and hold very still — there is a low vibration. Not a sound exactly. More like the memory of a sound, or like a sound that is so far below the threshold of hearing that it has become a texture. A hum. The frequency of something massive rotating very slowly.

The industrial heart of this city is buried. It was always buried. The factories and processing plants and whatever else they built to fuel whatever this civilization did with itself — these were never on the surface. They are down there, in the warm dark, still drawing heat from the planet's core and converting it into... something. Energy, maybe. Stored in forms that don't have obvious names. The machinery doesn't care that no one is operating it. It was designed to run without supervision, or it was designed for operators who thought on timescales that make "supervision" mean something different. It will run until it stops, and it has not stopped.

Occasionally — and by "occasionally" I mean perhaps once every few centuries — a structure on the surface will shift. A wall will settle a few millimeters. A pipe will crack with a sound like a distant ice shelf calving, and a plume of warm gas will hiss upward into the dark atmosphere and dissipate. These are the loudest sounds the city makes. Between them, there is almost nothing: the faint thermal creaking of stone expanding and contracting in its endless cycle, the subsonic hum from below, and silence.

---

**The strangeness** is quiet too. It doesn't announce itself.

It lives in the geometry of the buildings. In the way that some corridors are too narrow for a human body but too tall, and the steps (if they are steps) are too shallow and too wide. In the way that the city has no center — or rather, it has too many centers, each cluster of buildings radiating outward from a point that seems important but is no different from any other point. In the way that the roads, when you can find them, don't connect things so much as they *orbit* things, curving in slow spirals that never quite arrive.

And in the markings. On some of the interior walls, if you bring your own light — or if the faint phosphorescence happens to catch the surface at the right angle — there are patterns. Not writing, or not writing as you understand it. More like topographical maps of places that don't exist. Or like notation for a mathematics that includes variables for things you don't have words for. The patterns are worn almost to invisibility, and they may have been wearing away for longer than your species has existed, and they will continue to wear away for longer still, and the city will not notice.

---

**The fauna** is almost nothing.

There are things that move in the deeper places — in the buried industrial corridors, in the gaps between structures, in the warm vents where gases rise from below. They are small. They are slow. They are difficult to see in the darkness and they are not especially interested in being seen. Whether they are animals, or machines, or something the distinction doesn't apply to, is not clear. They have no eyes. They navigate by warmth. They have been here for a very long time — perhaps as long as the city, perhaps longer — and they do not reproduce quickly and they do not die quickly and they do not do very much. They are the only things in the city that are not stone or metal or ancient heat, and their presence feels less like life and more like a very slow process of erosion that happens to have legs.

---

**What this place is** depends on your timescale.

On a human timescale, it is static. Nothing changes. You could leave and return in a hundred years and the city would be exactly as you left it, minus a few millimeters of weathering on the windward faces. The warmth would be the same. The darkness would be the same. The silence would be the same.

On a geological timescale, it is *almost* static. The plateau is settling. The buildings are sinking, very slowly, into the rock that supports them. The machinery is degrading, component by component, on schedules that span millennia. The atmosphere is thickening, imperceptibly, as more particulate matter is outgassed from below and added to the haze. The city is merging with the plateau. In a few hundred million years, there will be no distinction between the two. The structures will be rounded, smoothed, incorporated. The machinery will be fossilized. The warm dark will continue.

This is a place that has already finished becoming itself. Everything that was going to happen here has happened. What remains is the long, patient, warm process of coming apart — which looks, from the outside, exactly like nothing happening at all.
