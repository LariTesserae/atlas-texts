The first thing you notice is the light. It doesn't arrive, it *occupies*. It is a solid presence, a substance poured into every crack and hollow of the world, thick and brilliant white. It comes from no visible sun, but from the entire bowl of the sky, a cloudless, depthless glare that seems to hum with a pressure against your eardrums. The cold is a thing of crystal; it doesn't feel wet, but the world is sheathed in a perpetual, brittle glaze of ice.

This is a place called the Sinter.

You are standing on what might be a street, but it is a street made of other things now. The ground is a mosaic of petrified wood, slabs of some dark, unknown ceramic, and the flat, bleached shells of immense, unknown crustaceans. All of it is fused together by a translucent, glassy matrix. Weeping from the cracks between these slabs is water, a constant, slow seep that freezes the instant it touches the air. This has created a landscape of delicate, up-thrusting needles of ice, a frozen coral reef that creaks and groans with the slightest thermal shift. The ice coats everything—walls, rooftops, the very air seems to shimmer with suspended crystals.

And the walls are everywhere. This is a dense, vertical maze. There are structures, yes—terraced buildings of rough-cut stone and bone-white coral, their architecture a brutal, functional stack of rooms and terraces. But they are overwhelmed, invaded. Immense, bone-like ribs of some long-dead leviathan form the primary archways and buttresses of the settlement. These are not mere decorations; they are the load-bearing bones of the town, their fossilized marrow fused with the masonry. The settlement is built upon and within the skeleton of something else.

From the walls, from the rooftops, from the very ribs, a kind of moss grows. It is not soft or green. It is a pale, almost colorless lichen, a fungal crust that drinks the overwhelming light. It grows in thick, layered patches, giving the stone a diseased, leprous texture. This is the sparse vegetation, and it pulses. In the heart of the blazing day, it seems to draw the light inward, its pale surface taking on a faint, internal luminescence. It is the only thing that moves with any predictability.

The silence is profound, yet it is a crowded silence. The air is thick with the sound of things *not making sound*. The skitter of a thousand tiny legs on glassy ice that is, in the end, just the settling of frost. The low groan of the great ribs flexing under the pressure of the light. The drip-hiss of water freezing. And everywhere, the *presence* of fauna. Pale, chitinous things with too many joints, they cling to the underside of the ice-needles, their bodies nearly transparent. Furred, six-legged rodents with eyes like polished obsidian dart through the dense lichen growth. In the deeper shadows of the alleyways, things with bioluminescent patches pulse softly, a cold blue or a sickly green, communicating in silent flashes. They are abundant, but they are all adapted to this brilliant, frozen world. They are quiet things.

The dynamic, the turbulence, is in the ice itself. The world is not still. The sheer amount of water, freezing and sublimating in the unrelenting light, creates a constant, slow-motion chaos. Ice shelves calve from rooftops with a sound like a tolling bell. Geysers of super-cooled vapor erupt silently from grates in the street, flash-freezing the air into glittering clouds that hang for a moment before settling. The great ribs shift, a centimeter over a year, tearing the buildings they support into new, unstable geometries. This is a place of constant, glacial violence. The inhabitants—squat, broad figures wrapped in layered furs and what looks like cured insect chitin—move with a heavy, deliberate pace, their tools made of sharpened bone, polished stone, and the shed mandibles of the larger fauna. They are not building a city; they are tending to a wound, or perhaps a garden, in the ribs of a dead god, under a sun made of pure, cold light.

It is a place that has been here forever, and will be here forever, changing only in the way a crystal grows: slowly, inevitably, and with absolute, beautiful, terrifying precision.
