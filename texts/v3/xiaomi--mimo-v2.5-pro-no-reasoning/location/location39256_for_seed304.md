The sky is a soft, uniform gray — not threatening, just present. It presses down low, as if trying to touch the rooftops, and sometimes it succeeds. Mist rolls in at ground level, not from any single source but from everywhere at once: from the cracks between stones, from the dark mouths of drains, from the mossy surfaces of walls that have been wet so long they've learned to sweat.

This is a place of weeping architecture.

The town — if you can call it that; it's perhaps forty structures packed into a space that should hold fifteen — clings to the edge of something. Not a cliff, not quite a riverbank. More like a shelf. A terrace of young stone, barely weathered, that juts out over a vast lowland of marsh and standing water. The stone is pale, almost white, and still sharp where it was broken or cut. It's limestone, or something like it — soft enough to carve with hand tools but young enough that it hasn't yet learned to crumble. Every surface that faces the sky has been shaped: into channels, into basins, into grooves that guide water down and around and through.

Because the water is everywhere, and the architecture is not fighting it. It's *managing* it.

The buildings are small. Most are single-room, or two rooms stacked. The walls are thick — stone and clay, sometimes reinforced with bundles of a pale reed that grows in the marshes below. The roofs are shallow-pitched and layered with overlapping slabs, each one carved with fine lines that catch condensation and feed it into clay pipes. These pipes run along the outsides of buildings, down to ground level, and into a network of channels — open, shallow, just wide enough for a hand. The channels are the connective tissue of the place. They run between buildings, under footbridges of flat stone, through the narrow alleys. Some are covered with removable slabs. Some are open to the air, their surfaces dark with moisture and a thin skin of green algae. Water moves through them constantly — not rushing, not still. A slow, deliberate circulation.

You can hear it: a soft, continuous murmur. Not a stream, not rain. Just the sound of water finding its way through stone. It's the loudest thing here, and it's barely a whisper.

The ground is never dry. The stone flags of the pathways are slightly convex, so that water runs to the edges and into the channels. Between the flags, in the joints, moss and liverwort grow in thick, dark cushions — the only vegetation of any note. There are no trees, no gardens, no visible food plants. Just moss, and occasionally a patch of something fungal: pale, shelf-like growths on the undersides of overhanging stones, or clusters of what might be a cultivated mold in shallow trays set into walls. The air smells of wet stone, of clay, of something faintly fungal and not unpleasant — like bread dough left to prove too long.

The people — there are perhaps sixty, maybe eighty — are quiet. Not silent, but speaking in low voices, as if the mist is a sleeping thing they don't want to wake. They move through the alleys with a practiced economy, stepping over channels, ducking under low lintels. They're small, dressed in layers of woven fiber — that same pale reed, split and softened, stitched together with cordage. Some wear garments of felted wool, a grayish-brown, but the wool is rare here, traded from elsewhere. Their skin is pale, their hair dark, and they carry things: bundles of reed, trays of mold, small clay vessels sealed with wax. A few carry tools — knives of knapped stone, awls of bone, hammers with heads of dense river-stone lashed to handles. There is metal here, but it's scarce: a copper knife, a bronze clasp, a small bronze mirror kept wrapped in cloth. These are treasures, passed down or traded at great cost.

The town has a center — not a square, but a widening. A place where three channels converge into a larger basin, perhaps three meters across, lined with fitted stone. The water in it is darker, slower. People come here to fill vessels, to wash, to stand and speak quietly. Above it, a structure larger than the rest: two stories, the upper one slightly overhanging, with a roof of thicker stone slabs. It might be a hall, or a temple, or a storehouse. Its walls are carved more deeply than the others — not with images, but with patterns. Grooves and channels that seem to serve no purpose, except that they sing when the wind moves through them. A low, breathy hum, barely audible. The building is both functional and strange. Something about it makes you feel that it is listening.

Below the town, the shelf drops away — not steeply, but in a series of uneven steps, each one slick with moisture and fringed with pale moss. The marsh stretches out from the base, a flat expanse of shallow water and dark mud, punctuated by stands of the pale reed. The water there is still, reflective, catching the gray sky in a way that makes the horizon disappear. Things move in the reeds — not often, but sometimes. A ripple that isn't the wind. A shape that slides between stems. The people here don't go into the marsh. They harvest the reeds from the edges, wading no deeper than their knees, and they come back quickly. There is something in the water — not a creature, exactly, but a presence. A sense that the marsh has its own logic, and it is not one that favors people.

At night — and the nights are long, the days short and gray — the channels glow. Faintly. A bioluminescence, pale blue-green, that comes from the algae or from something in the water itself. The town becomes a web of light, lines and nodes, pulsing gently with the slow movement of the water. The people sleep in their small rooms, curled on beds of dried reed, and the light moves through the streets like something breathing. The building at the center hums. The marsh below is dark.

Something is being built here. Not just the town — the town is a symptom, an effect. The stone is young, the water is abundant, and the people are shaping something. Every channel, every groove, every carved surface is part of a design that is larger than any one person understands. It's not a machine — or if it is, its purpose is not obvious. It might be a way of living. It might be a way of listening. It might be a way of asking the stone and the water to remember something.

The mist rolls in. The channels flow. The pale reeds sway at the edge of the marsh. And the town, packed and small and wet, persists — patient, deliberate, and strange.
