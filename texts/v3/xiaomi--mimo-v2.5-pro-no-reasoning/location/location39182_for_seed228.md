# The Drowned Hallows

You descend. Not far — perhaps forty meters of spiraling passage cut through limestone that sweats under your palms, warm as skin — and then the shaft opens and you step out onto a ledge, and the light hits you first.

It is *brilliant*. Not daylight — something cleaner, sharper, more deliberate. The ceiling arches above at perhaps fifteen meters, and across its surface, embedded in the rock itself, run long filaments of something that is not glass and not metal and not quite anything you have a name for. They glow with a steady, almost surgical white-blue intensity, and they have been glowing for a very long time. You can tell because the stone around them has *changed* — the minerals nearest the filaments have been gently, persistently irradiated into halos of pale crystal, quartz-like growths that catch and refract the light into a thousand small rainbows that drift across every wet surface. The ceiling looks like a map of frozen lightning, veins of light branching and rebranching through a skin of luminous stone.

The chamber below is roughly oval, perhaps two hundred meters across at its widest — not a cavern exactly, but not a room either. Something between a cathedral and a greenhouse. The floor is terraced, stepping down in broad natural shelves toward a central basin where water has collected, dark and shallow and restless. The shelves are limestone, weathered into smooth scallops and runnels by millennia of water moving over them, and they gleam wetly in the constant bright light.

**The water.** It is everywhere. It seeps from the walls in sheet-thin waterfalls that catch the light and turn to hanging glass. It pools in every depression, in the cupped hollows of rock, in the footprints of old foundations. It drips from the ceiling in a steady percussion — *not quiet*, not quiet at all, because the acoustics of this place are merciless. Every drop that strikes the pool sends a percussive ring echoing outward, and these rings overlap and interfere with each other, so that the basin sings a constant bright chattering, like a room full of typewriters. Beyond that: the deeper sound of water *moving* — a subterranean river somewhere below you, audible through the stone as a low, churning roar that you feel in your sternum as much as hear. And beneath even that, occasionally, a groan — the rock itself shifting, settling, releasing stress in long seismic sighs that make the surface of the pool tremble into rapid concentric circles.

The air is warm — humid and close, maybe thirty degrees, carrying the mineral smell of wet limestone and something faintly sulfurous from deeper below. Your skin beads with sweat immediately. The warmth is geological; this place sits above some deeper heat source, and the water that seeps through is heated on its way down.

**The structures.** They are sparse and ancient, and they are losing their argument with the water. Along the terraces, set into the rock at irregular intervals, are *frames* — upright structures of the same not-quite-material as the ceiling filaments, pale and smooth and jointless, standing two or three meters high. Some are intact, their surfaces beaded with condensation. Others have been undermined by water eating at the limestone beneath them, and they lean at angles, or have toppled entirely, half-submerged in the shallows. They do not appear to be buildings. They might be *instruments* — some have concave faces, dish-like, aimed at different points in the chamber, as if they were once listening or projecting or measuring. A few still faintly hum, a vibration you feel more than hear, a ghostly subsonic that occasionally aligns with the river-sound below and produces a moment of resonance that makes the water in the basin jump and spatter.

There are no walls between them, no streets, no arrangement that implies habitation. They are scattered like grave markers.

**Vegetation** is minimal — the light is bright but it is the wrong light for most photosynthesis, or perhaps it is simply too much, too constant, too *white*. But life has found its way in, as it does. Thin mats of dark green-black algae coat the wet stone in the shallows, slick underfoot. In the crevices of the terraces, where small ledges catch sediment, there are clusters of a pale, almost translucent fern — leafless, just branching stalks — that seem to feed on the light filaments directly, growing *toward* the ceiling with a slow vegetable yearning. And at the water's edge, in the deepest terraces, you can see the occasional tuft of something that looks like moss but is too rigid, too mineral — it crunches when you touch it, and leaves a chalky residue. Silica-fixing organisms, maybe. Building themselves out of the dissolved stone.

**Fauna** is scarce but present. In the pools: small, pale, eyeless shrimp-like things that dart through the algae mats when the resonance-groans startle them. Once, on the far ledge, you catch movement — something low and fast, lizard-shaped, that slips between two toppled frames and vanishes into a crack in the wall. Its skin was the same wet limestone color as the rock. You did not see it clearly. You suspect it did not want to be seen clearly.

**The turbulence.** This is the thing that makes the place feel *alive* rather than merely old. It is not stable. The water level in the basin is rising — slowly, maybe centimeters per year, but visibly enough that the lowest terrace is already submerged, and the structures there have been given over to algae and silt. Periodically — every few minutes — something shifts below. A pressure change. A pocket of gas finding its way up through fractured rock. When this happens, the basin *erupts*: a column of bubbles breaks the surface with a sound like applause, the water churns and sloshes against the terraces, and the old frames shiver in their settings. The ceiling filaments flicker — not off, never off, but *pulse*, as if the system that powers them is responsive to whatever is happening in the deep geology, and the entire chamber strobes for three or four seconds in a rhythm that is not quite regular, not quite random, before settling back to its steady glow.

And then, in the wake of the eruption: a wave of new water, warmer than what's already pooled, rising from below, carrying dissolved minerals that react with the air and produce thin veils of steam that curl upward through the light, catching every spectrum, turning the chamber briefly into something sacred and unspeakable — a cathedral of white fire and rising vapor — before the steam dissipates and the dripping starts again, and the shrimp resume their darting, and the river roars on beneath you.

You understand, standing here, that you are inside something that was *built* — the filaments, the frames, the engineered light — but that was built so long ago that the geology has reclaimed it. The water table has risen. The limestone is dissolving. The structures are sinking, tilting, being slowly unwritten. And yet the filaments still glow. Whatever powered them was designed to outlast the stone itself. They will be the last things to go, you think — still shining in a flooded chamber a hundred thousand years from now, illuminating nothing but water and dark.

The sound never stops. The drips. The resonance hum. The river below. The occasional groan of rock. It is not unpleasant — it has a *logic*, a rhythm, the way a busy river has a rhythm, the way a factory has a rhythm, the way a living thing has a rhythm. Loud and warm and wet and bright and slowly, slowly changing.

You are beneath the world, in a room the world has forgotten, and the water is rising, and the lights are still on.
