At first listen, there is only the roar—a wall of white noise so dense it feels like a physical weight pressing against the eardrums. The setting is blindingly bright, a crisp, searing sunlight that reflects off a geography defined by its lack of liquid. You are standing in what appears to be a dry, mineral-crusted basin, a low-lying depression maybe three hundred meters across, the floor compacted and littered with the husks of ancient, calcified sediment. It looks like a dried sea bed, but there is no salt, only a fine, baked dust that glitters like crushed glass.

This is a hidden ecosystem, a park or atrium enclosed entirely within a structure so vast it generates its own weather. The "sky" above is a matte sheet of high-frequency light, blinding and flat. The enclosure walls, covered in a fossilized layer of glistening black plating, are barely visible through the heat haze and the distance.

Despite the lack of water, the vegetation is startlingly lush—but not green. Instead, the flora consists of towering, rigid structures resembling "sound-plants"—Xerophytic vegetation evolved to thrive on vibration rather than hydration. These are not trees, but fibrous, ochre-and-white stalks of a silica-based biology. They grow in dense clumps of pale, waxy fronds, derived energy from the heat and the noise.

The reason for the abundance of plant life—and the deafening sound—is the settlement woven between them. This is a vertical, primitive urbanity. The inhabitants, small, skittish beings rarely seen outside the nests, have built a city of resonating clay and fired mud. The architecture is organic and chaotic: piles of hollow, horn-shaped containers stacked precariously atop one another, layered with the fibrous stalks of the plants.

There is no machinery, only manual tools of sharpened stone and bone, yet the city screams. Every structure, every stalk, and every artifact is designed to vibrate. The moving air currents within the enclosure are channeled through the hollow buildings and the rigid plant stalks, creating a harmonic, terrifyingly loud resonance. From the lowest pits to the highest stacked dwellings, the air is filled with the thrumming of a thousand wind-harps tuned to a maddening pitch.

Life here is sparse, hidden, and strange. The rare fauna consists of colossal, heavily armored beetles that scuttle across the blindingly bright floor, their carapaces thick enough to withstand the crushing sound waves. They graze on the lichen-like crust using simple, primitive mandibles, moving slowly in the baking heat, ignoring the auditory violence of the world above them.
