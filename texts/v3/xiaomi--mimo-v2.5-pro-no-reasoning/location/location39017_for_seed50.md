The world is a roar.

It is not the roar of a crowd or a fire, but the roar of a machine whose purpose is noise itself. You stand — if ‘stand’ is the right word for clinging to a porous, un-geologically stable perch — on a slope of fresh-minted rock, the colour of a fresh scab or a deep bruise. This is the Erosion Level One: the land is still being born. The slope beneath your feet is not soil but raw, weeping basalt, slick and cold, pocked with vents that spew not heat, but a fine, mineral-rich mist. The temperature is cool, the kind that seeps into your joints and settles there, a permanent resident.

The scale is intimate, but the feeling is not one of comfort. You are in a pocket universe, a wound in the skin of a much larger, and incomprehensibly wetter, world. Above and all around, as far as the eye can see through the perpetual downpour, is the Deep Water. It is not a sky. It is a ceiling of liquid glass, miles thick, millions of tons of pressure held in a trembling, impossible equilibrium. The light is normal, a soft, diffused, pearlescent glow that seems to emanate from the water itself, from the billions of suspended mineral particles and the vast, slow-moving leviathans of bioluminescent jelly that drift like ghosts of mountains far above.

The sound is the thing. It is a wall of white noise, the sonic signature of the entire water column above you vibrating, of currents the size of continents shearing against each other, of the ceaseless, deafening hiss of the direct rainfall that falls not as drops, but as a solid, shimmering curtain. To speak, you would have to place your mouth directly against another’s ear and scream. To think, you must learn a new syntax of silence *within* the noise, a mental tuning fork.

Water is the medium and the tyranny. It is *in* you, a film on your skin, in your lungs if you breathe too deep of the mist, in the very porous rock that groans and shifts under its saturation. The sheer dynamic energy is terrifying. The slope trembles. A section of fresh basalt, the size of a house, groans, cracks, and slides away into a sudden chasm that opens with a wet, grinding sigh, only to be immediately filled with a cascade of superheated water from a newly exposed vent, creating a violent, localized geyser. This is the construction site of a new continent, and you are a flea on the foreman’s boot.

Vegetation is sparse, but strange. Not plants as you know them. Instead, in crevices and on the most stable ledges, grow what the locals (if there were locals, there are not) call Veil Stalks. They are not rooted. They are filamentous, hair-thin structures of silicon and carbon that grow directly out of the rock, swaying violently in the sub-aquatic currents. They have no leaves. They are simply… filters. They draw nutrients from the ceaseless rain and the mineral mist, their tissues incorporating the raw geology. They are less alive than they are *becoming*, their growth so slow it is a form of mineral accretion. They cast the only shadows in this world of diffused light, thin, wavering lines on the weeping rock.

Fauna is rare, and hidden. You see them only as suggestions. A flash of a piez… something. A creature of pressure and darkness, made of crystalline plates and multiple, lidless eyes, clinging to the underside of a newly formed overhang. It sees in spectrums of pressure and vibration, the sudden *crack* of rock, the subtle shift in the roar that means a new current has formed. It is not hunting. It is *feeling* the birth of its world.

The scattered artifacts are the true mystery. Half-submerged in the slurry of new rock, you find them: structures not of natural crystal or basalt, but of a dark, oily, non-reflective metal. They are simple shapes — arches, cubes, spikes — but their surfaces are covered in faint, geometric lines that do not quite follow the rules of perspective. They are pre-metallic, or perhaps a-metallic, forged from a substance the geology here hasn’t invented yet. They hum, a sub-audible vibration that you feel in your bones more than hear, a counter-rhythm to the world’s roar. They are the only things that do not weep. The mineral mist beads and rolls off them, leaving no trace. They are anchors, perhaps. Or tuning forks for a frequency only the water itself understands.

You realize the purpose of this place. It is a nursery. For a world. The sheer weirdness is not in the creatures or the artifacts, but in the *process*. You are not in a landscape. You are in a *happening*. The scale is small — a pocket of creation amidst the deep, drowning oceans of a planet in its violent, watery youth. You are not observing a place. You are witnessing an event, an event whose sound could shatter stone and whose development is measured in the cracking of continents. The air is water, the ground is being made, the light is borrowed, and the only constant is the deafening, beautiful, terrifying roar of it all being built around you.
