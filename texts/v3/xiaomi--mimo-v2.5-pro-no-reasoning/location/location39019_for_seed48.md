# The Basin City

---

You cannot see the sky.

This is the first and most important thing to understand about the Basin. The sky exists — there is a sun up there, and clouds, and whatever else one expects to find above — but from ground level it is invisible. The air is too thick with moisture and heat-haze and the pale mineral haze that rises from the waterways. Looking up, you see brightness: a diffuse, directionless glow that provides just enough light to navigate by but casts no shadows. The glow has a slightly yellowed quality, like old paper. It has been this way for longer than anyone can remember.

The ground is flat. Absolutely, oppressively flat. The Basin sits near sea level in a vast coastal depression — a floodplain that was never quite floodplain, a delta that was never quite delta — and the land extends in every direction without meaningful rise or fall. The horizon, such as it is, dissolves into the haze within a few hundred meters. You cannot see the edges of the city. You cannot see the edges of anything. Direction is a matter of faith and signage.

---

## The Water

It is everywhere, and it is warm.

The water table sits less than a meter below the surface in most of the Basin. Dig down — through the pale, compacted soil, through the thin crust ofite — and you hit water within moments. It rises to meet you. It is not cool, groundwater-cold, the way water underground is supposed to be. It is hot. Thirty-five, forty degrees. In some districts, near the older thermal vents, it steams.

The city is built on top of this water, and the water is slowly winning.

Canals cut through the Basin in long, straight lines — engineered, not natural, carved generations ago and maintained with varying levels of success. They serve as the primary transportation network: wide enough for flat-bottomed barges and processing boats, lined with poured concrete that has calcified and stained over decades into something that looks almost geological. The water in the canals is pale green-gray, opaque with dissolved minerals. It moves slowly. Pumps at major junctions keep it flowing in the correct direction; without them, the water would simply sit, stagnant and steaming, until it found its own way to the sea.

Beyond the canals, the water asserts itself in less organized ways. It seeps through basement floors and lower walls. It pools in the foundations of buildings that weren't designed well enough, or that have settled unevenly over the years. It condenses on every surface — pipes, ceilings, the inside of windows, the skin of anyone who stands still long enough. There are weeping walls in the older districts where the mineral deposits have built up into slow, beautiful terraces of white and rust-orange calcium formations, and the water that seeps through them is almost scalding.

Springs dot the Basin at irregular intervals. Some have been harnessed — enclosed in concrete housings, piped into the city's thermal processing infrastructure. Others are left more or less as they are: hot, mineral-heavy water rising from below, steaming gently in the haze, building small mounds of deposited mineral around their vents. These springs are warm enough to burn. They are fenced, usually, with rusting iron barriers that nobody leans on twice.

The smell of the water is the smell of the city: sulfurous, mineral, faintly metallic, warm. You stop noticing it after a day or two. Visitors notice it immediately.

## The Built World

The Basin is a city, and it has been a city for a long time. Two hundred years of continuous industrial habitation, at least, layered on top of whatever existed before — and something did exist before, because the oldest foundations, the ones exposed where structures have collapsed or been demolished, show stonework that predates the current city by centuries.

The oldest standing buildings are in the central districts. Heavy masonry, thick walls, high ceilings designed to trap heat above the level where people live and work. The stone is local — a dense, dark-grayite quarried from... somewhere, though there are no quarries visible now, and the stone's source has been lost to general indifference. These buildings have settled. They lean, sometimes perceptibly, and their lower courses are encrusted with mineral deposits from decades of contact with the warm, wet ground. They look like formations. Some have stalactite-like mineral growths hanging from their eaves.

The newer construction — and "newer" means the last hundred years, roughly — is steel-frame and poured concrete, with sheet metal cladding in some districts. Industrial architecture: high, flat roofs with ventilation stacks; wide loading bays; large windows that are invariably sealed shut and filmed with condensation. The newer buildings are sharper-edged than the old ones, more angular, but they are already beginning to show the same mineral staining, the same slight settling, the same negotiation with water and heat. Everything in the Basin is in the process of becoming something older-looking than it is.

The infrastructure is extensive and omnipresent. Water pumping stations — squat, heavy buildings with the constant muffled sound of engines inside — anchor every district. Power generation happens in clusters: large plants in the industrial zones, smaller substations throughout the residential and commercial areas. The power grid is overhead in most places, heavy cables strung between iron poles, the poles leaning at various angles, the cables sagging under the weight of mineral deposits that build up on them in the wet air. Rail lines run along the main boulevards, carrying freight on flatbed cars that move slowly and deliberately through the haze. Cooling towers rise in the distance, always visible as slightly darker shapes in the pale bright sky-glow, always steaming.

The city is organized. The streets are laid out on a grid — wide boulevards for rail and heavy vehicle traffic, narrower cross-streets for foot and lighter transport. Buildings are numbered. Districts are named. There are maps posted at intersections, under glass, the glass spotted with mineral deposits. There is a government, a postal service, a system of addresses. The city functions.

But it is emptier than it should be.

## The Emptiness

The Basin was built — or has been built, continuously, over generations — for a population it does not have. The infrastructure implies density: housing blocks for tens of thousands, factory complexes that could employ whole districts, water systems designed to serve a city of millions. What exists, instead, is a city of perhaps fifty thousand, occupying a built environment meant for ten times that number.

The result is a landscape of negative space.

Whole blocks stand dark and sealed, their windows shuttered, their ground-floor entrances bricked up or chained shut. Residential towers have floors that have been closed off — not abandoned, exactly, but taken out of use, locked at the stairwell doors, left to accumulate dust and condensation and the slow mineral creep. Industrial facilities operate at partial capacity: one building in a complex of four, one line in a factory that has five. The machines are old, maintained rather than replaced, and they run at a pace that fills perhaps a third of what they were designed to produce.

Walking through the Basin, you are surrounded by built space that is mostly unused. The streets are wide enough for crowds that never come. The buildings are tall enough to create canyons that very few people walk through. It is not ruin — the infrastructure is maintained, the streets are clean, the water flows — but it is hollow. There is a feeling of being inside something designed for a purpose that no longer fully applies.

The people who are here — the workers, the administrators, the families — are concentrated in certain districts. These districts have a lived-in quality: laundry on lines (it never fully dries, in the Basin), light in windows, voices from open doorways, children in the streets. But walk two blocks in any direction and the density drops sharply. Walk five blocks and you are alone in a canyon of sealed buildings and wet concrete, with only the hum of distant machinery and the sound of water for company.

This is not considered unusual. This is simply how the Basin is. People who live here talk about the "old population" the way other places talk about the weather — a background fact, a thing that was and is no longer, not worth dwelling on. The city was fuller once. The factories ran at capacity. The housing blocks filled every floor. Something changed — economics, probably, or resource depletion, or the simple human preference for places that are less hot, less wet, less dim — and the population shrank to what it is now. The infrastructure was not dismantled. It was simply left where it was, and it continues to function, and the people who remain live within it.

## The Fauna

This is the other defining fact of the Basin, and the one that visitors find most difficult.

The animals are everywhere.

Not metaphorically. Not in the way that a forest has animals, where you might see a bird or a deer if you're quiet and lucky. The Basin's fauna is omnipresent, occupying every space, every surface, every volume of air and water that is not specifically and actively defended against them.

The dominant life forms are crustaceans. The hot, mineral-rich water supports massive bacterial and algal colonies — visible as thick, colored mats on the surface of the canals and the standing water in foundations and basements — and these colonies feed an extraordinary food web. The base of the web is microbial. Everything above it is arthropod.

Small crustaceans — ranging from centimeter-long swimmers to hand-sized crawlers — fill the canals in numbers that make the water seem to move on its own. They are in every puddle, every seep, every condensation drip that pools on a flat surface for more than a few hours. They are pale, mostly, translucent or white, with occasional darker species in the deeper water. They are not aggressive. They are simply there, the way water is there, the way air is there.

Larger species inhabit the spaces between buildings, the interiors of disused structures, the undersides of bridges and overpasses. Some of these are the size of a dog. They are slow-moving, armored, patient. They eat the smaller crustaceans, and the bacterial mats, and occasionally each other. They leave mineral deposits wherever they rest, building small, regular cairns of calcium andite that dot the edges of buildings and the banks of canals.

Above the waterline, the air belongs to the insects. Heat-loving species, adapted to the Basin's temperature and humidity, swarm in clouds that are visible as darkening of the haze. They are not all the same kind — there are dozens of species, filling different niches, from the tiny swarming midges that cluster around light sources to the larger, slower beetles that crawl over every vertical surface. They are attracted to heat, moisture, and organic material. They are inescapable. They land on skin, on clothing, on food left uncovered for more than a few seconds. They die constantly — the Basin's floors and window sills are perpetually littered with small carcasses — and reproduce constantly, and their numbers do not change.

There are also larger animals. Eel-like creatures in the deeper canals, glimpsed as dark shapes in the green-gray water. Birds — or something like birds, adapted to the dim light and the insect abundance, roosting in the upper stories of buildings in great numbers, their droppings coating the rooftops in thick, mineral-enriched layers. Bats, or bat-analogues, that emerge in the slightly cooler hours and fill the air with erratic, silent flight. Worms in the saturated soil, visible wherever the ground is dug, thick and pale and numerous.

None of this is considered unusual by the Basin's residents. It is managed, not eliminated. Buildings are sealed — screens on every vent, rubber gaskets on every door, mesh over every window that can be opened. Entry-ways are designed as airlocks of sorts: an outer door, a small chamber, an inner door, with the space between kept clear of debris and standing water. The industrial facilities have dedicated pest-management teams that maintain the barriers, clear the drainage systems, and remove fauna that breaches the seals. It is routine, unremarkable, the same way that snow removal is routine in a northern city.

Outside, in the streets, the fauna is simply part of the landscape. Workers walk through clouds of insects without swatting. Children kick aside the small crustaceans that accumulate in the gutters. The larger animals are given space — everyone knows where the eels are, where the roosting colonies are, where the big crawlers tend to rest — and this space is respected the way one respects a busy road. You stay out of their way. They stay out of yours. The equilibrium is old and stable and not particularly fraught.

The noise of the fauna is constant but quiet — a background texture, like distant traffic or wind. The clicking and skittering of crustaceans. The thin hum of insect wings. The occasional wet splash of something entering or exiting the water. It blends with the sound of the city's machinery until the two are indistinguishable.

## The Heat and the Dim

The Basin is hot. Not dangerously so — not the heat of a furnace or a desert — but relentlessly, thoroughly, damply hot. Thirty-five degrees on an average day, forty on a bad one. The heat comes from the latitude, from the geothermal activity below, and from the city's own industry. The factories and processing plants generate waste heat, and in a landscape this flat and enclosed by haze, the heat has nowhere to go. It pools.

The humidity makes the heat worse. The air is saturated — not quite to the point of constant rain, but close. Sweat does not evaporate. The body does not cool itself. People in the Basin move slowly, deliberately, conserving energy. They drink constantly — water, always water, drawn from the city's filtration systems, which remove the sulfur and the heavy minerals but cannot remove the heat. Water in the Basin is warm from the tap. There is no cold water. People who want cold water use mechanical chillers, and the chillers are one of the most common pieces of household equipment.

The dimness is a product of the haze. The sun's light enters the atmosphere above the Basin and is scattered, diffused, softened by the moisture and the mineral particulates before it reaches the ground. The result is a perpetual overcast that is not quite overcast — there are no distinct clouds, just a uniform brightening that provides illumination without direction. There are no shadows in the Basin. There are no sharp edges of light and dark. Everything is lit from every direction, faintly, evenly, a gray-white glow that renders colors muted and distances uncertain.

The city compensates with artificial light. Every building is lit from within, and the streets have lamps — older ones are gas-fed, newer ones electric — that provide pools of warmer, yellower light at regular intervals. The industrial districts blaze at night, or what passes for night: the difference between the "day" dimness and the "night" dimness is subtle, a slight deepening of the gray, but the factories run on mechanical schedules and their lights mark the hours clearly. The residential districts dim their streetlamps at designated hours, and the interiors of occupied buildings go dark one by one as people sleep.

In the darkness — in the real darkness, inside a sealed room with the lights off — the sounds of the Basin become very clear. The machinery. The water. The fauna. The slow settling of old buildings. The drip of condensation.

## What Happens Here

The Basin is a production center. It mines and processes the minerals that the water carries — dissolved metals, calcium compounds, sulfur, various other elements leached from the geology below — and it manufactures goods from those minerals. The economy is industrial: extraction, refinement, fabrication, transport. The goods leave the Basin on rail lines that run in four directions to... elsewhere. The Basin does not much concern itself with elsewhere.

People work in shifts. The factories run around the clock, three shifts of eight hours, and the city's rhythm is organized around them. Shift changes are the busiest times on the streets: small crowds of workers moving between the residential districts and the industrial zones, heads down, moving efficiently through the heat and the insects and the wet. Between shifts, the streets are quiet. The sound of the city is the sound of its machinery and its fauna and its water, without much human contribution.

The workers are skilled, in their way. They know the machines, the water systems, the fauna patterns. They know which streets flood when the pumps falter, which buildings are too settled to maintain safely, which springs are hottest and where new ones are likely to emerge. This knowledge is practical and passed down through employment, not through any formal system. A new worker in the Basin learns by working alongside experienced ones, and what they learn is not theory but practice: how to live here, how to work here, how to keep the water moving and the machines running and the fauna out of the food supply.

There is culture, if you look for it. Drinking establishments in the residential districts, warm and close and dimly lit, where people gather after shifts and talk quietly. A music tradition that favors low frequencies and long sustains — sounds that carry through the haze and the humidity without being swallowed by it. A cuisine built around the Basin's one abundant resource: hot water, used for cooking, for preservation, for the slow extraction of flavor from the dried and preserved goods that come in on the rail lines. Hot-water baths, public and private, where people sit in the mineral-rich water that is chemically identical to the water that floods their basements, but controlled, enclosed, clean, and relaxing rather than threatening.

It is, in its way, a complete world. A difficult one, certainly — hot, wet, dim, loud with the quietness of fauna and machinery, hollow with the emptiness of unused infrastructure — but complete. People are born here, grow up here, work here, raise children here, grow old here, and die here. They are buried in the saturated ground, in cemeteries that steam faintly in the mornings, their graves marked with metal plates that corrode slowly into new mineral formations.

It is, despite everything, normal. That is perhaps the most remarkable thing about the Basin: that none of this is strange to the people who live in it. The heat is weather. The water is landscape. The fauna is ecology. The emptiness is economy. The dimness is climate. It is a place, like any other place, shaped by its conditions and inhabited by people who have shaped themselves to fit those conditions, and the fit is old enough and deep enough that neither the people nor the place can easily be imagined apart from each other.
