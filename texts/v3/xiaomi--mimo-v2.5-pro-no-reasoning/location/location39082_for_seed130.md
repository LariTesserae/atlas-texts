# The Discharge

There is no ground. You must understand this first. What you walk on is a floor — plate iron, riveted, perforated in places to let something drain downward into darkness that has no bottom anyone has found. The iron has been there so long it has its own geology: layers of corrosion on top of corrosion, a stratum of rust so old it has become something else,iteite smooth andite dark, almostite likeite shale. You could chip it with a hammer and read the epochs in the cross-section. Where your boots meet the surface, the sound is a dull, wrong tone — metal under metal — and beneath that, always beneath that, the roar.

---

## The Roar

It is deafening. It is constant. It comes from everywhere and nowhere because it *is* the place, the way a heartbeat is not a sound a body makes but the condition of a body being alive.

The source, as far as anyone has ever mapped it, is the machinery — and there is so much machinery. Enormous turbines housed in structures the size of cathedrals, set far apart across the flat iron plain, connected by pipe-forests and conduit-lattices that run overhead and underfoot. The turbines spin. The pistons reciprocate. The gears engage and disengage on cycles no one has timed completely, because timing them would require being close enough to hear a single mechanism over the thousand others, and there is no such place.

The sound has texture. Near the great halls it is a physical pressure in the sternum, a vibration that makes your vision blur. In the open yards between — and the yards are vast, a hundred meters across, two hundred, iron floor stretching in every direction under dark sky — the roar is more diffuse, a wash of low frequencies overlaid with higher metallic shrieks from distant pipe-joints and the arrhythmic *clang-ga-clang* of valve assemblies opening and closing against corroded seats. You learn to interpret it the way you'd learn to read weather. A rising harmonic from the northwest means the Number Four condensate exchange is cycling. A sudden cessation of a particular high tone followed by a deep, bowel-shaking *whump* means a pressure gate somewhere has tripped and reset. The place speaks constantly in a language of industrial noise, and it has been speaking for longer than any record.

---

## The Wet

Everything drips.

The air is cool — perpetually, obstinately cool, maybe eight degrees, maybe ten — and the machinery generates heat, and the collision of these two facts means condensation on every surface. The great pipes overhead weep. The walls of the turbine halls run with rivulets that trace the vertical seams between bolted plates before collecting in rust-colored streams along the floor channels. The floor itself, being iron, is always damp, always slightly slick, and where it is perforated you can hear water falling into spaces below — dripping, trickling, occasionally rushing when a larger collection finds a drain.

There is standing water in every low place. Not deep — the floors are graded, sloped, channeled by design, because whoever built this place understood that water would be a permanent condition and engineered accordingly. The channels are perhaps a hand's width deep, carved into the iron plating or constructed as separate gutters bolted to the surface. They carry the condensate and the weeping-water toward collection basins — great reservoir tanks sunk into the floor between the larger structures, their surfaces black and still, reflecting nothing because there is almost nothing to reflect.

Sometimes fog. The temperature differentials create it — warm air rising from a turbine vent meets the cool ambient air and a wall of white materializes across the middle distance, hangs for minutes or hours, then shreds apart as conditions shift. Walking through one is like walking through the breath of the place. The moisture clings. Your clothes are never dry. Your skin is never dry. Everything you carry develops a skin of damp, and metal objects in your possession begin to rust within days, as though the Discharge is incorporating them.

---

## The Structures

They are sparse — that is the strangeness. For a fully built environment, there is so much *space*. The great halls stand far apart, separated by the iron plains and the pipe-lattices and the reservoir tanks, each one an industrial cathedral hundreds of meters long, its walls sheer and featureless except for the bolt-lines and the rivet-patterns and the condensation tracks. They are built of different metals — some iron, some something heavier and darker that does not rust in the same way, some a pale alloy that weeps a white mineral deposit instead of rust. Each hall is given over to a single enormous process: turbines, compressors, smelters (though what they smelt is unclear; the intake is just corroded material from elsewhere in the Discharge), electrolysis tanks, chemical processors. All still running. All cycling. All producing... something. Materials move along conveyors from hall to hall, traveling the long open distances on elevated rail-systems that groan and click overhead. What the materials are at any given stage of processing is hard to say. Sludge. Granulate. A paste. Bars of dull metal. Powder in sealed drums. It cycles and cycles and the end-product, if there is one, has not been identified.

Between the halls, in the open yards, there are smaller structures: relay stations, junction housings, access points to the sub-floor, and the reservoir tanks, and the pipe-forests — some pipes thin as an arm, some wide enough to walk through, all of them ancient, all of them sweating, all of them singing with internal pressure. Some pipes are buried in accumulations of mineral deposit so thick they've become walls themselves, organic-looking ridges ofite calcium and iron oxide that the weeping water has built over centuries, stalactites forming beneath pipe-joints and meeting stalagmites growing up from the floor channels below.

You could walk for hours across the flat iron plain between structures and see nothing but the perforated metal underfoot, the pipe-lattice overhead, the distant black walls of the next hall resolving slowly through the gloom, and the fog.

---

## The Dark

There is almost no light. The sky, what you can see of it through the pipe-lattice and the conduit-web, is a uniform dark grey — overcast to the point of opacity, a lid of cloud that presses down and seems to merge with the upper structures. Whether there is a sun behind it is an academic question. The light that reaches the floor is a residual glow, grey and directionless, enough to see shapes and movement but not color. Everything is the color of iron and damp.

The machinery provides some light — the occasional furnace-glow through a vent, the amber of a warning indicator on a relay station, the cold blue-white flicker of electrical discharge from an ancient transformer that sparks and sputters but never quite fails. In the deep interior of the turbine halls, it is properly dark, and the noise is a wall you can't see past. In the open yards at what passes for the Discharge's version of midday, the light is like the inside of a very old pewter bowl: dim, grey, reflective on the wet iron surfaces, giving everything a muffled quality as though the world has been wrapped in cloth.

There are no lights installed for the benefit of anyone. The Discharge does not illuminate itself for visitors. Any light you bring — and you should bring light — is swallowed within meters by the scale and the dark, reduced to a small cone of relevance in an ocean of grey.

---

## The Absence

No animals. No birds. No insects. The sound alone would make this place uninhabitable for anything with ears tuned to the world's normal frequencies. But even beyond the sound, there is something about the Discharge that repels life — not hostile, not toxic (the air is chemically strange but breathable, the water is mineral-heavy but not poisonous), just... *not for them*. The place does not notice the living. It was not built for the living. Whatever intelligence designed it, whatever crew once tended it, they are gone so completely that the question feels meaningless, like asking who tends the tides.

And yet. In the darkest corners of the reservoir tanks, where the water is deepest and stillest, there are mats of something on the surface — not quite plant, not quite fungus, a dark grey-green film that might be alive or might be a mineral precipitate that happens to resemble life. Near the oldest smelters, where the mineral deposits have built up into shelf-structures the size of tables, there are growths that could be lichen, or could be crystalline formations that mimic lichen's fractal logic. Nothing moves with purpose. Nothing eats or breathes or reproduces in any way that has been confirmed. The Discharge is not dead — it is far too loud, far too active, far too *busy* to be dead — but it is not alive in any category that includes the biological.

---

## The Age

This is what unsettles most. The erosion is not the erosion of ruin — nothing is collapsing, nothing is failing catastrophically — but the erosion of *deep time*. Every surface is worn. Every edge is rounded. The rivet-heads are smooth domes where once they were hexagonal. The bolt-lines are gentle undulations. The floor-plating has been walked or flowed or weathered so long that its surface texture is more geological than architectural — and no one has walked here in any memory. The wear is from water, from vibration, from the slow creep of thermal expansion across millennia of heat-cycling. The place has been running so long that it has *eroded into its final form*. It is not decaying; it has finished decaying. What remains is the stable state — the bones of the machine, stripped of anything that could be stripped, settled into a configuration that will persist until the metal itself atomizes.

Some structures have fused. The pipe-forests in places have become solid masses, individual pipes corroding into each other until they are one object, a lattice of metal that has the texture of bone. The conveyor systems have in places become one with the floors they are bolted to, the boundaries between mechanism and substrate dissolving over time. In the oldest halls — and they are all old, but some are *older*, their foundations sunk deeper into whatever lies beneath the iron floor, their walls thicker with mineral accretion — the machinery has achieved a kind of petrification. The turbines still turn, but the housings have become geological formations around them. The gears still engage, but they are embedded in walls of corrosion-product that have the density and fracture-pattern of sedimentary rock. The machine has become its own fossil, and the fossil still runs.

---

## The Activity

It does not rest. The Discharge cycles.

Steam vents open on schedules that span hours — you can be standing in an empty yard and suddenly a plume of hot vapor erupts from a pipe fifty meters away, roaring white in the gloom, hanging in the cool air, then dissipating into the ambient damp. Conveyor systems carry material from hall to hall in slow, deliberate loads, the clanking of the carriages on the tracks a rhythmic punctuation beneath the constant roar. The reservoir tanks rise and fall on cycles of their own, water accumulating from condensation and weeping, then draining through sub-floor channels into processing areas where it is heated, filtered, mineralized, and either returned to the surface through vent-lines or stored in deeper reservoirs. Pressure builds and releases. Heat accumulates and dissipates. The fog forms and breaks and reforms.

There are moments of synchronization — when several processes align and the sound shifts, deepens, acquires a harmonic that you feel in your teeth, and the ground vibrates at a frequency that makes the standing water in the channels tremble in standing-wave patterns. These moments last minutes. Then the cycles fall out of alignment again and the sound returns to its baseline chaos.

Nothing is being made. Nothing is being achieved. The Discharge processes its own remnants, maintains its own systems, produces the conditions for its own continuation. It is a machine whose purpose is to be a machine. It has been doing this for so long that the question of original purpose has the same relevance as asking what the Earth's mantle is *for*. It is. It runs. It roars in the dark, in the wet, in the cool, and the iron floor stretches flat to the horizon in every direction, and the pipe-lattice crosses the grey sky like a cage, and the fog rolls through, and the condensation drips, and the ancient turbines turn, and turn, and turn.
