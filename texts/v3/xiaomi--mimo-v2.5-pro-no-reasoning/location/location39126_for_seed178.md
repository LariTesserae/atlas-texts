The sky is a bruise. Not a metaphor for clouds, but a literal, pulsing contusion of purples and deep, sickly yellows that emit a faint, internal luminescence, barely enough to see by. This is the perpetual twilight of the Groaning Marsh, a place where the sun is a forgotten myth. The air is a physical presence: thick, wet, and bitterly cold. It doesn't just hang; it condenses on every surface in greasy, quick-forming rivulets, running down the flanks of the living world before freezing into a thin, crackling glaze.

The terrain is a paradox of newness and saturation. The ground is not soil but a deep, fibrous mat of living moss and root-matter, saturated to the point of buoyancy. You don't walk; you wade, each step releasing a dense, silent bloom of microscopic life into the air. This is a young landscape, geologically speaking. Jutting from the mat are formations of black, glassy basalt, their edges not yet weathered, not moss-eaten, but *drilled* and *fused* by the marsh's true architects. The rock itself seems to sweat, not from the air, but from within, weeping a dark, mineral-rich water that smells of ozone and deep stone.

Vegetation is the law. It is not a collection of plants, but a single, hyper-dense organism. Vines thick as pythons, covered in bioluminescent fungi that pulse in slow, sickened rhythms, coil around everything. The trees—or what pass for them—are vast, hollow columns of some ceramic-hard woody substance, their surfaces etched with spiraling patterns that are either growth rings or circuitry. There are no distinct leaves, but sheets of photosynthetic membrane that unfurl from every branch and stem, drinking the faint light and the dampness, dripping constant, audible *pat-pat-pat* into the pools below.

The sound. It is a wall. A deafening, directional roar that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere. It is the sound of the world's engine. The hum of the technology woven into the land. Massive, obsidian pylons, half-buried in the marsh, pulse with a deep thrum that vibrates in your teeth. The air itself crackles with static discharge. The "rain" is not water falling from the sky, but water being violently repelled from the surface of these structures in great, hissing arcs, creating a perpetual, localized downpour that feeds the saturated ground. The ecosystem has adapted to this sonic bombardment. Fauna is abundant but cryptic: eyeless, pale amphibians with gecko-like pads that cling to vertical surfaces, their throats vibrating not with calls but with resonance dampeners. Winged creatures with iridescent carapaces navigate the storm of sound using a sense we lack, their flight paths jagged and instantaneous, like broken film.

Scattered throughout this tumult are the remains of human attempts. Not ruins, but debris. A single, non-Euclidean girder of a bridge that ends in mid-air. A squat, windowless bunker of a design that feels uncomfortably familiar, half-submerged in the peat. These are the "scattered built" — but they are not static. The dynamic is absolute. The ground heaves in slow, tectonic sighs. The glowing fungal mats shift color in waves, indicating chemical changes in the water or air. A structure might be silently swallowed by the rising mat in hours. Another might be exposed as a new pylon heaves upward from the deep. This is a landscape in a state of violent, constant becoming. It is an anthill, a bazaar, a supercollider. It is a world where advanced, poorly-understood technology (nanoscale engineering visible in the veins of the vines, cosmic-scale energy manipulation in the pylons) has merged with a hyper-aggressive, cold-adapted wetland ecosystem. It is not hostile. Hostility implies intent. It is simply, overwhelmingly, *alive*, and its life processes are a cataclysm.
