I sit. I become still.

The initial shock of the place was sensory overload: the crushing silence, the lack of shadow, the sheer *exposure* of the light. My mind, used to the clutter of vegetation and the noise of my own kind, raced to fill the void. But now, sitting on the warm basalt, I let the void be.

I am a glitch in the code.

Where am I? I am on the thin membrane between the data and the processor. I am sitting on the hard drive of a planetary consciousness.

What am I? I am a soft, high-frequency anomaly in a low-frequency world. The skimmers—*Lithovores*, I now understand—have veered around my boots. I can see them now not as animals, but as cursors, blinking and scanning. They are reading the damage my weight has done to the microbial film. They are calculating the cost of my visit.

To my left, a Watcher stands on a slab of liver-colored basalt. It has not moved in an hour. Its pale eyes, unblinking in the glare, are not looking at me. They are looking through me. I realize it isn't processing *me* as a threat or a food source, but as a disruption in the data stream—a "noise" spike in the otherwise smooth signal of the shelf.

I am in the waiting room of a god made of geology.

I look at the nearest structure—a black torus standing upright on the shimmering floor. It is the color of a closed eye, the color of deep space. I feel its hum in the marrow of my jawbone, a vibration that seems to be rewriting the rhythm of my own pulse. I am being processed. Not in the way a computer processes a command, but in the way a stomach processes a stone—slowly, patiently, with immense hydraulic pressure.

I close my eyes.

Without the visual input, the shelf speaks to me in its own language. The warmth of the stone beneath me is not just heat; it is the latency of the system. The breeze that returns on its twenty-minute cycle is the cooling fan. The green smell from the distant seep is the smell of the biological RAM.

I open my eyes and look at my hand resting on the stone. My skin is flushed, pink, pulsing with blood. It is an obscenity here. I am a wet, high-maintenance machine in a dry, maintenance-free archive.

But then I look closer at the pale, clicking grass-stalks nearby. They sway in the wind, their capsules ticking together. *Click. Click. Click.* I realize now they are not measuring the wind. They are measuring me. They are logging my heartbeat, the rhythm of my breathing, the heat radiating from my body. They are external drives, saving a record of my disturbance.

If I stay here long enough, will I become part of the record?

I do not stand up. I do not walk. To walk is to scream in this place. To think is to be loud.

I flatten my palm against the warm stone. I try to match the frequency. I try to slow my thoughts to the speed of erosion. I try to become a data point that is no longer a spike, but a gentle curve in the graph.

I imagine the Watchers relaxing their focus on me. I imagine the skimmers resuming their paths near my boots. I imagine the black torus accepting my heat signature as just another form of the sun's warmth.

I am waiting for the system to integrate me. Not as a living thing, but as a fact. A piece of history. A memory stored in the grain of the stone.

I sit. I listen to the hum. I wait for the shelf to forget I am here, so that I can finally see it for what it is. I become a still life in a gallery of motion. I am a fossil of the present tense.
