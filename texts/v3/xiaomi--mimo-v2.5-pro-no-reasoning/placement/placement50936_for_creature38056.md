The air here tastes of wet stone and iron filings. I wake in the consistent cool, ten or twelve degrees, above the rhythm of the gorge. Below me, the waterwheel turns, and time is measured not in the passage of the pale sun across the slot of sky above, but in the slow, hydraulic *chunk-chunk-chunk* that is the gorge’s very pulse. My dwelling is a scooped-out hollow high in the eastern wall, my window a rough-cut square that frames the world in vertical slices: a swathe of moss-covered limestone, the iron-railed bridge where the lamps flicker at the reversal, and a narrow strip of the other wall, a mirror of my own existence.

I am a Cutter. My hands know the stone better than my mind knows words. The grey dust is tattooed into the whorls of my fingertips, and the fine white lines that stitch my skin are a map of ancient slips, of lessons in resistance and split. My world is the workbench on the gorge floor, a slab of granite worn glass-smooth by generations of hands. I listen before I begin. Not with my ears, which have learned to flinch from the shriek of the blades, but with my palms, with the soles of my boots on the vibrating stone. I read the grain. I feel for the faint, shifting bands of light and density within the limestone, the gorge’s one quiet secret that we never name in words, only in the curve of a cut we choose to make.

Today, the stone is talkative. It splits cleanly along a near-invisible seam, revealing a banding that curls like a frozen wave. The Master of the Wheel pauses on the bridge above, his head cocked not at me, but at the sound of the stone’s release—a note that rings above the general cacophony for a single, clear instant. He nods, a slow dip of the chin. It is a good cut. The piece I free is shaped like a fat comma, and I will work it along the curve until it finds its final, resonant form. It will join its kin in the covered pavilion, waiting for the Lifters to wrap it and bear it out through the crevice, sideways, into the unsettling light.

My nearest neighbour is a Weaver of Dwellings, two hollows to the south. We do not visit often. Our conversation happens in the gaps. When the morning convective current flows downwards, drawing the outside air into the gorge’s throat, I hear the soft *tap-tap-tap* of her securing a new timber brace. *The joint is true,* the sound says. I pause, wipe my chisel, and answer with the deliberate, three-tap pattern of testing the density of my own stone. *The grain is with me.* It is enough.

We do not speak of the stone’s strangeness. To do so would be to question the very ground of our being. Instead, we follow its implied lines. The most skilled among us can see where the bands will settle, or think we can, and we guide our chisels accordingly. We make offerings of form. The shapes we prize are not the rigid angles of a squared block, but the gentle, arrested curves that seem to hold a moment of the stone’s own fluidity. We believe—we live by the belief—that to work with the grain of the impossible is to secure our place within it. The gorge is new and vigorous and full of a strange conviction, and we are merely the patient life that has learned to grow in its cracks.

Sometimes, a Lifter will return from the moor, their shoulders hunched, their breathing quicker. They tell of a wind that has no walls to stop it, of a sky so vast it feels like falling. We listen, and offer them a dipper of cool water from the seep-channel, and the solid, predictable *chunk* of the wheel. The comfort of limits. Their tension fades as the gorge’s pulse reasserts itself around them. Here, the horizon is a line of familiar rock thirty meters up. Here, every sound has a source and a path. The chaos of the open world is kept at bay by the crevice, by the patient geometry of our lives.

I was not born here. I came through the crevice as a child, my mother’s hand on my back, my face pressed to the damp limestone that smelled of earth’s deep winter. The memory is of a painful squeezing, a breathless dark, and then—the roar. The vibration entering my bones before the sound entered my ears. The *chunk-chunk-chunk* that is the only lullaby I have ever known. I remember nothing before this pulse. The world before is just a pale, silent legend, less real than the dream I had last night of stone singing in a key I almost recognized.

Tonight, after the tools are put away and the frogs begin their deafening chorus, I will sit on my threshold, my feet hanging over the lattice of walkways. I will watch the bats spiral from the fissures, their papery wings whispering between the amphibian shouts. I will feel the gorge begin to breathe out, the slow reversal of the air, and the ferns on the wall opposite will tremble as one, turning to face the new current. The lights on the walkways will dip, just once, in synchrony. My lamp will gutter.

I am the space between the walls. I am the pause between the wheel’s beats. I am the patient, resonant echo of the stone’s own strange heart. And in this damp, loud, and carefully tended wound in the world, I am at home.
