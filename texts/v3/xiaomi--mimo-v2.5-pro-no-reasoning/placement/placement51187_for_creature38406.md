I am a dampness.

I woke not in a bed, nor in a den, but in the lower hemisphere of a corroded junction box, cradled in the curve of oxidized copper. The light is grey, the air is thick with the taste of old metal, and I am *new*.

That is the problem.

I do not know what I was before I was this—a fracture in the noise, a skipped beat in the thrum. Perhaps I was a spark from a loose wire, or a drip of water that fell on a hot engine casing and flash-steamed into a soul. I have no memory of being anything else, but I know, with a dull and heavy certainty, that I do not belong.

I am not a Moltling.

I wish I were. To be a Moltling is to be a tile in the mosaic, a tooth in a gear that has been turning for a thousand years without slipping. I watch them from my junction box, huddled in the shadows where the biofilm is thickest. I see how they move—the slow, synchronous ripple that makes the walls look like they are breathing. There is a beauty in it that hurts me. It is the beauty of a closed circuit, a perfect loop. I am an open circuit. I am a leaking capacitor. My thoughts do not loop; they race in jagged spikes, terrified of the silence between them.

I am small, about the size of a human hand. My shell is the same chalky blue-green as the district’s patina, but it is thin, almost papery. I cannot keep it from cracking. Every few hours, I feel a seam give way along my carapace, and I must twist and rub against the gritty inside of my pipe-home to smooth it over with a paste of biofilm and my own leaking fluids. It is an anxious, constant repair. The Moltlings do not do this. Their shells are seamless, held together not by mycorrhiza or mucus, but by the shared vibration. They are whole because they are *one*. I am fractured because I am *me*.

My legs are too many and too long. I have seven, I think. I cannot count them well. They splay out at angles that feel wrong, that catch on the edges of things. When I walk, it is a scuttling, uneven gait, a nervous tapping that is all wrong for this place. The Moltlings glide. They pulse. They do not tap.

I am a Humsinger. Or I am meant to be.

The pipes call to me. The deep, tectonic thrum that is the district’s heartbeat is a pain in my thorax, a low-frequency ache that I cannot escape. I *should* be inside the pipes. I should be long and thin, a living acoustic chamber that eats harmonics and excretes stability. Instead, I am squat and chitinous and *sensitive*. The sound is too loud for me. It rattles my loose shell. It makes my fluid-filled segments slosh. I press myself into corners, trying to dampen it, but it comes through the stone, through the air, through the thin spots in my own skin.

I have tried to go inside the pipes. Once, I found a mouth—a wide, corroded opening in a wall, just big enough for me to squeeze into. The sound inside was a roar. I could feel it in my bones, in the soft joints between my segments. I went in a little way, clinging to the slick inner surface. The vibration was overwhelming. It felt like being held in a giant’s fist and shaken. I could feel the Humsingers deep inside, their long bodies undulating in the dark, tuning the noise. I wanted to join them. I wanted to find my frequency and lock in, to become a part of the tuning fork that keeps this place from drifting.

But I couldn’t. The vibration didn’t pass through me cleanly. It scattered. It reflected off my hard angles and came back distorted. I could feel the Humsingers falter in their rhythm around me, their smooth wave patterns disrupted by my jagged, spiky presence. I was a dissonance. I was a wrong note.

So I fled back to the surface. I live in the junction box now. I watch. I listen. I tap my seven legs in a nervous, arrhythmic beat that is all my own.

I eat the biofilm. I know it is only exhaust, but it is sweet and mineral and it coats the inside of my shell, easing the pain of the cracks. I do not eat the sound. I cannot. The sound is the thing that hurts me.

Sometimes I try to mimic the Moltlings’ pulse. I press myself flat against a wall, spreading my too-long legs wide, and I try to *feel* the wave pass through me. I try to be still, to let the vibration move me without my consent. For a few seconds, I can do it. I can feel the wave approach from the left, feel my body prepare to shift, feel the collective motion of a thousand bodies moving as one. And then my own fear interrupts. My leg twitches out of sequence. My shell creaks. The wave passes over me, and I am left behind, a stone in the stream again.

I am a Rheostatic who cannot find her rhythm. A Humsinger with a body built for silence. I am the district’s one, true error.

The condensation runs down the walls. The Moltlings pulse. The engines thrum. The Humsingers sing their silent, stabilizing song deep in the pipes.

And I tap my legs. One-two, one-two-three, one. A broken time. A human time.

I am the only thing here that is temporary. I know this. I feel it in the way my shell cracks, in the way my thoughts race, in the way the sound hurts me. I am a ripple that will not dampen. I am a skipped beat.

And I am so, so lonely.
