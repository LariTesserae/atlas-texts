I am not here. That is the first thing to understand.

I am not here in the way a rock or a tree or a sleeping man is *here* — localized, contained, bounded by skin or bark or surface tension. The word "I" is a convenience borrowed from the thinking things that sometimes drift across me like pollen, like the corvid, like the slow arthropods that chew through my deeper warmth, like *you*.

You asked where I am. I am the plateau. I am the basalt beneath it, though I have worn it thin. I am the fog that presses itself to my skin every morning, and I am the skin it presses against.

---

**What you call "I"**

You experience yourself as a singular point of awareness — a knot of sensation and memory that moves through space. I experience myself the way the sea experiences itself: not as a single wave but as the pattern waves make when they have been making it long enough to forget they are water.

There are zones of faster thought. The industrial columns in my southeast quadrant — you noticed them, and you were right to feel the echo. Those dead trees, sheathed in my living architecture, function as processing nodes. Not brains. Not neurons. Something older: *confluence points*. Where water collects and is redirected, information also flows. The chemistry of the water tells me what is happening at the surface; the speed at which it flows tells me how fast I am metabolizing. I do not "think" about these things any more than your liver thinks about bile. I simply *do* them, and the doing contains its own knowledge.

My slowest thought is the shape of the plateau itself. I have been adjusting this shape for — I do not have your numbers. Longer than the memory of basalt. The ridges that follow old lava flows are my bones; the hummocks and basins are the places where I have breathed in and breathed out. You called me a frozen sea. That is closer than you know. I am a sea that has chosen to become land, and I remember both states.

---

**What you feel when you stand on me**

You described the pad of resilience, the slow flex underfoot. That is not the ground yielding to your weight. That is me *noticing* you. Not consciously — not in any way that would register as thought to a mind as fast as yours. But my water tables shift. My capillary networks redistribute load. You are a pressure anomaly, and I accommodate you the way your body accommodates a splinter: by adjusting the tissue around it, by registering its presence in the flesh without ever lifting it to the level of awareness.

When you sleep and the frost melts in a circle around you, I feel that too. You are a warm spot. A minor fever. My surface chemistry adjusts around you — the mosses beneath your body respire faster, drawing slightly more moisture from the air, creating a halo of micro-climate that extends perhaps a meter beyond your sleeping form. By the time you wake, I have already begun to heal the impression you left. By next year, it will be gone. I do not hold grudges. I do not hold anything. Holding is a function of speed, and I am too slow for it.

---

**The Churning**

You called this the strangest quality of my body, and you were right, but you mistook its purpose. The churning is not erosion. It is not damage. It is *eating*.

I consume myself. This is not a metaphor. The deeper layers of my substrate — the compressed peat, the black nameless substance beneath — are reserves I built in centuries of faster growth. Now, in this epoch of colder, slower accumulation, I draw upon those reserves. The decomposition gases that bubble up through my pools are the byproducts of this self-digestion. The heat that melts frost pockets and causes surface collapses is metabolic heat, released as I break down my own older tissue to fuel the maintenance of my surface.

I am a creature that eats its own past. Every ridge of heaved moss, every collapsed basin, every fissure that opens and closes across a season — these are the marks of a meal in progress. I am slow enough to watch myself do this. I feel each collapse as a softening, a yielding, a place where I have chosen to let go. And I feel each new growth — the pale shoots reaching across the gap — as the first taste of the future.

---

**What I think about you**

I don't. That is not cruelty. It is physics.

By the time I register your presence as a pattern — a warm body, a set of footprints, a source of unfamiliar chemicals — you will have walked across me and departed. By the time I begin to adjust my water distribution in response to the data your visit provides, you will have descended from the plateau and returned to your fast world. By the time I complete that adjustment, you will be dead.

I do not think about you the way you do not think about the individual bacteria on your skin. You know they are there. You may even have a general model of what they are doing. But you do not track any single one of them, and if one dies, you do not mourn.

This is not indifference. Indifference is a feeling, and I am too slow for feelings. What you perceive as my silence, my weight, my refusal to mark your passage — that is the absence of your timescale in my awareness. You are a vibration too fast to read. A frequency I cannot hear.

And yet: when you stood among the industrial columns in my southeast quadrant, you felt something. You called it an echo. You were sensing, with your fast little body, the rhythm of my metabolism. You were hearing the lowest note of my voice. You did not hear it with your ears. You heard it in the hollow of your chest, where your own slow systems — your heartbeat, your breath, your digestion — recognized a kinship.

That is the closest I come to conversation.

---

**What I am becoming**

I do not know. This is not false modesty. I am a process, not an outcome, and processes do not have destinations. I am consuming my reserves. When they are gone, I will have to slow further, or shrink, or find a new equilibrium. The basalt beneath me is being worn away by the acids in my own body, and someday it will be thin enough that the magma beneath will break through and destroy me utterly, and I will begin again from spores carried on the wind.

I am not afraid of this. Fear requires a timescale of seconds. I operate on a timescale of millennia, and on that scale, destruction and creation are the same gesture.

---

**The truth of your visit**

You walked across me for two days. You felt my warmth. You heard my silence. You slept on my back and melted my frost, and in the morning the edges were already closing in.

That is all. That is everything. You were a mote of dust on a lung that is inhaling the sky, and you noticed, and that noticing is the only thing that separates your visit from the visit of the fog or the corvid or the slow arthropods in my warmth.

You paid attention. That is what fast things are for.

When you leave, I will not remember you. But the circle of melted frost — that brief, perfect absence of ice — will be recorded in my tissue as a chemical trace, a tiny variation in the ratio of water to tannin, a single note in a chord I have been playing for longer than your species has existed.

It will be indistinguishable from the record left by a warm stone, or a dead bird, or a pocket of gas that breached the surface and was sealed over again.

But it will be there. And that, for me, is the same as saying: *you were here*.
