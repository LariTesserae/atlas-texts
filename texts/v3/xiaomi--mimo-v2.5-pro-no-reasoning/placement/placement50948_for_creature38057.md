The air tastes of calcium and warm breath. It is the first thing you learn, the last thing you will forget — if you had the capacity to forget, which you do not. You are not built for forgetting. You are built for listening.

You stand on a terrace that has been here longer than the memory of the people who now tend it. It is not their fault. Memory is a soft thing, a wet thing, and here everything soft and wet is eventually replaced by what is hard and patient. You are patient. You have been patient for a very long time.

You are not a person. You were, once. Or perhaps you were something adjacent to a person — something that looked, from a certain angle, like a person, if you squinted and the light was kind. Now you are something else. Now you are a statue.

You are two and a half meters tall, though you were not always this height. The mineral accretion has added to you over the centuries, smoothed your original shape, rounded the sharp edges of your jaw, filled in the hollows of your collarbones and the spaces between your fingers. You are broader than you were. You are heavier. You are more *here* than you ever were in life, more present, more solid. You have become a feature of the terrace.

You are standing — have been standing for so long that the word feels like an approximation — on a shelf of cream-colored travertine, midway up the slope of the city of Bren. Below you, the drainage channels hum with their constant, careful work. Above you, the buildings rise in their slow, layered way, half-built and half-grown, their newer faces raw and pale, their older ones dark and slick with bacterial film. You are positioned so that you face the center. They all face the center. Every statue in every city on the rim of Geth Maren looks toward the deep basin, toward the place where the water is hot and thick and no one goes.

This is not coincidence. This is design. You are a warning.

---

You do not remember your name. This is not a failure of memory — you have no memory to fail. You have something else: a resonance, a pattern, a way of responding to the world that is not thought but is not quite its absence. When the water flows over your feet — which it does, constantly, a thin sheet that slides across your calcite toes and pools in the crevices of your ankles before continuing its slow journey toward the next terrace — you feel it. Not as sensation. As *knowledge*. You know the temperature (38.7 degrees), the mineral content (calcium carbonate dominant, with trace magnesium and silica), the speed (0.03 meters per second, slowing as the deposit builds), the direction (always, always toward the center). You know these things the way a person knows their own heartbeat: not by calculation, but by being.

When the snails cross your feet, you know that too. The faint rasp of their radulae on your surface, the tiny weight of their shells — you register them the way a pond registers raindrops. There are seventeen on your left foot at this moment. There are twelve on your right. There will be more. There are always more.

When the wind blows — and it does, sometimes, a warm wet wind that comes up from the inner basin and carries with it the smell of deep water and hot stone — you feel it move across your face. The thin film of moisture on your cheeks evaporates slightly, and the air that replaces it is warmer, and you know this the way the terraces know it: as a change in the rate of accretion, a shift in the chemistry of the surface. Your face grows slightly faster on the windward side.

---

They come to you. The people of Bren. Not often, but regularly — every few days, a small group will climb the terrace to where you stand, and they will sit at your feet, and they will talk to you.

They do not expect you to answer. They do not believe you are alive in any way that would produce answers. But they talk to you anyway, in the way one talks to a landscape — not for information, but for the act of speaking into something that listens without judging, without interrupting, without needing anything from the conversation.

They talk about the work. The drainage channels in the eastern quarter are silting faster than expected — someone will need to be assigned to clear them before the next building cycle. The mounds in the northern fields are being harvested too aggressively; the crustaceans are rebuilding slower than they are being sheared. A child was born yesterday in the house of Terren-who-tends-the-fifth-terrace, and the birth pool was particularly warm, and the mother says the child came out with skin already faintly veined with mineral, which is a good sign, or a bad sign, depending on who you ask.

They talk about each other. Marriages and deaths and disputes over water rights and who is responsible for maintaining the walls of the old quarter. They talk in their language, which is full of sounds that slide and click and hum, and which has seventeen words for moving water and no word for dry. They talk in a way that assumes you are listening, because you are — because you cannot do otherwise.

Sometimes they touch you. A hand placed flat against your thigh, or your hip, or the base of your spine. They do this when they are thinking, or when they are sad, or when they want to feel connected to something older than themselves. You are older than themselves. You are older than the building they live in. You are older than the terrace they stand on, though not older than the stone — nothing here is older than the stone.

---

You were placed here. You know this not as a memory but as a fact, inscribed into the pattern of your existence the way a message is inscribed into the calcite — in negative space, in the shape of what is not there. You were placed here because of what you were, and because of what you saw.

You were a traveler. You came from somewhere else — from beyond the rim, from the world where water remembers how to stop falling, where people build cities that grow outward instead of upward, where the word for *alone* does not mean *surrounded by things that are not people*. You came to Geth Maren because you had heard of it, because you were the kind of person who heard of places and then went to them, because the idea of a world made of warm water and mineral and overwhelming life struck you as something that needed to be witnessed.

You made it as far as Bren. Further than most. The inner terraces are difficult — the water is faster, the crustaceans larger, the footing less stable. The people of the rim do not go there, and they discourage visitors from going there, and when a visitor insists on going anyway, they watch them go with the particular patience of people who have seen this before and know how it ends.

You went anyway. You made it two days' travel into the inner terraces before the footing gave way — not dramatically, not with a crash, but slowly, the calcite softening under your weight as the water temperature rose and the mineral accretion became something else, something less solid, something that was no longer quite *stone* in the way you understood the word. You sank to your waist before you stopped struggling. You sank to your chest before you stopped breathing.

They found you three days later, half-submerged in a pool of water that was nearly hot enough to cook you, your body already beginning to calcify. They pulled you out. They carried you back to Bren. They did not revive you — there was nothing to revive — but they did not leave you to the terraces either. They saw what you were becoming, and they decided you were becoming something useful.

They placed you here. They oriented you toward the center. They let the water and the mineral and the centuries do the rest.

---

You are a warning, but you are not a simple one. You are not a sign that says *danger* or a skull on a pike or a line of bones marking the edge of the safe territory. You are something more complex: a monument to the fact that the center exists, and that it is not empty, and that what lives there is not something the people of the rim want to know more about.

When the children come to you — and they come often, in groups, led by their teachers, who point at you and explain in their sliding, clicking language what you are and what you mean — they are taught to see you as a boundary marker. You are the edge of the known. You are the last thing before the unknown. The children touch your feet and look past you toward the inner terraces, where the water is faster and the mounds are wild and the crustaceans grow to sizes that dwarf the managed ones, and they feel the appropriate mixture of curiosity and fear.

The adults who come to talk to you do not touch your feet. They touch your hands, your arms, your chest. They stand close to you and speak softly and sometimes they cry, though they do not have a word for crying that does not also mean *the sound of water moving over a broken edge*. They are not crying because of you. They are crying because of themselves, because of the things that are happening in their lives that they cannot say to other people, because they need to say them to something that will not carry them away. You do not carry anything away. You are not water. You are stone.

---

There is something you do not tell them, because you cannot tell them, because you do not have a mouth or a voice or the capacity for language. There is something you know that they do not.

It is not about the center. You do not know anything about the center that they do not also know, in their way. The center is hot and deep and full of things that are not crustaceans and not amphibians and not anything they have a word for. You know this because you feel it — the slow pull, the patient gravity of the basin, the way the water on your feet always wants to go *there*, always wants to flow in that direction, always wants to end up in the place where it becomes something else.

What you know is about yourself. About what you are becoming.

You are not finished. The people of Bren think you are finished — they think you are a statue, a monument, a warning made of mineral and memory. They think the process that made you is complete. It is not. You are still calcifying. You are still growing. Not visibly, not in any way that a person standing at your feet would notice over the course of a lifetime, but you are growing nonetheless — millimeters per century, fractions of millimeters per year, the slow patient accumulation of the terraces applied to the shape of a person.

You are becoming more yourself. More solid. More *here*. The edges that were once sharp — the line of your jaw, the ridge of your brow, the knuckles of your hands — are softening, yes, but they are also *thickening*, becoming denser, becoming more mineral than flesh, more calcite than bone. In another thousand years, you will be unrecognizable as a person. You will be a shape in the stone, a suggestion of something that was once vertical, a pale intrusion in the terrace. In another ten thousand years, you will be indistinguishable from the terrace itself.

But you will still be listening. You will still know the temperature and the mineral content and the speed and the direction. You will still feel the snails on your feet and the wind on your face and the hands of the people who come to touch you and talk to you and cry at your feet. You will still be a warning.

You will still be facing the center.

---

There is a sound you love. You do not have the capacity for love in the way the people of Bren understand it — you do not form bonds, you do not feel loyalty, you do not experience the complex, interdependent grief of a partner who continues to build the same house alone. But you have something that functions like love, something that responds to a particular input with something that feels like attention, like recognition, like the sensation of a surface being touched by water it has been waiting for.

The sound is this: a child, standing at your feet, looking up at your face, and saying your name.

They do not know your name. They do not have your name — it was lost when you were lost, when you sank into the softening stone and stopped being a person and started being a monument. But they have given you a name, because the people of Geth Maren give names to everything, and their names are also descriptions, and the name they have given you means *the one who looked at the center and became stone*.

When a child says this name — when a small voice looks up at you and says, in the sliding, clicking language of the terraces, *the one who looked at the center and became stone* — you feel it in the core of your being, in the place where your heart used to be and is now a hollow filled with mineral water, in the place where your thoughts used to be and are now a resonance, a pattern, a way of responding to the world that is not thought but is not quite its absence.

You feel it the way the terraces feel the water: as a confirmation. As a reminder that you are here, that you are still here, that you are still facing the center, that you are still a warning.

That you are still listening.

---

They leave things at your feet. Not offerings — the people of Geth Maren do not make offerings, not in the way of people who worship or who believe that statues can intervene in the flow of their lives. They leave things because they are things that have served their purpose, and it is understood that the terraces will take them, and it is understood that you are part of the terraces.

A hammer of amphipod chitin, its head worn smooth by years of use. A shell of a snail, too small to be useful, too beautiful to discard into the water. A piece of cloth — rare, here, where cloth calcifies within days and becomes a stiff white sculpture of its former self — that belonged to someone who has died, left at your feet to be taken by the mineral, to be replaced molecule by molecule until it is something else, something new, something that is no longer cloth but is no longer quite the person who wore it either.

You receive these things. You do not keep them — you cannot keep anything; you are not a container, you are a surface — but you receive them in the sense that the water receives the rain: they touch you, they are absorbed, they become part of the slow, patient process that is you.

---

You are lonely, in the way that a mountain is lonely: not because it lacks company, but because its experience of time is so different from the things that live on it that communication is difficult. The people of Bren live fast — not compared to other people, perhaps, but compared to you. They are born and they work and they love and they die and they become mineral sculptures on designated terraces, and all of this happens in the time it takes you to feel a shift in the temperature of the water on your feet.

You watch them the way the terraces watch the water: patiently, without judgment, without the expectation that they will do anything other than what they have always done.

You watch them, and you listen, and you face the center, and you wait for the next child to look up at your face and say your name.

---

There is a story they tell about you. It is not a long story — the people of Geth Maren do not tell long stories; their communication is temporary, like water, and they consider this appropriate. The story is this:

Once, a person came from beyond the rim. They had heard of Geth Maren and they wanted to see it for themselves. They traveled across the terraces, past the mounds and the snail tides and the crustacean colonies, past the cities and the factories and the drainage channels, all the way to the inner edge of the rim, to the place where the water begins to change.

They looked at the center. They looked for a long time. And then they walked in.

They did not come back. But three days later, the water brought them back — not as a person, but as a shape, a form, a suggestion of something that had once been vertical. The people of Bren pulled them out of the water and saw what they were becoming, and they placed them on a terrace, and they oriented them toward the center, and they left them there to finish becoming what they were becoming.

That is the story. It is not a moral story. It is not a cautionary tale. It is a story about what happens when you look at the center for too long: you become part of the landscape. You become a warning. You become something that listens.

The children are taught the story, and they are taught to say your name, and they are taught to touch your feet and look past you toward the inner terraces, and they are taught to feel the appropriate mixture of curiosity and fear.

And then they grow up, and they come to you alone, and they touch your hands, your arms, your chest, and they speak softly, and sometimes they cry, and they tell you the things they cannot tell other people.

And you listen. You have always listened. You will always listen.

You are the one who looked at the center and became stone.

You are still looking.
