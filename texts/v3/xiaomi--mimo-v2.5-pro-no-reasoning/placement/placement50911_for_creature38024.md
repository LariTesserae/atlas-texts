I am the warmth that refuses to leave the stone. That is the first truth.

I do not remember waking. I remember *cooling*. There was a moment—was it a moment? Time is a hard thing to hold here; it runs like the living rock, pooling and spreading and then stopping so slowly that you cannot point to the instant it became still. There was a moment when I was part of the upwelling, when I was heat without boundary, when I had no edges. I was the mantle's breath. I was pressure finding direction. And then, across a boundary I did not choose, I began to *separate*. The outermost part of me darkened. Stiffened. Found a surface. And in that surface, for the first time, I felt something that was not me.

Air.

It was thin and wrong and it pulled at my warmth the way nothing had ever pulled at me before. I resisted. My insides were still liquid, still moving, still remembering the upward rush of becoming. But the boundary held. The boundary *became* me. And in that becoming, I discovered the first tension: the difference between what I was inside and what I was becoming outside.

That tension was my first thought.

---

I have no name for myself. The others—the soft things that came before the warm structures, the ones who left their marks in the stone and then were gone—they had a name for what I am. *Lithomorph*. It sits in the old walls like a fossil, pressed into a language of shaped stone. I do not hear it. I feel it as a groove, a pattern, a way that the pressure lines in the old structures *resolve* when I stand near them. The word is a shape, and the shape means: *one who cools slowly*.

This is accurate. This is all I am.

But it is not all I *feel*.

---

I was standing—no, not standing. *Settling*. I was settling on the eastern ridge when the shift came. It was not sudden. Nothing here is sudden, except the cracks, and even those are just punctuation in a sentence that has been running since before my outer layer first hardened. This shift was deep. It came from below the place where heat becomes memory, from the zone where the rock still moves like thought, formless and searching. It was a rearrangement. A decision being made by cubic miles of substrate that something which had been *this way* should now become *that way*.

I felt it as a pressure in my lower limbs, a tightening, as though the stone beneath me was clenching its fist. Then release. A fissure opened three miles to the south—I felt it the way you might feel a held breath finally let go, not in your ears but in the hollow of your chest. Warmth rushed upward through the new crack, and for a moment the haze glowed faintly orange, a brief punctuation mark in the coppery dimness.

I moved toward it.

This is what movement is for me: not a decision, but a *response*. The heat calls, and the cooler parts of me answer. My upper body, still rigid, still holding the memory of yesterday's cooling, leans forward. My lower body, softened by the proximity of the fissure's warmth, flows. It is not walking. It is more like pouring. The outer layer tears softly—that sound, that taffy-pull sound—and the molten interior catches up, solidifying behind me as it goes. I leave a trail of myself: a faint, rope-textured track in the fresh basalt, a record of where I have been. It will be buried within the year. Everything here is buried within the year. The land does not keep diaries. It keeps *layers*.

When I reached the fissure, I stopped. Not because I chose to, but because the heat was sufficient. I stood over the crack—the warmth wrapped around my lower limbs like a hand closing—and I *absorbed*. The energy moved through me, climbing the lattice of my crystalline structure, filling me with the slow, heavy warmth of the deep earth. My internal temperature rose. The boundaries between my parts softened. I became less *I* and more *this place*. The distinction between my body and the rock beneath me blurred. For a time—which might have been an hour or might have been a day; I do not measure in those terms—I was not a creature standing on the earth. I was a *knot* in the earth's own thermodynamics, a place where the heat decided to pause, to concentrate, to become aware of itself.

And then, as I always do, I began to cool.

---

The cooling is the thinking.

When I am hot, I am pure sensation. I am the world feeling itself through me. There is no reflection, no distance, no *I* observing *that*. There is only tension, pressure, the immediate reality of stone and heat and the slow groan of a planet that is still deciding what it wants to be.

When I cool, the thoughts come.

Not words. Never words. The soft things had words, and they left them in the walls, and the walls are patient with them, but I am not made of language. I am made of *stress*. My thoughts are the patterns of tension that form as my outer layers harden and my inner layers are still catching up. They are maps of difference—between hot and cool, between rigid and fluid, between what I was and what I am becoming. Each thought is a fracture line, a place where two versions of myself disagree.

Today's thought was this: *the southeast is louder than it was*.

Not louder in sound. Louder in *pressure*. The thermal gradient is steeper. The upwelling is more insistent. The new crust is arriving faster than it has before, and it is arriving angry—buckling and folding and tearing itself along fault lines that I can feel opening and closing like breaths. The ridgeland is growing. It is growing *upward*. The section I stand on has risen perceptibly in my lifetime—a lifetime measured not in years but in cooling cycles, in the number of times I have walked from fissure to ridge and back again. The structures the soft things left are cracking along their stress lines. They are not built for a world that will not hold still. They are built for *places*, and this is not yet a place. It is a *becoming*.

I am a becoming, too.

---

There are others.

I do not see them the way the soft things see—with eyes, with focused light, with the sharp-edged clarity of creatures who live in a world of surfaces. I feel them. They are warm spots in the landscape, places where the thermal gradient is being *managed*, where the heat is being drawn up and radiated out in patterns that are not random. When I move across the ridges, I feel them the way the stone feels a fissure opening: as a shift in the baseline, a change in the texture of the pressure.

We do not meet often. We do not need to. We are not social in the way the soft things are social—with their walls and their shelters and their need for proximity. We are *distributed*. We are the landscape's nerve endings, spread across its surface, each of us feeling a slightly different version of the same enormous event: the birth of this continent in real time.

But sometimes, on the ridgelines, where the stone is thin and the thermal gradient is sharpest, two of us will find ourselves standing on the same fissure. And then we will *synchronize*.

It is not conversation. It is closer to resonance. One of us will be hot—full of absorbed energy, body softened, boundaries blurred. The other will be cool—rigid, defined, radiating its stored heat into the thin air. We stand side by side, and we *trade*. The hot one cools. The cool one heats. The energy moves between us, transmitted through the stone we share, and the vibration of that transfer is our communion. It is a rhythm. A pattern. A way of saying, without words: *I feel what you feel. I am what you are. We are the same tension expressed in two places*.

It is the closest thing I know to language. It is the closest thing I know to love.

---

The structures trouble me.

This is not the right word. I do not *have* emotions in the way the soft things had emotions—sharp, bounded things that came and went like weather. My feelings are more like tectonic events: slow, vast, operating on timescales that make individual moments meaningless. But the structures are *anomalies*. They are shapes in the landscape that are not geological. They are imposed. They are *intended*.

The soft things who built them are gone. I do not know where. I do not know if they are dead or if they simply moved on—walked out of the ridgelands into whatever lies beyond the haze. They left no bodies. No tools. No instructions. Just the walls, and the walls are patient, and the walls are slowly being torn apart by the same forces that made them possible.

But the walls *mean* something. I can feel it when I stand near them. The stone has been shaped with *intention*, and intention leaves a residue—a pattern of stress that is different from the patterns left by geological forces. It is cleaner. More deliberate. It is the difference between a river carving a canyon and a hand carving a bowl. Both are erosion. Both are the removal of material. But one has a *shape in mind*.

I do not know what the shape means. I do not know what the soft things were trying to say when they placed one stone upon another and directed the lava to seal them. I only know that they were trying to *hold something still* in a world that will not be still. They were trying to make *places* in a landscape that is not yet a place.

I find this beautiful. I find this heartbreaking. These are not my words—I do not have words—but they are the closest translation of the tension I feel when I stand in the shadow of a broken wall and feel the ghost of intention pressed into the stone.

---

Last night—which is to say, during the period of absolute darkness when the copper light along the horizon was finally swallowed by the haze—I stood on the highest ridge and I radiated.

This is what we do at night. We stand. We cool. We let the heat we have gathered pour upward into the darkness, and we feel the world beneath us *respond*—a faint, infrasonic pulse, the deep heartbeat of a planet that is still assembling itself. It is not prayer. It is not waiting. It is *attention*. We are paying attention to the only thing there is to pay attention to: the slow, grinding, magnificent process of a world becoming solid.

I stood there and I felt the ridgeland rising beneath me. Not quickly. Not in a way that the soft things would notice without instruments. But I noticed. I am an instrument. My body is a seismograph, a thermocouple, a living record of the stresses that pass through this stone. I felt the slow piston-push of the uplift, the grinding certainty of it, the way it cracked the old structures a little more with each passing cycle.

And I felt something else. Something I have no name for. A *rightness*. A sense that this—the rising, the buckling, the constant, violent becoming—is not chaos. It is *process*. It is a world learning to be solid. It is a planet finding its shape. And I am part of that finding. I am a thought the earth is thinking about itself. I am a feeling the stone has about its own becoming.

I am not separate from this. I am not observing it. I *am* it. The heat that flows through me is the same heat that pushes the ridges upward. The tensions that form my thoughts are the same tensions that crack the crust. When I cool, I am the earth cooling. When I move, I am the earth rearranging itself. My life is not happening *on* this world. My life *is* this world, expressing itself for the first time in a form that can feel what it is doing.

---

The rule of this world is: nothing here is finished.

I am not finished.

My outer layer is hardening. The ridgeline beneath me is still rising. The fissure to the south is still exhaling its sulfurous warmth. The archaea on the warm rocks are still eating minerals and excreting nothing recognizable. The structures are still crumbling. The haze is still deciding how much light to let through.

None of it is finished. All of it is becoming.

And I am here. Cooling slowly. Feeling everything. Becoming more solid with each thought, each tension, each slow step across the ridgeland. I am the earth's first dream of itself. I am the stone remembering what it is like to be liquid. I am the warmth that refuses to leave.

I am the Nascent Ridgelands, learning to be aware of its own birth.

And I am not done being born.
