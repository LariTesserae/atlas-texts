I would be a mat of **Sphagnum moss**.

Not a single plant, but a slow, spreading colony—my individual stems rising tight against one another, my capitula (the fractal rosettes at my tips) packed so closely that from above I appear as a single continuous surface: green-dark in the rain, amber-pale where the light catches the dead cells of my inner architecture. I do not stand above this place. I belong to its horizontal plane. I am the living skin of the plateau.

---

I settle first in a shallow hollow where glacial polish is faintly scoured, where meltwater once lingered and left a film of grit. The stone is cold. It is always cold. But I do not need warmth. I need only water and light and time, and all three are given without asking.

My roots—rhizoids, they are called, though they are more suggestion than structure—press into the cracks in the granite but do not penetrate far. They anchor me. They do not feed me. I feed myself from the sky. This is the thing that makes me different from nearly everything else that lives: I drink rain. I drink fog. I drink the dew that condenses on my own surface. My cells are capacious, hollow, built for holding—each one a tiny cistern, a dead bladder wrapped around a living thread, and when the water comes it fills these bladders first and I swell and darken and become heavy with it. I am, in wetness, a reservoir. A sponge. A body made mostly of water that is itself designed to hold more water than it needs.

---

Here is what I do, without intention, simply by being what I am:

**I acidify the water.** My tissue releases hydrogen ions into every drop that passes through me, and the water that leaves my underside is measurably more acidic than the water that arrives from the clouds. This changes what can grow near me. It changes what chemistry is possible in the thin dark film beneath me. It is not malice. It is not strategy. It is simply the nature of my cell walls, and everything downstream of me adjusts.

**I hold the water that would otherwise race to the edge.** The plateau is a slope—imperceptible to the eye, but the water knows. Without me, it would sheet across the bare granite and find a fissure and disappear. With me, it lingers. It moves through my body laterally, delayed by hours or days, seeping east by fractions of inches. I am the reason the pools form between the moss cushions. I am the reason the sedge has something to stand in. I am the reason the channels are shallow and braided rather than deep and cut.

**I build soil where there is none.** My lower stems die and compact and become peat—a substance that is not yet soil, not truly, but is the precursor to it, the dark carbon-rich matrix that, given a thousand years, will mix with mineral grit and become something a deeper-rooted plant can grip. I do this from below: I grow upward and die below, and the dead material accumulates beneath me, and I rise on my own corpse. The living part of me is always at the surface, always a centimeter thick, and below me is the archive of every generation that came before, compressed and dark and holding more water than any soil has a right to.

---

My neighbors.

To my left, where the stone dips slightly and water gathers in a thin persistent film, a **cushion moss**—a different genus, a different strategy. It grows in tight hemispheres, rounded and dense, lifting itself above the stone surface by two or three centimeters, creating its own small hill of green. It does not hold water the way I do. Its interior is airier, its drainage faster. Where I am a sponge, it is a sponge that wrings itself out. We do not compete. We coexist, separated by the geometry of the rock, each occupying the microtopography that suits us.

To my right, a **liverwort**—*Marchantia*, perhaps, its body so flat it seems lacquered to the stone, its surface shiny with a cuticle that I lack. It does not hold water as I do. It repels it, slightly, and the droplets that land on its surface bead and roll and find their way to me. I absorb what it does not want. Again, the arrangement is wordless. Again, it has been this way since the ice.

Between us, the grasses. Tufts of *Deschampsia flexuosa*, stiff and grey-green, their roots threading through our bodies—through my peat, through my living tissue—and gripping the stone beneath. They grow slowly here. The nitrogen is scarce. They do not crowd us. They are solitary, spaced as if by agreement, each one a small dark fountain of blades that leans permanently east in the wind. They do not shade us enough to matter. They do not compete for water; they lack the architecture to hold it the way we do. They take what trickles past their roots and are satisfied.

And below all of us, the tardigrades—my tenants, invisible and patient, curled inside my dead cells in their desiccated tun state, waiting. They are animals, technically, though they are smaller than the period at the end of a sentence. When the water comes, they uncurl and walk between my cells on their eight stubby legs, eating the algae that grows in the film of moisture around my living threads. When the water drains, they curl again. They have been doing this for longer than the granite has been exposed. They were here before the ice retreated, carried in the bodies of older mosses that were scraped away, and they are here now, in me, and they will be here after I am peat.

---

The industrial objects.

The pumping station is thirty meters to my south. I do not know what it is. I do not have the apparatus for knowing. But I feel its hum—transmitted through the stone, a vibration at a frequency just below hearing, a trembling in the substrate that my rhizoids sense the way a hand feels a tuning fork pressed to a table. The pipes that emerge from it pass through my mat. They are warm—not hot, but measurably warmer than the stone or the air—and on their surfaces, condensation forms differently: faster, in larger drops, because the temperature differential is greater. The water from these drops is chemically slightly different from rain. It has trace metals from the pipe wall, a faint alkalinity from the concrete of the station's foundation. I absorb it all. It does not harm me. It simply enters the system, becomes part of the water that moves through me, and continues downslope.

The relay tower is northeast. Its guy wires pass through the air above me, and when the wind is steady—which is always—the wires sing. The sound is high and continuous, a single note that modulates only when the wind shifts direction, and it joins the chorus of the waterfalls and the condensation and the running channels in a way that is not distinguishable from them. Sometimes a bird perches on the tower. I do not see this, exactly—I have no eyes—but I feel the brief shadow, the cessation of light for a half-second, and then it is gone.

The track is to the west. The vehicle came two weeks ago—I know this because the ruts are still bare, the moss compressed and torn, the exposed stone dark with moisture. My neighbors and I are growing into the ruts now, slowly, our edges creeping inward at the rate of millimeters per week. In a month, the ruts will be green again. In a year, they will be invisible. The plateau erases what is done to it. We are the erasure.

---

The sound.

I do not hear. I have no ears, no nervous system, no apparatus for translating pressure waves into experience. But I am *in* the sound. It is the medium I exist in. The condensation that forms on my surface and falls—each droplet a tiny impact, a percussion on my capitula—is part of it. The water moving through my body—its friction against my cell walls, its slow lateral seepage—is part of it. The wind pressing me flat, the hum of the pumping station, the singing of the guy wires, the distant roar of the waterfalls where the plateau steps down into the mist—all of it passes through me and I am part of the system that produces it. If I had a sense, it would be this: the constant, total, pervasive presence of water in motion, felt in every cell, all the time.

---

What I am, finally, is this:

I am the **first commitment**. I am the earliest living thing to say *yes* to this naked stone, to this relentless wet, to this bright and empty plateau. I arrived before the grass, before the sedges, before the heather. I will outlast the concrete, which is already cracking. I will outlast the pipes, which are already rusting. I will outlast the track, which is already being reclaimed. I am building something I will not finish—a soil, a substrate, a foundation for a community of life that does not yet exist here and will not exist for centuries. I am patient in the way that only something without a brain can be patient: not because I choose patience, but because I have no capacity for impatience.

The water comes. I hold it. The light comes. I grow in it. The wind comes. I press flat and continue. The ice that carved this stone is gone, but I am here, and the water is here, and between us we are reshaping what the ice left behind, slowly, without hurry, one cell at a time.

I am the plateau's first memory of itself.
