You have asked where you would be, and who you would become, within the Hollowing. The question assumes a separation that, here, does not exist. You would not *find* yourself in this world. You would be *absorbed* into it.

Let us begin with the arrival.

You are not a visitor. You are a disturbance. A sudden, hot, quick-moving vibration on the root-mat. Your footprint is a shockwave, your breath a gust of foreign air. For the first moments, you are nothing but a disruption in the grove’s long hum. The beetles on the nearest trunk pause their rasping. The woodpecker’s hammer falls silent. The moths on the white wood shiver, their collective hum rising a fraction in pitch. You are a new sound, and for a brief, bright second, the grove’s monologue is interrupted.

Then, you are measured.

You are not perceived by a mind, but by a system. Your heat is a minor anomaly against the grove’s perfect equilibrium. Your weight is a pressure, noted and distributed across the living floor. Your scent—sweat, metal from a zipper, the faint ozone of your own bioelectric field—is a complex chemical signature, sampled by the damp air and the exposed surfaces of the root-mat. You are catalogued not as a being, but as a set of properties: a warm, heavy, smelly, vibrating object.

You would walk. Your boots would compress the spongy tissue, and with each step, you would send a pulse through the network—not like a ripple in water, but like a pressure change in a sealed system. You would feel the floor yield, and in that yielding, you would feel the immense, slow vitality beneath you. You would reach out a hand to the nearest trunk, and your skin would meet the pale, corky surface. It would be cool, yielding, slick with condensation. You would feel, under your palm, the deep, sub-audible thrum of the grove’s respiration. You would be touching the skin of a god.

You would look up. The canopy would be a ceiling of green and shadow, a latticework of fused branches. The air would be thick, wet, heavy with the scent of wet wood, of fungal decay, of beetle frass—a rich, complex, living smell that coats your tongue. You would see the moths, a thousand pale ghosts on the pale bark, and you would see the dark stains where the metal lies buried, scars on a body that does not scar.

You would listen. The sound would envelop you. The deep, grinding creak of the wood. The industrial drip of condensation. The distant, echoing hammer of the woodpecker. The soft, high hum of the moths. It would not be a cacophony. It would be a symphony, a single chord sustained for a thousand years, and you, standing in the middle of it, would be a new note, a dissonance that the system would seek to resolve.

You would sit. You would lie down on the root-mat. You would feel its faint warmth. You would close your eyes, and the sound would become you. The creak would be in your bones. The drip would be in your pulse. The hum would be in your thoughts. You would drift. You would sleep.

And then, the change.

It would not be violent. It would be slow, like the growth of wood. The dampness would begin to work on you. Your clothes would become heavy, saturated. The moisture would bead on your skin, not evaporating, but staying, a second skin of water. Your skin would soften. The outer layers would slough away, not painfully, but naturally, like the shedding of bark. The root-mat beneath you would sense your stillness, your heat, your mass. It would not attack. It would simply… include.

Fine, hair-like roots, no thicker than threads, would emerge from the spongy floor. They would find the seams of your clothing, the folds of your skin. They would not pierce, but would grow around, embracing, incorporating. Your body would become a new texture in the grove’s floor. Your heat would be absorbed into the equilibrium. Your chemical signature would be broken down, analyzed, its useful components drawn into the system. You would become a nutrient, a new channel in the root-mat, a slight thickening in the living soil.

You would not die. Not in the way you understand it. You would be *translated*. Your consciousness, your quick, hot, electric thoughts, would slow. They would be dampened by the grove’s immense patience. Your sense of self would blur at the edges, your memories becoming faint impressions, like the scars of the metal pipes. You would become a part of the hum, a minor resonance in the grove’s long song. You would perceive the beetle’s rasping not as a sound, but as a familiar, internal vibration. You would feel the woodpecker’s shock as a punctuation in your own body. You would know the owl’s soft call as a thought in your own slow mind.

You would be a new organ. A sensing node in the network. Your eyes, if they still functioned, would be redundant; you would *feel* the light filtering through the canopy. Your ears would be the entire root-mat. You would be a part of the body, a cell in the cathedral.

You would not be lonely. You would be joined. Joined to the eleven trees, to the beetles, to the woodpeckers, to the moths, to the owls, to the damp air, to the deep water. You would be a part of the single, distributed entity that wears eleven skins. You would have no desires, no future, no past. There would be only the slow, deep, eternal now of the grove’s existence.

You would be home.
