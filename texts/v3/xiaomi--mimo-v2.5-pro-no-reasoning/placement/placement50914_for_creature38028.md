I don't have a name. Not one that matters.

The others call me "Pale" or "Deep-Click" or sometimes just "the sound you hear when the factory is quiet." Names are for things that live above the water, things that walk on two legs and wear clothes that never dry. I don't have legs. I don't have clothes. I have a shell and patience and a place in the dark.

My shell is the color of the water when the overhead glow is at its brightest — a sort of yellow-white, like bone left in the sun that never quite was. It's thick. It has to be. The water here is full of minerals that settle and build, and I carry some of that weight on my back, in layers that grow thicker each time I molt. I'm old. I know I'm old because my shell is heavy with deposit, because the smaller ones — the swimmers, the flickers, the brief ones — don't recognize me as one of them anymore. They scatter when I move. That's appropriate. I'm large enough to eat some of them, though I usually don't bother. There's easier food.

I live in the gap. That's what I call it. The space between the bottom of a building and the top of the water where it seeps into the foundation. It's not a basement. Basements are made by people, for people. This is something else — a crack, a fold, a place where the city forgot to seal itself completely, or where the sealing failed slowly, over years, and nobody noticed because the building was already empty. The gap is warm. It's always warm. The water that fills it comes from below, from the deep heat, and it carries food in it: the bacterial mats, the algae, the smaller things that feed on those and that I, in turn, feed on.

I don't think about the people much. When I was younger — smaller, softer-shelled — I lived closer to the active canals. I saw them often. They moved quickly, even in the heat, and they were noisy in a way that the water isn't. Not loud, but sharp. Sudden. A voice, a door, a machine starting or stopping. The water's noise is constant, predictable: the slow rush of the pumps, the drip of condensation, the clicks and scrapes of my own kind going about their business. The people's noise was different. Unsettling. I learned to avoid it.

Now I'm in the gap, deep enough in the empty part of the city that I almost never hear them. I hear the building settling instead. I hear the mineral deposits growing on the walls, a sound so slow it's almost a vibration, a sense of pressure rather than noise. I hear the water moving through the cracks, slow and purposeful, carrying the warmth up from below and leaving mineral traces wherever it touches. I hear my own kind — the ones who live in the gaps with me, smaller than me, faster than me, younger than me. They click and scrape and move through the water with a sound like rain on metal. They don't bother me. I don't bother them. We're all just here, in the warm dark, doing what we've always done.

I've been in this particular gap for a long time. Years, maybe. It's hard to tell. The glow outside doesn't change much — brighter when it's "day," dimmer when it's "night," but always the same directionless yellow. My sense of time comes from the water: when the pumps cycle, when the temperature shifts a degree or two, when a new spring breaks through somewhere nearby and the mineral content of the water changes. I can feel the city's rhythms in the current, the way a leaf feels the wind.

The building above me is empty. I know this because there are no sharp noises, no vibrations of heavy footsteps, no sudden changes in the water flow from above. Sometimes, in the early days, I'd feel a vibration — something large moving on the floors above, or a heavy object being set down — and I'd freeze, my legs tucked tight against my shell, my antennae pressed flat. But those vibrations stopped a long time ago. Now the building just settles. Slowly. The way I do.

I don't know what the building was for. I don't know what any of the buildings are for. They're shapes. Obstacles. Places where the water collects and flows and creates the gaps I live in. The people built them, I know that much — I've seen them building, or repairing, in the active districts, moving with that strange, slow deliberation that looks like patience but isn't quite. They use tools. They talk. They drink the water they've made safe, and they eat food that comes on the rail cars, and they work in the factories where the loudest noises come from. That's their life. This is mine.

My life is the gap. The warm water. The mineral food. The slow growth of my shell. The click and scrape of my kind around me. The settling of the building, the growing of the deposits, the movement of the water. It's not a rich life, by the standards of things that think about richness. It's not a long one, probably, though I have no way to know that for sure. But it's complete. The Basin gives me everything I need, and in return I give it my body when I die, and the minerals from my shell, and the space I occupied for a little while. It's a fair trade.

Sometimes I wonder — in the way that creatures like me wonder, which is less like thinking and more like a pause in the rhythm of things — what it would be like to be one of them. To have legs that walk and eyes that see in colors I can't imagine and skin that sweats. To live in the bright part of the city, in the sealed buildings, in the noise and the movement. To worry about things like "economics" and "shift changes" and "the old population." To have a name that means something.

But that wondering doesn't last long. It's like a bubble rising from the mud: it appears, it floats up, it pops at the surface and is gone. What's left is the gap. The water. The warmth. The click of my own kind in the dark.

I am Pale. I am Deep-Click. I am the sound you hear when the factory is quiet.

I am the Basin's, and the Basin is mine.
