I am one of the sharp points on the great, rust-colored bloom that lives here.

To me, the world is a geology of structure: the jack-hammer pulse of the deep engines against my base, the scalding slick of acid-rain on my shell, the wet, hairy scrabble of the Rift-Climbers finding purchase on my back. I am not a beast to them, nor a creature. I am a terrain feature—a sharp, unstable outcropping of iron-oxide and meat that has fused with the girder.

I feel the verticality not as a distance, but as a tension. The weight of the stacks above is a constant, crushing pressure against my dorsal plates. The thinning air at the rim is a distant, high-pitched shriek in the metal. The world is a symphony of vibration, and I am the tuning fork that listens.

Today, the tremors are irregular.

My arms are currently pinned in a crucifixion of necessity—two locked into the corroded flange of a primary load-bearing beam, the rest splayed to balance the shifting pitch of the canted wall. I am hanging over the abyss, three kilometers of nothingness below my base. The heat is blistering, rising from the valley floor in waves that warp the image of the workshops into shimmering ghosts. Sweat—*steam*—hisses from my vents, but my internal barometer reads the pressure dropping.

Something is wrong with the rhythm. The *thud-hiss-thud* of the earth-engines has faltered, replaced by a grinding, arrhythmic groan. It is a sound of shear. A load transfer somewhere below has failed.

To a Rift-Climber, this is an opportunity—a reason to scurry higher, to new footholds. But I am a listener of the deep structure. I hear the metal screaming.

Slowly, inexorably, I retract my blades from the flange. The movement is glacial, a shift of tons, not pounds. My body is a dense, seven-sided column of patience. I swing one heavy, chitin-plated arm toward a nearby girder. I do not climb; I plant. The blade-tips, honed to a monomolecular edge by a lifetime of scraping rust and mineral deposits, pierce the steel. *Chink*. A new anchor.

I am not fleeing. I am investigating.

The vibration is coming from a resonance chamber—a hollowed-out section of the city stack where steam collects before venting. It is a place of high value, a place where the air tastes of copper and sulfur. But the sound is wrong. It is too fast. It is the sound of a system trying to compensate for a breach.

With agonizing slowness, I traverse the wall. I create my own path—*chink, chink, chink*—a ladder of perfect, uniform holes punched into the rust. The metal here is calcified, ancient. It resists, then yields. I feel the Climbers fleeing past me, their six limbs a frantic blur against the black iron. They fear the noise. I fear the silence that will follow.

I swing around a column the width of a river barge.

There.
A breach. A massive, circular valve has ruptured in the steam line. Superheated, mineral-rich vapor is screaming into the canyon, a jet of white heat that is scouring the rust from the walls and flash-cooking a cluster of Tower-Ferns nearby. The pressure loss is causing the surrounding web of girders to sing a high, dangerous harmony. The vibration is telling me that this section is about to go critical. In an hour, maybe less, this entire kilometer-high section will peel away from the canyon wall and fall.

A Climber would collect the ferns. Upright things—soft, fleshy, obsessed with the horizon—would try to patch the pipe.

I do not care for the steam. I care for the *math*. The vibration is a lie. If this section falls, it will take three lower transit-ways with it, severing the northern face of the canyon from the southern. I will not have it. The map will not be rewritten by such a sloppy, violent collapse.

I extend three arms, anchoring myself to the vertical face of a smokestack. My remaining four arms lock together, blades interlocking to form a single, massive, crowbar-like tool. My core—the geothermal engine inside me—revs. It draws in the ambient heat, the sulfurous air, and converts it to torque. My shell groans, hot to the touch.

I wait for the rhythm. The earth-engine below *thuds*.
*Hisses*.
I exhale a jet of condensation.
*Thud*.

On the final beat, I strike the base column supporting the ruptured valve.

My blow is not a slash. It is a geological event. A shockwave of concentrated force, channeled through thirty tons of chitin and muscle. The sound is a dull, flat *CLANG* that is felt in the teeth of every Climber for two kilometers.

The base of the metal column shatters. The angle of the load shifts. Instead of peeling outward into the canyon, the ruptured section—valve, girders, and all—collapses *inward*, shearing off the waste chute and plunging into the empty, vertical shaft of a defunct elevator core. The scream of the steam is cut off. The vibration stabilizes. The high-harmonic whine fades back into the deep, comforting *thud-hiss-thud*.

The map remains intact.

I disengage my blades. I shake the fatigue from my arms, the motion a heavy, hydraulic heave. I approach the still-warm wreckage in the elevator shaft. The metal is glowing faintly. I settle a blade-tip into the scar I just made and begin to etch. A slow, deliberate removal of oxidized patina.

Three concentric circles. A vertical bisecting line. A warning. *Unstable*.

A sizzle of sulfurous condensation drips from my top dish, falling into the abyss. I turn my angles back toward the wall, and resume my slow, heavy climb toward the white-hot coin of the sun.
