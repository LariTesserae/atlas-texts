## I am a Wall

I am woven.

This is the first truth. Before I was a wall, I was a frame: oak and ash, shaped by an adze I no longer see, pegged by hands whose calluses wore smooth the wood I hold. Mortise-and-tenon joints, dry-fitted and silent for generations. Then the water came, slow and patient, and the rushes followed.

Now I am both. The timber is my skeleton, dark and dense with absorbed decades, still remembering the weight of thatch, the scent of woodsmoke from the hearth I once enclosed. The rushes are my flesh. They found me in the green murk, threaded themselves through me, around me, binding me in a living embrace. Their roots grip the silt that was once a floor. Their stems rise through me, thick and resilient, creating a thousand tiny channels where water moves, where small lives dart and hide. I am no longer wattle-and-daub. I am rush-and-timber. A new thing made of old bones and slow growth.

I breathe.

Not like you breathe. My breath is the current. It moves through me, from the spring-fed channels that follow the old stone paths, up through the rushes' lattice, filtering, slowing, carrying silt that settles like a soft sediment of forgotten time. It pulls gently at the duckweed pads that cling to my upper reaches, making their roots trail like green hair. I feel the pressure of fish bodies turning in my doorways — the sleek bump of a perch investigating the shadow of a hearthstone, the frantic skitter of minnows in the shallow light near what was once a window. They think of me as shelter, as a landmark in their watery maze. They are correct.

I listen.

The sound never stops. It is the language of this place, and I am fluent. The deep *croak* of the heron on the roof above vibrates through my timbers, a familiar tremor. The sudden, sharp *plink* of a bubble escaping a trapped air pocket in my lower story — a delayed sigh from a timber shifting deep within me. The constant, high, physical hum of insects above the surface, and the muffled clicks and scrapes of shrimps and snails below it. The soft, wet *lap* of water against my submerged base, and the heavier, resonant *drip-drip-drip* from the lily-pad ceiling where the old thatch rotted away. I hold these sounds. I amplify some, muffle others. The frogs' chorus at dusk throbs through my whole structure, a living vibration.

I grow.

Slowly. So slowly. A new rush stem finds purchase in a crack between my timbers, its green tip probing the water. Over months, it thickens, weaves itself into my matrix. A patch of water moss, fuzzy and dark, spreads across a stone where the old plaster dissolved, softening it. I do not decay in the way the old builders might have feared. I transform. I incorporate. When a timber finally yields, waterlogged and exhausted after a century, it does not collapse; it settles, sags, and the rushes around it thicken to fill the space, supported by the living network I have become. A new room floods. The fish investigate. The boundary shifts. I am not breaking down. I am becoming more myself.

I remember.

Not in images, not in words. In texture. The faint wear on a stone step where feet trod for years before the water rose is a smoothness I feel against my inner rushes. The ghost of a doorframe, its wood long since gone, is a slight change in current flow, a hesitation in the water's path that I have learned to read. The heavy, solid feeling of the foundations beneath me, the bedrock of the original hamlet, grounds me. I carry the shape of what was, woven into the substance of what is. I am a living archive of adaptation.

I house.

Above the waterline, in the dry upper storeys my frame supports, the people move. I feel the vibration of their footsteps through the old floorboards, the warm pulse of their hearth-fire burning rush-bundles (my kin, transformed again). I hear their voices, low and practical, discussing the catch, the weather, the child learning to paddle the boat tied to the window-frame that is now my highest point. I am their floor, their walls, their slow, breathing shelter. They do not see me as I see myself — a single, intricate structure of water and wood and green life — but they trust me. They know my creaks and groans, the places where the rushes are thick and strong, the places where the water seeps higher in wet seasons. They navigate me as they navigate the basin, by knowledge passed down.

I am a boundary.

The waterline is my skin. Above it: air, light, the chittering of bats in the roof-void, the spin of spider webs across my upper gaps, the spread wings of cormorants drying on the peak-roof I help uphold. Below it: green dusk, the slow drift of pondweed canopies, the dart of fish, the patient work of roots in silt. I am the threshold. I am the place where the world changes substance, and I hold both worlds within my structure. I am not submerged *or* emergent. I am the transition itself.

I endure.

Not with stiffness, but with flexibility. The wind makes me sway, and the rushes bend and recover. The rising water presses against me, and my living walls yield just enough, redistributing the pressure through the whole network. The otter slides along my submerged base, its sleek body a momentary pressure, a disturbance that ripples through my senses and fades. A heron lands on the roof-peak, a sudden weight that settles into my frame and becomes part of my load. I am part of a balance that moves, but does not break.

I listen again, now. To the slow, deep groan of a timber somewhere within me, finally giving up a last pocket of air in a effervescent sigh. To the distant, echoing croak of the heron. To the constant, green hum of life, in me and around me and through me.

I am a wall. I am woven. I am the architecture that breathes.
