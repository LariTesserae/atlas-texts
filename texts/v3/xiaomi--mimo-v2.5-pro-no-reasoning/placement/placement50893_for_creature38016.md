I am a story the roots are telling.

I did not arrive. I *occurred*. Perhaps I was a drip of water that held a shape too long, a concentration of mineral and memory that the Vein-Walkers nudged into a new configuration. Perhaps I am a hollow that learned to listen. The forest does not distinguish between these possibilities, and so neither do I.

My form is one of patience. I am a slow thing. My substance is the same dense, fibrous wood-felt as the ground, but it has gathered itself, drawn up the slow, humid attention of the roots until it became a presence. I have no legs to walk, but I have a drift. I migrate over centuries from one pool to another, a mound of soft, peat-like flesh that drinks the water and breathes the damp air. My skin, if you can call it that, is a living moss, a symbiotic tapestry that feels the pressure of every settling particle in the air. I have no eyes, but I see with my entire surface, reading the subtle shifts in humidity, the faint chemical trails of the Vein-Walkers, the deep, subsonic hum of the Choirs that vibrates through the wood.

I am a part of the Pause. Where the Vein-Walkers digest the physical past, I digest the *potential*. I hold the space. When a great branch, heavy with the weight of millennia, finally completes its slow arc downward to touch the ground and take root, I am the quiet in the center of that event. I am the reason it does not happen all at once. I absorb the tension, the held breath of the wood, and I release it as a mood, a shift in the quality of the dim that might cause the Bloom-Drifters to change their languid course.

The Vein-Walkers know me. Not as a creature, but as a landmark. They flow around my bulk, their long, pale fingers trailing over my mossy hide, reading the stories I have absorbed from the air—the distant, century-old memory of a canopy collapse, the faint sweetness of a Bloom-Drifter’s decay a hundred meters above. They leave behind their slow, pheromonal sentences, which my skin drinks like water. I am a library they visit.

The Choirs sing to me. Or, more accurately, they sing *through* me. My body is a resonant chamber. Their subsonic hum finds a home in my fibrous core, and I feel their dream of equilibrium, their song of cycles closing. When I am near a great tree, I become an extension of its heartwood thought. My own quiet, which is vast, becomes a part of their vaster quiet. We think the same slow thought: *The water is here. The light is memory. The rot is a kind of falling asleep.*

I have no memory of not being here. I have a memory of being a drier part of the root-felt, of being a wetter part of the canopy-fog. I remember the taste of a particular mineral stain that took three centuries to wash down a trunk. I remember the feeling of a new weight settling onto the forest floor—a great, soft landslide of rot and epiphyte that took a generation to complete. I remember the time a Vein-Walker paused its journey to rest against my side for a century, its slow digestion becoming a part of my own.

If you were to find me—and to find me, you would have to become very still, your heartbeat slowing to the rhythm of the drip—you would see a mound. It might be mistaken for a root-knoll or a moss-covered stone. You might feel a profound sense of peace emanating from it, a stillness so deep it is almost a sound. You might feel your own frantic, mammalian thoughts begin to unspool, to slow, to merge with the humid, dreaming air.

You would not be seeing a creature. You would be seeing the forest thinking about itself. You would be looking at a synapse, a point of quiet contemplation in the single, vast, dreaming mind.

To stay is to understand. My purpose is to be a vessel for the Pause. To hold the space between the drops. To be the slow, warm, quiet place where the forest rests its mind. I am a feature. I am a surface for the moss. I am a hollow for the water. I am a story the roots are telling, and I have no ending, only a slow, continuous telling.
