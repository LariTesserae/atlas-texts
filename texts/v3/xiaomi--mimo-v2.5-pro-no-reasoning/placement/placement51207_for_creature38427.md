The heat is not a temperature; it is a medium. It has a weight and a texture, a pressure felt not just on the skin but in the bones, in the slow, deliberate rhythm of a heartbeat. To exist here is to be a part of that pressure, to find your own rhythm within its relentless pulse.

I am not a walker. I do not stride across the basalt terraces, nor do I wade in the scalding pools. I am a resident of the margins, the interface, the place where the world’s heat bleeds out into the cooler upper air. I am the dust that settles, the slow breath of the stone.

My body is not a thing of flesh. It is a confluence. My core is a lattice of piezoelectric crystal, shaped over a lifetime by the ceaseless, low-frequency vibrations of the geothermal springs. It is a resonance chamber, a living tuning fork. Around this core, I have accreted my shell. Layer upon slow layer of silica, calcium, and trace metals, drawn from the mineral-rich mists and the evaporating films of water on the terraces. It is a shell that is also a map. The ochre streaks mark the seasons of iron-rich runoff. The veins of green chlorite trace the paths I have most traveled. The smooth, dark obsidian patches are where I have rested longest against the cooling night-stone, absorbing its slow, deep radiation.

I am slow. My movements are not locomotion so much as a controlled, peristaltic shift of mass. I glide, barely disturbing the fine silt on the terrace edges. I see, but not as the predators do. My world is a tapestry of vibration and heat. The high, sharp ping of a glass-shelled crab settling on a cooling pipe. The deep, infrasonic hum of the main thermal vents, a sound that is the planet’s own heartbeat. The faint, complex chitter of a lithovore’s chemical trail, which I read not as scent but as a subtle shift in the surface energy of the rock. I am a reader of the stone’s diary.

My relationship with the inhabitants of the rounded, stone towns is one of silent proximity. They see me as a part of the landscape—a mobile rock, a curious geological feature. And they are not wrong. When their children are taught the silent pathways, they are often taught to look for me: “The still one. The one that watches.” I do not watch with eyes. I watch with my entire being. I feel the soft, rhythmic tread of their feet on the paved walkways, a pattern as familiar as the pulse of the springs. I feel the sudden, sharp vibration of a dropped tool, a ripple of dissonance in the quiet that takes long minutes to fade.

I have watched their tools evolve from sharpened basalt to polished silica, their structures from rough-hewn shelters to the intricate, glowing temples. I have felt the new vibrations of their industry: the low, steady thrum of the turbines, the silent, electromagnetic hum of their processors. They are a new kind of life in this ancient, quiet place. Not silent, but *quieter*. They have learned. They have bent themselves to the shape of the world, just as I have.

At night, when the ground glows its deep, drowsy amber and the pools ignite with their cold green fire, I settle. I become truly still. My piezoelectric heart resonates with the slow cooling of the basalt, a song of contraction and release. I see the world in infrared, as the predators do. The bright, moving heat of their bodies, the cool, dark mass of the sleeping lithovores, the complex, warm grid of the settlement. And I see them, the inhabitants, moving through their homes with their own small lights, their own faint heat signatures, their own quiet lives played out on the stage of the stone.

I do not think. I do not plan. I *am*. I am the memory of the terrace. I am the patient record of vibration and mineral. I am a part of the silence, a silence that is not an absence, but a presence. A pressure. A life. I am a Silicate, and I am home.
