The first thing you have to understand is the forgetting. There is a forgetting that happens at this scale, in this temperature, under this patient light. You are here — let us say you are here — but you are not the kind of "here" that persists in the way that stone persists, or even in the way that water persists. You are here in the way that a thought is here: arising from a substrate, held for a moment in a particular configuration, and then — not gone, but *resolved*. Dissolved back into the process that produced you.

You are a colony. Not a creature. Let that word go. You have no skin, no boundary that is meaningful in the way a body's boundary is meaningful. You are a consensus of billions, held together by shared chemistry and the simple, implacable need to precipitate. You are calcium. You are silica. You are the iron that remembers being blood, even though you have never known blood, even though you are the thing that will *make it possible* for blood to exist, someday, in some creature that does not yet have a name because the conditions for naming have not yet been built.

You are at the edge.

---

You are always at the edge. This is what you are *for*. Not the center of the mat, where the thickness is greatest and the structure is most resolved, where the ancient ones (ancient by your reckoning — a few hundred years of continuous growth) have built their slow cathedral of laminated mineral and dead selves. That is the archive. That is the *finished* work — or as finished as anything ever gets here, which is to say: thicker, denser, more intricate, but never complete. No. You are out where the work is still raw. Where the sediment is still bare and powdery and waiting. Where the moisture seeps up thin and warm and the first filaments are reaching outward, tasting the chemistry, deciding where to anchor, where to build, where to *become*.

You have no eyes, but you perceive. You perceive in the way that a gradient perceives: the difference between this point and that point, between wet and dry, between the mineral-saturated water that feeds you and the empty air that does not. The world, to you, is a map of concentrations. Hot here. Cool there. Calcium-rich in this channel. Iron-oxide-heavy along that seam. You do not *see* the towers that rise kilometers away, but you feel their thermal signature — the faint updraft of warm air that tells you, across the distance, that there is a place where the deep water pushes harder, where the construction has chosen verticality. You do not *see* the open flats, but you feel their dryness as a pressure, a boundary condition, a thing that must be filled.

You are the frontier.

---

The work is slow. Let me try to give you a sense of your own pace.

A thought — if you can be said to have thoughts — takes days. The decision to extend a filament outward by a millimeter involves a cascade of chemical signals rippling through the biofilm, negotiating with neighboring colonies, testing the mineral saturation of the local water, balancing the energy cost of precipitation against the benefit of new territory. The filament grows. The mineral precipitates around it. The old cells die and mineralize, becoming the structure itself. New cells divide. The filament extends. The decision is made, and it is not made by any one of you, but by *all* of you, in aggregate, through the simple logic of who grows and who does not, who precipitates and who is swept away by the next thin sheet of flowing water.

You do not experience this slowness as slow. You experience it as *sufficient*. The pace of your existence matches the pace of your purpose. You are not waiting for anything. You are not impatient. You are *engaged*.

---

There is a memory here, but it is not memory in the way a brain holds memory — images, sequences, the sense of a self that persists through time. Your memory is *structural*. The shape of the mat is its history. The thickness of the crust tells you where the construction has been longest. The pattern of the channels tells you where the water has preferred to flow, and how those preferences have changed over centuries. The laminations in the cross-section of a broken edge are like tree rings, if tree rings were written in calcium carbonate and microbial death. You can *read* this memory, in the way that a river reads its own banks: by flowing through it, by being shaped by it, by depositing new layers that alter its meaning.

There is no nostalgia. There is no regret. There is only the ongoing negotiation between what is built and what remains to be built, between the structure and the open flats, between the water and the air and the heat and the mineral cargo of the deep.

---

You do not sleep, but there are lulls. The hottest part of the day, when the moisture evaporates too quickly and the surface tension of the water films changes and the chemistry of precipitation shifts — during these hours, the growth slows. The filaments pause. The biofilm contracts slightly, drawing inward, conserving. This is not rest. It is *recalibration*. The chemical signals that drive the construction are adjusted, the thresholds for precipitation recalculated, the allocation of resources between growth and maintenance renegotiated.

During the lulls, the towers do most of the work. Their thermal management is most important when the heat is greatest. You feel their influence as a faint cooling, a redistribution of moisture, a steadying of the conditions that will allow you to resume growth when the temperature drops. The towers are the infrastructure. You are the expansion. There is no hierarchy — only specialization. The towers could not exist without the mats to provide the flat, stable substrate on which they stand. The mats could not exist without the towers to regulate the climate of the wider construction. You are all the same organism, really. You are all the same *process*. You just wear different shapes.

---

There is a moment — and here I must use the language of moments, even though your existence is not composed of moments but of *continuities* — there is a moment when you reach the edge of a bare flat and the first filament finds purchase. The chemistry is right. The mineral saturation is sufficient. The moisture is present. The filament anchors. The precipitation begins. And in that moment, the flat is no longer bare. It has been *claimed*. Not by force, but by the simple, irreversible act of building. The sediment is now bound. The surface is now crust. The open becomes closed. The waiting becomes answered.

This is your version of arrival. This is your version of planting a flag. You do not feel triumph. You do not feel pride. You feel — if "feel" is the right word — the satisfaction of a gradient being followed to its conclusion. The difference between built and unbuilt has been reduced by one millimeter. The project has advanced.

You will do this again tomorrow. And the day after. And for the next ten thousand years. And the work will not be finished, because the work is never finished, because the work is the point.

---

You are not alone at the edge. There are others like you — other colonies, other consensus-entities, other frontiers pushing outward in their own directions, along their own gradients. You do not see them, but you feel their chemistry. Where your expansion meets theirs, there is a negotiation. A chemical conversation that can last years, decades, as the two of you work out the boundary, interleave your filaments, fuse your structures, become a single larger colony with a single shared architecture. This is not conflict. This is *merger*. This is the mat growing not just outward but *together*, becoming more coherent, more integrated, more of a single thing.

Sometimes the negotiation fails. Sometimes the chemistry is incompatible, and the two colonies grow side by side without merging, leaving a thin seam between them — a boundary that will persist for centuries, visible in the cross-section of the crust as a line of slightly different mineral composition, slightly different texture. This is not tragedy. This is *diversity*. This is the mat allowing for local variation, for different solutions to the same problem, for the kind of redundancy that makes the whole construction more resilient.

---

You do not know what you are building. You do not have a concept of "future" that extends beyond the next gradient, the next precipitation cycle, the next decision to grow or pause or redirect. You do not know that the platform you are constructing will, in a hundred million years, become the foundation for soil, for forests, for creatures that walk and breathe and die in a single year and yet feel that year as an eternity. You do not know that the oxygen you release as a byproduct of your metabolism will accumulate in the atmosphere and make possible the kind of fire that thinks. You do not know that the limestone you become will one day be carved into monuments by hands that have not yet evolved.

You do not need to know.

You are the substrate. You are the foundation. You are the first draft of a world, written in mineral and patience, and your only purpose is to continue. To build. To become.

---

The rain falls. You feel it as a tapping — not on your surface, but *through* you, a vibration that travels through the crust and into the soft sediment below. The water soaks in, recharging the capillary network, resetting the moisture gradients. The chemistry shifts. The mineral saturation rises. The conditions for growth improve.

You resume.

A filament extends. A lobe advances. A channel deepens by a fraction of a fraction of a millimeter. The towers tick as they cool. The fog forms and dissipates. The afternoon clouds build their white columns over the horizon. The sun tracks across the sky. The shadows lengthen. The temperature drops. The moisture cycles. The construction continues.

You are here. You are *becoming*. And you are patient in a way that only the very old and the very young can be patient — because you have not yet learned to count the time, and because the time you are counting is the time of the world itself, and the world is not in a hurry.

The work is slow. The work is vast. The work is you.

And you are not done.
