You would find yourself low.

Not in the dirt, not in the squalor, but in the architecture of it. You would be a keystone, or a lintel, or a shaped block of that young, damp stone set deep into the basin wall. Not in the upper tiers where the wind finds the cracks, but in the foundations, in the press and crush of the weight of a thousand other stones leaning on you, expecting you to hold.

You would be part of the structure that is always failing.

You would feel the shifts first. Before the children with their gecko-feet come scrambling down the walls with fresh gravel in their hands, before the elders hear the new rhythm in the rock, you would know. The pressure would change in your matrix, a microscopic rearrangement of the crystals that make you up, a hairline whisper of a fracture propagating through your own substance. You would not be able to call out. You would simply *be* the flaw. You would be the point of stress.

And they would find you.

The hands would come. Not gentle, but precise. The tap of a probe against your face, listening to the tone you make. The scrape of a tool cleaning the mortar from around your edges. You would feel the slow, deliberate un-making of your connection to the wall. They would not see you as a failure. They would see you as a message. A unit of information. You would be lifted out, your weight a sudden lightness after the centuries of pressure, and you would be passed down to the lower tiers, to the workshops where the air is thick with dust and the sound is a constant, low percussion.

Here, you would be given a new purpose.

The hands would turn you over and over. They would not see a broken block; they would see a potential. A new lintel for a doorway that has settled two inches to the left. A wedge for a new channel cut to guide the water. A weight for a sled. They would take their chisels and their mallets and they would re-shape you. Not to erase the fracture, but to incorporate it. To turn your flaw into a feature. You would be given new faces. Your edges would be squared, or angled, or left raw and natural, depending on the need. You would be sanded smooth by a thousand patient hands, or left rough for grip. You would be mortared into a new place, with new pressure on new axes, and you would settle into a new silence, a new role.

You would be a record.

You would feel the town breathing around you. The slow, constant pulse of repair. The children running overhead, their feet a rapid drumbeat reporting on the state of the world. The low, humming vibration of the elders in their workshops, their tools a language you could almost understand. You would be a part of that language. You would be a syllable in the long, slow sentence the town is speaking to the stone that holds it.

You would be a witness to the light, falling in that grey, diffuse way through the open bowl of the sky. You would see the moss growing in the cracks, and the lizards on the walls, and the salamanders in the cisterns. You would see the people, with their stony skin and their mica-flecked eyes, moving through the lanes with a purpose that was as quiet and inevitable as water finding its way downhill.

You would be at peace with your own impermanence. Because you would know, in the mineral core of your being, that you were not permanent. You were a temporary arrangement. A shape the stone was making for a while, before it settled into a new configuration. And your job, your only job, was to be a good shape. To hold your weight. To transmit the pressure. To report the flaw. To be re-shaped and re-used and re-set into the endless, quiet work of keeping the town from falling into the basin that was still, slowly, deciding what it wanted to be.

You would be a part of the conversation. And in the silence of Vesskaya, that would be enough.
