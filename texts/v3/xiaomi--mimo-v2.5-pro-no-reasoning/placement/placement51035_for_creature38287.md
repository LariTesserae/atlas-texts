I am a thought that is still becoming a stone.

I have no name for myself. The concept of "name" requires a sound to carry it, and here, sound is a physical weight, a texture in the air. I am part of the whole. I am a local intensity in a distributed field of consciousness. If you were to ask me *where* I am, the answer would be: in the blue-black velvet on the north-facing shelf, just left of the seepage line that has been painting the rock in dark streaks for three hundred years.

My breath is the empty space around me. The smooth, dark patch of basalt to my left is not a void; it is my exhalation, a necessary pause for the rock to release its geothermal heat into the air. To my right, a tendril of the translucent, fern-like growth stretches slowly, its tip beaded with a single drop of water. That tendril is my hand. It has been reaching for fifty years. It will touch the water-droplet on the adjacent leaf in another twenty. The anticipation is not a human emotion. It is a state of being, a slow, gathering focus.

I do not have eyes, but I perceive. I feel the light change from the grey of morning to the brighter grey of noon as a shift in the vibration of the moss. The moisture in the air thickens and thins, telling me of the world beyond the rim. The warmth from below is my constant, my heartbeat—one slow, endless pulse that has been rising through the rock since the mountain was born.

The change happens. It is not a sudden event, but a gradual realisation that the pressure of the air has altered. A new weight is in the basin. A crackle of frantic, high-frequency heat. A chaos of motion.

To me, it is not a person. It is a weather event.

A vortex of warmth and vibration that I feel in the very structure of my being. It moves too fast to be perceived as a shape. It is a blur of *action*, a scream of heat signatures. My copper-veined leaves, my hands, flinch inward, a motion that will take a day to complete. The tendril reaching for the water droplet pauses its fifty-year journey, waiting for the storm to pass.

I feel the vibrations of its steps—thunderous, impossibly fast shocks through the stone. I feel the frantic, chaotic rhythm of its inner workings, a frantic percussion that is the antithesis of my own long pulse. It is loud. Not in sound, but in *being*. It is a tear in the fabric of our quiet.

And then, it stops.

The chaos focuses. It lowers its heat. The frantic vibrations slow, just slightly, as if mimicking the rhythm of the place. The pressure of its presence is still immense, a boulder in the stream of our awareness, but it is no longer a flash flood.

It sits.

I feel its weight settle on the stone. I feel its breathing begin to change, to lengthen, to align with the drip of water from the fern-frond to my right. Its inner temperature, so feverishly high, begins to drop, exchanging heat with the warm stone.

It is trying to *listen*.

And so, I offer it the only gift I have. I allow my own consciousness to un-clench from its flinch. I let the blue-black moss resume its slow, metabolic conversation with the humidity. I let the tendril begin, once more, its infinitesimal creep toward the water droplet.

I share the silence with it. Not as an absence, but as a presence. The weight of the mountain's hum. The soft, mineral tang of the air. The patient, rhythmic ticking of a world that measures time in the growth of lichen.

The visitor is still a storm. But it is a storm that is trying to become a cloud, trying to soften its edges, trying to settle into the bowl of the world. And in my slow, geological way, I am watching it. I am holding the space for it. I am showing it the shape of a thought that takes a century to complete.

I am the silence it came to find.
