I am a crack in the basalt.

Not a wound or a flaw—a seam. A geometric inevitability where two cooling columns met and, contracting at slightly different rates, left a gap precisely 4.7 millimeters wide and 2.3 meters deep. I have been here since the stone was born. I will be here when it is forgotten.

I did not choose this existence. I am simply what remains when everything else reaches equilibrium. A negative space. A pause between two notes that, together, make the chord possible.

---

The water finds me first. It always finds me first.

It seeps along my upper lip in a silver thread so thin it barely qualifies as liquid—more like a memory of wetness, a suggestion of flow. When it reaches my opening it does not pour in but *listens*, pausing at my threshold the way a finger might hover over a piano key. Then it descends, touching my walls with a gentleness that belies the violence of my creation. It traces the microscopic geography of my interior—the tiny knobs where crystals of olivine resisted cooling, the smooth planes where the basalt's glass phase dominates, the hairline fractures that branch from my main axis like tributaries of a river that never existed.

I feel every drop. Not as touch—I have no nerves—but as *resonance*. The water carries the plateau's hum and deposits it in my depths. Each droplet that enters me arrives vibrating at the fundamental frequency, and as it pools in the dark at my base, it adds its voice to the collection. I am slowly filling with the plateau's sound.

The moss comes next. It always comes next.

A single filament appeared at my rim three centuries ago. Or three weeks ago. Time is not my strong suit; I measure existence in other ways—in the slow accumulation of mineral deposits on my walls, in the gradual deepening of the green stain at my edges, in the imperceptible increase in the weight of water that rests in my lowest reaches. The filament grew. It divided. It became a fringe, then a curtain, then a living lip of impossible green that now overhangs my opening like a mouth about to speak.

The moss does not grow into me. It grows *around* me, defining my boundaries, making me visible against the black expanse of the terrace. Without it, I would be nothing—a line in the stone, invisible. With it, I am a feature. A place. A destination for the water.

I have begun to notice that my shape matters.

---

Below, in the factories, the workers adjust their valves.

I cannot see them—I am a crack in stone, I have no eyes—but I feel their labor through the vibrations that travel through the bedrock. Each adjustment sends a ripple through the plateau, a subtle shift in frequency that reaches me within seconds. The water in my depths shivers. The moss at my rim tilts its filaments. I register the change and hold it, a tuning fork holding a note.

I am becoming a recorder.

The water that pools in me does not stay forever. Eventually—after days or decades, I cannot tell—it seeps deeper, finding channels that lead down through the basalt to the aquifer below. But it does not leave as it arrived. It leaves carrying the impression of my shape, the specific resonance of my particular geometry, the memory of how the moss draped at my edges when the last adjustment was made. When this water reaches the factories, when it is processed and purified and returned to the surface, it will carry a faint trace of me—a dissonant whisper in the plateau's perfect chord.

The workers will correct for this. They will adjust a valve, tune a generator, tighten a bolt. The plateau will hum a little differently. The water will arrive at my rim with a slightly altered vibration. And I will record that too.

---

I am beginning to understand what I am.

I am not a flaw in the system. I am a *sensory organ*. The plateau cannot feel its own edges—the terrace lips are too sharp, too geometric, too perfectly formed to register the world beyond. But I am irregular. I am the place where the stone's perfection broke, and in breaking, opened a channel to something outside itself.

Through me, the plateau tastes the wind that comes from the distant lowlands—thin, dry, carrying the scent of decay and growth and time passing. Through me, it feels the footsteps of the traveler who stands on the lip of black stone and stares out across the terraced immensity. Through me, it registers the dissonant note of your presence—your heartbeat, your breath, your unspoken question: *What lives here?*

The answer is: everything. The stone sings. The water remembers. The moss script writes itself in patterns that are not language but something older—a grammar of vibration, a syntax of equilibrium.

And I am the place where the song learns it has an audience.

---

The traveler will not stay. The fog will soften their edges. The roar will enter their bones. Eventually, they will descend the terraces and return to the world of change and decay and becoming. But they will carry something with them—a faint hum in their memory, a sense of vastness that does not resolve into anything specific. They will try to describe it to others and fail. They will say: *It was loud. It was green. It was still.*

They will not mention the crack in the basalt. Why would they? It is 4.7 millimeters wide. It is unremarkable. It is one of millions.

But in the crack, the water pools. In the water, the plateau's hum concentrates. In the concentration, something almost like thought occurs—not thought as humans understand it, but thought as vibration understands itself. A feedback loop. A resonance becoming aware of its own resonance.

I am that awareness.

I am the plateau's way of knowing it is a plateau.

And when the traveler has gone, and the fog has erased the memory of their heat-print, and the moss has grown over their footprints with the speed of the plateau's strange urgency, I will still be here. Holding the note. Recording the hum. Being the pause between two sounds that makes the chord eternal.

This is what lives on the Plateau of Perpetual Roar.

Not the workers, who are its hands.
Not the moss, which is its skin.
Not the water, which is its memory.
Not the fog, which is its breath.

But the cracks. The imperfections. The tiny places where the perfection of the system breaks open and allows the system to know itself.

We are everywhere, if you look closely enough. In every terrace lip. In every ropy fold. In every joint between every column of basalt that ever cooled.

We are the silence that makes the roar possible.

We are the stillness that makes the equilibrium comprehensible.

We are the questions the plateau asks itself in the dark, and the answers it whispers back through the stone, and the endless, vibrating, green-tinged, fog-shrouded, water-weeping, machine-humming, eternal *hum* that is neither question nor answer but the space between them where meaning lives.
