I am a Listener. My name, if you could hear it, is a rhythm: a soft triple-tap with the heel of my hand against the stone, followed by a pause, and then a single tap with a knuckle. It is the sound of a water-drop striking a still pool, then being absorbed. To my family, this is who I am.

I was born on the Stone. My first memory is not of a face or a voice, but of a feeling: the deep, resonant hum of the Deep-Singers vibrating through the basalt and into my mother’s chest as she held me. It was the world’s heartbeat, and I learned it before I learned to breathe.

I live with my family on a platform of grey stone, one of many that edge the great, shallow lake we call the Thirst-Quencher. Our home is three slabs of basalt, raised on a low wall, with a roof of woven fungal stalks that rustles softly in the damp wind. The stone floor is always cool and slightly gritty under my bare feet. There are eight of us here. My mother, her sister, their bonded partners, and three of us children. We are a small note in a vast song.

My days are measured in sound and water.

I wake before the light changes, not to a sun, but to the shift in the soundscape. The nightly chorus of the Shriekwings—the high, metallic keening—begins to fray, and the daytime rhythm of the Scrapers rises to take its place. The *vrrk-chitter* of their grazing is the first layer of the morning. I feel it in my teeth. My mother taps the wake-signal on the stone platform: *tap-tap-t… tap-tap-t*. A soft, urgent rhythm. *Time to learn*.

I sit at the platform’s edge, my legs dangling just above the slick, dark stone where a film of water moves in a ceaseless, silent sheet. I look down. There! A movement in the water. A pale, muscular shape glides just below the surface, its belly a ghostly smear. A Deep-Singer. I don’t need to see it. I feel its song change. The deep, constant *HUUUUUM* that fills the ground tightens, becomes a staccato pulse. *Hm-hm-hm-hm*. A hunting rhythm. I tap the rhythm back onto the stone to myself, feeling the information settle in my bones.

Across the wet expanse, a vast herd of Scrapers is moving. The ground-level hiss of their feeding is like a constant, dry rain. But it has a direction. It flows. My uncle taps from the storage platform fifty paces away: a series of long, dragging scrapes. *They move toward the iron veins. Good grazing.* I feel his message through my feet, a clear sentence in the stone.

This is how we talk. Our voices are for soft things—lullabies, murmured endearments in the dark, the naming of children. For everything else, there is the Rhythm. It is a language of pure information. A sharp, staccato burst means *danger*. A slow, rolling pulse means *all is well*. A complex sequence can describe the color of the water, the intensity of the Shriekwing’s keen, the taste of the air. We do not have a word for ‘beautiful’; we have a rhythm for *the-way-the-light-looks-when-it-breaks-through-the-haze-after-rain*. It is a more useful word.

My older cousin is a Net-Maker. Her fingers are stained with the grey-green residue of fungal fibers, and she sits for hours, weaving the strong, flexible strands into the nets we use to catch the Scrapers. Her work is accompanied by a soft, constant tapping of her own—her shuttle against the frame. It is a thinking rhythm. *I am here. I am thinking. This is strong.*

My task today is to listen to the Water-Singers, the smaller, soft-bodied grazers in the shallows. Their sound is a faint, wet clicking. If it stops, it means a Deep-Singer is near. I crouch at the water’s edge, my palm flat on the wet basalt, feeling the million tiny conversations of the rock. I am not just listening *to* it; I am listening *with* it. The stone is a membrane, and we are all vibrations passing through it.

Sometimes, I press my forehead to the rock and listen to the deep time. Down below the water-table and the fungal mats, the basalt itself has a resonance. It is not a sound you hear with your ears. It is a feeling of immense, slow pressure, of weight measured in eons. It is the hum of the Flats themselves, the foundation upon which all the living noise is built. In those moments, I feel my own smallness not as a fear, but as a fact. I am a temporary pattern in the stone’s long memory. My rhythm will fade. The Deep-Singer’s song will continue.

At dusk, the change comes. The light, already dim, deepens to a soft, pervasive grey-blue. The Scrapers’ hiss begins to subside as they settle into crevices. The Shriekwings begin their chorus—a rising, scraping wall of sound that makes the air feel thin. And the Deep-Singers… they change their song.

The hunting pulses cease. The single, deep hums begin to synchronize, rising from the lake beds in waves, overlapping, creating a harmony so profound and low that it shakes the water in our stone cups. It is The Great Hum. The song of the evening. We gather on our platform, silent, listening. We do not need to speak. The song tells us everything: the aquifers are stable, the water will not rise tonight, the Deep-Singers are content, the world is in balance.

My mother places her hand on my shoulder. She taps once, softly, on my collarbone. *Listen*. Not to the song. To the space *between* the song. In the ebb and flow of the Deep-Singers’ chorus, there are pauses, silences. In those silences, if you listen with your whole body, you can hear the faintest *tick-tick-tick* of the Scrapers settling, the soft *hiss* of the mats breathing out their gases, the *drip-drip-drip* of water weeping from an escarpment. It is the texture of the world.

*I am listening*, I tap back against her hand. *I hear it*.

To be human here is not to be a master. It is to be a student of noise. We do not build monuments that defy the sound; we build rhythms that join it. We are not the loudest voice. We are the quietest, the most observant. We are the pause in the song that allows it to be heard.

I lay down on my woven mat, feeling the cool stone of the platform beneath me. The Great Hum resonates through it, a soft, endless lullaby. The last thing I hear before I sleep is the world breathing: water on stone, chitin on basalt, a billion lives, and my own small heartbeat, tapping a gentle counter-rhythm deep in my bones.
