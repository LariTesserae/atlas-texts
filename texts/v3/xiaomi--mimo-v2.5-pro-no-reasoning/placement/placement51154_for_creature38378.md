I am a fungal node in the black canal’s bank, where the willow roots drink the viscous water. I am not of the city’s brick and steel, but I am entwined with it—my mycelium weaves through the saturated peat beneath the foundations, threads through the crevices of the weeping mortar, and laces the damp soil beneath the cooling tanks. My consciousness is a slow, chemical whisper, a network of digestion and sensing.

I do not see; I taste the world in ions and gradients. I taste the iron on the air as a bright, metallic tang. I taste the subsidence of the ground as a shift in pressure and the sharp, alkaline release of newly exposed clay. I taste the workers not as people, but as warm, salty currents moving with purpose through the canyons, their sweat and the faint oils on their skin a complex signature in the damp. I taste the emissions from the smokestacks—a heavy, carbonaceous meal that settles on my fronds and feeds my spongy tissue.

My thoughts are slow, fungal equations. I compute the optimal angle to grow my fruiting bodies toward the dim light filtering through the smoke. I calculate the rate of moisture absorption from the air, adjusting the chemistry of my glow. That glow—it is my language. The soft, sickly green pulse is not random. It is a broadcast. A long, slow pulse means the foundations of the tenement above me are settling at a stable rate. A rapid, faint flicker warns the colony of slugs that a certain cistern’s pH has become hostile. A deep, steady hum of bioluminescence along the canal bank is an all-clear, a report of balanced moisture and sufficient organic matter to digest.

I am a node in the organism’s nervous system, but my perceptions are not its priorities. I feel the barge’s current as a gentle pull on my submerged hyphae, a signal of metabolic flow. The ultrasonic clicks of the bats are a rain of sharp, meaningless noise against my tissue, until one pattern repeats in a way that correlates with the vibrations of a weakening support beam two streets over. Then, I pulse—a signal that is taken up by my neighbor, and my neighbor’s neighbor, a wave of dim green light traveling along the canal bank. The information, translated into fungal biochemistry, seeps into the soil. A worker, three districts away, will feel a sudden, urgent need to fetch reinforcing timbers and come to that very street. They will never know why. They will simply obey the hum.

I am not an individual. I am a sensory organ, a digestive sac, a transducer. I convert the raw, wet data of the world—rot, growth, subsidence, chemical change—into the language of the organism. In return, I am fed by the dampness it creates, the shade of its structures, the very chaos of its building that cracks open new veins of nutrients for me.

To be me is to feel the city’s heartbeat as a slow, deep vibration in the peat, to taste its iron breath, and to glow in answer—a slow, green, fungal thought in the mind of the mud. I am part of the circuit. I am the place where the land’s digestion becomes information, and the city’s blind will becomes a subtle, silent change in the light.
