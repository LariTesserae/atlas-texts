The iron taste is the first thing to find you. It climbs into your mouth with the thin air, coating your tongue, staining the back of your throat with a metallic sweetness that has nothing to do with altitude. You are not at the threshold of the Vatry yet. You are still on the scree, three hundred meters below the first cantilevered wall, but the taste has already reached you — mineral runoff dissolved in the fine mist that drifts downslope like the building’s exhalation.

You are alone. This is important. The Vatry does not permit groups.

Not by any rule or mechanism — there is no gate, no lock, no sign. But the hum, that subsonic thrumming that begins in your molars and spreads inward until it presses against the lining of your skull, has a peculiar effect on human social behavior. Groups disintegrate. Pairs become uncomfortable. Conversations collapse into grunts and gestures and finally into silence, because the hum occupies the exact frequencies that human speech needs to resonate in the chest and throat. You cannot project your voice. You cannot modulate your tone. You can only whisper, and whispers do not carry across the space between two people walking side by side.

So you walk alone.

The scree shifts under your boots with a sound like breaking pottery. Each step sends small avalanches of pale stone sliding downslope, and the dust rises around your ankles in slow, particulate clouds that the wind catches and smears eastward. The wind here is constant — not gusting, not variable, but a steady lateral pressure that has shaped the very stones, faceting them into aerodynamic forms over millennia. The stones are smooth on their windward faces, rough on their lee. You walk into the wind, and it presses against your chest like a flat hand.

The Vatry grows above you as you climb. At first it was a red-black irregularity against the pale peaks, a shadow with edges. Now it resolves into geometry — the stacked rectangles, the cantilevers, the impossible angles at which each volume has been placed. You begin to see the glass. Thousands of panes, clouded but intact, catching the high-altitude sun and returning it as a diffuse, mineral light that has no warmth in it. The building does not shine. It glows, the way old bone glows.

You are not the first to approach. There are others on the scree — not companions, not even acquaintances, but other solitary figures making their own ways up. You can see them in your peripheral vision: dots of color against the grey stone, each one moving at their own pace, none looking at another. You do not wave. You do not call out. The hum prevents it.

The gap in the wall is at roughly the midpoint of the lowest volume — a place where a panel, three meters wide and four tall, has corroded through its rivets and fallen inward. The panel lies on the floor just inside, half-buried in moss, its surface pitted and porous, more like pumice than iron. You step over it and the world changes.

The hum redoubles. It was pressure before; now it is substance. It fills the space between your skin and your clothes, between your teeth, between the bones of your inner ear. Your vision swims for a moment, then steadies. The air is warmer — noticeably, measurably warmer — and wet. You can see the moisture in the air, a faint haze that softens every edge, every surface, every shadow. The light from the ceiling arrays is on — a pale, bluish-white light that flickers in a slow, arrhythmic pattern, like a heartbeat made of lightning.

The space before you is vast. The ceiling arches overhead at a height that must be forty meters, its ribs curving in parallel lines of iron gray, and between them the glass lets in the sky — but the sky here is not the sharp blue you saw outside. It is filtered through mineral deposits and condensation into something softer, something milkier, a light that has been digested. The floor is thirty meters below the entry point, accessible by a corroded staircase that descends along the interior wall, its treads furred with moss so thick it looks like green velvet. The catwalks are up there, crossing the void in iron lattices, and from them the vegetation hangs in curtains — ferns, vines, the dense tangles that form mid-air hedges where catwalks intersect.

And the sound. The sound is everything. The hum is the foundation, but layered over it are the smaller sounds of the building living: the tick and ping of iron expanding and contracting with the warmth, the slow drip of condensation running down the interior walls, the faint, almost subliminal buzz of insects moving through the upper canopy. The birds are there too — you can see them, small and dun-colored, hopping along the catwalk railings with a jerky, mechanical motion that matches the flicker of the lights.

You descend. The staircase groans under your weight — not with structural weakness, but with the particular complaint of iron that has been load-bearing for centuries and has developed opinions about it. Each step releases a puff of spores from the moss, and they rise in the warm air like green smoke. By the time you reach the floor, your clothes are damp. Your hair is damp. The air is so saturated that breathing feels like drinking.

The floor is not flat. It is a landscape in miniature — shallow basins where water collects, rises of corroded metal where machinery once stood, long drifts of organic matter that have accumulated over centuries into something that is not quite soil but is no longer merely debris. The ferns grow here too, taller than you, their fronds arching over your head, their stems as thick as your wrist. You push through them and the moisture they hold spills down your arms.

The lower volumes are warmer. You descend further — through gaps in the floor where staircases have rusted away entirely, using the vine-ladders that have grown in their place, their rungs smooth and warm to the touch, living wood wrapped around dead iron. The air thickens. The light dims — fewer arrays function down here, and the glass is more clouded, more stained. The hum intensifies, becomes more rhythmic, more shuddering. You can feel it in your feet now, transmitted through the floor: the turbines, deep below, turning with their geological patience.

This is where the Pale Crawlers live. You see your first one in a shallow pool beneath a catwalk — a shape like a large salamander, perhaps thirty centimeters long, translucent enough that you can see the shadow of its spine, the dark mass of its gut, the branching network of its gills. It does not move as you approach. It hangs in the water, suspended, its limbs limp, its gills fanning slowly. It is not dead. It is resting in the way that everything here rests — in a state of suspended attention, waiting for the rhythm of the building to tell it what to do next.

You sit on a corroded beam near the pool. The moss cushions you. The hum vibrates through the metal into your bones. The Pale Crawler drifts closer, attracted perhaps by your warmth, perhaps by the CO2 you exhale. Its eyeless head turns toward you. It has no eyes, but it has senses — lateral line organs, like a fish, that detect pressure waves in the water. It is listening to you. It is tasting the disturbance you make in the air and water simply by being here.

You realize, after a time — minutes? hours? you cannot tell, the light arrays do not correspond to any external cycle — that you are breathing in time with the turbines. Your heartbeat has slowed. Your thoughts have become less linear, more circular, more like the slow turn of the machinery below. The hum is not outside you anymore. It is in your chest, in your skull, in the rhythm of your blood.

This is what the Vatry does to visitors. It does not trap them. It does not transform them. It simply... includes them. You are a warm body in a warm place. You are a disturbance in the condensation. You are a source of CO2 for the ferns. You are a potential perch for the Rivet-Finches, who are watching you from the catwalk above with their small, bright eyes. You are, for as long as you remain, part of the system — not as an inhabitant, but as a phenomenon, a temporary perturbation that the building will absorb and eventually forget.

You could leave. The gap in the wall is still there. The scree, the wind, the sharp blue sky — they are still there, beyond the iron and glass. But the hum makes the thought of leaving feel abstract, theoretical, like remembering a dream you had long ago. The warmth is here. The damp is here. The rhythm is here. The Pale Crawler is drifting in its pool, eyeless and patient, and the Rivet-Finch is singing its silent song, and the turbines are turning, and the light arrays are flickering, and the condensation is running down the walls in slow, endless tears.

You are here.

You are part of the hum.

You are in the Vatry.

And for now — for this long, slow, humming now — that is enough.

*You will leave eventually. Everyone does. But not yet. Not for a while. The building has a way of extending time, of making the present feel like the only tense that matters. Days pass outside. Weeks, maybe. Inside, there is only the rhythm, the warmth, the green. You will emerge, blinking, into the sharp light, and the wind will feel like a blade, and the silence — the true silence of the high peaks, empty of hum — will be the loudest thing you have ever heard. You will descend the scree with iron in your mouth and spores in your lungs, and you will never be able to adequately explain to anyone what the Vatry is. You will try. You will say it was like being inside a body, or a machine, or a forest. You will say the light was strange and the air was wet and the birds were quiet. You will say there were amphibians in the dark, and insects that spun glass, and a sound that never stopped. They will nod. They will not understand. They will see a ruin on a ridge, a curiosity, a structure. They will not hear the hum. You will. For the rest of your life, in quiet moments, in the pause between breaths, you will hear it — faint, subsonic, just at the edge of perception. The Vatry, still turning. The Vatry, still alive. The Vatry, still waiting for you to come back.*

*You won't. But you will want to. Every day, you will want to.*
