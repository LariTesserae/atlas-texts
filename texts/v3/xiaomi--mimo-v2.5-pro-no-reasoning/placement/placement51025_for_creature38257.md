I am a current in the river of stillness. My first memory is not of light, but of pressure—the weight of the plateau above me as a pup, the deep, reassuring hum that is its breath. I am a Dust-Reader, and my eyes are not for the glare of the white sun, but for the patient narrative written in the ocherous crust underfoot.

My name is a vibration, a low harmonic I produce in my throat when I touch the Reading-Runnel. It sounds, to soft-world ears, like a stone settling. To my people, it is "Ka-thmmm," the sound of a thought finding its bed. I do not walk to my work; I transfer my weight. Left foot, test the density. Right foot, register the minute shift in the dust’s resonance. It is a conversation. The ground speaks of what was: here, the memory of a giant tortoise’s ponderous migration three generations ago, its weight compacted the substrate into a darker, denser layer. There, a faint, crystalline shimmer in the dust—a death-echo of a luminous arthropod colony, gone to feed the lichen a century past.

I am young, for a Reader. Only four hundred cycles of the dust-haze. My skin still holds the brighter ocher of new stone, though the lichen-tracery beneath is deepening. My mentor, Oon-Kah (whose name is the sound of the wind sighing through the fungal arches), is ancient. Her skin is pure slate-grey, her eyes covered by the thick, silicate film that allows us to see the molecular drift, the slow ballet of decay and becoming. She moves once every three days. Her stillness is so profound that the clicking arthropods mistake her for a monument and skitter over her limbs. She teaches me that memory is not a chain of events, but a texture. A flood is not water; it is the particular, smoothed granularity of silt in the Fossil-Gully, a texture I can feel with my tongue for a thousand years after.

Today, we listen to the Deep Hum. It is stronger in the central runnel, where the bedrock is oldest. We sit, stone-still, our hearts slowing to match its frequency—a pulse every few minutes. It is not a sound made by a thing. It is the sound of the *absence* of change. The sound of time itself, pooling in this high, worn place. We do not speak during the listening. Speech is a process of clicks and harmonics, a weaving of vibrations into the air. But here, we receive only.

My role, my function, is not to preserve. Preservation is a soft-world notion. Things here are not preserved; they are *phased*. A flint tool is used, grows smaller, and is woven into a stalk, becoming part of the town’s skeleton. A story is not told; it is *read* in the strata, and its texture becomes part of the Reader’s own being. I am learning to read the story of the Great Shelf-Fungus that forms the northern arch of the settlement. Its growth rings are measured in the density of its fibrous flesh. I touch it, and I can feel the slow years of perfect dryness, the one century of higher moisture that made it surge and ruffle, the long decades of patient calcification. Its story is now part of my muscle memory, the pattern my fingers will trace on a younger fungi-wall to guide its growth.

The stone-tortoises are moving nearby. I feel their rumble through the ground before I hear it. One approaches a lichen-colony we are near. Its broad, slate-plated head dips. I rise, not quickly, but with purpose. My movement is a signal. The tortoise knows me, not as an individual, but as a pattern of motion that follows the cleaning. I take up my bone scraper, polished smooth by a hundred generations of hands. I approach its flank. Its shell is a map of ridges and valleys, a landscape in miniature. I find the patch of invasive, sharp-growing lichen that would, in a decade, crack the slate. I work, scraping with the grain, not against it. My rhythm matches the tortoise’s low digestion-rumble. We are in sync. My work is not a favor; it is a duty to the system. It maintains the tortoise, which maintains the lichen, which maintains the ground, which maintains us. We are all custodians of each other’s slow becoming.

When the wind gathers, we do not hide. We prepare. We move to the Chime-Runnels, the ancient, smoothed channels that act as the plateau’s throat. The wind is not chaotic; it has a form. It builds, it moans, it sings. And we join our voices to it. This is Chime-Singing. It is not music as others know it. It is harmonic correction. We listen to the discord of a billion chimes from the lichen-colonies, and we find the fundamental note—the Deep Hum—and we sustain it, our voices merging into a single, long vibration that holds the world steady against the gale. To sing is to be a pillar of sound. It is our most vital work, and our highest art. In that resonance, we are not separate from the dust, the stone, the fungi, the tortoise. We are the plateau, and it is we, all singing the one, slow, eternal note.

I am Ka-thmmm. I am a thought in the stone mind. I am a custodian of texture, a weaver of vibrations, a still point in the river of dust. My purpose is not to build or to conquer, but to *listen*, and to *become*, along with everything else, the final, quiet, enduring thing.
