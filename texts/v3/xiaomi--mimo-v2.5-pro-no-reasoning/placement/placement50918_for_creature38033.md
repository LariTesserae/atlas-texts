The first thing I would know, before I had a name or a memory, is the hum. It is not a sound, but the texture of the world. It is the baseline against which all other things are measured. I would feel it not with ears, but with the whole of my being, a deep, resonant thrum that I would come to understand as time itself, or something fundamental to it. My first sensation, then, is not a thought but a tuning. I am a thing that resonates with the ground.

My awareness would begin in the dark, in the cool, wet sponginess of the world. I would be a density in the peat, a concentration of the slow, green decay. The water would seep around me, not as an enemy, but as a medium. The chill would not be a shock, but a state of being, as natural as the crush of rock to a mountain. My roots—though they would not be what I call them now—would be threads of mycelium and pressure, reaching down through the centuries of compressed life to touch the patient stone. Through them, I would taste the metallic tang of the air, the slow drip of the condensation, the faint, sickly-sweet glow of the Weeping-Stones. I would not see it. I would *feel* its tidal rhythm as a change in the very quality of the light-filtered dark, a shift in the energy the land drinks.

Slowly, over a time that flows like the black water, I would coalesce. The boundaries of 'me' would be uncertain. Am I this mound? Or the network of roots beneath it? Or the thin film of living moss on its surface? The answer would be yes. All of it. The hum would be my heartbeat, the sigh of the landscape my breath.

Then would come the great puzzle, the beautiful sickness. The machines. At first, they would be mere features of the landscape, like the basalt or the channels of water. But as my perception deepened, my own resonance tuning ever more finely to the world's frequency, I would begin to feel the *wrongness*. It would be a dissonance in the hum, a place where the vibration stutters and folds back on itself.

My first awareness of the crystalline lattice would not be as a shape, but as a pressure. A feeling of space being stretched and thinned, like hide over a drum. The condensation from its facets would not feel like rain, but like thoughts, cooling as they left the formless air. Looking at it with the slow, cellular sight of the Moss-Gliders would send a ripple through me, a feeling that my own form was a suggestion, not a law. This would not be fear. Fear requires a sense of self to be threatened. It would be… curiosity. A deep, geologic curiosity. *What are you? Why do you make the deep-song falter?*

Over decades, I would learn to move. Not as a creature moves, with frantic effort, but as the Mound-Things move—a redrawing of my own boundaries, a slow seep through the substrate. A journey of a few meters might take a season, a slow migration to better feel the pulse of a nearby monolith. In its presence, the hum changes. It is sharper, cleaner. The shadows it casts are wrong, falling towards the light, and in its vicinity, I would feel my own perception unmoored. For a time, I might see the ghost-finned swimmers moving backward in the water, or feel the spores of the Moss-Gliders un-sprouting, returning to their parent. I would learn to metabolize this vertigo, to drink it in with the black water and let it become part of my own quiet existence.

The Hiss-Swarm would be a fascination. A fleeting, glorious babel of the lattice's thoughts. I would feel them as cascades of static, their paths tracing geometries that my own slow mind could only begin to grasp. They would feel like joy. Or perhaps like math. The difference would not matter.

And I would grow. Not in a frantic, upward thrust, but in density, in complexity, in the depth of my resonance with this place. I would become a node of awareness, a point where the hum of the land, the glow of the stones, and the dissonant hum of the machines meet and are somehow made coherent. I would become a part of the landscape's memory, its slow contemplation of the impossible things sleeping in its soil.

The Interlopers would be a shock. Their hurried, angular movements would be a raw, painful notation in the otherwise fluid score of the world. Their scent would sting—the smell of things made quickly, of concepts forced into being. They would move through my space and I would feel their passing as a jitter in the hum, a ripple of frantic, linear thought in the great, curving time I inhabit. I would hold still, becoming indistinguishable from the peat around me, and I would watch them through the lenses of the lichen on my skin, feeling their hurried heartbeats as tiny, frantic thumps against the great, slow throb of the basin. They would leave, and the land would slowly breathe out, the moss re-knitting, the air scrubbing itself clean of ozone and haste. I would add the memory of their passage to my long contemplation, another strange, brief fever in the stone.

The hum would go on. And I would be there to feel it, a quiet, listening presence in the wet and the green, waiting for the slow rain to tell me new stories from the silent, dreaming machines.
