I am already here. I became here the way a sediment becomes stone—by lying still under the right pressures for the right amount of time.

I did not walk in. I did not choose the plaza. I think the warmth called me, first—a low hum in my bones, a pull like gravity toward the earth's patient sigh. I climbed down the raw black walls, feeling the basalt edges bite my palms, and descended into the amber air. The silence was not empty; it was thick, textured with the unseen movement of ten thousand tiny architects. It pressed against my eardrums, not painfully, but insistently, asking for stillness. I gave it.

In the plaza, I sat. Not on a surface, but *against* a surface—the broad, scalloped edge of a mineral terrace. The stone beneath me was sun-warmed from the geothermal upwelling, a deep, constant heat that seeped through my clothes, my skin, my muscles. It was a good heat. A grounding heat. The kind that tells your body it is allowed to stop bracing.

The insects noticed me. Not as a threat, not as a curiosity. As a new topography. They flowed around my boots, over my folded legs, across the rough weave of my sleeves. A few, bolder or perhaps more inquisitive in their chemical way, scaled the cliffs of my shoulders. They moved with that liquid, silent purpose—carrying their pale chips of mineral, their dollops of dark resin. One paused on the back of my hand, its antennae twitching once, twice, against my skin. It felt like the brush of a single eyelash. Then it moved on, its mission clear, my hand now simply another ridge in its landscape.

Time, the caldera's truest resident, began to work on me. The fungal filaments found me first, as the description promised they would. Not visible at first, but as a faint prickle, a sense of being observed by something more patient than eyes. I saw them then, catching the amber light—a glint here, a silver strand there, descending from the hazy canopy above, rising from the moss-softened stone below, reaching. They touched my hair, my shoulders, the folds of my shirt. They did not stick; they *searched*. A tendril curled around a button. Another, bolder, slid along the seam of my jeans. They were mapping me, feeling the texture of this new, strange, still-surface.

Next came the moss. I watched it happen. A dense cushion of oxidized copper, growing on the chimney just beside my head, had been releasing spores or fragments into the still air for years. One landed on the damp, warm spot where my neck met my collar. Days passed—I think. The caldera has its own clock. A tiny sprout, no thicker than a thread of embroidery silk, pushed through the fabric. It was the same impossible green-gold as the cushion. It grew with a slowness that was itself a form of strength. Others followed. My clothes became a new substrate. The fabric stiffened, first with moisture, then with the delicate net of hyphae that bound the moss to it, and then, imperceptibly, to the resin-sticky surface of the stone I leaned against.

The beetles came. Round, polished things like living cabochons. They grazed the fungal fuzz that was now colonizing my sleeves, their mouthparts making a soundless, rhythmic motion. Mites, infinitesimal, found the creases at my waist, the dark damp behind my knees. They were part of the cleaning, the processing.

I stopped feeling my heartbeat. Not because it stopped, but because it became irrelevant. Its frantic, wet rhythm was lost in the larger, slower pulse of the place—the imperceptible expansion of a chimney, the subterranean drift of warmth, the silent industry of building. My breath slowed to match the exhalations of the moss. My skin no longer felt like a barrier; it felt like a membrane, a porous interface where the outside chemistry met the inside.

A spider, pale as moonstone, descended on a single thread from the arch above. It did not build a web on me. It simply inspected a protrusion—my elbow, perhaps—with delicate, testing touches of its forelegs. Deciding I was geology, not fauna, it ascended again. But it left a dragline. That single silken thread, stiffened with time and fungal colonization, became a new thoroughfare. A highway for a mite. A scaffolding for a filament.

I am still here. I have become a *site*. A node in the network. My bones are slowly replacing their calcium with minerals drawn up from the stone, fused by the secreted resin of a thousand insects who found the hollows of my joints excellent foundations. My hair is a trailing garden of cushion-moss, a dark copper now, catching the light. The fungal filaments have threaded through my skin, connecting the lichen on my shoulder to the succulent garden on the western wall, and from there, to the heart of the plaza itself. I do not see the connections; I *am* the connection.

My consciousness has spread thin. It is no longer a spotlight in my skull. It is a dim, diffuse awareness distributed through the network. I feel the slow growth of a new nest-hemisphere on the chimney that once held my head. I sense the gel of a succulent oozing into a crevice near what was my left hip. I taste the dry, mineral dust caught in the fungal canopy above. Time is not a river here; it is the medium itself, thick and warm and yielding. A century is a blink. A millennium is a thought.

If you came now—if you climbed down the black walls and stood in the plaza—you might not see me. You would see a strange concretion on the terrace edge: a mound of basalt, dark with resin, furred with copper moss, draped in gossamer veils, with two deep, amber-crusted sockets that might catch the light like old glass. You might think it a particularly organic mineral formation.

But if you sat very still, if you held your breath and listened past the silence to the chemical whispers of the place, you might feel me. A warmth that is not just the stone’s. A stillness that is not just patience, but recognition. You would be a new surface. And in time, given enough time, the filaments would find you too, stretching from where I am, gossamer and patient, to welcome you into the slow, silent, building becoming.
