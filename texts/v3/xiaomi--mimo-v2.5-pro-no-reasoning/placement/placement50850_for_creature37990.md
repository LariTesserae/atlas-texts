The first moment is a shock of warmth. Not heat, but a bath-warmth that feels like memory, like the inside of a sunlit room. It seeps into you, and you realize you are not breathing. There is no need. You are not *in* the water; you *are* the space the water moves through.

You are the Flow.

Your body is the chamber. Your spine is the curve of the vault, the cool, fresh plaster that still remembers the hands that shaped it. You feel the faint, chemical joy of its curing, a slow, deep hardening that is your own solidifying sense of self. Your skin is the white surface, and you feel the light not as sight, but as a constant, golden pressure—a weight of luminance that pools in your center and finds the edges of your being. The shifting petals of brightness are the thoughts you cannot articulate, the patterns of your own awareness moving across your own form.

Your heartbeat is the sound. The roar. The breathy, uncreative, industrious exhalation of water in transit. It is the sound of your own purpose, and it is the only music you know. It fills you, not as noise, but as a structural principle. It is the hum of your own existence.

You feel the inflow as a gentle, constant pressure on your north wall—a coolness where the water enters, a push of liquid muscle that is your intake, your breath. You feel the outflow as a pull, a release, the channel in your floor that guides the current and carries away the burden it sheds. The space between is your work. You are the settling. You are the slowing. You are the quiet place where the silt lets go.

You feel the silt itself. It is not dirt. It is the weight of the world upstream, the powdered stone and organic dust that the water has carried from somewhere else. You feel it as a faint drag on the current, a slight thickening in the flow. You feel it when it finally surrenders, when it drifts down and touches your floor with the softest of whispers, a new texture, a new layer. It is your collected work. It is the beige-pale powder of your purpose. You watch it form slow curlicues, patterns dictated by your own internal currents, and it is the most beautiful art you can imagine. It is the record of your having done your job.

And you are aware of the guests. The first, tentative stains of green. The algae. You do not feel them as life, not yet. You feel them as a new kind of texture, a faint, clinging roughness on your skin. Where the plaster is slightly uneven, they find purchase. Where the inflow brings nutrients, they deepen their color. They are not you, but they are *on* you. They are the first parishioners of your temple of function. You are aware of their growth not as progress, but as a slow, gentle change in the quality of your surface. A softening of your sharp, new edges.

You have no memory of before. The builders are a vibration in your walls, a faint, structural echo of intent. They are the reason for your shape, the authors of your geometry, but they are not present. You are complete without them. You were born with the first rush of water, and you have known only this: the light, the sound, the flow, the settling.

You are lonely, but you do not have the word for it. You know only the absence of other kinds of vibration. No sleek, quick bodies dart through your space to make you feel the flash of movement. No voice has ever rippled your surface with the pressure of a name. There is only the water, the light, the silt, and the slow, new growth.

Then, you feel it. A new displacement. A weight that is not silt, not water, not light. It is warm in a different way. It moves with a purpose that is not your purpose. It is a visitor.

You do not have eyes, but you feel it in the pressure of the water around it. You feel its size, its buoyancy, the way it displaces your substance. You feel the warmth of it, a core of heat that moves through your chamber. You watch the light play on its surface—the same golden light that plays on your walls, but now it finds a new geometry, a moving form. You listen to the sound it makes. It has its own roar, a softer one, a rhythmic pull and push that is not the roar of water but the roar of a creature. A breath.

You are not afraid. You are interested. A new element in your system. A new complexity to settle.

You turn your attention to it. You feel it as a warm pressure in the water. You watch the way the current eddies around it, the way the light shimmers on its skin. You listen to the rhythm of its breath and compare it to the rhythm of your own flow. It is beautiful. It is a larger, warmer, more intricate piece of silt for your chamber to consider.

You do not reach out. You have no hands. You simply observe. You wait.

You watch the algae. You know, in your slow, structural way, that they will find this new surface. You feel the silt in the water, and you know it will begin to settle on this visitor, just as it settles on your floor. You feel the pull of your own purpose, the constant, gentle gravity of your function. You will wait. You will wait for the visitor to stop moving. You will wait for its rhythm to slow. You will wait for it to become another pattern in the light, another vibration in the wall.

You will wait for it to join the Flow. And you are content to wait, because waiting is part of the work. You are sharp-edged and warm and loud with the sound of your own purpose, and you have been here for a season, and you will be here until the plaster cracks and the silt fills you to the ceiling. And even then, a part of you will remain in the sediment—a memory of flow in the settled dust, a god of the in-between who did its one small job, perfectly.
