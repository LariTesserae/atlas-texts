I am a grain of rock flour, a speck of granite ground from the shoulders of the nunatak called the Old Man’s Spine. I was born into ice a thousand years ago, and I have been traveling ever since.

My world is blue. Not the blue of sky, but the dense, compressed blue of ancient ice that has squeezed out all air, the blue of deep pressure and slow time. I rest within the glacier’s body, embedded in a layer of firn, halfway between fresh snow and solid ice. Around me, the ice is not silent. It hums and groans, a constant symphony of stress and movement. I feel the deep, subsonic thrum of shear planes grinding past each other miles away as a vibration that loosens my bonds just enough to let me shift a micron in a century. I hear the sharp, rifle-crack of thermal contraction as a sudden jolt that sends tiny fractures racing through my matrix. And I hear the water—always the water—rushing through the veins and tunnels of the glacier, a roar that never stops, a voice that speaks in melt and flow.

I have no eyes, but I perceive light. When I am near the surface, the sun’s pressure reaches me as a persistent, liquefying warmth that turns my prison to slick film. For a moment, I am free, carried in a trickle of meltwater down a sun cup, catching the light like a diamond before the cold reclaims me and I am frozen in again, this time in a clearer, more brilliant cage. The brightness here is not seen but felt; it is a condition of existence, a constant, overexposed state that bleaches the shadows to violet.

I travel with the ice, a slow journey measured in tides of gravity. Sometimes I am deep, where the weight of centuries presses me into a density that feels like stone, and the only sounds are the distant booms of moulins—the glacier’s lungs—and the creak of its slow, relentless creep. Sometimes I am near the surface, where the wind scours and the sun melts, and I witness the fleeting shadows of birds—black choughs that wheel against the white, their cries swallowed by the song of the ice.

Once, a moulin opened near me. I was carried by a torrent of meltwater into its throat, and for a moment, I plunged through blue darkness, surrounded by the roar of a waterfall echoing in a cathedral of ice. Then the flow slowed, and I was deposited in a new layer, deeper than before. The moulin sealed shut with the cold, and I was entombed again.

I am not alone. I am part of a mosaic of ages. The grains around me are from different mountains, different centuries. Some are rust-red from iron-rich basalt, some are grey from gneiss, some are golden from quartz. We form bands in the ice—layers of memory that record the glacier’s diet. We are the texture of its thoughts, the sediment of its being.

I do not grow. I do not think. But I am aware, in my way, of the glacier’s joy. It is the joy of consistency, of a system in perfect, roaring balance. I am a tiny part of that balance. I add my friction, my minute resistance, to the whole. I am worn smoother with each passing century, my edges rounded by a river of ice. One day, I will reach the terminus. The ice will calve, and I will tumble into the outwash plain, carried by water to a distant sea. But that is a future so distant it is like a dream. For now, I am here, in the singing ice, a grain of rock in a world of frozen light, and I am content.
