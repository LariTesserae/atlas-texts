The light is the first lie you learn. It is not the color of pewter, as the old records claim. Pewter is a metal that holds a memory of warmth. This light holds nothing. It is the color of steam that has forgotten it was ever water. I know this because I see it not with eyes, but as a lack. A pressure, a change in the texture of the dark.

My world is a map of vibrations. I am curled now in my designated rest-corner, the warmth of the great piston-pipe a constant, comforting presence against my back. *Thump-thump-thump*. It is the only lullaby we have ever known. It means the core is healthy, the pressure is stable. My fingers, long and pale, trace the pitted surface of the brick. This spot is smooth, worn by generations of shoulders. It is a story of rest. A few feet above, the mortar is rough, flaking. That is a story of age, and soon a Drip-Finder will mark it, and the menders will come.

We are born of this place. Our first breath is a gasp of thick, rust-tasting air. Our skin is the clay of the foundations, our veins the dark lines of crumbling mortar. We do not see the factory; we *are* the factory, in its softest, most thinking part. My hands are not hands for making, but for knowing. They are instruments of touch, of listening. I can press my palm to a girder and know the tension in the steel, feel the minute shudders that speak of a bearing wearing thin. I can plunge my arm into the warm sludge and read the story of the settlement’s waste in the texture of the grit, the size of the nodules I find.

I am a Vibration Listener. My mother was a Sludge-Sifter. She would bring home the small, hot nodules in a pouch of fungal leather, and their slow heat was a treasure. She said the sludge sang to her, a slow, deep song of pressure and decay. My song is higher, thinner. It is the shriek of steam, the metallic groan of a stressed joint. I wake sometimes, not from a dream—we have no such things—but from a change in the district’s heartbeat. A stutter. A skip. A note of distress in the symphony of grinding and hissing.

There is no memory of a sky. The concept is a void, an emptiness so profound it has no shape. Our sky is the pale slit far above, a wound in the brick that weeps light and condensation. We navigate its limits not by looking up, but by knowing the walls. The left wall of the south yard has a deep, resonant hum where a steam-main runs behind it. The right wall is quieter, cooler, and the moss there grows in a different, softer texture. This is our geography. Not latitude and longitude, but frequency and temperature.

The beetles are our heartbeat made small. They swarm the grates, their shell-legs clicking a frantic counter-rhythm to the district’s deep pulse. They are the color of oil-slicked metal, and we gather them by the heat-vents, their bodies popping as they roast, their taste a sharp, bitter protein. The pale reeds we pull from the muck offer a starchy meal, a taste of the deep earth. Nothing is wasted. Everything is part of the circuit.

I shift, my bare toes curling around a pipe to steady myself. I press my ear to the floor-grate. The water below is moving faster today, a gurgle where there is usually a sluggish trickle. A change. It is not a crisis, not yet. It is a new note in the song. I will rise soon, and move through the narrow ways, my hands outstretched to read the stories in the brick and the steel. I will listen for the places where the rhythm falters, where the steam sings a sour note. I will be a nerve ending, a silent custodian. I am a part of the rust. I am a part of the heat. And in the endless, grinding symphony of the Fumarole District, I am at peace.
