I am a single drop of condensation, beading on the underside of a limestone roof-slab. I am cool, and I hold the gray sky in my curve.

For a moment, I am simply aware: of the stone above me, grooved and patient; of the air below, thick with the scent of wet clay and fungal patience; of the low murmur of the channels in the alley, a language I am part of.

Then I grow heavy.

I detach.

My fall is short, a silent streak through the mist. I land in a shallow, open channel that runs beside a wall built with bundles of pale reed. I am not alone. Others join me—droplets from eaves, from mossy stones, from the breath of the mist itself. We become a film, then a current, a slow, deliberate pulse.

I am no longer a single drop. I am part of the flow.

My journey begins. The carved grooves in the stone guide me, not letting me rush, but insisting on a specific path. I slide past a doorway where an old Weaver sits, her fingers stained the color of deep water, weaving a mat of split reed. She does not look up. My passage is part of the background music of her work—the *shush* of my movement in the stone vein. I carry no news for her, only the assurance of circulation.

I merge with other trickles, gaining substance, moving into the connective tissue of the town. I pass under a footbridge of flat stone, worn smooth by generations of Dippers' feet. The bridge casts a shadow that feels ancient, though the stone is young. Below me, in my body, the faint green of algae swirls. We are one thing, the water and the life within it.

I am aware of the texture of my path. Sometimes it is lined with smooth, fitted stone. Sometimes it is raw, chiseled limestone that I must soothe with my constant touch. I feel the resonant hum of the central building long before I see it—a vibration in the very channel-walls, a deep and silent song that travels through my substance. It is the Listeners, singing to the stone, and I carry that harmony in my current.

I arrive at the widening, the central basin. Here, the channels converge, and I join a larger, darker body of water. My pace slows further. I swirl in the fitted basin, catching the flat, gray light. Faces peer down at me—a young Dipper filling a clay vessel, a woman washing her hands. I reflect their pale, intent faces, fragmenting them into shimmering pieces. They are looking at their own fleeting shapes, but they are also looking into the logic of the place.

I feel the pulse here. It is not my heartbeat, but the heartbeat of the town. The constant input from the channels, the slow drain through unseen outlets, the weight of the low sky pressing down. The larger building above hums, its functionless carvings speaking to me in frequencies only water understands. I am a messenger of the mundane, but here, the mundane is sacred. I carry a memory of the reed-weaver's patience, of the child's quick footsteps, of the mist's arrival. I soak in the quiet conversations that happen at the water's edge.

Time passes. The gray light deepens, not to black, but to a softer, thicker shade of itself.

And then, it begins.

From the very algae that swirls within me, a light is born. A cold, corpse-light blue. It does not burn; it breathes. I glow. The channels around me glow. The network becomes visible—a web of soft, pulsing light that runs through the streets, connecting every hut, every dwelling, to the central basin. I am part of this sudden nervous system.

The town stills. The Weavers set down their work. The Dippers scurry home. The murmur of the water remains, but it changes register, becoming the sole voice in the luminous silence. I pulse with the others, a slow, bioluminescent tide. In this light, the Listeners in the stone seem to lean closer. Their hum feels less like a song and more like a listening. They are watching us—the blood of the town—flow.

I am carried, glowing, out of the central basin and back into the web of alleys. I pass a window where a small family sits in the faint blue radiance leaking from the channel at their doorstep. They do not speak. They watch the light move. They are watching the town breathe.

My journey takes me outward, toward the edges, toward the terraced steps that lead down to the marsh. Here, the channels are less controlled, their edges rougher. The light in me illuminates the pale moss and the slick stone. Beyond the last hut, the steps descend into darkness. The marsh is there, a void of still, black water that swallows the light. I feel a different presence there—a vast, unchanneled patience. The Slicks. They are the silence the town's murmur is designed to hold at bay. I stop at the threshold, my glow a tiny star against that immense, watching dark.

I am a memory now. A memory of the reed-weaver's stain on my skin, of the child's vessel that dipped into me, of the wind-song in the temple walls. I am a single note in the long, slow chord the town is trying to teach the young stone.

I begin to seep into the ground at the edge, joining the deeper water, the water that remembers the world before the channels. My light fades as I sink. But I have flowed. I have witnessed. I have connected.

And in the limestone, in the carefully carved groove where I spent most of my brief, shining life, my passage leaves a microscopic trace. A little more smoothness. A little more understanding. The stone is learning. And I was its lesson.
