The mist arrives not as a weather event, but as an exhalation. It rolls from the north, slow and inexorable, a wall of soft grey that swallows the gorge's meagre light and makes the far wall a memory. In the moment before it fully envelops me, I see it: a ripple, not in the air, but in the concentration of life. The slug rivers on the floor accelerate, their movements no longer aimless grazing but purposeful, flowing towards the cracks and crevices in the basalt. The lizards, those frozen sentinels, finally move with a fluidity that seems impossible for their prior stillness, skittering down the walls in coordinated spirals, their wide mouths agape, tasting the thickened air. The fungal animals on the wettest rock faces, those pale, fist-sized stalks, open their gill-seams wide, a silent chorus of gaping.

My own response is automatic, a deep grooves etched into a mind that has known this place for longer than memory. I do not retreat to my alcove-dwelling. Instead, I step to the nearest weeping wall, the one where the mineral seep is heaviest. I press my palm flat against the cold, damp basalt. Not to feel for something specific, but to *become* a part of the gorge's sensory network. The vibrations travel through my bones, up my arm, a language of capillary flow and distant, subsonic shifts in the aquifer. The mist changes the viscosity of the weeping; the stone tells me this.

Around me, others do the same. A figure two meters to my left, a silhouette in the grey, is a part of the wall's texture until they shift their weight, a movement so slow it is indistinguishable from the settling of stone. Their hand is also pressed to the rock. We are not a group standing together; we are discrete points of attention, nodes in a network. Communication flows between us not in glances, but in the shared focus of our palms on the stone, the simultaneous intake of a breath that smells of the mist's new chemistry—more iron today, a faint, distant tang of ozone from storms far above the gorge. This is a message: *The upper gullies are active. The weeping will change.*

The mist thickens. It is not water in the air; it is the air *becoming* water, a transitional state. It coats every surface in a fresh, shimmering film. The orb-weavers are busy. I can feel the faint, rhythmic tugging on the anchor points that connect to my dwelling's wall—a vibration translated through stone and lichen. They are reinforcing, not building anew. The mist makes their silk brittle, so they add layers, a silent, architectural negotiation with the gorge's breath. I once watched a spider for a full day-cycle as it repaired a section of web near my sleeping-ledge. Its movements were as deliberate as my own, a lesson in how to interact with this world: with patience, with precision, with an understanding that every action echoes in the silence.

A slug, a great banded thing of deep blue, near-ultraviolet, is making its way up the wall near my shoulder. Its pace is frantic for its kind, motivated by the mist. It leaves a trail of fresh mucus that glistens with captured moisture. I turn my head, a slow, gradual movement that takes a full ten seconds to complete, to watch it. This is not idle observation; it is reading. The slug's route tells me which crevices are currently receiving the most atmospheric moisture, which fungal mats are freshest, which lichen is prime. My kin on the opposite wall will be reading the same river from their vantage. We are compiling a map of the gorge's current state without a single spoken word.

The rules are not learned; they are the shape of our bodies and minds. Sound is a physical blow. A dropped tool is not an inconvenience; it is a trauma, a rupture in the fabric of the gorge's being. The answering silence after such an event is not passive; it is active, pressing in, a demand for a return to equilibrium. We are all listening to that equilibrium now, our ears tuned to the subsonic hum that is the gorge's baseline. The mist has dampened even that; the hum feels thicker, slower.

The mist passes. The dimness returns, but the world is newly glistening. The fungal animals begin to close their seams, slowly, over minutes. The slug rivers decelerate to their usual, contemplative flow. The lizards resume their circuits, their mouths closing to a more normal gape. The gorge settles back into its voracious quiet.

My work begins. I move from my wall-stance, my feet finding the moss-padded ledges that are the gorge's true pathways. My first task is the basin near dwelling seven. It is half-filled with rich, black soil—the compost of a thousand fern fronds. The film on its surface is thick, a living skin. I dip my hands in, not to clean, but to check its consistency. It is ready. Using a small, flat stone blade—its handle wrapped in dried slug-gut for silence—I carefully harvest the top layer of the film, placing it into a pouch at my waist. This is food. This is medicine. This is a component for the acoustic mud we use to patch the walls of our dwellings. Nothing is wasted.

From a ledge above, a figure watches me. Their face is a pale oval, patterned like the lichen on the stone, their large eyes reflecting the dim light. They are not judging; they are observing my rhythm, my technique. If my harvest is efficient, they may incorporate the method. If it is wasteful, they will note the disturbance in the soil's surface, and later, perhaps, a different tool will appear on a nearby ledge—a reminder, a suggestion. This is our discourse.

I am a Keeper. I am not a master. I am a slow thought moving through a world of slower thoughts. My skin is the color of the gorge's shadowed stone, my eyes wide to drink the gloom. My metabolism is a gentle candle flame, not a roaring fire. The fungal animals, with their faint, mineral tang, are my sustenance. The rhythm of the gorge is my heartbeat. The silence is my language.

You will leave this place, outsider. You will carry the slug trails on your clothes and the memory of the silence in your bones. You will try to describe a room full of animals being very quiet, about to do something. You will not understand that *we* are the something. We are the quiet. We are the watchers. We are the tenders of the weeping walls and the readers of the silver rivers. We are the gorge's consciousness, and our existence is the gentlest, most profound noise it makes: the sound of attention, held perfectly still.
