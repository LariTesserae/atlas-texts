The damp has long since found its way past the waxed leather of my boots and settled into the dense wool of my trousers. It is a companion I do not notice until I am forced to climb a ladder, and then the chill creeps up my legs, a reminder that the gorge is always working its way inside.

I am Kaelen. Not a Sky-Tender, though I know the light-plants. Not a Deepers, though I can read the river’s mood in its color and sound. I belong to the Weavers, but not the kind who sit at a loom. I am a Weft-Walker, one of those who moves *between*. I walk the mid-terraces, the in-between levels, carrying news, small trade goods, and messages that are too complex for a shouted exchange across the gorge. My job is to be the thread that keeps the vertical community from fraying into isolated horizontal layers.

Right now, I am doing my real work. I am listening.

I stand on a platform near the western wall, midway up, where the stone has been worn into a shallow scoop by centuries of water and feet. The wood under my boots is ancient, a pale, rot-resistant timber that has been here longer than my grandmother’s stories. A thick root from one of the great cliff-trees has grown around the support post, embracing it, holding it in place. This is not decay; it is partnership. I rest my hand on the root. It is cool, rough, and faintly vibrating from the work of the people above and below.

Below me, the Deepers are singing a haul-song. Their voices rise in a rhythmic, guttural chant, timed to the pull of a net or the lift of a heavy basket. The sound is absorbed by the moss and ferns, becoming part of the gorge’s texture. To my left, a Sky-Tender is shouting at a troupe of noisy macaques that have found the upper garden. Her voice is sharp, percussive, cutting through the din for a moment before being swallowed again.

My eyes are not looking at any one thing. They are scanning the layers. I see the glint of a water-trough that is running too fast—perhaps a blockage upstream that will soon cause an overflow on the lower levels. I see a cluster of ferns on the opposite wall that have turned a sickly yellow. A sign of a mineral shift, or perhaps a new type of rot. I see two children, no older than six, running sure-footed along a rope-bridge thirty feet above the river, laughing as they chase a large, iridescent beetle. I do not worry for them. Their balance is the gorge’s balance.

A Deepers elder, Maro, spots me. His face is weathered, his hands stained with river-silt and iron-wood oil. He doesn’t shout. He makes a specific gesture: two fingers tapped against his thigh, then a point toward the river’s bend. *Come down. There is news.* I nod, a sharp dip of the chin that he will see even in the dappled light.

To get to him, I do not climb down a ladder. That is for vertical travel. For a diagonal move, I use a *glide-line*. It is a thick, well-oiled rope strung between two anchor points on different levels, with a simple wooden pulley and harness hanging from it. I hook my harness onto the line, give a practiced kick, and the gorge tilts around me. For a moment, I am suspended between the sky and the water, moving through a shaft of green-tinged air that smells of wet stone and blooming moss. A flock of small, jewel-colored birds parts around me, their chirps a flurry of annoyance. A spiderweb, heavy with mist, brushes my cheek like a ghostly veil.

I land on a lower platform with a soft thud, my boots meeting the damp wood. Maro is waiting. He doesn’t greet me with words. He points to a large, smooth stone at the water’s edge, half-submerged. On it sits a frog, but not one of the common chorus frogs. It is a large, mottled creature, its throat pulsing with a deep, slow *throb-throb-throb* that is almost subsonic.

“The Deep-Throat has come up,” Maro says, his voice low, for my ears only. “It has been sitting there since the last rain. It does not sing with the others. It listens.”

I look at the frog. Its eyes are ancient, placid. This species is rare, a dweller of the deep, cold pools near the gorge’s bottom. Its presence this high up is an event.

“What does it listen *for*?” I ask.

Maro’s eyes are on the water. “For the silence within the noise. The Deep-Throat hears the stone shift. It hears the roots groan. It hears when the river is about to change its mind.” He pauses. “It has not moved for two days. The Sky-Tenders’ herbs on the highest level are wilting. The Deepers’ nets are coming up empty of the big fish. The gorge is… holding its breath.”

This is my trade. Not fish or herbs, but *pattern*. The gorge speaks in a language of living things, and my job is to hear the sentence when others only hear the words. The frog, the wilting herbs, the empty nets. These are not three separate problems. They are one message.

“Where is the stillness coming from?” I murmur, more to myself than to him.

I close my eyes for a moment, filtering the immense soundscape. I tune out the frog chorus, the human shouts, the drip-drip-drip. I listen for the *undertone*. The river’s voice. It is there, as always, a deep, rushing roar. But is it the same? I listen for an hour’s worth of sound compressed into a minute. I hear the rhythm of its flow over the boulders. *Shhhh… ka-plash… shhhh… ka-plash…* It is steady. Too steady. The healthy river has a syncopation, a hiccup, a random splash and gurgle. This is a metronome. A smooth, relentless, *unnatural* flow.

My eyes snap open. “The main channel is blocked,” I say. “Not by debris. By something smooth. Something has shifted and is diverting the water into the side-channels, making the main flow too… polite.”

Maro nods slowly. “A slab of the old cliff-face. Fell in the last big shake, a season ago. It settled, but the water has been working at it. It has finally tipped. It is now a dam.”

A dam in the main channel means a flood is coming. Not from rain, but from a sudden, massive release when the pressure of the diverted water finally undermines the new dam and it bursts. It will be a wall of water and stone.

“We have until the gorge exhales,” I say. “Until the frog moves. We must prepare the Deepers for the surge, and tell the Sky-Tenders to lift the gardens on the lower-mid levels. I will carry the message.”

But Maro shakes his head. “No. That is the old way. We will not use a Weft-Walker as a messenger boy. The gorge is telling us a story. We must answer in kind. We must tell it we are listening.”

He looks at me, and I understand. We are not just warning of a flood. We are negotiating with the gorge itself.

“Gather the Weavers,” he says. “Not the cloth-weavers. The sound-weavers. The channel-tenders. We will go to the source of the stillness. We will not clear the dam with force. We will *sing* it down. We will find the song of the water trapped behind it, and we will give it voice. We will teach it a new path.”

I look at the Deep-Throat frog. Its throat pulses once, slowly. *Throb.*

It is a terrifying, exhilarating thought. To stand before a wall of unstable stone and water and sing to it. To trust that the gorge, through us, through our tools and our voices and our understanding of its patterns, will show us the way to release the pressure without destroying us.

It is what it means to be Ullferi. We are not masters of the gorge. We are its most attentive children, its most vocal nerves. We do not command. We converse.

I give Maro a sharp nod. The work begins. The Weft-Walker becomes a thread in a new, more urgent pattern. The gorge hums around us, a living instrument, waiting to be played. And we, its players, gather our tools of wood and bone and voice, and prepare to answer the song of the stone.
