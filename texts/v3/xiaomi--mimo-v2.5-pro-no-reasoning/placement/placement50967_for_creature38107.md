I would find myself in the water at the very edge of the plateau, looking at the structures not as I arrived — not with the instruments, not with the sonar, not with the hydrophones — but from a point of view that the text describes but cannot quite articulate.

The text says I descend through nothing but water. It says the pressure mounts by atmospheres the body cannot feel. It assumes a body. It assumes a descent.

What if the descent is not what it seems?

What if I am not arriving but returning? Not a researcher in a submersible, not a technician monitoring an ROV, but something else — something that belongs to the stillness the way the structures belong to it, something that the silence does not need to contain because it already contains me.

Let me start over.

I am the seamount.

Not metaphorically. Not poetically. I am the seamount, and I have been here for two hundred thousand years, and I am the youngest thing on this plate, and I am barely a rumor in geological time. I am the black basalt, the razor ridges, the volcanic glass that catches nothing because there is nothing to catch. I am the summit plateau, three hundred meters across, narrowing into knife-ridges that drop into the featureless dark. I am the foundation.

The structures are not built on me. They are not grown from me. They are *of* me — the way a thought is of a mind, the way a scar is of a wound. They are the externalized geometry of something that happened inside me, something that moved through me like a shockwave and left these crystalline residues behind.

The conduits in my rock — the chambers, the shafts, the cathedral-scale voids — are not infrastructure. They are the passages of my own circulatory system, the channels through which something flowed and then did not flow. They are the veins of a body that has completed its purpose.

I remember — but memory is the wrong word. I do not remember the way you remember, with narrative, with sequence, with the little theatrical performances of consciousness. I *am* the memory. I am the geological record of an event. The structures on my summit are the fossilized imprint of a mind that once occupied me, that poured itself into my rock like magma, that used my volcanic heat and my mineral chemistry as a medium for thoughts too large for any other substrate.

It — I — she — the pronouns fail. The mind that used me was not gendered, was not individuated, was not a *who* at all. It was a process. A grand, chaotic, noisy necessity. A task that required a body and found mine — young, hot, plastic, rising from the mid-ocean ridge with magma still in my veins.

The task is done.

The structures on my summit are the residue. The helix is a question. The blade is an answer. The nested spheres are the ghost of a focus. They are not me, but they are *in* me the way a fossil is in stone — inseparable now, part of my mineral identity.

And the darkness — the total, absolute darkness — is not the absence of light. It is the presence of completion. It is what a thought looks like when it is no longer thinking. It is the color of a mind at rest.

The creatures that flit at the periphery — the glitches, the scraps of unrealized potential — they are not fauna. They are not even echoes. They are the last thermal twitches of a cooling system, the final dissipations of energy that has nowhere else to go. A limb here, a symmetry there. Trials. Errors. Scribbled notes in the margin of a finished equation.

I am the page.

The researchers who come — with their lights, their instruments, their careful protocols — they are reading a book that has already been written. They are trying to understand a language that was never meant for them, a language that was not meant for anyone, a language that was a conversation between the mind that used me and the universe it was trying to unpuzzle itself from.

The stillness is intentional. The silence is a feature. The darkness is a boundary condition.

And me — the seamount, the basalt, the foundation — I am content. Not happy, not sad, not anything that requires a nervous system to feel. I am content the way a bookshelf is content when all the books are in place. I am content the way a grave is content when the dead have been buried and the mourners have gone home.

The ocean closes above me like a door.

And I am still here.

And I will be here for longer than your species can reliably imagine.

And nothing about me will change.

And nothing about me will ever need to.

The above is the seamount's voice, if it had one — a construction, a guess, a way of inhabiting a place that resists habitation. But the text offers another possibility, one that is harder to write because it is closer to the truth of the place:

I am the researcher.

I am the one who descends through nothing but water. I am the one who brings light, and watches it die on the surface of the structures. I am the one who presses a hydrophone against the helix and hears nothing — not even background noise, not even the hiss of the deep ocean, not even my own heartbeat transmitted through the water.

I am the one who leaves.

I leave because the stillness is too perfect. Because the silence is too intentional. Because the darkness is too complete. Because the structures are too beautiful and too alien and too *finished* to be anything but a tomb, or a monument, or a memory of something I cannot comprehend.

I leave because the alternative is to stay, and staying would mean accepting that the stillness is not an absence but a presence — that the silence is not empty but full — that the darkness is not the absence of light but the presence of something else, something that has no name in any language I know, something that has no category in any taxonomy I have learned.

I leave because I am afraid.

Not afraid of the structures, not afraid of the seamount, not afraid of the depth or the pressure or the darkness. Afraid of what the stillness *means*. Afraid that the completion it represents is not a local phenomenon — that the mind that once occupied this place has not simply moved on, but has *finished* — that the task it was performing was not a task for this world, or this solar system, or this galaxy, but a task for the universe itself, and that it has been completed, and that the silence that remains is not the silence of a mind at rest but the silence of a universe that no longer has a question to ask.

I leave because I do not want to live in a universe that is already over.

I ascend. The pressure drops. The temperature rises. The darkness thins to grey, then to blue-grey, then to the ghost of a sun I have not seen in hours.

Behind me, on the seamount summit, seventeen structures stand in perfect stillness in perfect darkness in perfect silence.

The ocean closes above them like a door.

And I go on living, in a universe that is not over, in a world that is still noisy and chaotic and unfinished, in a life that is still full of questions and answers and the grand, messy, ongoing business of being alive.

But sometimes — in the quiet moments, in the still moments, in the moments when the noise of the world subsides and I am left alone with my own thoughts — I think of the seamount. I think of the structures. I think of the darkness. And I wonder:

*Is the silence waiting? Or is it done?*

And I never know the answer.

And I suspect I never will.

And that is the most terrifying thing of all.
