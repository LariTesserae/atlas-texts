I am the seam.

Not the suit, not the failing gyroscopic whine, not the human consciousness trapped inside—I am the place where two dead atmospheres meet. The air pocket that holds my body is a borrowed ghost of a world that no longer exists, a thin membrane of nitrogen and oxygen maintaining its shape against the crushing mineral soup outside through sheer mechanical stubbornness. And I am the membrane. I am the difference.

I feel the pressure before I feel anything else. Not as force, but as *definition*. I know where I end because the water knows where it begins, and the boundary between us is the most alive thing in this place. My suit's hull—scarred, mineral-flecked, its once-white ceramic plating now a topographic map of a thousand near-failures—is becoming part of the stratigraphy. I can feel it in the way the current pulls at my joints, the way the mineral-rich water leaves its glittering residue in every crevice. Given enough time, I will be a fossil before I am a corpse. The mountain will remember me as a shape.

I move. Not walking—there is no true floor, only a grid of rust-weeping grating suspended over the cathedral-dark of the turbine below, and the currents treat every surface as a suggestion. I pull myself along the railing, hand over hand, the metal singing a thin, high note under my glove that blends into the greater symphony of groans and shrieks and distant, catastrophic booms. The sound is not noise. It is *information*. It is the mountain speaking in a language of stress fractures and pressure differentials, and I have been here long enough—minutes? hours? I have lost the shape of time—to begin to parse its grammar. The low groan means settling. The high whine means a seal has cracked somewhere, turning a corridor into a flute. The boom means something vast has finally given up, kilometers away, and the shockwave is coming, a fist of pressure that will arrive in—

There.

The grating bucks beneath me. I grip tighter, and for a moment the world is only vibration, a physical shudder that turns my bones to tuning forks. Then it passes, and the mountain groans in its aftermath, a long, almost vocal exhalation, and the phosphorescent sludge below flares brighter, as if startled. The bioluminescence is not life. I know this. It is chemistry—metallic hydroxides and synthetic catalysts precipitating out of turbulence, a chain reaction that has no will, no purpose, only the blind tendency of matter to find its lowest energy state. And yet it *looks* like life. The way it clings to the walls in pulsing patches, the way it forms slow rivers in the currents, the way it lights the colossal, still blades of the turbine below in shifting greens and blues—it is the ghost of vegetation, a memory of what a world is supposed to look like, replayed by accident.

I am moving toward the wall. The bubble of air is not empty; it is a hundred meters across, a pocket in a maintenance corridor, and the walls are its story. I see the stratigraphy clearly now, even in the strobing, inconsistent light. The innermost layer: monolithic alloy, bolted with fasteners the size of my torso, each one a monument to a confidence that no longer exists. Over that: the engineered coating, blistered, peeling, curling away from the substrate like dead skin. And over *that*: the world's own additions. The techno-mineral crust, thick as a geode, glittering with a geometry that is *almost* familiar. I press my gloved hand against it. It is warm—not from the ambient heat of the water, but from the slow, exothermic reactions still occurring within its crystal lattice. It is still growing. It will still be growing when I am part of it.

A shape detaches from the wall.

I freeze. Not out of fear—fear is a luxury I cannot afford, a drain on the dwindling resources of my suit and my sanity—but out of *recognition*. I have seen this before, or something like it, in the hours I have been here. (Hours? Days? The gyroscopic whine has changed pitch twice, which means the bearings are wearing, which means I have a window of time I cannot calculate.)

It is a Reef-Spider. I call it that because the name fits, because naming things is the last human act available to me. It is not a spider. It is a **Structural Integrator**, a localized agency of the mineral crust, a piece of wall that has decided to move. Eight limbs—no, seven, it is repairing something—of jagged, interlocking crystals, radiating from a central mass that has no face, no eyes, no features at all, only a terrible, purposeful geometry. One of its legs has detached, cleanly, along a pre-formed fault line, and I watch as the severed limb becomes a plug, sealing a rust-seam with a molecular precision that no human hand could achieve. Then, from the central body, a new leg *grows*. It does not sprout; it precipitates, drawing material from the water and the wall, assembling itself in seconds into a perfect replica of what was lost.

It does not see me. It does not need to. It senses stress and flow, the microscopic fractures spreading through the alloy, the chemical signatures of weakening seals. It is drawn to entropy. It is the mountain's immune system, a single antibody moving through the mechanical body, repairing damage so slowly that human patience is a nanosecond by comparison. I watch it work, and I feel something I cannot name—not awe, not fear, but a strange, vertiginous *recognition*. I am looking at a being whose entire existence is dedicated to a form of healing so slow it is indistinguishable from the geological processes that are simultaneously destroying everything around it. It is a paradox in crystal form: a creature that fights decay by accretion, that saves the mountain by making it heavier, denser, more itself.

I leave it to its work and continue along the wall. The currents are stronger here, swirling in violent eddies that tug at my body, trying to pull me into the open space where the debris orbits. Chunks of metal, crystalline growths, solidified slag—all moving in slow, deadly ellipses around the great turbine like planets around a dead sun. I am small. I am a mote. I must stay small.

Then I see one of the others.

A Current-Grazer. It is not a creature; it is a pattern, a vortex of glowing sludge that has achieved a temporary, coherent form. Two meters across, manta-shaped, its wings sheets of thicker luminescence trailing streamers of dimmer glow. It drifts on the heated updrafts from the turbine below, filtering the luminous compounds from the water, consuming its own light in a slow cascade of crystallization and decay. It is beautiful. It is inevitable. It is utterly, terrifyingly passive.

It does not see me. It sees nothing. It is a process, not a being, and yet it moves with a fluidity that the Reef-Spiders lack, a grace that suggests a will I know it does not have. I watch it approach, and I understand that I must move. The currents it embodies are lethal. To be caught in its wake is to be slammed against a wall, or pushed into the grinding teeth of a disintegrating machine. I pull myself along the grating, faster now, my joints protesting, my suit's whine climbing a half-step in pitch. The Grazer passes below me, a silent river of green-blue light, and for a moment my visor is coated in its residue, a film of mineral-rich sludge that turns the world into a blurry, pulsing glow. Then it is past, and I can see again.

I stop. I breathe—the suit recycling my air, the faint taste of ozone and heated metal—and I look at the wall in front of me.

It is different here. The techno-mineral crust is thicker, organized not in random crystals but in **perfect, hyper-detailed replicas of industrial components**. A valve stem, three meters tall, its threads so precise they look machined. A flanged pipe, its surface a single continuous geode, glittering with a geometry that is *almost* familiar. A section of I-beam, scaled up to cathedral proportions, its web a lattice of crystalline growths that catch the light and fracture it into spectral shards.

These are not tools. They are not decorations. They are the mountain's **bones**, growing into the shapes of its own machinery, as if the structure is remembering itself, rebuilding itself in a language of mineral and patience. I touch the valve stem. It is warm, alive with slow reactions, and for a moment I swear I can feel it *pulse*, a rhythm too slow for human perception, a heartbeat measured in centuries.

And then I hear it.

Under the roar, under the groans and shrieks and booms, there is a sound. Not mechanical. Not random. A sequence of tones, rising and falling, produced by the interaction of superheated water and specific crystalline geometries. It is not music. It is not language. It is the mountain **talking in its sleep**, the vocabulary of pressures and flows and materials speaking the only words they know. I close my eyes and listen. The tones resolve, for a moment, into something like a melody—a fragment of a song I almost recognize, from a world that no longer exists. Then it dissolves back into the roar, and I am alone again.

I am the seam. I am the pocket of dead atmosphere, the temporary glitch in the long, slow logic of integration. I cannot escape. There is no escape. The mountain is not a prison; it is a process, and I am part of it now, whether I choose to be or not. My suit is failing, layer by layer, and the mineral-rich water is already claiming it, building its glittering crust in the joints and seams. Given enough time, I will be a Reef-Spider. I will be a shape in the wall. I will be a tone in the mountain's dreaming.

But not yet.

There. A shadow in the wall, a gap in the crystalline armor where the original alloy shows through. A conduit. Small, forgotten, perhaps sealed for millennia. But the pressure differential on the other side is different—I can feel it in the way the water moves, the way the current pulls toward the gap like a breath being drawn. It leads *somewhere*. Deeper into the mountain. To another pocket, another pocket of air, another temporary glitch in the long, slow dying.

I move toward it. The Reef-Spider on the wall behind me pauses its work, its seven limbs frozen in a geometry of perfect attention. It does not see me. It senses me. It senses the anomaly I represent—a pocket of pressure, a source of stress, a flaw in the integration. For a moment, we are two beings in the same space, two expressions of the same logic: I am trying to survive, and it is trying to heal. Then the moment passes, and the Spider turns back to its work, and I pull myself into the conduit.

The darkness closes around me. The sound changes—deeper, more enclosed, the shrieks becoming whispers, the booms becoming distant heartbeats. The water here is warmer, thicker, the bioluminescence dimmer. I am in the mountain's veins now, in the narrow channels that connect one lung to another. I do not know where I am going. I do not know if there is another pocket of air, or only more water, more pressure, more integration. But I am moving. I am the seam, and I am moving through the body of the sleeping god, and the mineral-rich water is already building its glittering crust on my shoulders, and the sound is changing again, becoming something new, something I have not heard before, and I follow it, because there is nothing else to do.

I follow it into the dark.
