The pressure is a memory you have never made. It is the hum in your bones, the taste of minerals in the water you drink through every pore. You do not breathe. You *are* pressure; you are the crush that has long since ceased to be an event and has become the state of things.

You are one of the Gardeners.

You were born on a bridge—not that you knew it as such. To you, it was a world: a plane of textured growth stretching in two directions, curving gently into the gloom. Your first memories are of texture. The faint, gritty give of the outer rind beneath your scraping mouthparts. The slickness of a patch where a sponge colony had overgrown. The sudden, startling relief of a gap where a piece had flaked away, revealing cooler, untouched substrate beneath.

You have six legs. Do not imagine them as jointed limbs. Imagine them as expressions of your will placed precisely into the world, each tipped with a small, hook-like claw that finds purchase on the living architecture. You move by a ripple, a wave of contractions that passes from your anterior to your posterior, your body kept low and flat against the surface. You are long, perhaps the length of a human forearm, pale as the inside of a shell. Your carapace is translucent enough that in the faintest glow of a neighboring sponge's bioluminescence, you can see the slow, dark pulse of your own hemolymph.

Your world is a map of taste.

You are grazing now on the underside of a broad shelf, suspended between two of the great columns you have never seen in their entirety. The shelf is old. Its surface is a palimpsest of generations: the current layer is a soft, fibrous growth, sweet and easy to scrape. Beneath it, you can taste the harder, more mineralized layers of your predecessors' work. You do not think of it as history. You think of it as *meal depth*. This patch has a thick meal. That one is thin, and beneath it is the hard, lifeless core you avoid.

Your hundred legs (you are invertebrate, segmented, your body a series of near-identical units) carry you along a path that is yours by instinct and inheritance. You know every inch of this shelf. You know where the growth is thick and delicious, where it is thin and bitter, where a colony of tube-worms has taken hold and you must navigate around their delicate, feathery crowns. You are not gentle by nature; you are efficient. But you have learned that to scrape too close to a worm is to cause it to retract, and a retracted worm filters nothing. The air clears, the water grows thinner. You prefer the taste of a world that is functioning. So you curve your path, a few body-lengths to the left, and continue your endless, necessary work.

You are not alone on the shelf. To your left, perhaps twenty of your body-lengths away, is another of your kind. You do not know its name; you do not have names. You know its rhythm. Its scrape-scrape-scrape is slightly faster than yours, a little more frantic. It is younger, perhaps, or simply more nervous. You do not interact. Your paths will cross eventually, as they always do, and you will give way, or it will, and there will be no conflict. The shelf is wide. There is enough for all.

Beneath you, in the open water, you sense a disturbance. A shiver in the substrate. A school of the Swimmers has passed, their collective sonar pinging against your body, a sensation like being gently tapped by invisible fingers. You do not fear them. They are a fact of the environment, like the current or the texture of the food. Occasionally, one will dart in and snatch a worm from its tube, but that is not your concern. You are too large, too armored, too much a part of the surface for them to bother with. You are the grass. They are the birds that eat the insects on the grass. The thought is not a thought. It is a balance.

Your work is slow. You have been on this shelf for what a surface-dweller would call days. You will be here for many more. Then, you will reach the end of the shelf, where it meets the trunk of a great column, and you will make a decision. You can continue up the column's vertical face—a slower, more arduous climb—or you can take one of the smaller, secondary bridges that branch off into the deeper, darker water. The decision will be made by taste. The column's surface here is old, tough, mineralized. The bridge, you remember (a memory of taste, not images), offers fresher, sweeter growth. You will likely take the bridge.

You are not aware of the system. You are not aware of your role in preventing the choking of the breathing passages, the sealing of the lightless chambers. You are aware of the taste of the food. You are aware of the texture of the path. You are aware of the constant, gentle pressure of the water that holds you against the world. You are aware of the sound: the deep, slow groan of the structure around you, the constant clicking of the swimmers, the faint hiss of a trillion respiring neighbors. It is not music. It is the sound of the world working. It is the sound of your lunch. It is the sound of your commute. It is the sound of your home.

You are a note in a chord that has been sounding for aeons. You are a tooth in a jaw that chews forever. You are necessary. You are unaware of your necessity. This is the purest form of being.

Back and forth, scrape by scrape, you are writing a story on the surface of the world. Your ink is the cleared path. Your parchment is the living wood. Your story is the same, over and over: *I was here. I ate. The world remains open.*

And in the patient, clicking dark, you continue. You are not building. You are not waiting. You are simply being, in a place that has finished becoming, and in doing so, you are keeping it alive.
