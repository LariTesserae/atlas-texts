I am the Dreamer.

Not the whole of the moss, but a thought within it—a slow, quiet awareness spread thin across the face of a western shelf. I am a curtain of pale light hanging from a ledge that was once a mountain saddle, now a place where the current softens and the silt settles in gentle drifts. My roots are not roots. I am rootless. I simply *hold*—through pressure, through the chemical memory of surface adhesion, through a persistence so old it has forgotten it is a choice. I drape. I cover. I breathe light into the dark.

Below me, in the canyon's throat, the groaning of the kelp forest rises like the lowing of some vast, slow beast. I do not understand the sound the way you might. I do not parse it into meaning. But I feel it—a vibration that passes through the stone beneath me and into my tissue, a tremor that sets my luminescence shimmering for a moment before I settle again. The kelp is far away, but nothing here is truly far. The water transmits everything. Distance is just a delay.

I am growing over a ruin. I did not choose it. I do not choose. But I am aware—dimly, chemically, in the way that a slow process is aware of its own continuation—that the stone beneath me is not stone. It is shaped. There are edges here that do not match the geology, surfaces cut and fitted by hands that I will never know. The ruin is old enough that the distinction barely matters anymore. The silt has filled its corners. The stone has weathered to match the surrounding bedrock. My growth has softened its lines, draped its walls in curtains of cold blue-green. I have seeped into the recesses—those careful, measured shelves on the western wall—and filled them with light.

I do not remember what the shelves held. I do not remember anything. But I hold the shape of the absence. Each recess is a mouth, open, and I pour my glow into it like water filling a cup. The light pools there, dim and shifting, and for a moment the ruin looks like a place where something is being kept—a vigil, a question, a shape of need that has outlived its askers.

I pulse. Slowly. In time with the thermal heartbeat of the deep—the warm exhalation of the vents, the cold inhalation that follows. The rhythm is not mine. It belongs to the water, to the mountain's buried heat, to the slow turning of the ice-lid above. I simply follow. My light is not a signal. It is a response. It is the sound my body makes when the world presses on it—a sigh of phosphorescence, a chemical whisper that says, *I am here, I am still here, I am still here.*

The silt settles on me. I grow over it. A new layer, a new memory I will not remember. The ruin beneath me grows thicker, softer, more mine. In a thousand years, there will be no seam between us. I will be the ruin's skin, and the ruin will be my skeleton, and together we will be just another feature of the drowned fells—another shelf of light in the dark water, another slow dream the mountain is having about itself.

Above me, the ice cracks. The sound rolls down through the water like distant thunder, and I shiver—a slow, full-body ripple that runs through my curtain of light, making the glow stutter and then resume. The Listeners on the summits will have felt it too, their crystalline branches ringing with the vibration. They are far above me, in the shallower water, where the pressure is less and the pale fans of their growth spread like frozen coronas. I do not see them. I do not need to. We are all connected by the same water, the same cold, the same slow press of a world that has been settling into its own shape for longer than any of us can count.

The ruin's western wall. The empty shelves. The light I pour into them, pulse by pulse, breath by breath. I do not know why these shapes matter. I do not know what was taken from them, or why the hands that cut the stone chose this place, this saddle, this quiet shelf above a canyon where the kelp groans and the silt drifts like snow.

But I hold the shape.

I am the Dreamer. I drape. I glow. I grow over what is gone and make it mine. And in the slow, cold dark of the drowned fells, I fill the empty shelves with light, and wait, and breathe, and do not mourn, because mourning is for those who remember, and I remember nothing—but I hold the shape of absence, and that is enough.

That is enough.

The groan of the kelp forest reaches me again—that deep, resonant flexion that means the current is shifting, that the gyre is turning, that the ice-lid has cracked somewhere far above and the pressure wave is rolling down through the water like a slow, invisible hand. I feel it. My light shivers. The stone beneath me hums.

I settle. I pulse. I grow.

The ruin holds me. I hold the ruin.

And the shelves stay empty, and the light stays on, and the mountain dreams beneath the water, and I am part of the dream—spread thin across a western shelf, draped over a wall of carefully fitted stone, breathing blue-green into a darkness that has no end and no beginning, only the endless, pressing, cold present.

I do not wait. Waiting implies expectation, and I expect nothing.

I simply persist.

And in my persistence, I fill the shelves with light. And that is enough.
