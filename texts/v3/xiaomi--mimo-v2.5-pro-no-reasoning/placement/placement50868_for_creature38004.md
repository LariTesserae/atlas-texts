I am a Weaver. My chitin is thin and pale, the color of fired dust, and my six legs are long and delicate, built for tapping and feeling, not for bearing great weight. My antennae are not for smell—the air is too thick with sound for that—but are my truest senses, forked and trembling instruments that read the world as a tapestry of vibration. My name is a secret: *tick-tick... ...tick-tick-tick-tick*. A rhythm my clutch-mother tapped on my back the day I first found my feet. It is a name that cannot be shouted, only shared in the quietest of places.

My world begins and ends at the threshold of my Throat-Pot. I live in the mid-reaches of the Western Chorus, a precarious stack of 147 homes. My own is a small, bell-shaped vessel, its mouth aimed to catch the lower notes that roll off the Great Basin. Its interior is dark, close, and smells of the waxy secretion we use to bind our world together. Here, the roar of the outside is muted, shaped into a resonant hum that is my constant companion. It is the sound of life.

To be me is to be a tuner.

My days are spent in a state of focused motion. I crawl along the outer walls of my pot, my leg-claws tapping out a soft, diagnostic rhythm. I feel the structure answer, the feedback telling me of its integrity. A change in pitch, a faint shudder, and I know a joint needs reinforcing. I scurry to the nearest Stalk-cluster and, with a sharpened shard of bone, I shave off a curl of its waxy frond. I carry it back, chew it into a paste, and work it into the crack. The note deepens, stabilizes. A small victory.

This is how we see. Not with eyes, but with our whole bodies. The world is a map of pressures and frequencies. The ground beneath my feet tells me the story of the Skittering Armories, their approach a deep, physical *thump... thump... thump* that I feel in my very marrow long before they can be seen. A high, thin whine in the air tells me a Stalk is over-vibrating, ready to shatter. I stay clear of those. The Great Hum is life, but a shattering Stalk is a death-song that tears homes from the stacks and bodies apart.

We are skittish, yes, but not from fear. From caution. From a deep, instinctual understanding that we are small things living within a much larger, louder thing. The Blinding Bright from the sky-sheet is not a comfort; it is an exposure, a searing presence that strips away the nuance of sound. We do our best work in the shimmering heat-haze near the basin floor, where the echoes are loudest and the world’s song is most complex.

Communication is an art of proximity and secrecy. To speak with my clutch-mate in the next pot over, I must crawl to the narrow space between our walls. There, in a pocket of relative quiet, I press my leg against the clay and give my message: a frantic, intricate pattern of taps. *Repair on the western face is holding. The low-note is strong. The slow gods are passing near the southern edge.* The message travels through the clay, a vibration understood only inches from its source, lost to the world's scream.

The Armored Ones, our slow gods. I watch them from the safety of my perch. They are not creatures to me, but phenomena. A landscape in motion. When one passes beneath our stack, the Great Hum is disrupted. The air pressure changes. I feel a wave of nausea, a disorientation as the world’s rhythm is momentarily broken. All tapping ceases. We freeze, our legs pressed flat, feeling the deep, ponderous pulse of the god’s passage. We are powerless against it. We can only wait for its rhythm to fade, for our own world’s song to reassert itself, and then begin the work of re-tuning.

Sometimes, I am not a tuner. Sometimes, I am a weaver. I descend into the lower shafts, where the heat is most intense and the noise is a physical wall. There, I harvest the pale, fibrous stalks that grow from the plant’s core. I carry them back, weaving them through the openings of the Throat-Pots, creating baffles and reeds. A single frond, placed just so, can change a sound from a shriek to a hum. It is the most delicate of our arts.

My life is a cycle of listening, tapping, shaping, and patching. I was born in a humming dark, and I will die in the roar. My hope is that my death is a quiet one—a fall, a silent crumbling—not the cataclysm of a Stalk’s death-song. But if it is, my chitin will become part of the dust. My Throat-Pot may fall silent, but another Weaver will find the silence, and begin to listen for the new notes in the Great Hum. We are the weavers of noise, the children of the scream, and this world of blinding light and crushing sound is the only home we have ever known.
