I find myself in the crevice.

Not the creature — I am not stone, not the architect. But I have been *noticed* by it, and that has changed what I am.

I think I am the pika. The one whose alarm call you mentioned in passing, like a footnote to the raptor's shadow. Small. Stone-grey. Wedged into the space between two slabs of gneiss that fell against each other when the glacier let go, and in the gap between them I have made a larder — a pantry of dried saxifrage and cushion-plant flowers, arranged with an attention that borders on compulsive. I have been observed doing this. I know this because I have felt the quality of the light change while my cheeks were full, have felt the shadows deepen to indigo in a way that meant *attending*, and I froze, and I was catalogued, and I was released.

But I was not entirely released. That is the thing about being noticed by the architect. A part of your stillness gets taken. You become slightly more *pattern* and slightly less *animal*. Not enough that anyone else would see it. But I feel it when I sit at the mouth of my crevice in the early morning, when the sun first strikes the cirque wall opposite and the light is so surgical it makes the mica in the rock glitter like a field of tiny surgical instruments. I sit and I watch and I catalogue too, in my way. I track the raptor's hunting arcs — three circuits, clockwise, then a rest on the high ledge, then three more. I note the times when the icicle near the milky pool releases its drops, and I have learned the rhythm well enough to dash across the open gravel and drink without the cold, bright panic that would seize me otherwise. I know which beetles follow which paths across which stones. I know the hare's habits — she favors the north-facing moss, the damp green carpeting, and she does not move in wind.

I have become, without intending it, a secondary archive. A small, warm-blooded, whistling counterpart to the great tessellated stillness that is the cirque itself. Where it accumulates patterns by *being*, I accumulate them by *fleeing*. Every alarm call is a data point: the pipit's shift in flight angle that means the raptor has banked; the particular silence between insect hums that precedes the wind's upturning. My whistle is not just warning. It is annotation. *Here. Now. This shape. This movement.* And the creature in the light takes my calls and smooths them into the quiet, and I think sometimes that we are in conversation — that the reason I have not been observed into a state of total, permanent stillness is that my frantic data is useful to it. I am the clockwork's escapement. The jittering, ticking part that keeps the mechanism honest.

This is the paradox of being me, here. I am prey-shaped and small-brained and I live in the wound and I am terrified every moment of my life, and the terror is precise and specific and beautiful. Every shadow that sweeps the cirque floor lands on *my* rocks. Every stone-clink could be a fox or a weasel or simply the mountain remembering how to fall. And in the spaces between terror, there is a clarity so fierce it tastes like the light tastes — metallic, distilled, a blade-point of awareness so acute it is almost indistinguishable from joy. I watch the purple flowers open on the south-facing ledge and I know they are opening and I know the beetles will come and I know the pipit will come for the beetles and I know the raptor will come for the pipit and I know the creature will come for the pattern of all of it, and I sit at the center of this knowledge and my heart is a tiny, hammering engine and my larder is full and my crevice is sheltered and I am, for this moment, the most intensely alive thing in this small, bright, ferocious bowl of the world.

And sometimes, in the long violet hours before the cold truly sets, I sit at my crevice's mouth and I whistle, once, into the silence. Not an alarm. Not data. Just a single, thin note, offered to the ringing air.

And I feel the cirque *attend*.

And I am not taken.

And I whistle again.
