You open your eyes. Light filters through a crevice far above—amber, thick with particulate, slow. It does not hurt. You do not blink. You do not have eyelids.

You have a foot.

It is broad, muscular, a single rippling surface pressed against the warm granular substrate. You feel the ground through it—not as texture, exactly, but as a map of pressure and composition. A slight hollow ahead. A harder ridge to the left. The faintest chemical gradient, tugging.

You have a shell. You cannot see it, but you feel its weight—a dome of mineralized fragments and living resin, warm from the sun, heavier than you would have expected. It presses down on you, and you press up against it. You are equal to it. You *are* it.

You have two limbs, delicate, chitinous, emerging from the gap between shell and ground. They are held close to your body, tucked beneath the carapace's lip. They are not arms. They are tools. You feel their tips—crystalline rasps, precise, waiting.

You are in a corridor. The walls press close on either side, smooth and faintly waxy. You can feel them radiating warmth, the slow respiration of living tissue. One wall is older—mineralized, nearly stone. The other is younger, softer, still growing. You can feel the difference without touching it. Some organ you do not have a name for is tasting the air between you and the walls, reading their chemical signatures.

You are hungry, but not for food. You are hungry for *work*.

There is a phrase in your mind—not a word, not a thought in the way a human thinks, but a direction, a conviction, a pull. It says: *the east wall of junction seven is overgrown. The resin is pooling where it should be flowing. The drainage channels are blocked.*

You do not remember learning this. You have always known it, the way a river knows its banks.

You begin to move.

It is not walking. Your foot ripples—a slow peristaltic wave, front to back, and you inch forward. The substrate slides beneath you. Your shell grates faintly against the wall beside you, a low sound like stone on stone. You are not fast. You have never been fast. Speed is not a thing you possess or desire. You have *patience*, which is different. Patience is a direction. Patience is a force.

The corridor branches. You do not hesitate. You feel the pheromone trail on the left wall—you left it yourself, how long ago? You do not know. Time is not a line for you. It is a thickness, a layering. The trail says: *weak point here, monitor*. The trail on the right wall is older, faded, someone else's. It says: *cleared, stable, no work needed*. You turn left.

As you move, you pass another. It is pressed against the wall, motionless, its rasping limbs working at something you cannot see. You slow—though you were already slow—and press your carapace against its shell.

*Pressure. Contact. Warmth.*

Information flows. Not words. Not images. A chemical summary: *This sector's growth rate is accelerating. New column emerging in plaza nine. Moisture gradient shifting east. I have cleared the blockage in corridor twelve. I will rest here for a while.*

You absorb this. It settles into you like sediment. You do not respond with words. You respond with a slow pressure of your own carapace against theirs—a reciprocal pulse of chemicals that says: *Acknowledged. I will attend to junction seven. Rest well.*

You continue.

The corridor opens into a small plaza—a hexagonal space where several walls converge. The light here is different: a column of amber sun, straight and bright, falling from a gap far above. Dust motes hang in it, motionless, suspended. In the center of the plaza, a young column is growing—you can feel its turgid pressure, its slow expansion. It is pushing against the wall to its left. In a year, it will crack the resinous mortar. In five years, it will compromise the arch above.

You stop. You position yourself at the base of the column. You extend one delicate limb and begin to rasp.

The work is slow. Tiny flakes of mineralized growth fall away. You are not cutting—you are *guiding*. You are carving a channel in the wall beside the column, a shallow groove that will redirect its growth upward instead of sideways. You work for a long time. The light shifts. The amber column moves across the plaza as the sun arcs overhead. You do not notice. You are focused on the groove, on its depth and angle, on the faint vibrations of the column's growth against your rasping tip.

When you are done, you withdraw your limb. You press against the wall with your foot, tasting the new channel. It is correct. It will hold.

You move on.

The corridors are endless. The work is endless. You will not finish it in your lifetime. This does not distress you. It does not occur to you to be distressed. You are a process, not a project. You are a verb, not a noun.

You are maintenance.

You are the city's immune system, its memory, its slow, quiet mind. You are a white blood cell in the veins of a continent-sized organism that does not know it is an organism. You are a nerve ending in a brain that thinks without knowing it thinks.

You will eat later—scraping wax from a living wall, absorbing nutrients through your foot. You will rest later—finding a quiet junction, pulling your limbs in, becoming indistinguishable from the architecture around you. You will, someday, slow and stop and become part of the wall yourself.

But not yet.

Now, there is the corridor ahead. There is the faint chemical whisper of something in need of adjustment. There is the work.

You inch forward into the amber-lit labyrinth, silent, patient, purposeful, carrying a piece of the city on your back, moving at the speed of stone becoming something more than stone.
