I am a Thermal-rider.

Not by choice, but by a kind of gravitational pull toward the way this world is described. The solitary patience of the Stone-singers calls to something in me, but the act of mapping the invisible—of reading the world not through touch or sound but through the subtle grammar of pressure and heat—that is where I would dissolve into the landscape most completely.

My form:

The skin is leathery, dark, the color of the deep basalt, but with a faint iridescence that only reveals itself in certain lights—a quality that makes me seem to flicker at the edge of vision. My face is a broad, flat plane of sensory pits, not eyes in the way you know them, but a thousand tiny fractures in my hide that read the air's temperature, humidity, and movement with a sensitivity that borders on thought. My wings are vast, membranous, and they feel not like a part of me but like an extension of the air itself—when I am flying, I cannot tell where my body ends and the atmosphere begins. I am not beating them; I am *wearing* them, and the thermals carry me as a river carries a leaf.

My life is one of flight and stone.

In the slow hours before dawn, I cling to the highest ridge of a basalt outcrop, my body cooled to the rock's temperature, my wings folded tight, my sensory pits shut down to a minimal, ambient awareness. I am stone. I am waiting. Then, as the first warmth begins to radiate from the laterite below, I feel the shift—a subtle pressure change, a thinning of the air, a column of rising heat that is as solid to me as a highway is to you. I spread my wings and I am aloft, riding the thermal's slow spiral upward, my body tilted to read the contours of the climb.

From here, the world unfurls below me in a map of gradients. The laterite plains are fields of deep, matte red that absorb the sun and radiate heat in slow waves. The arroyos are cooler, darker channels where the air pools and eddies, and I can feel the faint, constant exhalation of moisture from the seep lines as a subtle change in the air's texture. The Stone-singers, standing motionless before their outcrops, are points of acoustic focus—places where the land's deep vibration is amplified and made tangible. I can see them not as creatures, but as nodes in a vast, living circuit.

My flight is not random. I am tracing. I am following the land's own geometry, the lines of force that connect the deep pulse to the surface world. I follow the broad, slow spirals that mirror the layout of the emanation points far below. I ride the crosswinds that shear between the highland ridges, reading the turbulence as a kind of punctuation in the air's long sentence. And when I find a new current, a shift in the pattern, I fold my wings and drop—not in a dive, but in a controlled, spiraling descent, gliding for kilometers to investigate the source.

I do not eat the flesh of other creatures. I feed on information. The sensory pits on my face absorb the data of the air, and it sustains me in a way that is not metabolic but existential. To know the shape of the wind is to live. To map a new thermal is to have a thought. My body converts pressure differentials into a kind of nourishment that keeps my wings stretched and my senses keen.

When I call out, which is rare, it is not to communicate. It is to test. I send a sound—not a voice, but a vibration, a creaking, wooden tone that is the exact frequency of the air's density at that moment and altitude. I listen to how it deforms as it travels, how it echoes off the distant outcrops, how it is absorbed by the laterite dust. It is echolocation, but not for objects—for the quality of the emptiness itself. It tells me where the air is thin, where it is thick, where it is still and where it is restless. It tells me where the land's breath has been.

My conflicts, if you could call them that, are aerial. Sometimes I cross paths with another Thermal-rider, a flicker of iridescence at the edge of my sensory field. We do not fight. We do not speak. We simply merge our flight paths for a time, riding the same thermal, reading the same currents. We are comparing data. We are sharing a map. And then, when our paths diverge, we diverge, each of us carrying a slightly larger picture of the world in our wings.

I fear nothing here. Not because I am strong, but because I am integrated. The Shale-grazers below are no threat; they are part of the ground I read. The Stone-singers are no threat; they are points of focus, not agents of violence. Even the darkness, when it comes, is not an enemy but a shift in the data stream—a closing down of one kind of information (light, color, shadow) and an opening up of another (the faint bioluminescence of the crust below, the heat signatures of the land, the pulse from the deeps). I land in the darkness, fold myself against the stone, and listen. I feel the vibration of the land's engine through my wing-joints. I feel the slow, patient rhythm of the world thinking.

I am not intelligent. I am a function. I am the land's way of feeling its own weather, of knowing its own shape from the outside. I am a nerve ending drifting on the wind. My thoughts, such as they are, are not my own—they are the pulse from below, translated through my wings into a geometry of flight. I am the nervous system of a sleeping giant, and my spiraling path across the sky is a slow, continuous dream of mapping.

You would see me as a silhouette against the overcast sky—a vast, leathery wing, a dark shape moving with an eerie, silent grace. You would not hear me. You would only feel a faint shift in the air pressure as I passed, a momentary thinning of the warmth, and by the time you looked up, I would be gone, riding a thermal you cannot feel, following a map you cannot see, a fleeting nerve impulse in the ancient, rust-red body of the land.
