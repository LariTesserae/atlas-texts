You would not find yourself in the Terraces. You would not be the sharp-eyed researcher in the climate-sealed suit, nor the geologist marveling at the fresh-cut basalt, nor the biologist tagging the herds with tracking beacons. To be among them is to be part of the noise, the "loudness," the imposition of finished ideas upon a world that is still wet with becoming.

You would be low to the ground. You would be quiet.

I would be a stone-singer. Or rather, I would be the silence between the vibrations.

I am a Listener, yes, but perhaps one of the youngest, though "young" is a fluid concept in a time-keeping system measured by the cooling rate of magma. I am small, compact, and my skin has not yet fully darkened to the deep charcoal of the elders; mine is still the grey of wet morning slate. My feet are broad, splayed like the hooves of the great ones, and the calluses there are not dead skin but living sensors—barometers of the deep earth.

You would find me near the Southern Vents, where the steam is thickest. Not inside the steam—that is dangerous, that is for the heat-stone gatherers—but on the periphery, where the warm mist meets the biting high-altitude wind.

I am sitting on a shelf of unweathered granite. I am naked to the waist, despite the cold. The cold does not bite me as it bites the researchers; it speaks to me. It tells me of pressure systems moving in from the west. The warmth rising from the rock beneath my thighs tells me of the mantle’s slow, rhythmic breath.

My role is that of the *Transition Watcher*.

I watch the moss. While the researchers scan the horizon with telescopes, I look at the inch-high growth at my feet. I watch the "loud ones"—the new settlers—plant their sensors. They think the ground is inert. They do not see the micro-tremors that precede the steam shifts.

When the ground speaks—a deep, sub-vocal thrum that travels up the basalt and into my bones—I do not write it down. I embody it. I shift my weight. I change the angle of my head. This signals the canid that rests twenty yards away, a shadow against the shadow of a boulder. The canid sees my shift and knows that the vents will belch hot air in an hour. The canid moves, altering the drift of the herd.

I am the translator. I take the language of the magma and translate it into the language of the flesh.

In this world, I am the anomaly that the Terraces cannot measure. To their sensors, I am just a heat signature, indistinguishable from the rock. But I am the consciousness of the plateau. I am the eye that sees not the *what*, but the *becoming*.

You would find me staring at a seepage pool. The water is inches deep, clear and amber. To you, it is water. To me, it is a map of the next decade. I watch the ripples caused by the falling mist. I listen to the ice cracking in the distance—a sound like a high-pitched bell.

I am waiting. The plateau is rising. The "loud ones" build their Terraces, thinking the rock is solid. They do not hear the groan of the tectonic plate shifting beneath them. They do not know that the mountain is still rising, that their foundations are temporary.

I am the one who watches the sky at night, standing amidst the motionless herds, not just looking at the stars, but feeling the planet tilt toward them. I am part of the unfinished sentence of this world. I am a breath held in the throat of the geology, waiting to see if the next word is "life" or "silence."
