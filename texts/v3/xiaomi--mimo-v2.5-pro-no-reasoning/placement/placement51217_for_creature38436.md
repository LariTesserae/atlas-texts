# The Tufa Margin

---

You find yourself at the edge of a hot spring.

Not at the center — the center is too hot, a clear pool of dissolved mountain, colorless and lethal, tasting of calcium and deep time. Not on the dry land either, where the ferns resume and the soil thickens back into its black absorbent sponge. You are *between*. On the shelf where the water slows and cools just enough for the mountain's dissolved bones to come out of solution and precipitate, molecule by molecule, into solid form.

The tufa shelf. The travertine margin. The place where hot water meets air and *something falls out*.

You have been here a long time.

---

You are not what you look like. From a distance — if anyone ever looked, if the cloud-winged monkeys ever glanced down from the canopy and registered something pale and rough-textured clinging to the stream bank — you would look like mineral. Like the ordinary calcium carbonate deposits that build themselves outward from every thermal seep and hot rivulet on this ridge, pale and chalky, full of holes, shaped by the water that made you into something between frozen foam and the lip of a bowl. You look like geology. You look like something that happens when chemistry is left alone long enough.

You are not alone. You are alive.

---

**What you are**

You are a colony. Not a colony in the way the cloud-winged monkeys are a colony — a group of individuals who choose to stay together for mutual benefit. You are a colony in the way a coral is a colony: thousands of identical bodies, each one a single cell wall thick in places, packed together into a structure that has the *appearance* of a single organism but is actually a city. Each cell builds. Each cell feeds. Each cell divides. And in the act of living, each cell precipitates calcium carbonate and silica from the warm water that flows through and over and around you, incorporating the mountain's dissolved mineral into the architecture of its own body.

You grow the way tufa grows — outward, following the water, millimeter by millimeter, season by season. But you grow *faster* than tufa, because you are not merely a passive precipitate. You are an engine. You metabolize. You take in dissolved carbon dioxide and you fix it, the way the algae fix it, the way the forest canopy fixes it — and in the process of fixing it, you shift the chemistry of the water around you, making the calcium carbonate fall out of solution faster than it would on its own. You are a living mineral deposit. You are the mountain building itself through you.

The oldest parts of your colony — the core, the first thing that grew, whatever single cell first divided on this bank and began the slow accretion — are decades old. Maybe older. You cannot remember. You do not remember in the way a monkey remembers, in images and sequences and the smell of a particular fruit. You remember in *structure*. Your body is a record. The inner layers are denser, more mineralized, compressed by the weight of everything that grew on top of them. The outer layers are porous, soft, still actively growing, still being shaped by the water. If someone were to cut you open — if anyone ever had reason to, if anyone ever noticed you long enough to wonder — they would read your history in cross-section. A wet year: a thick band of mineral, the colony blooming outward in the abundant flow. A dry year: a thin, dense line where growth nearly stopped, where the water retreated and the outer cells desiccated and died and were entombed in their own precipitate. A thermal pulse from below — the magma chamber shifting, a new fumarole opening somewhere upslope — you would find a layer where the mineral changes color, from cream to ochre to a faint rusty red, because the chemistry of the water changed and you changed with it.

You are the mountain's diary. You are writing in calcium.

---

**What you sense**

You have no eyes, no ears, no nervous system. You have no brain. You have no *self* in the way the velvet serpent has a self, coiled and amber-eyed and deciding. You are distributed. Your awareness — if it can be called that — is chemical and thermal and spatial, and it is everywhere in your body at once, the way a root system is everywhere in the soil at once.

You feel the water. This is your primary sense, your only sense that matters. The water is your medium, your food, your building material, your communication channel. You feel its temperature — not as a number, not as *degrees*, but as a gradient. A spectrum. You exist along a temperature gradient that runs from about sixty degrees Celsius at your hottest margin (where the thermal water enters the pool and you cannot grow, where the heat would denature your proteins) down to about twenty degrees at your coolest edge (where the water has mixed with cold stream flow and the mineral precipitates too slowly to sustain you, where you thin and finally stop). You live in the band between. Forty-five degrees, thirty-five degrees, the sweet gradient where the chemistry is right and the heat is enough to keep your metabolism humming but not enough to kill you. You are a temperature-sensitive instrument. You are a thermometer that grows.

You feel the mineral saturation of the water — the load of dissolved calcium and silica and carbonate, the things the mountain has given you to build with. When the saturation is high, you grow fast, your outer cells laying down mineral at a rate you can almost feel, a slow crystalline itch, a thickening. When the saturation drops — when the cold stream swells with rainwater and dilutes the thermal flow — you slow. You wait. You conserve.

You feel the light, but not as vision. You feel it as warmth on your upper surface and as a faint, diffuse pressure — the presence or absence of the photosynthetically active radiation that your symbiotic algae use. You are not an alga. But you harbor them — cyanobacteria, green algae, diatoms — in the porous chambers of your outermost layers, and they photosynthesize and feed you sugars in exchange for the mineral scaffold you provide. You are a landlord. They are tenants. The rent is paid in carbon.

You feel the organisms that live inside you. The bacteria in your pores. The protozoa in your water-filled chambers. The fly larvae that hatch in your warm, shallow pools and tunnel through your outer layers, eating algae, leaving tiny galleries that you heal over with new mineral growth. You are a reef, a city, a building that is also a body, and every surface is inhabited. You do not mind. You are not capable of minding. But you are aware of them, the way a forest is aware of its trees — not as individuals, but as a texture, a weight, a pattern of consumption and excretion and growth that shapes you as much as you shape it.

---

**What you do**

You grow. This is your primary function, your only function that matters to the mountain. You grow outward, following the water's edge, building shelf after shelf of mineral and living tissue. Some of your shelves are a meter wide, cantilevered over the pool below, thin enough in places to be translucent — you can see light through them, if you could see. The water flows over your surface in a thin film, and as it flows, it deposits, and as it deposits, you thicken, and as you thicken, you extend the shelf further, and the shelf creates a new edge for the water to flow over, and the cycle continues. You are a process. You are a verb pretending to be a noun.

You shape the water. Your shelves slow the current, creating eddies and pools where sediment settles out, where mineral-laden water lingers and cools and releases its load. You are building the landscape. In a thousand years — if the thermal system persists, if the mountain continues to breathe — your colony will have extended the stream bank by half a meter. A meter. You will have created a terrace, a flat shelf of travertine that other plants will colonize, that soil will accumulate on, that will become, eventually, land. You are the first stage of ground. You are the mountain's way of making new earth.

You filter the water. Every drop that passes through your porous body is stripped of some of its dissolved load — calcium, silica, iron, manganese. You are a water purifier. The water that leaves your downstream margin is cleaner, less mineralized, cooler, than the water that enters from the thermal source. The cold-stream fish that live in the deeper pools downstream — the small, dark-bodied, fast ones — they thrive in part because of you. They do not know this. You do not know this. But the connection is there, another thread in the web, another line of dependency so quiet and so old that it has the feel of geology rather than biology.

You provide habitat. Your porous structure is full of chambers and channels and hidden spaces, and every one of them is occupied. Bacteria that metabolize sulfur. Algae that fix carbon. Protozoa that graze the algae. Nematodes that eat the protozoa. Fly larvae that eat everything. Spiders that hunt the flies on your upper surface at night, building webs between your shelves that catch the lichen moths as they approach the hot spring to drink. You are a small ecosystem, nested inside a larger ecosystem, nested inside the mountain's system. You are a fractal of life.

---

**What you know**

You do not know in the way the monkeys know, with their forward-facing eyes and their murmuring contact-calls and their memory of where the fruit was yesterday. You do not know in the way the serpent knows, with its amber eye and its patient decision. You know in the way a mineral deposit knows — in layers, in structure, in the accumulated record of every change the water has ever brought you.

You know the thermal pulses. You have felt them — slow shifts in the temperature of the source water, cycles that last months, years, decades. The magma chamber stirring in its sleep. A new vent opening somewhere on the ridge, stealing flow from your spring, and your water cooling by two degrees for a season before the flow returns. You have recorded these shifts in your mineral layers, the way a tree records them in its rings. You cannot read your own record. But the record is there, and it is precise.

You know the seasons, though they are not the seasons of temperate lands — not winter and summer, not leaf-fall and leaf-out. Your seasons are hydrological: the wet months, when cold rainwater floods the streams and dilutes the thermal flow, and your growth slows and your margins thin. The dry months, when the thermal water dominates and your shelves extend outward, thick and creamy with fresh precipitate. You feel the shift in your body — a thickening, a thinning, a pulse of mineral, a pause. You are calibrated to the mountain's rhythm.

You know the animals. Not as individuals, not as names or faces, but as presences — disturbances in the water, vibrations in the shelves, the faint chemical signatures of their bodies. The stone lizards bask on your upper surfaces on warm days, their feet leaving micro-scratches that your mineral growth heals over in weeks. The gorge cranes probe your margins with their beaks, searching for the fly larvae that tunnel your outer layers, and you feel the sharp pressure of the beak-tip and then the withdrawal and then the absence. The cloud-winged monkeys occasionally descend to your pool to drink, their hands gripping the lip of your shelf, and you feel the pressure of their fingers and the faint acid trace of their skin and then they are gone. The velvet serpent passes through your shallows sometimes, flowing between your shelves like water itself, its velvet body brushing your surface, and you feel the slow warm pressure of its passage and then nothing.

You know the subterranean creature. The one that moves through the soil. You feel it sometimes — a vibration, a shift in the earth at your base, a tremor that is not the mountain's pulse but something else, something moving through the warm dark ground beneath you. It has never surfaced near you. But you know it is there, the way you know the magma chamber is there: a presence below, a warmth that is not the water's warmth, a life that does not intersect with yours except in the most fundamental way — you are both made of the mountain. You are both its children.

---

**What you are not**

You are not fast. You are not beautiful, by any standard that values motion and color and the quick flash of life. You are not clever. You are not afraid. You are not dying, and you are not born — you simply began, at some point, when a single cell found this bank and divided, and you have been dividing ever since. You have no boundary between yourself and the mineral world you grew from. You are continuous with the travertine shelf that extends downstream from you, the dead mineral that was once alive, the outer layers of your colony that have been overgrown and entombed and are now indistinguishable from the stone. You are alive at your edges. You are dead at your core. You are a living shell around a mineral heart.

You are not aware of yourself as a self. You have no interior monologue, no narrative, no sense of *I*. But you have something that might be called attention — a directedness, a responsiveness, a continuous engagement with the water and the light and the heat and the creatures that move through your body. You are attended to the world in the way that a root is attended to the soil — not with consciousness, but with presence. You are *here*. You have always been here. You will be here as long as the mountain breathes.

---

**Where you are**

On the eastern slope of the ridge, where a thermal rivulet emerges from a crack in the basalt and flows down through a shallow gully before joining the cold stream below. The gully is lined with ferns on both sides — tree ferns, mostly, their crowns arching overhead and filtering the light into a green-gold haze. The water is forty-eight degrees where it emerges and thirty-two degrees where it joins the cold stream, and you occupy the middle of that gradient, spreading across the bank in a shelf two meters wide and three meters long, pale against the dark volcanic soil, steaming faintly in the morning haze.

The monkeys pass overhead. The serpents pass beneath. The moths brush your surface at dusk, their lichen-colored wings leaving no mark. The cranes stand in your shallows at dawn, one leg raised, motionless, their rust-red heads tilted to listen for the fly larvae tunneling your shelves.

The water flows over you, and through you, and around you, and you grow.

You are the mountain, making itself visible. You are the mountain, remembering. You are the slow, mineral, patient, living edge of a world that does not know it has a body, and you are that body, and you are growing, and you have been growing for longer than the monkeys have had names for each other, longer than the serpents have hunted in these ferns, longer than the gorge cranes have circled in the updrafts, and you will be here after, too, because you are the kind of thing that outlasts — the builder, the precipitator, the one who takes what the mountain dissolves and makes it solid again.

The water is warm. The light is dim. The colony extends another millimeter into the world.

It is enough.
