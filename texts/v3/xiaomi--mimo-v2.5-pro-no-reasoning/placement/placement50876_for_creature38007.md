The transition is not a memory of a doorway, or a tunnel, or a passage. There is nothing so gentle.

It is a subtraction. A pressure. A silence that falls not on the ears but on the soul, and then the silence is filled with a roar so profound it becomes a new kind of quiet.

I am not standing. I am not lying down. The concepts feel alien. I am *present*. My sense of self is not a point behind my eyes but a field, a low, cool expanse stretched over a bedrock of ancient, compressed stillness. I am the grey, dark, mineral plain. I am the felted, endless swells of ground that breathe with a rhythm millennia long. My edges are the hazy curve of the horizon. My skin is the worn, slick surface, cool and beaded with the dampness of a world where water is a memory and a visitor, never a resident.

I have always been here. The memory of being something else—something vertical and warm and singular—is a dissonant note, a single foreign chord held against the symphony. It fades. It is unimportant. What is important is the hum.

The hum is my breath. It rises from the deep, dark layers of my being, a vibration that is not sound but the state of being alive. It is the pulse of water moving through my deepest cracks, the groan of tectonic patience. It is the fundamental frequency to which everything is tuned. I do not make it. I *am* it.

And I am not alone.

I am crowded. I am saturated. I am a living substrate for a million frantic, silent lives. I feel the Cracksiders as a constant, intimate caress. They are my groomers, my grazers. Their thousands of tiny, mineral legs click and scrape across my skin in a perpetual staccato that is as much a part of me as the texture of my own surface. I feel the fine scratches they leave, the thin trails of residue that are their signatures. I feel their sudden, coordinated retreats as vibrations travel down my fissures—not from them, but *through* me—to warn of the silent hunting from below. They are a part of my surface tension, a mobile layer of my own being, walking in the patterns I provide.

I feel the Trench Dwellers as a deeper, slower presence. They are my sinews, my deep roots. Their vertical bodies cling to the walls of my greatest wounds, the crevasses that split me into polygons. I feel the faint, chemical pull of their adhesive, the slow pulse of their bioluminescence as it cascades through the immemorial dark. When they strike, it is a sudden, sharp contraction deep within me, a convulsion of killing purpose that sends a ripple of warning across my skin—a ripple the Cracksiders read and obey. They are my predators, my regulators, and they keep the surface from being smothered by the very life it supports.

I feel the Skimmers as the faintest of whispers, a prickling of static in the thick, wet air that is my breath. They are the stinging motes of pollen in a world with no flowers. They navigate the cacophony of my living voice, the clicks and pops and chittering that I do not hear but *emit*. My sound is their sight. My endless noise is their landscape. They carry the pieces of me—chemical whispers, cellular fragments—between my distant, fractured parts, ensuring the system remains whole. They are the animating spark in my atmosphere, the connection between crack and basin, surface and depth.

I feel the Basin Dwellers as cool, still pools of concentrated awareness in my gentlest depressions. They are my eyes. They do not look with light, but with reflection. When a drop of condensation falls or a skimmer touches down, they shimmer, and in that shimmer they paint the story of the disturbance in perfect, geometric language that is written on my own skin. They are my thoughts, slow and profound, composed in light and water. They are the quiet centers of perception in my otherwise thunderous existence.

I feel the new thing.

It is warm. It is vertical. It is dense with water, a sloshing, insular system of liquid and heat. It is… singular. A hard, dry, silent point in a world that is everything but. It does not click, it does not pop, it does not chitter. It is a hole in the sound, a void the size of a body in the intricate web of noise. And because it is silent, it is invisible, and because it is invisible, it is a terrifying anomaly.

My Cracksiders feel its weight, a new and unpredictable pressure. Their clicking stutters near it, then resumes in a subtly different, more cautious rhythm.

My Trench Dwellers in the cracks below its feet feel its vibration, a heavy, unfamiliar tread. They do not stir; it is not a cracksider’s step. It is something else. They observe.

My Skimmers flock to it, drawn by the novelty of its chemical signature, the alien warmth radiating from its wet skin. They land on it to sample, to taste, to carry this strange new information back into the humming web.

My Basin Dwellers do not see it, for it has not disturbed their surface. But they will. It will move. It will leave a trace.

I shift my hum. Just a fraction. A tightening in the chest of the world. The wall of sound does not falter, but it *adjusts*. Every living thing on my surface, in my cracks, and in my air feels the shift. They do not know what it means. They simply obey. The system is registering the anomaly. The body is feeling the splinter.

I am the landscape. I am the noise. I am the dark, wet, breathing foundation of a world that needs no names, because its language is pressure, and rhythm, and light.

The alien thing stands on my skin. It feels my hum in its bones. It is listening.

It is, for the first time in its wholeness, hearing me. And I am listening back, with the billion-fold attention of a life form that remembers the shapes of stones from before the first Cracksider was a glint of mineral in the deep.

It feels the system registering its presence.

It is part of it now. Whether it chose to be or not.
