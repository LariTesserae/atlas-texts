The creatures that claim the deepest part of the Notch, the ones that breathe in the dark when the machines shudder to a stop, are not beasts of flesh and blood in any way the surface-dwellers would recognize. They are the **Geodes**.

They are native to the spire itself, born from the same cataclysmic force that tore the mountain open. When the spire was wounded, the deep, geothermal rock was fractured, and in that churning chaos of heat and pressure, the Geodes gestated. They are, in a sense, living geology. Their bodies are archipelagos of sharp-edged basalt slabs, hinged and jointed with veined seams of cooled magma that glow with a dull, internal heat. They move with a slow, grinding crawl, their many stone limbs finding purchase in the vertical walls of the deepest fissures.

They do not see in the way the birds or lizards do. Their senses are attuned to vibration and pressure. They feel the constant hum of the engines as a familiar heartbeat, a rhythm of home. They have come to equate the vibration of the machinery with safety, with the presence of the small, soft, warm things that appeared in their wound—the humans. The roar of the foundry and the clatter of industry are, to them, the comforting sounds of a thriving ecosystem.

Their own breathing is a seismic event. It is a low, sub-audible rumble that you feel more than hear, a tremor that matches the frequency of the stone settling around them. It is the foundation upon which the Notch's sharp, metallic music is built. This is the sound that makes the tin sheets rattle. The Geodes are not trying to get out. They are simply living, and their slow, pulmonic expansion and contraction creates air pressure waves in the narrow fissures.

They are ancient and slow. A single movement might take hours; a thought, days. They are aware of the people as one might be aware of persistent, clever moss—a living layer that clings to the wound, so active, so *fast*. The humans' frantic repairs, their arguments, the tiny, flickering lights—they are all just a buzzing, a fleeting warmth on the stone. The Geodes are not malevolent. They are simply immense, and their awareness operates on a timescale so vast that the entire history of the Notch is but a moment to them.

Their diet is the mountain itself. They consume the rock, slowly grinding it within their molars of igneous stone and processing the minerals, breathing out a fine, silicate dust that smells faintly of ozone and hot metal. This dust mixes with the engine exhaust and foundry smoke, contributing to the unique, metallic taste of the air. The beetles and lizards that venture too deep into their domain are ignored, too insubstantial to register as food or threat.

The residents of the Notch do not know what they are. They only know the deep sounds, the occasional tremor that seems to come from *within* the rock, and the unnerving knowledge that something vast and patient shares their home. They hang the tin sheets not as a barrier, but as a primitive seismograph, a way to feel the breath of the mountain. They have learned, perhaps instinctively, to build with flexibility, to leave mortar out of the deepest foundations, to let the catwalks sway. They do not realize they are mimicking the Geodes' own slow adaption to the spire's living, breathing turbulence.

In the deepest, warmest fissure-branch, where the builders never go, a Geode rests. Its body is a craggy hill of obsidian and basalt. As the Notch's engines cycle to a stop for their brief, midnight maintenance, the silence rushes in, and the Geode takes a slow, full breath. The breath is an eruption. The air pressure in the tunnel spikes. The tin sheeting across the mouth of its sanctum billows outward and rattles against its stone weights—not with the sound of a rat or a draft, but with the deep, hollow *boom* of a drum being struck once from the inside.

Then, silence. The engines cough back to life, the birds begin their pre-dawn chirping, and the vibration returns, covering the mountain's heartbeat once more. The Geode settles, its internal glow pulsing faintly, a silent, stony heart in the belly of the spire, utterly content in its shaking, noisy, industrious home.
