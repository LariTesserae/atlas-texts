In the damp, breathing dark of the Guttering Peaks, life is a tapestry woven from water, light, and slow, patient hunger. What lives here is not merely *in* the landscape; it is the landscape’s active, dreaming mind.

The foundation of the fauna are the **Weepers**. They are not animals as we categorize them, but motile, colonial organisms that are part coral, part amphibian. They grow in vast, intricate lattices on the weeping rock faces, their translucent bodies shaped like goblets or sea anemones. They spend their lives filtering spores and microscopic life from the perpetual drip, their tissues shimmering with a captured blue-green glow from the very moss they consume. When a colony is disturbed, it emits a soft, harmonic chime, a note that resonates with the percussive music of the water, a brief, coherent song in the constant noise.

Skittering among the fungal stalks and over the corroded machinery are the **Chitin-Clickers**. These are arthropods the size of a human hand, their carapaces not just obsidian-dark, but faceted and polished to a mirror sheen. They are the world’s cleaners and recyclers. Their mandibles are delicate, intricate tools for scraping moss, peeling rust, and dissecting fallen spore-caps. Their clicking communication is complex and rapid, a language of vibration that travels through the wet stone and the dense mycelial mats beneath the bog. A swarm of them descending upon a section of oxidized copper tubing sounds like a thousand tiny typewriters composing a frantic memoir.

The large, silent grazers are the **Boulderbacks**. They are immense, hexapodal creatures with long, flexible necks and bodies covered in a symbiotic, matted fur that is itself a living moss garden. They move with a ponderous, gliding grace, their broad, padded feet distributing their weight so they barely disturb the treacherous peat. They are not truly grazing; they are *communing*. Their prehensile tongues, tipped with a cluster of sensory nodes, are used to gently stimulate the bioluminescent mosses, encouraging a brighter, more nutritious bloom before consuming it. Their presence is signaled by a deep, infrasonic hum, felt more than heard, a vibration that calms the skittering creatures and stills the clicks for a moment.

Hunting them are the **Flicker-Limbs**. Six powerful legs propel them in bursts of terrifying speed through the fungal-pillar forests. Their bodies are lean and covered in a patchy, luminous hide that shifts in pattern—dull and patternless when still, rippling with bright streaks and spots as they move, a disorienting signal to prey and rival. They are ambush predators, often clinging motionless to the vertical surfaces of great machines or cliff faces, their light dimmed to match the surrounding moss. The strike is not a roar, but a series of sharp, sonic pops as their limbs crack the air, a sound that echoes and multiplies in the mist, confusing the quarry.

In the bogs and the deep pools of black water glide the **Resonance Eels**. They are long, ribbon-like, and almost entirely transparent, their skeletal structure and organs visible as a faint, blue network of light. They navigate the luminous peat by sensing the bio-electric fields of other life and the faint, dying energy pulses from the submerged Industry. Their hunt is acoustic; they emit a constant, low-frequency drone that interferes with the environment. When their prey—smaller creatures, even Chitin-Clickers—moves, it disrupts the drone in a way the eel can perceive, turning the very act of living into a beacon.

And then, there are the thinkers, the ones who may have built, or may have simply inherited, the thoughts of the builders. They are called, in the onomatopoeia of the Clickers, **The Silence**. They are humanoid in outline only, tall and gaunt, with skin the color and texture of wet slate. They have no eyes; their entire face is a smooth, convex plate of a material that resembles mother-of-pearl, faintly luminescent, which senses heat, vibration, and the subtle electrical fields of the living world. They move without sound, their feet splaying wide to distribute weight on the sucking ground. They are the only creatures that touch the Industry with purpose. Their long, multi-jointed fingers trace the corroded lines of aqueducts, press against the cold metal of pistons, not with nostalgia, but with a kind of reading. They seem to be listening to the memory of function, to the residual harmonics trapped in the alloy. They do not speak, but they communicate in faint, shifting patterns of light across their facial plates, a language of empathetic resonance with the world around them. They are the ghosts of the machine, and the keepers of its slow, green digestion. They are the world’s conscious thought, adrift in a sea of seeping, dripping, luminous life.
