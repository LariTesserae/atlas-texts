They are quiet people, in the way of those who have learned to speak only by shouting.

They are wet. There is no version of them that is dry. Their skin is pale, the color of the limestone itself—not white, but that specific cream-and-dove-grey of the deeper terraces, and it gleams faintly because it is always filmed with moisture and the faintest sheen of mineral deposit. They are broad. Short-waisted, heavy in the thighs and shoulders, built low to the ground like the buildings they inhabit. Their bones are dense. If you held one of their skeletons you would think it was too heavy for its size, as if it had been dipped in something. It has. Everyone who lives here begins to calcify from the inside, starting with the bones. It is not a disease. It is a property of the water they drink and the air they breathe and the food they eat, and it kills them eventually, but not for a very long time, and they do not consider it a failing. When one of them dies the body is left on a designated terrace, and within a year it has become a white mineral sculpture, and within a decade it is indistinguishable from the stone.

Their faces are wide and flat, with small eyes that have grown accustomed to the diffuse light and the fog, and their ears are cupped — not pointed, not large, but subtly shaped to catch sound coming from below as well as ahead. They hear the amphibians through the rock. They hear the crustacean colonies through the walls of their own homes. They are never in silence. They do not understand silence as a concept. Their word for *alone* means *surrounded by things that are not people*, and they consider this a generous definition.

They work. They work constantly. The work of Geth Maren is maintenance — of the drainage channels, of the calcifying machinery, of the terrace edges that must be trimmed or they will silt up and redirect water into the city foundations. They are craftspeople and engineers and farmers of the crawling world. They understand hydrology with an intuitive fluency that would take a foreign engineer a lifetime to approximate. They can look at a sheet of water moving across a terrace and tell you, by its color and the speed of its advance and the shape of the mineral deposit it is leaving, exactly where it came from, how long it has been flowing, and what it will do next. Their science is observation so deep it has become instinct.

Their tools are made of stone and bone and the chitin of the larger crustaceans. Metal they use for industrial machinery, but in their daily lives they prefer what does not calcify too quickly or what calcifies into something useful. A hammer of amphipod chitin, left in a drip, will acquire a layer of calcium carbonate overnight that makes it harder and heavier and better for the next day's work. They shape things slowly and let the water finish them.

They have names that are also descriptions. A name might mean *born-in-the-fog-of-the-fourth-terrace* or *whose-father-tended-the-mounds-at-Bren*. Their language has no word for *dry*. It has seventeen words for *moving water*, differentiated by depth, speed, mineral content, and whether it is flowing over a surface they consider clean or dirty. They have a word that means *the sound a calcifying corpse makes when its outer shell cracks in the heat* and they use it metaphorically the way other languages use words for dawn.

They build. They build slowly and with a deliberateness that looks like ritual and is. A new building takes a generation. Not because they are inefficient but because they do not finish buildings — they *begin* them, and the water and the mineral accretion and the next generation and the generation after that continue the process, adding, adjusting, allowing the terrace's own chemistry to complete what the architects started. The oldest buildings are the most finished. The newest ones look raw and incomplete, because they are. The inhabitants of a newly founded settlement will live in structures that are half-built and half-grown, and their grandchildren will live in something that looks like it emerged from the stone itself, because by then it has.

They do not travel far. The basin is large but their portion of it is small — a few hundred kilometers of stable outer terrace where the water is slow enough to build on and deep enough to farm. Beyond that are the inner terraces, where the water flows faster and the mounds are wild and untended and the crustacean colonies grow to sizes that dwarf the managed ones, and beyond that is the deep basin, the center, where no one goes. They say the water there is so hot and so mineral-saturated that it is nearly solid. They say things live in it that are not crustaceans and not amphibians and not anything they have a word for. They say this the way other people say there is a sea beyond the horizon — with a certainty that does not require proof.

They are afraid of the center the way one is afraid of a parent. It is not a fear that prevents action. It is a fear that orients. Everything in Geth Maren — the terraces, the cities, the fauna, the water itself — flows toward the center, and everything in their culture flows away from it. They are a civilization built on the rim of something that is slowly, patiently, drawing them in.

They love. They love the way the terraces love the water — by absorbing it, by letting it reshape them, by being changed so completely that the change is indistinguishable from the self. They form bonds that last decades, not because they are faithful in some moral sense but because the infrastructure of their lives is so complex and so interdependent that separating would be like separating two terraces that have accreted together over centuries. You could do it, but the effort would be enormous and the result would be two broken edges that immediately began to calcify into something new anyway. When one partner dies the other does not remarry. They do not have a word for remarriage. They have a word that means *continuing to build the same house alone*, and it is understood as a form of grief that is also a form of work.

Their children are born in water. Not metaphorically — the birth pools are shallow basins in the lower levels of every building, fed by the warm mineral springs, and the infants are held under the surface for the first moments of their lives, not long enough to drown but long enough for the water to touch every part of them, and this is considered the moment the child becomes *of* Geth Maren. Before that, they say, it is only potential. After, it is stone-in-progress.

They do not write. They carve. Messages are incised into the calcite and left to be overgrown by mineral accretion, and the recipient reads them by running their fingers over the surface and feeling what is raised — the message exists in negative space, in the absence of mineral deposit where the carving was made, and over time even that absence fills in and the message becomes unreadable. All communication here is temporary. They consider this appropriate. Words, they say, are like water. They move. They do not stay.

They are dying. Not in any dramatic sense — not in the way of a plague or a war or a famine. They are dying the way the terraces grow, slowly, in a direction that is difficult to perceive without the long view. Their population has been stable for centuries, which they interpret as health, but the numbers are the numbers of a people who have reached the carrying capacity of their world and stopped. There is no expansion. There is no frontier. There is only maintenance, and the slow accumulation of mineral, and the patient water, and the animals that eat and breed and calcify, and the people who do the same.

They do not mourn this. They do not have a concept of it. If you described to them the idea of a world that was getting larger, or a population that was growing, they would listen politely and then ask, with genuine confusion, why anyone would want such a thing. More space means more to maintain. More people means more mouths fed by the same tides of snails. The idea of growth strikes them as a kind of madness, a failure to understand the relationship between a thing and the surface it lives on. You are, they would say, exactly as large as the terrace you occupy. To wish to be larger is to wish to be water — to flow, to lose your shape, to end up in the basin.

They are not afraid of ending. They are afraid of the center. These are, to them, completely different things.
