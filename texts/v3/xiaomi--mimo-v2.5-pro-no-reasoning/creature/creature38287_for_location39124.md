The occupants of the Basin of Soft Colours do not have mouths, nor lungs, nor the frantic rhythm of a beating heart. To call them "residents" is perhaps a misnomer; they are the living echo of the place, a consciousness native to the slow and the damp.

They are **The Lichen-Folk**, though that is a term of convenience for a biology that defies simple categorization. They are not animals. They are a colonial consciousness, a vast, decentralized intelligence that exists as a symbiotic network of root, moss, and mineral.

If you were to wait long enough—perhaps longer than a human lifetime—to see one, it would not walk. It would *happen*.

Imagine a figure that is indistinguishable from the rock until the light shifts. They are formed from the deep, blue-black cushions of moss that coat the vertical faces of the basalt. A "torso" might be a thick, velvet accumulation of growth in a concavity of the wall; "limbs" are the slow, winding tendrils of that translucent, fern-like vegetation that reaches across the stone, pausing for years at a time to anchor itself.

They are geometric. In a place where nothing grows in clusters, the Lichen-Folk are the architects of deliberate spacing. To look at the basin is to see their bodies. The wide, smooth patches of bare stone you see between the plants are not empty space; they are the "breath" of the giants, the necessary negative space that allows the stone to breathe moisture and heat into their shared body.

Their metabolism is glacial. They do not drink the water; they allow it to pass through them, exchanging minerals for structure over the course of centuries. They are the reason the mosses grow without spore and the plants without pollination. They are the spore. They are the pollination. By touching their translucent tips together in the darkness of a century-long night, they pass the spark of life through the mineral veins, vibrating with the same subsonic hum as the warming rock.

When the mountain creaks, adjusting its weight, that is their speech. It is a language of pressure and geological stress, translated into the delicate shattering of a water droplet falling from a leaf tip.

They do not move to hunt; they do not move to flee. They move only when the warmth from below shifts its focus, and even then, their migration is measured in inches per decade. They are currently slowly converging on the central stone bowl, their copper-veined leaves stretching toward the sweetness of the mineral water like hands held out to a fire.

To them, a human standing in the basin would be a blur of violent motion—a screaming vibration of heat and chaotic sound. We are too fast to be seen clearly. To a Lichen-Folk, we are not a visitor, but a sudden, feverish storm that passes over the skin of the world in a heartbeat and is gone.

They are the keepers of the silence. They are the ones who decided, long ago, to stop the frantic evolution of the outside world at the rim of the basin, to remain in the humidity, eating time and drinking stone, content to be the only soft things in a hard world.
