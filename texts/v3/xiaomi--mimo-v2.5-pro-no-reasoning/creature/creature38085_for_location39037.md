In this suspended world, the dominant creature—the one most truly native—is the **Skimmer**.

It is not so much a flying beast as it is a *kiting* one. Its body is a broad, flattened disc, no thicker than a palm, but spanning three meters from tip to tip. It possesses no discernible head, only a slight thickening at the leading edge where two clusters of simple, light-sensitive pits sit, like damp dimples on a drum skin. Its underside is a vast, softly undulating membrane, pale as a frog’s belly, through which water and nutrients are osmotically drawn from the very air. It does not breathe; it is *saturated*. It *is* the air.

Movement is a continuous, languid negotiation with the fog. Along its trailing edge, a fringe of incredibly long, hair-thin cilia beat in slow, metachronal waves, not to propel it, but to *steer* it against the faintest density gradients in the atmosphere. It has no muscles, only contractile fibers that can alter the tension of its membrane, allowing it to curl into a loose U-shape to shed excess water droplets, or to flatten into a perfect, silent kite to catch an updraft of warmer, lighter fog. Its "flight" is a drift, a permanent, gentle falling sideways and upwards. It grazes on the epiphytes and mosses that blanket the cliffs, extending a fine, raspy tongue—more like a gritty ribbon—to lap at the living sponges, leaving behind shallow, weeping grooves that heal in a season.

They are loners, each staking out a vast, cubic territory of fog. Encounters are rare, marked by a subtle shift in the diffused light as one passes below another, a faint, subsonic hum felt in the bones rather than heard. Reproduction is a passive event. In a season when the light-patch holds steady for days, a Skimmer will extend a cluster of reproductive nodules from its underside—glowing faintly with the same cool light as the fungal life—and simply let them go. These buoyant spore-sacs float for weeks, carried by convection currents, until they lodge in a suitable patch of moss or decayed frond, where they slowly unfurl into new, flat, wet lives.

But the Skimmers are merely the most visible tenants of the wet. The true architects are the **Lithovores**, the stone-eaters. These are not creatures in the conventional sense, but vast, colonial mats of a unique, silicate-fixing organism. They resemble a living, felted peat, dark and dense, several inches thick, clinging to the cliff faces. They move at a glacial pace, millimeters per year, their leading edge secreting a mild acid that dissolves the basalt, while their trailing edge leaves a perfectly etched trail of intricate, fractal grooves in the rock. They are the source of much of the soil-like mat, their own slow digestion and decay creating a substrate for ferns and mosses. When a section grows too heavy or waterlogged, it sloughs off in a thick, wet slab with a deep, organic *thud*, a slow-motion avalanche that reshapes the vertical landscape over centuries.

In the pools collected in the cups of giant bromeliads, life is a miniature, self-contained cosmos. Here dwell the **Bubble-Snails**, gastropods the size of a fist, whose shells are not of calcium carbonate, but of tightly woven, air-filled fungal hyphae. They float, buoyant, on the surface, grazing on algae and bacterial films. They breathe the bubble within their shell, periodically surfacing to tap their shell against the water’s tension, releasing used air and drawing in a fresh bubble of the wet, breathable fog.

And in the deep crevices, where the light is a mere memory, one might find the **Felt-Worms**. These are legless, eyeless creatures, their bodies a soft, endless braid of fibrous tissue, indistinguishable from the rotting plant matter they consume. They are the ultimate decomposers, the silent recyclers of this dripping world. They do not hunt; they are simply part of the continuous, slow cascade of matter from life to life. To touch one is to touch the very process of the plateau—the cool, damp, patient turning of water and rock and green into more of itself.

This is a world without hurry, without struggle against the environment, for there is no distinction between organism and medium. The inhabitants are not fighting the wet; they are expressions of it, temporary, beautiful coalescences of moisture and life, suspended in the great, slow breath of the cloud forest.
