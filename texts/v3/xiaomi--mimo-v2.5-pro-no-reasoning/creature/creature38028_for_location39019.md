What lives here is people, and things that live with people, and things that live despite people, and things that were here before people and will likely be after. They are all, in their way, *of* the Basin.

**The People**

They are slow. Not dull, not lazy—economical. Every motion is calibrated against the heat. You see it in the way a hand rests on a table, in the deliberate turn of a head, in the gait of a worker walking to a shift: a kinetic patience, a conservation of effort that has become physical inheritance. Their skin is often pale, not from absence of sun but from its diffusion; they are lit from all sides, perpetually. Complexions are ruddy or sallow, depending on the district, on the diet of preserved imports versus locally caught crustacean. They tend toward stocky, durable builds—heat and humidity discourage height and slenderness. They sweat, always, a glisten that mirrors the walls, and they are accustomed to the feel of moisture on their skin as a second, temporary dermis.

Their clothes are practical, light, in layers that can be adjusted. Light cotton and linen dominate, in pale colors that reflect the haze-light. Footwear is utilitarian: waterproof boots, thick-soled, necessary for the damp streets and the occasional seepage. There is little ornamentation, but not out of asceticism; things simply tarnish, corrode, or grow films of mineral deposit too quickly to make delicate decoration worthwhile. What jewelry exists is heavy, simple, often of the dense dark stone quarried from ancient sources—things that resist the Basin’s chemistry.

Their eyes are their most distinctive feature. Generations of living in the permanent, directionless dim have given many of them a slightly wider pupil, a greater sensitivity to low light. They see well in the haze, distinguishing shades of gray and the subtle play of condensation on surfaces with a subtlety that would escape an outsider. They do not look up for weather. They check the humidity gauges on public columns, the color of the mineral seep on certain landmark walls, the behavior of the canal crustaceans. These are their forecasts.

**The Ones Who Share the Space**

The fauna does not belong to them, nor they to it. It is a co-tenancy. The people have their sealed apartments, their airlocked workshops, their filtered water. The rest—the canals, the hollow buildings, the saturated ground, the very air of the streets—belongs to the others.

The small crustaceans, the swimmers and crawlers, are not seen as vermin. They are too fundamental, too inevitable. They are *the water’s population*. Children are taught early not to drink from any unfiltered source, not because of microbes, but because of the eggs and larvae. In the quieter residential districts, some elderly keep terrariums of select species, studying their molting cycles or the way they build calcium towers on bits of wire—a low-energy hobby suited to the climate. The larger crawlers, the dog-sized ones, are granted a kind of reluctant, practical respect. They clear organic debris from clogged canals and foundations. It is a symbiotic, non-sentimental arrangement. You do not feed them. You do not name them. You note their location and give them berth.

The insects are the true constant, the living texture of the air. People develop unconscious gestures: a slight, constant waving of the hand near the face while talking, a turning of the head to avoid inhaling them. Buildings are designed to exclude them, and a functioning screen is a point of civic pride. A failed screen is not an annoyance; it is a breach, a serious matter for the building’s maintenance committee. The birds—or the leathery, bat-like things—are tolerated for their role in insect control. Their roosts are mapped. Walking beneath a known roost after dusk is considered foolish.

**The Echoes of Emptiness**

The people are not lonely in the emptiness. They are defined by it, as much as by the heat. They live in the *occupied quarter*, a dense, functioning core of maybe fifteen square kilometers, amidst the vast, silent, maintained architecture of a city built for more. Their relationship with the empty spaces is not one of fear or mystery, but of practical boundary. The sealed towers are not haunted; they are *inactive*. Like a machine on standby. Children are warned away not by ghosts, but by the risk of unseen structural decay, of compromised airlocks letting in the fauna, of flooded basements that could become traps.

This creates a particular social topology. Life is intensely local. Your block, your district, your shift cohort—these are your world. Travel to another district for a social visit is an undertaking, requiring planning, a journey through long, quiet, echoing streets. Communication across the city is done via the pneumatic tube network, a clanking, hissing system of message canisters that is more reliable and more intimate than shouting across the haze. The radio bands are busy, a constant background chatter of workers coordinating between factories and pumping stations, their voices flat and practical.

**The Culture of the Warm Damp**

Their art is tactile and acoustic. Sculpture is made of the locally fired clay and the dark, resistant stone, often depicting the mineral formations—stalactites, terraces, concretions—as abstract forms. Music, as noted, favors deep, resonant sounds. The most common instrument is a kind of large, skin-covered drum tuned to a low frequency that propagates clearly through the dense air, often accompanied by long, mournful horn-like instruments made from polished conduit pipe. Songs are about water—its flow, its weight, its heat—and about the machines that manage it. There are epics sung about great pump failures, about the discovery of a new thermal spring that threatened a housing block, about the slow, heroic resealing of a breached district.

Their myths are practical. The great stories are not of gods, but of engineers. The "Old Surveyors" are mythical figures who laid the first grid and the first canals, fighting the primordial swamp. The "First Mechanics" are revered for understanding the geothermal hearts and installing the initial pumps. The enemy in all stories is entropy: the clog, the leak, the corrosion, the unstoppable seep. The hero is the one who maintains, who patches, who keeps the system running one more cycle.

They die here, and they are buried shallow, in the warm, saturated earth. The metal grave markers oxidize quickly into beautiful, twisted forms, often becoming one with the small mineral cairns left by the resting crustaceans. In this, too, there is a final, quiet integration. The Basin takes everything, in the end. It takes the heat of their bodies, the minerals from their bones, the space their lives occupied. And it returns it, slowly, into its warm, green-gray, flowing whole.
