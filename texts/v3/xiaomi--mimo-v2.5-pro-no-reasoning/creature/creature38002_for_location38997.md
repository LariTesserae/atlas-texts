Here, in the deep of the steel, what lives is not so much a people as a residue of humanity, a specific morphology shaped by pressure and time. They are the **Iron-Lunged**, the dwellers of the **Hull-Deep**. To see them is to see a subspecies evolved for the belly of the beast.

**The Physicality of the Cold and the Water**

They are pale, yes, but not in the way of those who hide from the sun. Theirs is a pallor of mineral deposition—a skin that has taken on the gray, matte texture of unpolite limestone or the underside of wet slate. The condensation that slicks every surface of the Hulls has worked its way into them. Their pores are permanently dilated, clogged with a mix of rust dust and calcium, giving their skin a speckled, gritty appearance, like coarse sandpaper.

They are thin, not from lack of food—the fungus and the nutrient paste from the dispensers provide enough calories—but from the energy cost of thermoregulation. They burn hot to keep the deep cold out. You can see the heat radiating from them in the dim light, a faint mist rising off their shoulders as the freezing air meets their warm, wet skin. Their hair is long and matted, often tied back with wire or strips of rubber, not for aesthetics but to keep the constant dripping from running down their necks. They smell of ozone, wet metal, and the faint, sour scent of the pale moss.

**Adaptation to the Soundscape**

Their vocal cords have adapted. The high, whining frequencies of the turbines and the low, chest-crushing thrum of the engines have taught them to speak in a narrow band of mid-tones. Their language is percussive, click-heavy, filled with hard stops to cut through the ambient reverb. A sentence sounds like a wrench hitting a pipe—sharp, distinct, over quickly. They have no word for "silence," only "gap" or "lull," referring to the brief seconds between the rhythmic gulps of the massive water pumps.

Their hearing is selective. They cannot hear the "groaning" of the thermal contraction anymore; it is the heartbeat of their world, as ignored as the beating of their own hearts. But they can hear a leak in a pressurized line three chambers away through the white noise. They navigate by sound as much as sight, clicking their tongues against their teeth to listen for the echo off wet metal—a biological sonar refined over generations.

**The Economy of the Drip**

They do not value gold or paper; such things rot or lose their sheen in the wet. They value *dryness* and *heat*. The currency is **"Dry-Chits"**—small, high-tech plastic tabs from the "Old Times" that repel water, used as tokens—or more practically, **absorbent materials**. A roll of dry, clean cloth is worth a fortune. A battery-powered heating coil is a king’s ransom.

Water is free, of course. It is the very air of the lower decks. But *clean* water, water that hasn't run through the rust-filter or the machinery-bleeds, is a luxury. They collect the "First Drip"—condensation that forms on the upper rivets before it touches the floor—into ceramic cups, drinking it for its faint sweetness, devoid of the iron tang of the runoff.

**The Totems of the Machine**

They do not worship the machines, for one worships a god. They *inhabit* the machine, the way a flea inhabits a dog. They treat the engines and the furnaces with a profound, casual disrespect born of intimacy. They sleep in the alcoves created by massive gear housings, warming themselves against the vibrating metal. They mark their territories with graffiti—not art, but technical diagrams. A spray-painted symbol on a wall doesn't say "I was here," but rather "Valve B-4 is sticky" or "Warm air flows north here."

They have a deep, instinctual understanding of physics, but no scientific theory. They know that if you push this lever, the room shakes. They know the "water table" is rising. They know the lower decks are flooding, not because of a weather report, but because the "Wet-Line"—the mark of algae and water stains—is climbing the pillars. They have a myth about the builders. They call them **The Architects of the Void**. They believe the Architects built the Hulls not to house people, but to catch the rain of a world above that is made entirely of water, and that one day, the Hulls will fill up, and they will swim to the sky.

**The Predator-Prey Dynamic**

The fauna here—the pale, blind crabs that scuttle in the pipes, the bat-like birds that click in the dark—are not pests to them; they are roommates. The Hull-Dwellers eat the crabs, roasted on engine blocks. They wear the feathers of the birds as charms against the "Groan" (the settling of the metal). The large, pale quadruped that was seen? They call it the **"Tunnel-Pig."** It is not an animal to them, but a *spirit of maintenance*, a holy thing that eats the rot in the walls. To kill a Tunnel-Pig is to invite a cave-in.

**The Outlook**

They are not unhappy. Sadness is a concept that requires a memory of something better. This is the world. The drip is the clock. The engine is the sun. The cold is the night. They move through the shadows with a practiced, silent economy of motion, their pale skin ghosting through the fog banks, their hard consonants echoing off the wet steel, living their lives in the belly of the great, indifferent beast.
