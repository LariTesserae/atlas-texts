# What Lives Here

They have no collective name, because nothing here has ever needed to name anything. They are the source of every sound you hear, though you would be wrong to think of them as *making* the sound. They do not make it. They *are* it — the hum, the clicks, the tonal pops, the wet chittering — the way a heartbeat is not something a body produces but something a body *is*. Remove them and the landscape would fall silent, and silence would be a kind of death the mineral surface has not experienced in longer than the concept of time could meaningfully describe.

---

## The Cracksiders

Begin with the most numerous. They are the ones whose collective motion gives the ground its faint sense of heaving.

They are flat. Not thin — *flat*, in the way a flounder is flat, in the way a manta ray is flat, but with no evolutionary relationship to either. Their bodies are compressed dorsoventrally to a degree that seems to make internal anatomy impossible, and yet they are clearly complex: the underside ripples with coordinated muscular waves as they move, and if you could somehow turn one over (you cannot; they are faster than your hands and they know where your hands are before you do), you would see that the ventral surface is not smooth but organized into dozens of small, articulated plates that overlap and slide against one another with a precision that produces the staccato clicking you hear everywhere. The clicking is locomotion. Every step is a sound. They cannot move silently, and they do not try.

Their color is the ground's color — that dark grey-brown, sometimes the dull blue-black. But they are not *colored like* the ground the way a camouflaged animal resembles its background. They are colored *by* the ground, or with it. Their exoskeletons — if exoskeletons is the right word for something that seems to be made of the same compressed mineral as the surface beneath them — incorporate actual sediment. They accumulate it. Young individuals are slightly translucent, slightly greenish at the margins, and as they age they darken and become more mineralized until old individuals are functionally indistinguishable from the surrounding terrain. They are, in a sense, wearing the land. Or becoming it. The distinction may not exist.

They have too many legs on the left side. This is not a deformity. It is species-wide, genus-wide, perhaps *phylum*-wide. The left side of the body bears between eight and twenty limbs depending on species, arranged in a dense array that functions less like individual legs and more like a continuous undulating fringe — a living skirt of articulated mineral. The right side has three limbs at most, often one, sometimes none. One species — the largest cracksider you are likely to perceive, roughly the length of your forearm — has a single massive right-side limb that ends in a broad, flat pad, and it uses this pad to brace against crack walls as it navigates vertical surfaces below the surface. It *descends*. Many of them descend. The cracks are not just refuges. They are the world. The surface is a place they visit.

They graze. Not on vegetation — there is none — but on the mineral surface itself, or rather on the film that coats it. Every surface out here carries a living patina: a community of microorganisms so small and so integrated with the mineral substrate that you cannot see them, cannot separate them from the rock with any tool you possess. The cracksiders eat this film. Their mouths are not mouths. They are broad, shallow depressions on the underside of the anterior end — more like a manta ray's gill slits than anything else — through which they press their bodies flat against the ground and *scrape*. The scraping produces a sound. It is the wet, organic chittering, layered across thousands of individuals feeding at once across kilometers of surface.

They do not see. Not in any sense you would recognize. Their entire body surface is sensitive to vibration, pressure, and chemical gradients. They feel the hum the way you feel sunlight — as a condition, a background, a thing that is simply there. When the hum shifts, they shift. When the hum thickens, they stop. They wait. They listen with their skin.

## The Trench Dwellers

Go deeper. Where the cracks widen to the width of a hand or a body, where the polygonal fractures become crevasses, where water vanishes and the mineral walls press close and the light above becomes nothing more than a faint memory of grey — that is where the trench dwellers live, and they are not like the cracksiders at all.

They are vertical creatures. They cling to crack walls, oriented head-down or head-up or sideways, and they are *long* — elongated in the axis of the crack, so that a creature the thickness of your thumb might be two meters in length, ribboning along the wall in a gentle sinusoidal curve. Their segments are visible, each one encircling the body like a ring, and between each segment the tissue is soft, flexible, and faintly luminous. Not brightly. Not usefully, from your perspective. But in the total dark of the deep cracks, the faint blue-white glow between their segments is visible for meters, and it pulses — slowly, like breathing, like tides — and it is coordinated. One begins, and its neighbor follows, and the next, and the next, so that a population of trench dwellers on a crack wall will light up in cascading waves that travel downward or upward through the darkness in long, slow, sinuous rhythms. This is the source of some of the bioluminescence you see from the surface: reflected upward off mineral walls, diffused by moisture, attenuated by distance until it becomes just a faint, directionless glow.

Their limbs — if they are limbs — are retractile. They emerge from beneath the overlapping segments like the feet of a caterpillar, but flat and broad and edged with a fringe of fine filaments that grip the mineral surface through a combination of mechanical adhesion and something else, something chemical, that leaves the faint adhesive residue you see in trails on the surface above. They can retract completely, and when they do they become rigid — locked in place, pressing against the wall with a force that would make them difficult to dislodge even if you could reach them. In this state they are silent. In motion, they produce the sustained, tonal pops: a sound created by the rapid extension and retraction of limbs against mineral, the way you might pop your finger from your cheek. But multiplied. Each individual produces eight to twelve pops per limb cycle, and a wall of trench dwellers in coordinated motion sounds like a rain of heavy drops on a hard floor, but *tonal*, each pop pitched slightly differently depending on the wall's mineral density, the depth in the crack, the humidity of the air.

They eat the cracksiders. Not all of them. Not even most of them. But the trench dwellers are the reason cracksiders do not linger near crack edges at certain times, and they are the reason for the choreographed waves of surface movement you observed — the coordinated retreats and advances that make the surface seem to breathe. The trench dwellers hunt by vibration. A cracksider at a crack edge sends vibrations through the wall with every step, every click of its ventral plates, and the trench dwellers feel this and orient toward it and extend their bodies upward — sometimes a meter, sometimes more — with a speed that is genuinely shocking in something so elongated and seemingly rigid. The strike is not a bite. It is an embrace. The trench dweller's body wraps around the cracksider and constricts, and the soft luminous tissue between its segments makes contact and *dissolves* — not with acid, not with enzyme, but through a process that resembles osmosis more than digestion, the cracksider's mineral exoskeleton softening and then flowing into the trench dweller's body through those soft intersegmental membranes. The cracksider does not struggle for long. The process takes minutes. The trench dweller glows brighter afterward.

## The Skimmers

They are in the air. They have always been in the air. You feel them more than see them — the faint prickling on your skin, the barely perceptible whistle, the movements at the edge of vision that resolve into nothing when you turn to look.

They are small. The largest is perhaps the size of your palm; most are smaller; many are so small that you would mistake them for motes of dust in the diffused light. They are membranous — their bodies are essentially a single sheet of living tissue stretched over a frame of thin, rigid rods that branch from a central axis. They look, in the instants you glimpse them, like origami — angular, geometric, folded. Their wings are their body. There is no distinction. They move by flexing the entire membrane in a wave that propagates from front to back, and this wave produces the whistle — a sound so faint and so high-pitched that you may not consciously hear it, though it sets your teeth on edge and raises the hair on your arms without your understanding why.

They navigate the darkness through echolocation, but not the kind you know. They do not emit a signal and listen for its return. They listen to the *existing* sound — the hum, the clicks, the pops, the chittering — and they read the reflections of that sound off the landscape the way you read light. The world, for them, is a constantly shifting sonar image painted by the activity of everything else. They are parasites on the sound of the system. They cannot navigate in true silence; they become disoriented, crash, die. This is why the density of sound is so critical, why it must never stop. Silence would be a kind of blindness — not for the skimmers alone, but for everything, because the skimmers are pollinators, or whatever the analogous function is in a world without plants. They carry things between crack systems. They carry chemical signals, carried on their membranes, transferred on contact. They carry fragments of organisms — spores, cells, seeds of a sort — from one crevasse to another. They are the connective tissue of this ecosystem, and they do it by riding the noise.

They land on you because you are warm and you are wet with the humidity and your skin carries chemical signatures they have never encountered. You are novel. They land and sample and launch away, carrying pieces of you into the system, and you will never know what you introduced.

## The Basin Dwellers

In the shallow depressions where moisture gathers and the mineral surface is slick and dark and reflects the low light from above — there, in water that is never more than a few centimeters deep and often less — live things that are almost impossible to describe because they are almost impossible to perceive.

They are flat with the surface. Not flat *on* it — flat *with* it. They are the same color, the same texture, the same reflective quality. They are a living film on the water's surface, or rather they are *the* surface of the water, organized and alive. When the water is still they are invisible. When it is disturbed — by a drop of condensation, by a breeze, by the passage of a cracksider at the basin's edge — they move, and the surface *shimmers* in a way that water alone would not. The shimmer propagates. It reflects the light above in patterns that are not random but shaped — expanding concentric rings, angular lattices, spirals — and these patterns are communication. The basin dwellers talk in light and geometry, and their language is written in reflections.

They feed on the moisture itself, or on what the moisture carries. They absorb condensation through their entire surface and extract dissolved minerals and organic compounds and the microscopic organisms that exist in every droplet in this world. They reproduce by division — a single basin dweller becomes two, becomes four, becomes a mat that covers the entire basin surface in a layer so thin it is functionally two-dimensional. A mature population in a large basin might have a biomass measurable in grams spread across an area of square meters — an organism of negligible mass and negligible thickness that is nonetheless *coherent*, that responds to disturbance as a unit, that coordinates its reflective patterns across its entire expanse.

They produce no sound. But the skimmers land on them and take pieces of them elsewhere, and the trench dwellers in the cracks beneath the basins orient upward toward the faint chemical signature they release, and the cracksiders avoid basin edges where the ground is too slick for their ventral plates to grip. Everything knows they are there. They are a landmark in a landmarkless world — the one place that shines.

## The Deep Hum

And then there is the hum. The source you cannot find. The sound that comes from everywhere and nowhere, from below and within and all around, that you feel in your chest before you hear it with your ears.

It is not one organism. It may not be an organism at all. It may be the mineral itself — the sedimentary layers kilometers thick, resonating with the movement of water through deep cracks, vibrating with tectonic stresses too faint to register as earthquakes but sufficient to set the entire plain ringing like a bell at a frequency just below your threshold of hearing. The living things here have tuned themselves to this frequency over time beyond reckoning. Their life cycles are keyed to its rhythm. They feed when it rises. They retreat when it falls. They breed in its peaks and go dormant in its troughs, and the troughs may last days or weeks or months, and during the troughs the surface goes quieter than you would have believed possible, and the bioluminescence dims, and the skimmers settle and fold and become indistinguishable from mineral flakes, and the world holds its breath.

But the hum always returns. It always has. And the cracksiders begin to click, and the trench dwellers begin to pop, and the skimmers begin to whistle, and the basin dwellers begin to shimmer, and the sound builds and builds until it is a wall, until it is a living thing in its own right, until you cannot tell where the sound ends and the creatures begin, because the distinction has never existed here. The sound is the life. The life is the sound. The mineral ground is their body and their voice and their world, and you are standing on it, and it is humming through your feet and into your bones, and you are part of it now whether you chose to be or not.
