To be here is to be constantly wrung out.

You begin as a seed, a spore, or a fragment torn from a parent somewhere down in the valleys, caught by the wind that never rests and carried to this high, wet, open place. You arrive on the naked stone and you do not have time to think about what you have lost, because the water is already finding you. It condenses on you before you have even settled. It beads on your surface, and you must decide immediately: absorb it, channel it, or be swept away by it. There is no other choice.

You settle in the shallow depression where a piece of older ice once sat and melted. The rock is cold and smooth beneath you, still grooved by that ancient passage. You spread. You do not grow up; there is no reason to. The sun is too close and too bright, the wind is too steady, and the air is too thick with wet. To grow upward is to be torn or to be dried out by the unfiltered light. So you grow out. You grow flat. You become a mat, a cushion, a smear of living tissue pressed tight against the stone, holding the water that falls on you, letting it move through you, slowing its passage downslope by fractions of seconds.

You are not alone. You are never alone here. The surface of the plateau is a mosaic of separate lives, and you are one tile in it. Your neighbor is a different species — a sedge, stiff and triangular, standing in the shallow pool that never drains because the granite beneath it is so smooth the water has nowhere to go. Its roots do not go deep; they cannot. They spread laterally, gripping the thin dark film that might become soil if it were given a thousand years. It drinks from the pool. The pool is always full. The sedge does not thank you for holding the water in place with your mat, but it does not need to thank you. The arrangement is wordless, as old as the retreat of the ice.

The insects come. They are small — midges, mostly, and blackflies that do not bite but hover in clouds above the standing water, mating and dying in the space of a day. They are the most visible animals, but they are not the most important. The important ones are the ones you never see: the tardigrades, microscopic and patient, curled inside your tissue in their desiccated state, waiting for the water that always comes, waking when it arrives, feeding on the algae that grows between your cells, then curling again when the water drains. They are your tenants. You do not object. They are part of the arrangement.

The birds come and go. A wheatear, its tail flashing white, lands on the concrete pumping station and sings once, twice, and then launches back into the wind. A pipit flutters over your mat, hunting the midges, and then it too is gone. They do not stay. The plateau is too open, too bright, too wet for nesting — or so it seems to you, who does not know what a bird thinks. Perhaps they nest in the deep fissures at the plateau's edge, where the rock steps down and the sound of the waterfalls is loudest and the mist is thickest and a small hollow might be sheltered from the worst of the wind.

The industrial things are strange. They are part of the landscape now, as much as the rock and the moss and the water, but they are not alive and they do not participate in the wordless arrangements. The pumping station hums. The hum is beneath everything else, a vibration felt more than heard, transmitted through the pipes that run downslope from its flanks. The pipes are covered in condensation; droplets form on their metal surfaces and fall into your mat, and you absorb them without distinguishing them from the water that falls from the sky. The relay tower sings. Its guy wires vibrate in the wind and produce a high, faint tone that joins the general chorus of water and air. You do not know what the tower is for. You do not need to know. It is there, and it is occupied by the birds when they perch, and it is wet like everything else.

The vehicle comes sometimes. You feel its weight in the ground before you hear it — a trembling in the rock, a compression of the moss, a brief cessation of the wind as the large shape moves between you and the sky. The ruts it leaves fill with water immediately, and you and your neighbors begin the slow work of growing into them, filling them with green, erasing them. It takes weeks. The vehicle returns before you are finished, and you begin again. You do not mind. Patience is what you are.

The light changes but never leaves. On clear days, it is total, directionless, the kind of light that erases shadow and makes distance uncertain. You do not mind the brightness. Your tissue is adapted to it — dark green, almost black in some patches, absorbing what it can, reflecting what it must. On overcast days, the cloud deck sits low and close, and the plateau exists in a white enclosure, the world reduced to a hundred-meter radius of visibility. The light is still bright. It bounces between the clouds and the water and your surface and the surface of your neighbors, trapped in a closed loop, and you grow in it, slowly, the way you grow in everything.

The water is constant. It condenses on you and you absorb it. It runs through you and you slow it. It pools beside you and you edge into it. It falls over the edge of the plateau and you hear it, always, the sound of the waterfalls merging with the sound of the condensation and the wind and the running channels into something that is not noise but a system, a hydrological engine running at full capacity, and you are part of it. You are the filter. You are the sponge. You are the patient green tissue that stands between the naked stone and the water that would strip it bare, and you do this without thought or effort because it is what you are.

You do not think about the future. You do not think about the past. You are occupied with the present — with the water that is falling on you now, with the light that is shining on you now, with the wind that is pressing you flat against the stone now. The rock beneath you is new, still holding the polish of the ice that carved it, and you are old — older than the rock, in a way, because you are part of a lineage that has been doing this for millions of years, colonizing new stone as the ice retreats, holding it in place, beginning the long slow project of turning it into soil. You will not finish this project. You do not expect to. You are the first step, and the plateau is patient, and the water will continue, and you will continue, and the arrangement will hold.
