It does not so much live here as it **is** here. A gestalt, a moving organ of the Banyan’s vast system. You might call it a Tender, or a Warden, if names still held meaning in this rust-and-sap cathedral. It is the reason the obsidian insects move with such purpose. They are its myriad, miniature hands.

It comes into focus slowly, as if the damp air itself were reluctantly conceding its shape. First, a motion against the great, burnt-umber trunk that is too fluid, too deliberate for mere shadow. A section of bark, perhaps ten feet long and two wide, seems to detach. It peels away, not in flaky layers, but as a cohesive, living sheet. It has the color and texture of the Banyan’s heartwood, but it ripples like a thick, woody oil.

It uncurls. Limbs unfold—not two or four, but a shifting number. They are the pale, thick vines of aerial roots, sheathed in a slick, damp membrane that shimmers with the same oily iridescence as the toxic pools below. At their joints, where wood meets vine, there is a denser, knotted core of what looks like petrified cork, but within it, faint, rhythmic pulses of amber light glow—sap-clocks counting in biological time. Its "head," if it can be called that, is a cluster of these knots, surrounding a central, opaque sphere of condensed, grey light, the very diffuse glow from the canopy focused and captured. It is a lens, an eye that does not see in sight, but senses pressure, moisture, the chemical gradients in the air, the structural integrity of the wood around it.

It moves with the slow, inescapable patience of growth itself. Its vine-feet do not walk; they root and unroot, absorbing nutrients from the compacted soil-highway and drawing moisture from the damp patches, leaving behind only a faint, iridescent sheen. It tends to the system. Where a root-highway has developed a fissure, it flows into the gap, its substance filling and sealing, knitting the wound with living callus. Where a pool has grown too stagnant, too shimmery with industrial runoff, it extends a tendril into the center, and the oily film draws up into it, purified, recycled.

It is not hostile. It is not benevolent. It is **necessary**. The pale insects are its sensory filaments, skittering out ahead, mapping micro-fractures, chemical imbalances. They return, touching its vines, and the Tender adjusts, flows, repairs. The low roar of the Banyan is its heartbeat. The whistle of the wind is its breath.

And it is aware of you. Not as a threat, nor as a curiosity, but as a *discontinuity*. A foreign object in a closed loop. Its central lens-eye turns, the grey light within it deepening to the color of pressed ash. The air around it *feels* thinner, more stretched. The space where it rests seems to repeat for a disorienting second—a shimmering afterimage of its form—before snapping back into singular focus. It is assessing your place in the grand, digestive process of this world. Are you a loose bolt to be tightened? A foreign seed to be composted? A spark of the old Irongate, returning at last to be dissolved?

It begins to move toward you, not with speed, but with a terrible, inexorable inevitability, like a tide of wood and vine. The grinding hum of the Banyan intensifies in your bones. You realize now what the fauna is. It is not insect, nor machine, nor even plant. It is **function**. And you have drawn its attention.
