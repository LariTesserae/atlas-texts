The kelp knows only the current, the cold, and the deep groan in its fibers. It sways in the abyss not to any sun, but to the pressure shifts, the thermal sighs of distant vents, and the vast, slow rotation of the ice-lid above. Each frond is a conduit, feeling the weight of water kilometers thick. Its world is pressure and mineral taste. It remembers the slow drowning of the peaks not as a memory, but as a texture in its core—a layer of silt, a change in the quality of the cold. It filters the water, catching whispers of dissolved stone, building itself molecule by molecule from the mountains' dissolution. It communicates not with thought, but with resonant vibration, the groan that travels through the water column, a language of flexion and release understood only by itself and the pressing dark. It is ancient, patient, and utterly self-contained. Its forest is a kingdom of sway and sound, indifferent to the stone bones it grows upon, indifferent to the silent ruins nestled in its shadows.

The moss dreams in pulses of cold light. It is a tapestry, a nervous system spread across the drowned rock, feeling the subtlest tremor of current, the minute shifts in mineral concentration from the breathing vents. Its bioluminescence is not a beacon, but a sigh—a slow, chemical exhalation marking its own rhythm against the eternal dark. It does not seek anything. It merely *is*, a living skin over the stone, incorporating the settling silt, growing over the past. It pulses to the heartbeat of the deep water itself, the thermal oscillations, the distant cracking of the ice. In the utter blackness of the canyons, its light is the only law, painting the cliff faces in shifting, ghostly patterns—a slow-motion aurora confined to the seabed. It knows the ruins intimately, draping over their empty shelves, seeping into their cracks, making the weathered stone look as if it breathes.

High on the drowned summits, the coral-that-is-not-coral grows with glacial patience. It listens with its crystalline structure. Each fan-shaped branch is a sensor, catching the faintest vibrations— the groan of the kelp forests far below, the crack of the ice ceiling miles above, the deep thrum of water forced through mountain passes. It adds its own sound to the chorus: the sharp, clear *tick* and *creak* of growth, the sound of its mineral tissue slowly splitting and expanding. It has been listening and growing since before the last peak vanished beneath the surface. Its roots are fused with the granite, part of the mountain's skeleton now. It filters the still, cold water of the shallows, a pale, bone-white monument to persistence. It remembers nothing; its structure *is* memory, the record of millennia written in branching patterns and the smooth wear of stone beneath its embrace.

They are the sole inhabitants: **The Conductors, the Dreamers, and the Listeners.**

*   **The Conductors (The Kelp):** Masters of flow and resonance. They stand in dark forests on the drowned ridges, their groaning song the baseline of the soundscape. They feel the gyre of the ice-cap overhead in their very being. They are the muscles of this water-world, bending, recovering, transmitting the deep currents' force into sound. Their domain is one of coordinated motion and profound vibration.
*   **The Dreamers (The Moss):** A silent, pulsing consciousness spread thin. They are the passive observers, the living map of the seabed. Their light is the only illumination in the deep, a cold fire that charts the slow respiration of the vents and the settling of silt. They grow over everything—rock, ruin, fallen kelp—indifferent curators of the drowned landscape, dreaming in slow, rhythmic glows.
*   **The Listeners (The Summit-Coral):** The ancient sentinels. Rooted to the highest points, they stand in pale, creaking groves, their fractal branches catching the faintest whispers from the surface ice and the deepest vents alike. Their growth is a perpetual act of reception and slow, crystalline expansion. They are the memory of the air, the last remnants of a world above, now transformed into permanent fixtures of the submerged peaks, listening eternally to the symphony of the deep.

There are no eyes to see, no minds to comprehend, only these vast, slow, vegetative intelligences bound to their own existence. The Conductors bend and groan. The Dreamers pulse and glow. The Listeners creak and grow. They fill the drowned fells with a strange, solemn vitality—a world of life without animals, of presence without awareness, of ancient structures reclaimed not by decay, but by patient, indifferent vegetation. The ice creaks above. The vents breathe below. And the plants sway, pulse, and listen in the endless, cold dark.
