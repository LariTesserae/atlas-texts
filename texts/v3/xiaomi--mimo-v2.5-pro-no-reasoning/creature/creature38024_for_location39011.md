To look at them, you might think them merely geological—a flourish of the basalt’s own internal logic, another wrinkle in a landscape defined by its inability to be still. You would be wrong, but you would be forgiven.

They are called, in the brief and fragmentary records left in the stone-walled structures, the *Lithomorphs*. The name is a translation of a concept that is less about form and more about process: "Those Who Cool Slowly." To see one is to see a thing that is not quite an object and not quite a creature, but something in the liminal space between—a living paradox of geology.

**The Body**

A Lithomorph is tall and slender, perhaps seven feet in height when fully extended, though it is never fully extended for long. Its silhouette is vaguely humanoid only in the sense that it possesses a central axis, two lower limbs for locomotion, and a cluster of upper appendages. But to call them limbs is a kindness. They are *flows*. Each part of the creature is a continuation of the same ropecord-textured basalt that forms the ground, but arrested mid-motion, cooled to a dull, matte black that drinks the coppery light.

The surface is not skin; it is a record. You can see, etched into its flanks, the history of its movement—a faint, rippling pattern where it moved too quickly and the outer layer solidified while the molten interior was still catching up. It is a creature of arrested flows, a living fossil of its own recent past. When it moves, it does so with a grinding slowness, the joints—where one flow meets another—exuding a faint, orange-red heat. The sound is not a footstep but a soft, tearing noise, like cooled taffy being pulled apart. The smell is that of new stone, hot and clean.

**The Metabolism**

It does not eat the archaea. It does not eat at all, in any way a biologist would recognize. It is a thermosynthetic organism. It *eats heat*. Its entire being is a complex lattice of crystalline structures that function as a biological thermocouple, drawing energy directly from the thermal gradient between the superheated rock below and the cooler, thin air above. It is, in essence, a living heat sink. It stands upon a fissure, absorbing the upwelling energy, its internal temperature rising until it is nearly indistinguishable from the molten stone it stands on. Then, slowly, it moves—away from the heat source, into the cooler, higher ridges. There, it *radiates*. Its body cools, the energy it has absorbed is slowly dissipated, its outer layers hardening further, its movements becoming even more sluggish. It is a creature that metabolizes by becoming more solid. Its life is a cycle of liquefaction and solidification, of heat and coolness, of motion and petrification.

**The Mind**

This is the part that unsettles. They do not have brains, not in the sense of a centralized organ of thought. Their cognition is distributed, a network of superheated mineral filaments that permeate their entire structure, functioning like a geological nervous system. They do not *think* in images or words. They *think* in **tensions**.

Their awareness is an awareness of stress, of pressure differentials, of the subtle, infrasonic groans of the world. When the ground shifts, they *feel* it as a thought, a sudden, sharp pressure in their distributed consciousness. When a new fissure opens miles away, they feel it as a release, a softening of a tension they had been unconsciously holding. They are the planet’s sentient nerve endings. Their purpose, if a being of such immediate, reactive existence can be said to have a purpose, is to *be* the landscape’s feeling of itself.

**The Builders**

They built the structures. Not with tools, but with their own bodies. A Lithomorph will flow into a fissure, absorbing the ambient heat until its lower half is malleable, while its upper half remains cool and rigid. It will then *extrude* itself, pushing the molten stone up and out, shaping it with the precise, slow gestures of its upper limbs. It is not construction as we know it. It is more like 3D printing with one’s own body. They build not for shelter—they need none—but as a form of *expression*. The structures are their art, their language, their record. They are the marks left by a mind that thinks in stone, a mind that is trying to make sense of the constant, violent change it feels by imposing temporary, stable forms upon it. The walls are not walls; they are sentences. They are attempts to say, "This is the shape of the pressure I felt on this day."

**The Silence and the Song**

They do not speak. They have no mouths. Their communication is seismic. When two Lithomorphs meet on a ridge, they do not face each other. They stand side-by-side, often on the same fissure, and synchronize their heat cycles. One will radiate, its body cooling, while the other absorbs, its body heating. They trade energy, back and forth, in a slow, silent dance. The vibration this creates, transmitted through the stone, is their conversation—a complex, sub-audible rhythm of stresses and releases. To an observer, it is nothing. To them, it is everything.

At night, in the absolute dark, they gather. Not in groups, but in a vast, slow-motion diaspora across the ridgelands. They stand on the highest points, their bodies cooling, their heat radiating upward into the haze. They are not praying. They are not waiting. They are *listening*. They are feeling the slow, continental groan of the world, the deep, tectonic pulse of its becoming. They are the world’s living seismographs, recording its birth pangs in the only language they know: the language of heat and stress and the slow, patient cooling of stone.

They are the children of a planet that has not yet finished being born. They are its first thought, its first feeling, its first, slow, geological dream. And as the land rises, and the ridges grow, and the silence deepens, they will be there, feeling it all, becoming it all, one cooling flow at a time.
