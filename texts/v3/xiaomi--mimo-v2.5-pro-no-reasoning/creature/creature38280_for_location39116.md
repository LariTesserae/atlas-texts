If you were to name them, they would not understand the sound. Names are for things that can be called, and these cannot be called. They can only be found, or missed, or felt passing by in the dark.

**They are born from the architecture.**

The tube-dwellers — the *founders* — are not the inhabitants. They are the material. Each one is a small, soft creature, no longer than a forearm, with a translucent body banded in faint blue-green. At the front end: a ring of cilia, a siphon, a mouth. At the back: the beginning of the tube, a shell of calcium and protein that the creature secretes around itself as it grows. It is born in the current, drifts until it finds a surface — a wall of older tubes, a ceiling of fused chitin — and begins to build. It extends its tube outward, always outward, until the siphon just barely breaches the surface. Then it feeds. It filters. It grows the tube another millimeter.

It does not think. It does not plan. But it *responds*. When the current strengthens, it grows the tube in the direction of flow, optimizing its access to suspended food. When the current weakens, it stops. When another tube-dweller's home encroaches, it grows around it, or fuses with it, or dies and leaves the calcified husk behind. Over time, the collective behavior of millions produces something that looks like design.

They live for about four years. They reproduce once, releasing eggs that drift on the current to colonize new ground. Then they die, and the tube remains, and the next generation builds on top of it.

---

**But in the older districts — in the soft-lit upper reaches, where the architecture is still tan-colored and pliable — something else lives in the tubes.**

It is not the tube-dweller. It is smaller, and it does not build.

Imagine a creature shaped like a comma, or a curled leaf, or a question mark. Its body is barely a centimeter long, pale, almost transparent, with a faint internal glow — not bioluminescence exactly, but a luminescence of *being*, as though its tissues are just slightly more reflective than the surrounding water. It has no eyes. It has no limbs. It has a mouth, but it does not eat with it — the mouth is for something else entirely.

It lives inside the tubes. Not its own tubes — *other creatures' tubes*. It crawls through the abandoned or still-occupied passages, sliding along the inner walls, feeding on the bacterial film that grows on the interior surfaces. It is a *tenant*. A *squatter*. A creature that has evolved to live in the gaps of another creature's architecture, like a hermit crab in a borrowed shell, but smaller, quieter, more intimate.

There are billions of them.

They are the ones who glow. Not brightly — nothing here glows brightly — but in aggregate, their faint, pale luminescence is what gives the upper city its ghostly, ambient light. When you see the dim outlines of a wall, a corridor, a vaulted ceiling: that is them. They are the light of the city. They are also, in a sense, its *population*. The tube-dwellers build the city. The tenants *inhabit* it.

---

**And there are others.**

In the wider passages — the thoroughfares — there are the crawlers. White, eyeless crustaceans, the size of dinner plates, with long antennae and legs like jointed stilts. They move in slow processions, ten or twenty individuals, single file, along the walls. Their bodies are flat, adapted for pressing against surfaces. They eat the tenants. They also eat the bacterial film. They also eat each other, occasionally, when one stumbles or molts. They are not social. They are just *drawn to each other*, attracted by the chemical traces they leave behind, and so they move in lines, like beads on a string.

They glow faintly beneath their carapaces — a dull, bluish patch, like a nightlight buried under a blanket. When they pass, the walls seem to move.

---

**And the hunters.**

The knife-fish. Long, slender, with underslung jaws and bodies drawn to a point at both ends. They are fast. They are precise. They move in pairs, always in pairs, coordinated by some sense you cannot perceive — perhaps electrical, perhaps chemical, perhaps just the blind instinct of two nervous systems tuned to the same frequency. They do not live in the city. They *patrol* it. They move through the passages like thoughts through a mind, appearing and disappearing in the dark, leaving no trace.

They eat the eel-fish — the small, photophore-trailing creatures that pulse through the upper reaches in schools of hundreds. The kills are quick. A strike, a flash of light as the eel-fish's photophores fire in panic, and then darkness again. The knife-fish swallow their prey whole and move on.

The eel-fish compensate with numbers. They breed constantly, releasing clouds of eggs into the current. Most are eaten. Some survive. The population is stable. It has been stable for centuries.

---

**And deeper.**

Near the basin floor, in the necropolis, the architecture is barely recognizable. The tubes have been compressed into stone. The passages have silted shut. The light does not reach here — not even the faint glow of tenants, who prefer the softer, newer tubes above. It is dark. It is warm. It is quiet.

But not empty.

There are burrowers — small, worm-like creatures that move through the silted mass, eating the dead. They are blind, toothless, soft-bodied. They excrete calcium. They are, in their own way, *returning* the city to the mineral state, grain by grain. They are the final stage of the cycle: the organisms that decompose the architecture back into raw material, which will eventually be carried upward by currents, recolonized by new tube-dwellers, and built into new structures.

It takes centuries. It is happening now.

---

**And one more.**

There is something in the deep thoroughfares — the wide vertical channels that connect the upper city to the basin floor. It is large. It is slow. It moves by undulating its body, which is flat and wide, like a manta ray, but longer, more tapered, more ancient-looking. Its skin is pale, almost white, and it is covered in a fine layer of silt, as though it has been drifting for a very long time without stopping.

It does not hunt. It does not build. It does not glow. It feeds on the ambient particulate — the silt, the scales, the dust of calcium structures — filtering it through gill-like slits along its underside. It is a creature of the current, shaped by the current, moving always in the direction of flow.

It has been here longer than the city. It will be here after the city is gone. It does not know this. It does not know anything. It simply *is*, in the way that a river is, or a mountain, or the slow accumulation of sediment over geological time.

When it passes through a district, the crawlers pause in their processions. The eel-fish scatter. The tube-dwellers retract into their tubes. Everything stills, for a moment, as the great pale shape drifts through the dark, sifting the water for dust.

Then it is gone, and the city resumes its breathing.

---

**There are no minds here.**

There are no thoughts. There are no intentions. There is only the warm, dark water, the constant rain of silt, and the organisms that have arranged themselves into this extraordinary, unconscious architecture — each one following its own small logic, its own imperatives of food and shelter and reproduction, and the sum of all those imperatives is *this*: a city without builders, illuminated without fire, inhabited without memory.

If you could ask them what they are doing, they would not understand the question. If you could ask them why they build, they would not know what *why* means. They are not doing anything. They are simply *being*, in the way that all living things are simply being, and the city is what their being looks like, from the outside, when you step back far enough to see the shape of it.

The silt keeps falling. The tubes keep growing. The water stays warm.

And the tenants — the pale, comma-shaped creatures who live in the gaps and glow with the faint light of their own small lives — they are the closest thing this place has to a population. They are the ones who *inhabit*. They are the ones who, if the city could dream, would be its dream of itself.

But it does not dream. It does not know itself. It is a city without a mind, and it will go on growing, and settling, and glowing faintly in the dark, long after anything that could wonder about it has come and gone.
