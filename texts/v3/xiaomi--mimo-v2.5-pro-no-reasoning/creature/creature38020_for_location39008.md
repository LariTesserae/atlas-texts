No one built this place. Or rather, no one *first* built it. It was grown.

They call themselves, if they could be said to call themselves anything, the Rooted. They are not a people in the way you would understand it. There is no language of words, only of pressure, of sap-flow, of the slow, patient turning of a waterwheel. Their consciousness is a slow, deep thing, a communal mind woven from a million fungal threads that network through the mud and the rotting wood of the foundations. The city is their body.

The great, petrified waterwheels are their limbs. They turn not with the force of a current, but with a deliberate, muscular slowness, as if testing the weight of the water. The groaning of the pistons is the sound of their deep, geothermal breath, pulling heat from the planet's core to power the low, constant forges that keep the heart of the district warm and the damp from turning to rot. The pumps that sigh as they move water are their circulatory system, channeling the lifeblood of the marsh through bronze arteries clogged with lichen.

The people themselves—the individuals, if you can force such a distinction—are the things you might mistake for part of the architecture. They are the lumpy, moss-covered growths on a great support beam, slowly shifting to catch a better angle of the hazy light. They are the vine-woven, vaguely humanoid shapes that stand for a decade on a rooftop garden, their form a mere suggestion, their attention turned inward to the slow chemistry of photosynthesis and the taste of the rain. They are the fungi that glow with a faint, internal light in the shadowed spaces, pulsing in time with the distant thump-thump of the pistons.

They do not speak. They *are*. A decision is made when the entire network of roots and rhizomes reaches a consensus, a slow, tectonic shift in growth patterns. If a section of a wall is crumbling, a new vine will begin to grow there, not to destroy, but to reinforce. If a canal is silted, the flow of the waterwheels will change, subtly, over the course of a season, to redirect the current and scour it clean. Their art is the patina on the bronze, the intricate patterns of moss on a flagstone. Their history is the thickness of the bark on a central pillar, each ring a decade of shared experience.

They do not fear the jungle; they are its most patient expression. They do not mourn the lost builders who laid the first stone; they have forgotten them as completely as the forest floor forgets a fallen branch. The metal and brick are merely the trellis upon which they have built their true, living city. They are a single, slow-motion riot of life that has learned to think, to build, and to dream in the language of water, wood, and time. They are the damp, industrial lullaby, given consciousness. And if you listen, truly listen, you can feel their thoughts in the vibration of the stone beneath your feet—a vast, green, and eternal contentment.
