The people of the Registries are as layered and stratified as the city itself, as solid and as hollow, as young and as enduringly ancient as the basalt they inhabit. They are not merely inhabitants; they are geological features, living accretions of flesh and metal, bone and memory, grafted onto the spine of a still-forming world.

They do not walk with the bounce of lowlanders. They move with a deliberate, grounded heaviness, feet planting flat and wide as if to root themselves against the wind or the tremor. Their skin is weathered, sun-scorched and wind-bitten, but it carries a subcutaneous flush, a permanent glow of warmth from the geothermal pipes running through every wall and floor. They do not shiver. They are, internally, always slightly feverish, their body temperatures running a fraction of a degree higher than the rest of the world. This manifests in their eyes, which are often bright, dilated, and quick, scanning not for threats but for changes in pressure, in texture, in the vibration of the ground.

The most distinct feature of the Registry native is the *scarring*. It is not accidental. It is a language.

The youngest generation bears scars that are crisp and geometric, cut deliberately into the skin of their forearms and cheeks: clean lines, sharp angles, mimicking the fracture patterns of the young basalt. It is a mimicry of the stone’s adolescence, a way of saying: *I am new here. I am sharp. I have not yet been weathered.*

The middle-aged—the technicians, the smelter-workers, the funicular operators—bear scars that are older, wider, and have begun to soften. These are often ritual burns, applied with heated metal tools, creating smooth, rope-like keloids across their shoulders and backs. These mimic the pahoehoe flows, the "musculature of something enormous caught mid-breath." It is a middle ground: *I have begun to flow. I am hardening. I am becoming part of the current.*

The elders are terrifying to outsiders. Their skin is a palimpsest of burns, cuts, and chemical stains. Their hands are permanently blackened with basalt dust that has been ground into the creases over decades, a patina that cannot be washed away. They move slowly, not from frailty, but from an absolute economy of motion. They have internalized the mountain's patience. They do not speak often, but when they do, it is in a low, resonant cadence that cuts through the thin air and the subsonic hum. They are the living registries, the ones who remember the last major tremor, the last thermal shift, the last time the fog shapes predicted a change in the magma chamber's mood.

Their clothing is heavy, layered, and utilitarian—thick canvas and treated leather to resist the abrasive basalt dust and the perpetual slick of condensation. But it is also adorned with the city’s primary material: metal. Everyone wears metal. Not jewelry, but *components*. Gears, rivets, plates, and rings of specialized alloys are woven into collars, cuffs, breadths of garments. It is a display of the city’s output, a wearable portfolio, but it is also practical: weight to keep them grounded, and a constant, low-level exposure to the city’s voice—the faint, high-pitched hum of the metal in the wind, a sound only they can hear.

They are a people of few words and dense meaning. They do not waste breath on pleasantries; the air is too thin for that. Their language is clipped, guttural, heavy with technical jargon and words borrowed from the mountain’s own vocabulary. They have words for specific types of silence, for the quality of steam, for the exact angle of light through the cobalt sky. They do not have a word for "home" that translates cleanly; their closest term means "the place that holds my weight."

They are fatalists, but not in a despairing way. They are fatalists in the way a tree is fatalist about the axe. They know the eruption will come. The seismographs sing its inevitability. This knowledge does not drive them to hedonism or nihilism. It drives them to *build*. They build with a ferocious, focused intensity, pouring all their energy into the act of creation on the edge of annihilation. Their art is not pretty; it is structural. They carve reliefs into the basalt of their buildings that tell the history of the caldera’s tremors. They compose music for low frequencies, performed on massive metal pipes that vibrate the foundations of concert halls. They have no concept of "eternity," only of "duration." Their greatest praise for a structure is that it is "honest"—that it is built to match the weight of the rock it stands on.

They are a people who listen to what lies below. Their children are taught to put their ears to the ground. Their scientists are seismologists and chemists, but their poets are the ones who interpret the shapes in the fog. They have a saying: *The mountain does not care, but it remembers. We must care, so we can remember it back.*

They are the Caldera Registries. They are not surviving the volcano. They are building a civilization in its throat, and they are doing it with the slow, deliberate, heat-blasted patience of something that knows it is being forged.
