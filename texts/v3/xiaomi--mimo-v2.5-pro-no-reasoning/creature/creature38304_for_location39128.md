The inhabitants of the Green Crucible do not live *in* it. They *are* it, and it is them. A slow, mutual digestion.

**The Keepers**
You would not call them citizens. You would not even call them workers, not anymore. They are the Keepers, or the Tenders, and there are perhaps a dozen left, though it is hard to tell. They move in a drift, a viscous momentum through the hot, wet air.

They are naked, not from lack of clothing, but because clothing would be an offense against the climate. Their skin is the color of old parchment, slick with a permanent sheen of condensation and fungal spores. Their bodies are lean but not strong; strength would be pointless. They are soft, their joints moving with a fluid, underwater grace, to avoid snagging on the profusion of growth.

Their faces are placid, mouths often slightly open as if listening to a frequency just below sound. Their eyes are milky, adapted to the bioluminescent gloom, and they rarely focus on any one thing. They do not speak. They communicate through touch, through a slow caress of a vine to signal "this needs more water," or a palm laid flat on a warm pipe to say "this section is sound."

Their task is not maintenance. It is accompaniment. They wipe the slime from a pressure gauge not to read it, but because the slime must be wiped *there*. They gently guide a creeper’s tendril away from a critical steam valve, only for it to find its way back in a week. They scrape the oil-thick moss from a walkway, leaving a temporary bare stripe of rusted metal, a path that will be reclaimed by morning. They are gardeners of entropy.

**The Symbiosis**
The fungi have no interest in consuming them. The fungi have already won. Spores carpet their lungs; a fine, pale mycelium threads through their hair and the creases of their skin, making them faintly luminous when they stand near a light-cap. They breathe the spore-heavy air and exhale a carbon dioxide-rich mist that the leathern ferns drink. They are a slow, walking part of the ecosystem.

They eat the fruit of the Crucible. Not the tropical rot, but the cultivated clusters of pale, tasteless mushrooms that grow in trays set out on the catwalks. They sip the constant, warm condensation that drips from the pipes—a mineral-tinged, metallic water. Their digestion is a sluggish, heat-addled process. They spend long hours just sitting, back-to-back against a warm vent, in a state that is not sleep, not meditation, but a kind of vegetative standby.

**The Fauna (and the Flora That Moves)**
The eyeless salamanders are their silent companions. A Keeper will often have one coiled around their forearm or nestled in the hollow of their neck, sharing body heat. There is no affection, only a mutual acknowledgment of warmth. The salamanders feed on the gnats and oil-mites, keeping the Keepers’ immediate vicinity clear of pests.

The most startling inhabitants are the **Root-Walkers**. These are not separate creatures. They are sections of the great creeper vines that have learned to move. Over decades, a vine thick as a thigh, encasing a length of old cable or pipe in its grasp, will slowly, imperceptibly contract and release, "walking" its woody trunk a few inches a month along a wall or ceiling. They are hunting for new heat sources. The Keepers treat them with the same placid deference as they do the static growth, sometimes trailing a hand along their bark as they pass.

**The Echo of the Past**
In the deepest, oldest habitation blocks, where the concrete is more fungal patch than stone, you might find the **Echoes**. These are not ghosts, but recordings. The fungal mycelium network, having consumed ancient wooden furniture, paper, and perhaps even the very bones of the first inhabitants, has incorporated their patterns. In certain light, on certain walls, the bioluminescence will shimmer into shapes: the outline of a chair, the shadow of a door, the fleeting impression of a face in the grain of the moss. The Keepers watch these displays with their milky eyes, their expressions unchanging. They are not remembering. They are witnessing the architecture completing itself.

They are born here, they live here, they will die here. Their bodies will be laid on a warm vent, wrapped in moss, and in a season, they will be indistinguishable from the soil. They are the living, breathing, slow-beating heart of the Green Crucible’s final, humid dream: a world where the machine has stopped, and only the garden, and the gardeners of the garden, remain.
