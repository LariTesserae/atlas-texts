They are the people of the steam and the sludge, as much a part of the district’s biome as the slick algae or the armored beetles. They do not have a name for themselves; names are for places that have a sky wide enough to hold them. Here, you are what you do, or what you withstand.

They are short and stooped, not from malnutrition, but from a lifetime of moving through narrow spaces, of ducking beneath pipes and crawling through access tunnels. Their skin is the color of unglazed clay, pale and grayish, stretched taut over prominent bones and etched with a fine lattice of dark veins, like roots seen through wet paper. It is cool to the touch, almost clammy, a permanent adaptation to the heat that rises from below. They do not sweat; the moisture on their skin is the district’s own breath, condensing on them as it does on the brick.

Their eyes are small, deep-set, and useless in the true dark. Instead, they navigate by the map of sound and vibration in their bones: the pitch of a stressed gear, the rhythm of a healthy piston, the subtle change in the drip of condensation that signals a new crack in the mortar. They read the temperature of the rising steam and the texture of the sludge underfoot with their bare, splayed toes, which are long and prehensile, gripping grates and pipes with ease.

They move with a silent, deliberate economy. Sudden movements are for the beetles and the salamanders; for them, a jerky motion might dislodge a crucial brace or startle a nest of biting, soot-winged flies. Their work is a form of listening and tending. They carry no tools of metal—metal here is for bearing weight or channeling force, not for delicate tasks. Their instruments are ceramic scrapers, hardened fungal leather pouches, and long, fibrous reeds stripped and bound into probes and brushes.

Their society is a circuit board of necessity. There are the **Drip-Finders**, who spend their lives tracing the paths of moisture on the walls, noting where new streaks form, where the blackest water gathers, predicting the slow failures of the brick. There are the **Vibration Listeners**, their ears and wrists pressed constantly to girders and pipes, their bodies a tuning fork for the district’s health, humming warnings in a complex pattern of taps and gestures. The **Sludge-Sifters** work the warm pools, their arms plunged to the elbow, sifting through the effluent not for treasure, but for the compact, coal-like nodules of solidified industrial waste that burn slow and hot, the district’s only currency.

They eat the beetles—roasted over the heat-vents of idle machines—and the starchy, bitter bulbs of the pale reeds. They drink the condensed steam, collected in ceramic bowls, which tastes of rust and ozone. They do not sleep in cycles. They sleep in shifts, in hammocks of woven fungal fiber slung in the driest, warmest corners of the workshops, their bodies curled around the thumping pipes like fetuses listening to a monstrous heartbeat.

There is no art. Their songs are mimics of the machinery—the wheeze of a steam-release, the rhythmic clang of a automatic hammer. Their stories are maps, genealogies of rust and repair. “My mother’s hands knew the third gear-housing before its teeth wore smooth.” “My clutch-mate stopped the leak in the south wall that was singing a high note.” They mark their age not in years, but in the layers of soot and repair on the walls they have tended.

They do not dream of elsewhere. The concept is as alien as a clear sky. The district is not their prison; it is their organism, and they are its cells, its nerves, its quiet, tenacious will to persist. They are the keepers of the stagnant equilibrium, their lives a single, sustained note in the district’s endless, grinding symphony. They are the living, breathing part of the rust, and they have no desire to be anything else.
