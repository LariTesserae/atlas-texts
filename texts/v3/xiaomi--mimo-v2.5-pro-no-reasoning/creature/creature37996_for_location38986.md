The people of the Caloric are a testament to persistence under pressure, shaped by heat, stone, and the slow breath of the earth. They are not numerous, but they are **present**—a congregation of warm blood and bone in a chamber of warm stone, their existence a quiet defiance against the silence and the sealing.

**Physically,** they are lean and compact, with minimal body fat to trap heat. Their skin is a uniform deep ochre, not from sun, but from generations of sulfur-rich diet and mineral dust; it is thick, smooth, and remarkably resilient, able to withstand brief touches of surfaces that would blister an outsider’s flesh. Sweat is a constant companion, glistening on their brows and shoulders, but it evaporates almost instantly in the dry air, leaving faint, crystalline mineral streaks on their skin. Their hair is coarse and dark, worn cropped short or tightly braided close to the skull to avoid catching on pipes or lithoform mats. The most striking feature is their **eyes**: large, with irises ranging from deep amber to dark grey, pupils perpetually dilated in the low light. They see in gradients of heat-shimmer and bioluminescent glow as easily as others see in daylight. Their hands are strong, calloused, and extraordinarily sensitive; they can read the texture of the rock, the dryness of the air, and the pulse of the steam in the pipes by touch alone.

**Culturally,** they are a society of **maintainers and readers.** Their primary social role is not warrior, priest, or farmer, but *reader*—one who can interpret the signs of the chamber. They read the color shifts in the lithoforms to predict gas emissions. They read the pitch and rhythm of the steam vents to know which pipes to adjust. They read the faint vibrations in the stone to sense the magma’s mood generations ahead. Knowledge is oral, mnemonic, and experiential. There are no books; there are *chains*—long, rhythmic chant-sequences that describe the location of hidden tunnels, the age of terrace sections, the genealogy of the lithoform colonies. A mistake in recitation isn’t punished; it’s simply a crack in the wall, a slow failure of memory that must be mended.

Their social structure is **terrace-based.** Each major terrace or cluster of rooms forms a kindred, a kinship group tied by blood, marriage, and shared labor on a section of wall and pipe. Terraces higher up the wall are generally newer and slightly more prestigious—drier, but closer to the ceiling’s open dark, where the youngest, most daring builders like to work. There is no ruler, only a shifting council of the most adept *readers* and *keepers*—those who know the ancient patterns of the vents and the deepest histories of the stone. Disputes are settled by testing knowledge: two rivals might be asked to predict the next eruption of the Throat, or to locate a buried cistern by sound alone.

**Daily life** is a cycle of perpetual, patient labor. They rise (not from sleep, for sleep is taken in shifts, whenever exhaustion or the deep thrum of the magma pulls them under) to check the pipes, scrape overgrown lithoforms from walkways, and chip away the mineral crusts that threaten to seal their doors and pipes shut. They harvest lithoform with obsidian scrapers, filling woven basalt-fiber baskets. They tend the fermentation pots, a revered and delicate process where the exact mixture of sulfur compounds, heat, and bacterial cultures is a closely guarded secret per terrace.

Water is their holiest substance. The condensation caught in the cooling pipe-bends is collected drop by drop into small, polished obsidian vials. A family’s water store is its most valuable heirloom, often older than the terrace itself, passed down and replenished. Water is not for drinking on a whim. It is for wetting the lips of the ill, for mixing the sacred mortar that seals a new child’s first sleeping niche, for a single, profound sip before undertaking the most dangerous task of a generation: crawling into an unmapped tunnel to clear a blockage. They are always thirsty. Their bodies have long since adapted to derive most moisture from their food and the ambient air, but the memory of thirst, the *idea* of water, is woven into their language and their prayers.

Their **technology** is organic and volcanic. They have no wood, no metal. They shape tools from obsidian, basalt, and the dense, calcium-rich residue from their own pipes. They weave fibers from certain mineral deposits into cloth and baskets. Their greatest mechanical achievement, the piston-hammers, are not seen as miraculous but as part of the chamber’s own heartbeat—they maintain them as one might maintain a limb. When a piston cracks, the sound of its failure is a day of mourning for that workshop, followed by weeks of carving a new one from the living rock.

**Art and expression** are ephemeral and functional. They sing, but their songs are often instructional: rhythmic lists of pipe repairs, or harmonic imitations of vent sounds to remember their sequences. They carve, but into the plaster of lithoform paste and mineral mortar that seals their rooms—not permanent idols, but temporary designs that will be overgrown or chipped away within a decade. The only true art they cultivate is the lithoform itself. A *reader* might spend a lifetime guiding the growth of a particular colony on a terrace wall, encouraging a swirl of bioluminescent blue and deep iron-red to form a pattern that tells a story only its cultivator knows. When that *reader* dies, the colony is left to grow wild, its pattern slowly consumed by newer growth, the story becoming texture, becoming silence, becoming part of the wall again.

To encounter them would be to encounter a profound stillness. They do not rush. They move with a deliberate economy, conserving energy in the pervasive heat. They speak in short, clear phrases, often using a tonal register that carries better in the echoes. Their laughter is rare, a low, breathy sound that doesn’t travel far. But in their eyes, in their steady hands on the warm stone, there is a deep, abiding connection to their world. They are not prisoners. They are the earth’s inhabitants, as native as the lithoforms, as patient as the cooling magma, their lives measured not in sunrises, but in the slow, steady accrual of mineral layers on the walls of their home.
