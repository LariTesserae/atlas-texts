The city breathes. You hear it first—a low, wet, tidal pulse that has nothing to do with your own heart. It is the sound of ten billion tiny mouths opening and closing in the dark.

**The Litho-Sponges** are the true inhabitants here. They are not merely growing on the walls; they *are* the walls now, a new skin of bone-white, porous flesh stretched tight over the ancient, synthetic basalt. Look closely at the nearest hump of masonry. The surface is not smooth but a dense, intricate lacework of calcified honeycomb, each pore a perfect, hexagonal mouth. They are warm to the touch, radiating a faint metabolic heat that contributes to the oppressive, humid atmosphere. They do not filter air—they filter *time*. The ancient dust, the microscopic fragments of eroded stone, the dissolved minerals from the eternal condensation—all are drawn into their pores, broken down, and incorporated. They are slowly digesting the city, converting it from sterile synthetics into a calcium-rich, living tissue. Where they are thickest, the walls glow with a faint, internal bioluminescence, a sickly green that is less a light and more a stain on the darkness.

Between these living mountains, the **Troglo-Cephalopods** drift. They are not hunters. They are scavengers of the suspended, of the things that fall from above or float up from below. Their bodies are translucent, gelatinous sacs, the size of a human torso, trailing delicate, frilled cilia that taste the vibrations in the thick air. They have no bones, no fixed shape—just a distributed nervous system and a slow, rhythmic pulsing that propels them through the narrow corridors. They feed on the dead and the dying. When a patch of Litho-Sponge falters and dies, the Cephalopods descend upon it in a silent, ghostly flock, enveloping it in their bodies, secreting digestive enzymes that turn the white skeleton into a soft, nutrient-rich slurry. Their waste, a fine, glittering calcium powder, drifts down to be caught and consumed by the sponges below. It is a closed loop of consumption and excretion, a perfect, glacially-paced ecosystem where the waste of one is the food of another.

And in the very deepest, most claustrophobic alcoves—places where the sponge-mat is so thick it has sealed the corridors shut—live the **Acolytes**. They are not fauna in the traditional sense. They are what the Troglo-Cephalopods become when they can no longer move. A Cephalopod, old and slow, will find a crevice, settle, and begin to change. Its body solidifies, its cilia harden into root-like tendrils that dig into the sponge-mat, its gelatinous flesh turns a deep, photosynthetic black. It becomes a living statue, a sessile filter-feeder, its only movement the slow, deliberate opening and closing of a single, large orifice on what was once its mantle. It is now part of the architecture, another organ in the breathing city. It no longer drifts; it *is* the deep. It subsists on the faintest of nutrients—the slow decay of the walls, the microscopic life in the gelatinous air, the distant, thermal pulse of the city’s own heart. It waits. It has all the time in the world.
