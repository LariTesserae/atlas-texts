The people of Vesskaya are the stone itself, given breath and purpose.

They are not tall. They cannot afford to be. Their frames are squat and broad, built low to the ground with the stability of foundations. To walk upright through the low, square-cut archways of the Burrowed Towns, they have developed a posture that is permanently hunched, a relaxed slump of the shoulders that speaks of a life spent ducking, crawling, and climbing rather than striding. Their legs are disproportionately powerful, their calves thick as tree roots, necessary for the constant vertical traversal of the terraced tiers.

Their skin is the texture of the unpolished basin walls—grey-brown, rough, and damp to the touch. It is not merely dirty; it is *calcified*. A Vesskayan’s skin weeps a thin, mineral-rich moisture that hardens in the air, leaving a crust in the deep creases of their knuckles and elbows. They appear to be slowly turning into the architecture that surrounds them. Dust does not settle on them; they are the dust, returned to form.

Their eyes are narrow, protected by heavy, stony brows that serve as natural eaves against the constant condensation. They do not blink often. Their irises are the colour of wet slate—shifting, deep greys with flecks of mica that catch the light. They are a people adapted to the dim, the twilight of the overcast sky, and they see with startling clarity in the deep, windowless back-rooms carved into the earth.

They are a people of silence. Their vocal cords are vestigial or perhaps atrophied from disuse. They communicate through the language of the tools: the *tap-tap-tap* of the chisel, the scrape of the adze, the rhythm of the mallet. Two Vesskayans passing in a narrow lane do not exchange greetings; they exchange *rhythms*—a specific cadence of heel-strikes on the slate floor that conveys a greeting, a warning, or an inquiry. They are drummers of the ground, reading the vibration of the town through the soles of their wide, flat feet.

Their craft is their existence. They do not merely live in the stone; they shepherd it. They are the curators of a landscape that refuses to settle. They are born with tools in their hands, or near them. A Vesskayan infant is soothed not by lullabies, but by the sound of a parent grinding slate. The children are the swiftest and lightest, often seen darting along the very rim of the basin walls, their bare feet gripping the raw stone with a gecko-like friction. They are the scouts, finding the fresh cracks, the new leaks, the shifting faults, reporting them back to the elders not with shouts, but by bringing a handful of fresh gravel to the main cisterns.

They are a people of practical fatalism. They do not mourn a collapsed wall; they celebrate the harvest of reusable blocks. They live in the moment of the stone, aware that their home is a temporary arrangement of matter that is still cooling, still settling, still deciding what it wants to be. They are the quiet anchors in a world that moves.
