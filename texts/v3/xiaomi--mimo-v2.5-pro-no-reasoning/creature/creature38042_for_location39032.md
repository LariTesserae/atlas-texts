To stand at the Shriek Table is to be inside a throat.

The first thing that occupies this place is the sound itself. It is not a resident, but a presence. A living wall of harmonic vibration that hums in the bones of the rock. It is a pressure that seeks out the sinuses and the fluid of the inner ear, a constant, aggressive white noise that flattens the world into a single, vibrating plane of existence. It is the baseline. The canvas.

Against this canvas, life has etched itself in defiance of softness.

Look to the stone, and the first tenants reveal themselves: the **Lichens**. They are not plants, but a slow-motion union of fungus and algae, a patient crust of gray-green and bone-white. They are the plateau’s first thoughts, its oldest citizens. They exist in geological time, measuring their lives in the erosion of the mountains they cling to. They do not grow so much as they *persist*, a stain of life on the face of indifferent basalt. They are the foundation, the slow digestion of stone into possibility.

But in this high, thin light, the dominant life form is feathered. The air belongs to the **Storm-Shearers**.

These are not birds in the gentle, terrestrial sense. They are biological kites, creatures evolved for a world of horizontal rain and perpetual gale. Their wings are long and stiff, their bodies dense with muscle. They possess a grip strength that allows them to cling to polished, wind-scoured rock where a human boot would find no purchase.

They do not soar; they are thrown. They launch themselves into the shrieking updrafts and are immediately swept away, only to turn and fight their way back, their flight a series of violent, calculated tacks against the wind. They are gray and slate-blue, the color of storm clouds, their bellies stained with the rust and white of their own waste. Their existence is a brutal, beautiful equation: energy spent versus energy gained.

They hunt the **Sky-Drift**. This is the collective name for the clouds of insects—moths, flies, and gnats—that are lifted on thermal columns from the world below, arriving at the plateau’s edge confused and exhausted. They are the fuel for this entire ecosystem. The Storm-Shearers intercept them in diving, shrieking flocks, their calls—sharp, two-note whistles—piercing the wind’s chord like needles through cloth. They are the primary consumers, the chaotic heart of the food web.

In the lee of the rocks, in the tafoni hollows where the wind’s roar dulls to a constant, deafening rush, a different kind of life holds on: the **Stone-Clingers**. These are lizards, small and dark, their scales the exact gray of the basalt. They are so still, so perfectly camouflaged, that they are often mistaken for part of the rock. They hunt the smaller insects the birds miss, their movements a quick, desperate dash from one shelter to the next. Their eyes, however, betray them—a faint, golden sheen that catches the light, a spark of awareness in the stone.

At the very bottom of the food chain, in the lifeblood of the system—the water—are the **Pool-Swayers**. These are the strangest inhabitants. In the deepest tafoni hollows, where condensation drips and pools persist for hours, these small, dark, segmented creatures move in slow, synchronized undulations. They are not insects, not worms, but something older, simpler. They feed on the biofilm of algae and microscopic life that grows on the wet stone. They are the recyclers, the quiet, unseen engines that keep the smallest reservoirs of life from choking on their own waste.

And finally, there is the moss. In the most sheltered depressions, where the wind is merely a constant roar, cushions of **Drip-Moss** grow. They are a violent, impossible green against the gray. They are the plateau’s kidneys, the slow-release reservoirs that feed the tafoni pools through the driest hours. They are the reason the Stone-Clingers and the Pool-Swayers can exist at all.

This is the community of the Shriek Table. It is not a family. It is a collection of specialists, each locked into their own niche by the relentless pressure of the wind, the light, and the lack of water. They are all part of a single, vibrating chord—a sustained, teeth-rattling harmony of predator and prey, of growth and erosion, of life and the slow, patient stone that bears it. They do not care if you are watching. They are too busy being.

They are the shriek. And they are the table.
