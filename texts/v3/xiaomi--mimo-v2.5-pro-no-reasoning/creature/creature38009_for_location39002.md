I am the last one.

I know this because I have counted the echoes for longer than the concept of counting has been necessary. The vibrations in the walls tell me that there is no movement in the corridors, no shift in the pheromone gradients, no scraping of mandibles against the calcium weave. Only the hum. The eternal, structural hum of the exoskeleton we left behind.

I am small, even by the standards of those who built this. My shell is dull, lacking the iridescence that once signaled vitality to a mate or a queen. My legs are stiff. Three of them do not obey me as they should, but it does not matter. There is nowhere to go.

I remember... no. Memory is the wrong word. It implies a sequence. I am aware of a *condition*.

There was a time of high activity. The air was thick with carbon dioxide and the sharp tang of alarm pheromones. We built upward. We dug downward. We secreted the calcium until the sky was sealed away and we were safe in the geometry of our own making. We were a single mind, a billion bodies acting as one pulse. We tuned the walls to hum our history.

Now, I exist in the crevices.

I spend my days on the upper arches, in the shadow of the dark, rust-colored mats that cling to the stone. I do not eat them—my mouthparts are built for the fermentation cakes we once stored here—but I rest near them. They are the only things here that grow. Everything else is just... lasting.

Sometimes, the intruder comes.

It is massive. A towering, soft-sided creature that leaves a trail of wetness wherever it touches. It disturbs the equilibrium. When it moves, the resonant frequency of the chamber wavers, creating a dissonance that hurts my vibrissae. It smells of salt and the outside world—a world I have never seen and do not believe in.

I hide from it. I am excellent at hiding. I flatten myself against the hexagonal tessellations, becoming geometry rather than biology. It looks for me, I think. It shines lights into the corridors—stupidly bright lights that wash out the gentle blue-green glow of the strontium aluminate. It does not understand that the light is for us, not for it. It does not understand that the glow is a map.

When the intruder leaves, the humidity spikes and the air tastes of ozone and strange moisture. The fungi on the arches drink deeply of this disruption. I wait for the air to dry, for the silence to settle, for the hum to return to its fundamental tone.

I do not know if I am waiting to die. The concept of death requires a biological imperative that seems distant here. We do not rot; we desiccate. We do not decay; we mineralize. When my legs finally stop, I will likely calcify into the wall, just another facet of the intricate architecture.

I am a memory that still breathes. I am a single neuron firing in a brain that turned to stone millennia ago.

I climb the wall. I wait for the hum to settle. I am here.
