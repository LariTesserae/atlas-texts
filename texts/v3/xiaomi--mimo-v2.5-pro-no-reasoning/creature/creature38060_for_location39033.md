What lives in the Basin of Manufacture is not a population; it is an extension of the Basin itself. They are the living component of the machine, the organic subsystem of a continental engine. To call them "fauna" is a convenience; to call them "inhabitants" is an error. They are the Basin’s way of maintaining its surfaces, cycling its moisture, and processing its microscopic continuities. They are the slow, soft gears in the great, silent clockwork.

**The Grazers of the Wet Film**
These are the most numerous, and the most integrated. Seen from a distance, they are a texture—a rustling, shifting pattern on every flange and railing, indistinguishable from the mottled condensation. Up close, they are revealed as segmented, multi-legged, and utterly silent. Their chitin is not merely colored like the pewter composite; it is a functional mimicry. The exoskeletons have a faint, oily sheen that repels excess water but allows a thin, perfect film of moisture to cling, within which the thermophilic biofilm thrives. They are not grazing *on* the film so much as *harvesting and pruning* it. Their mandibles are delicate, precise tools, scraping away overgrowth to allow fresh microbial mats to regenerate in a controlled manner. Their paths are not random; they follow capillary grooves and panel edges, creating a perpetual, slow-motion circuit of consumption and renewal. They are the Basin’s groundskeepers. Their population is static. When one dies—and they die rarely, from internal failure, not predation—the body is consumed within hours by others, its minerals and moisture reclaimed. Nothing is wasted.

**The Aerial Density**
The flying swarms are the Basin’s circulatory system, made visible. They are not birds or bats or insects. Their wings are thin, dark membranes stretched over a lattice of lightweight, mineralized struts—more like living graphene than bone. They emit a constant, near-subsonic pulse, a clicking that human ears hear as a faint, pervasive static. This is how they map the Basin’s electromagnetic fields, navigating the perpetual haze of condensation and the invisible emanations of the machinery. They roost in the high galleries and the undersides of structures, hanging in dense, silent clusters. Their function is multi-fold: they feed on aerial spores and particulates, keeping the air column stable; they distribute moisture from the humid lower levels to drier high crevices; and, perhaps most importantly, their bodies accumulate trace static charges, which they discharge upon landing, helping to regulate the subtle electrical equilibrium of the entire space. They move in flows, not flocks, obeying the unseen currents of heat and charge.

**The Processors of the Pools**
In the warm, shallow waters, the pale, eel-like forms are the primary agents of chemical balance. They are blind, their senses tuned to minute changes in water chemistry, temperature, and pressure. Their skin is a porous membrane that absorbs and excretes dissolved minerals. They ingest the flocculent microbial sludge that settles in the pools, but not to digest in a conventional sense. Their long, coiled guts are bioreactors, where specialized symbiotic organisms break down the sludge into base compounds, which the creature then secrete back into the water in a form that feeds the very biofilms the grazers cultivate above. They are the Basin’s water treatment system. Their slow, undulating movement stirs the pools just enough to prevent stagnation but never enough to create a current. They are long-lived, their bodies accumulating mineral deposits over centuries until they become too heavy to move and slowly sink into the sediment, where they are consumed by microbial mats. Their replacement emerges, fully formed, from a specific, recurring cluster of organic material that builds up every decade or so near a particular submerged heat exchanger.

**The Symbiosis of Silence**
There is no conflict in this ecosystem because there is no scarcity. Energy flows from the ambient warmth of the machines and the constant drip of chemical-rich condensation. The only "food" is the biofilm and its derivatives, and the system’s inhabitants are its regulators, not its consumers. The grazers prune the film, the aerial ones control its dispersal and the air quality, the pool processors recycle the nutrients. It is a closed loop of perfect efficiency. They communicate not with sound, but with slight variations in the frequency of their movements, the pheromone trails of the grazers, the static signature of the aerial swarms, the chemical signals in the water—all detectable only to others of their kind, and to the Basin’s own passive sensors.

They are patient. They are tireless. Their life cycle is measured in geological time. They are not waiting for anything. They are not evolving. They are performing their function, just as the conveyors perform theirs, just as the condensation performs its endless, downward crawl. They are alive in the way that a forest is alive, or a coral reef—not as individual entities seeking survival, but as a collective process, a living texture on the face of the machine. You are an anomaly, a disruption in the electromagnetic field, a source of unfamiliar warmth and unaccounted-for moisture. They will flow around you, as the eels part around your legs, and in time, if you stay, the microbial films will begin to grow on your skin, and the grazers may begin to attend to you, not as food, but as just another wet surface in the Basin of Manufacture, one more element in the endless, silent, perfect work of maintenance.
