The inhabitants of this place do not walk, for the floor is too sharp and the air too heavy for such an inefficient motion. They are the **Drifters**, and they move through the bruised twilight as if they are part of the humidity itself.

To call them flesh is a kindness they do not require; to call them machine is a dismissal of their strange, melancholic biology. They are tall, elongated silhouettes wrapped in thermal-absorption weaves that mimic the matte black of the towers. They do not live *in* the buildings so much as they adhere to them, resting in the negative spaces between the force-geometry arches like dormant moths.

Their most distinct feature is the **respiration apparatus**. Because the air is thick with ozone and nanobot saturation, their lungs have atrophied or been replaced. Instead, they wear intricate, cascading filigree—harnesses of spun glass and porous ceramic that sit across their chests and shoulders. These devices do not pump air; they filter the heavy atmosphere, extracting moisture and nutrients directly from the thermal blanket of the city. A soft, rhythmic hiss accompanies their movements, the only sound that competes with the city’s deep, resonant hum.

They are beings of **Industrial Languor**. They have not known war, nor hunger, nor the need to build, for the smart-matter does the building for them. Consequently, their culture is defined by a radical, almost painful slowness. They are the watchers of the decay.

You might see them at the edges of the stagnant reservoirs in the deep chasms. They do not fish or drink; they simply stare into the thick, black water. They are obsessed with the **Vegetation**. While the nanobots in the soil work tirelessly to repair the jagged terrain, the Drifters work to encourage the bioluminescent moss. They act as gardeners of the entropy, pruning the rubbery ferns not to control them, but to help them grow faster over the pristine, impossible geometry of the towers. They want to see the green light conquer the black stone.

They are the **Geometry Weavers**. Perched upon the kilometers-high spires, they manipulate the invisible force fields that hold the city together. They do not speak to one another with voices—sound travels poorly in the wet, static-charged air. Instead, they communicate through the alteration of the city’s lights and the vibration of the glass walkways. A sudden shift in the violet hue of the skyline is a greeting; a tremor in the suspended bridges is a philosophical debate.

They are the melancholic custodians of a world that is already finished. They watch the fog roll in, blurring the lines between the ceiling and the floor, and they wait. They are waiting for the moss to finally swallow the last black tower, returning the technological marvel to the primal, wet silence of the valley.
