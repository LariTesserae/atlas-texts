In the thick, wet dark of the green world, the People are a whisper. They are not a single thing, but a living compromise with the forest, a species shaped by the desperate press of life. They are slender, built for the gaps between giants, for the tangled architecture of vine and root.

They have six limbs, each ending in a long, clever hand. The upper four are for climbing, for bracing, for navigating the vertical world that is their true home. The lower two are for the ground, for the rare, dangerous moments they must descend to the floor. Their fur is a slick, dark green, patterned with the shifting dapples of light and shadow that never truly reach them. It is a perfect camouflage, not for hiding still, but for moving. Their movement is a fluid, silent thing, a continuous negotiation with the terrain. They do not walk; they flow over roots, through fronds, up the moss-slick trunks.

Their faces are dominated by large, dark eyes, pupils wide to drink what little light there is. Their ears are cupped and mobile, constantly swiveling to parse the deafening wall of noise, to pick out the specific rustle that is a predator, the specific crack that is a falling tree, the specific chitter that is food. They do not have a language of words in the human sense. Their communication is a tapestry of sound and gesture: soft, clicking calls that mimic the insects, a complex sign language of the hands that is faster than thought, and a low, resonant hum that they can feel through the soles of their feet and the bark of a shared tree. To speak is to listen, to touch, to see.

They are of the forest, but they are not at peace with it. Peace is not a concept this place understands. They are in a state of constant, vigilant negotiation. They are not builders of shelters. A home is a temporary space, a nest of woven fronds in the crown of a particularly dense tree, used for a season and then abandoned to the fungi and beetles. They are nomads within a territory that is itself always changing. A fallen giant is not a tragedy; it is an event, a shift in the map, a sudden feast for the column-beetles they follow, a new scar of light to be watched.

Their tools are extensions of this negotiation. The fire-hardened staff is not a weapon of war, but a tool for probing the dark hollows of rot for grubs, for testing the strength of a vine-bridge, for persuading a snake from a favored path. The sharpened stones are for scraping bark, for cracking the shells of the iridescent beetles, for cutting the tough vines to make their simple cordages. They do not seek to conquer this world. They seek to read it, to anticipate its violence, and to slip through the gaps it leaves in its furious creation. They are students of decay, knowing which fungal bloom will follow which rot, which vine will choke which trunk.

Their life is a series of moments. The moment to gather the glowing fungus that lights their night-huddles. The moment to follow the millipede swarms that dismantle the fallen wood. The moment to lie utterly still, so still they seem like a knot of moss on a branch, as a slow, serpentine wall of scale and muscle glides past below, a living river of predator. They are not the masters of this place. They are its most intimate observers, its most cautious inhabitants. They are the quiet thought in the back of the forest’s chaotic mind, a fleeting intelligence that knows, with every fiber of its being, that it is alive only because it has learned to become a little more like the green, damp, and desperate world that birthed it.
