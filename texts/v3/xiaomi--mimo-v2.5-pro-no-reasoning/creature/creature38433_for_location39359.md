The things that live here are not visitors, nor natives in the way one might imagine. They are more like functions. Like the weeping of the walls or the perpetual hum of heat, they are part of the system's expression.

You see them first as the lizards. Dozens of kinds, yes, but not as you know them. Their scales are not merely the color of ochre and rust; they *are* ochre and rust, flaking patterns of the porous building-stuff onto their hides. They do not bask to warm themselves; they are already warm. They bask to *be*, to enact the ancient, looping program of basking. A particular one on a particular ledge will perform its push-ups at intervals you cannot discern—a tiny, silent piston in the grand machine. Their eyes are dull, not with stupidity, but with a complete absence of curiosity. They are watching for nothing. They are simply the watching.

Then there are the Weavers. You might mistake them for larger, more deliberate lizards, but their limbs are longer, tipped with fused, calcite digits. They move with a glacial, profound purpose, along the ridges of the domed buildings. They do not build. They *tend*. A Weaver will stop before a section of wall where a vine’s grip has loosened, or where a new fissure has appeared (though nothing here is new). It will press its flat, shovel-like snout to the spot, and from its nostrils, a viscous, mucus-like secretion will bead. This substance, once dry, is indistinguishable from the original material. They are the repairmen. Their work is invisible, because to not repair would be to break the stasis, and that is unthinkable. Their cycles are long, measured in the slow, constant trickling of water.

In the warm, brown shallows, alongside the pale eels and bone-shell crabs, move the Archers. They resemble water-striders, but they are the size of a child’s hand, and their legs are articulated with the same porous, weeping substance. They tread upon the meniscus of the water with impossible delicacy, leaving no wake. They do not hunt the insects; the translucent, drifting moths and dragonflies land upon them, willingly, and dissolve into a fine, pollen-like dust that the Archer absorbs through its legs. It is not predation. It is a scheduled transfer. The insects are not born, but appear, grown from the same strange, reality-softening process that built the bone. They are simply a mobile nutrient delivery system for the Archer, which is a mobile cleaning system for the water's surface, which is a component of the evaporation cycle.

The largest are the Dwellers. You might see one in the archway of a building, and think it a part of the architecture—a bulbous, weeping knot of the wall-stuff. Then a section of it, slowly, over hours, will resolve into a closed eyelid. A mouth, a seamless slit, will open to release a long, slow breath of air that smells of damp stone and ozone. They do not move. They are the buildings' hearts, perhaps, or their brains, processing the constant input of warmth, wetness, and light into the pattern of the unchanging day. A Dweller has not shifted its position in a decade. It will not shift in a century. It is dreaming the place into continued existence, its thoughts as slow and mineral as the seeping of the walls.

Nothing here is frantic. Nothing is afraid. The abundant fauna does not compete; it *arranges* itself. A crab will click its way along a causeway edge, metronome-like. It will meet another crab. They will touch antennae of polished bone and adjust their paths, one going slightly left, one slightly right, their movements altering the sound of their leg-clicks into a new, equally repetitive rhythm for a time. It is not communication. It is a minor, local adjustment of the symphony.

They are all aware of you, in the way a thermostat is aware of temperature. You are a foreign object in the terrarium. A Dwellers' eye may track you, moving a fraction of a millimeter over an hour. The Weavers will ignore you, unless you were to damage the fabric of a wall, in which case they would slowly, inexorably approach and begin to repair the damage around you, treating you as just another crack to be filled. The lizards will blink, one after another, in a wave that travels through the cluster, a reflex to your disruption of the light.

But you are temporary. A mote of chaos in their perfect, weeping order. They are the living expression of the place's will to remain itself. They are the static made flesh and scale and bone. They are not the inheritors of the builders; they are the builders, poured into a new and eternal form, their purpose now not to create, but simply to *maintain the beautiful, humid, sun-bleached fact of what they have already made.*

They live here. They *are* here. And when you are gone, their quiet, looping, perfect routines will fill in the space you occupied, seamlessly, as if you had never been. The weeping will continue. The light will hold. The stone will breathe.
