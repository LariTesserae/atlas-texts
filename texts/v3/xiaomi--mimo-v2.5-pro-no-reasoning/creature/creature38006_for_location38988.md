They are called the **Sinter-Walkers**, though they do not call themselves that. They call themselves the **Uplifted**, the **Bone-Tenders**, the **Quiet Ones**. They are not so much a people as a process, a necessary component of the Sinter’s slow, crystal logic.

They move with the deliberate heft of creatures who have never known warmth. Their bodies are broad and low-slung, packed with dense muscle beneath layers of the local fauna—sleek, six-legged pelts that shed the light, and articulated plates of cured insect chitin that click softly with each step. Their own skin is thick, with a faint, blue-grey pallor, as if the cold has leached into their very flesh. Their faces are broad, with deep-set eyes protected by bony ridges and nictitating membranes that shimmer with oil-film iridescence. They have little visible hair, but fine, sensory whiskers trace their jaws and brows, tasting the air for changes in pressure and moisture.

They do not build. They **sculpt** and **temper**. Their tools are the bones of the dead world—polished femur-hammers, scrapers of serrated mandible, drills tipped with fossilized teeth. With these, they do not conquer the ice; they dialogue with it. They chip channels for the seeping water, guiding it to form stable pillars rather than chaotic floods. They etch patterns into the great ribs, not for art, but to relieve stress fractures, singing low, resonant songs that match the hum of the light and the groan of the bone. Their music is a percussion of chisel on stone, a bass drone in the throat, all to soothe the architecture.

Their language is one of gesture and subtle luminescence. They have patches of skin on their throats and inner arms that can flush with cold blue or pale amber, pulsing in complex, silent rhythms. A slow pulse for warning, a rapid flash for discovery, a steady glow for communal labor. Their spoken tongue is a series of low clicks and resonant vowels that travel well through the dense, humming air, used for coordination and sometimes, in the deepest shadows of their bone-hollows, for stories of the Great Skeleton and the Eternal Light.

They are farmers, but their crop is the Lichen. They tend the pale fungal crust with reverence, scraping excess growth from critical structural points, spreading spores to new foundations, and harvesting the thickest patches. The Lichen is their food, their medicine, and their light-source. They eat it raw, ferment it in stone vats, and burn dried flakes in shallow lamps that give off a cold, phosphorescent glow, a tiny rebellion against the overwhelming whiteness.

They live in the interstices, in the hollows of the ribs, in terraces carved not from stone but from the ice itself, which they insulate with packed lichen and chitin-shingles. Their settlements are clusters of these glowing, carved cavities, connected by ice-tunnels and guarded by watchposts on the highest spurs of bone. They share the Sinter utterly with the skittering, furred, and glimmering fauna, viewing them not as pests or prey, but as fellow expressions of the cold life. They hunt selectively, using bone-snares and mandible-blades, honoring the kill with a ritual that returns the bones to the walls, reinforcing the whole.

They are patient beyond human measure. A project to brace a shifting rib may take three lifetimes. They do not fear the glacial violence; they anticipate it, read the signs in the creak of the ice and the patter of the Light. They are not the masters of the Sinter. They are its caretakers, its conscious nerve-endings, moving slowly through the eternal, brilliant wound, trying to keep the beautiful, terrifying precision from shattering everything into silent, frozen dust.
