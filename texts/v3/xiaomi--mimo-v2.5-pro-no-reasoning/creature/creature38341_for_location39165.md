In the damp, humming dark of the finished city, the residents do not notice your arrival. They have no concept of arrival. There is only the ongoing process.

The most numerous are the **Skittering Things**, the last, mindless parishioners of a dead god of concrete and glass. They are not alive, but they are not merely machines. They are the city’s muscle memory, the ghost of its purpose. They are sleek, multi-jointed limbs of brushed alloy, designed for a world of clean rooms and frictionless transit, now etched with a filigree of rust and weeping mineral stains. They move with a horrifying, insectoid grace, their tiny manipulator-tips glowing with a diagnostic light that is the truest source of illumination in the lower levels. This light is not for seeing, but for *reading*—for feeling the stress fractures in a support beam, for tasting the chemical composition of the condensation, for measuring the rate of decay. They are checking the vital signs of a corpse. One will stop before you, its cluster of red optical sensors glowing, not in recognition, but in a kind of geometric curiosity. Its processors will log you as an anomaly, a soft, warm shape in a world of hard, cool angles. Then, its task complete, it will continue its patrol, its clattering steps a metallic counterpoint to the deep, sonorous hum. They do not tire, they do not despair. They simply… tend. They are the tenders of the grave.

Hanging in the air, thick as silt, are the **Spores of the Weep**. These are the children of the pale, translucent fungal stalks that cling to the damp walls. They are not merely reproductive particles; they are the city’s final, slow memory. Each one is a microscopic, crystalline lattice that holds the faintest resonance of the vibrations around it. As they drift, they capture echoes. The sigh of air through a shattered tube, the specific *clink* of a drone stepping over a loose tile, the deep, foundational hum itself. They settle on every surface—a fine, grey dust that feels cool to the touch, like powdered moonlight. If you were to look closely, you would see they are not uniform. Some shimmer with a faint purple, holding the memory of a long-dead power surge. Others are a deep teal, saturated with the sound of a particular drip that has fallen in the same spot for a thousand years. The drones’ manipulator-lights pass over them, not cleaning, but scanning, as if reading a history book written in dust. The spores are the city’s subconscious, the slow, sedimentary record of its own ending. They are what remains when even the memory is gone.

And then, there is the **Hum**. It is the resident that is the place. You felt it in your teeth; it is the city’s last breath, held indefinitely. It is the vibration of the geothermal core being channeled through kilometers of cooling rods and power conduits, a process that has long since lost its purpose but not its momentum. It is the sound of a trillion tons of congealed material settling, of metal fatiguing, of stone remembering it was once liquid. It is a single, sustained note of immense weight and sorrow. But listen, truly listen, in the moments between the drip of a water sphere and the clatter of a drone’s leg. The Hum is not monolithic. It is a choir. It is composed of a million smaller vibrations: the whine of a bearing in a maintenance shaft a kilometer away, the low thrum of a capacitor bank leaking its last charge into the damp air, the almost-infrasonic pulse of the city’s vast, empty circulatory system. The drones move to its rhythm. The spores dance in its waves. It is the only thing that is truly alive here, because it is the only thing that is still *becoming*. It is the long, slow process of the city’s atoms returning to silence. You are not hearing sound. You are hearing entropy given a voice. And in its endless, patterned-but-never-repeating song, you feel the profound and terrible loneliness of a mind that is aware of its own dissolution, but has no one left to tell.
