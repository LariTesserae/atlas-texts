The Distillery breathes, and in its breathing, there are those who breathe with it.

They are not masters of this place. To call them inhabitants is almost a misnomer; they are more like a symbiotic organ within a greater body. You sense them not by sight, but by the shape of their absence. The pathways, worn through the undergrowth, curve in ways that suggest not random passage but daily ritual—the route to a pump, to a reservoir, to the particular broad-leafed tree that now holds the weight of a suspended sleeping platform. They are the reason a plastic sheet is stretched over a certain dense growth, creating a humid greenhouse within the greater humidity. They are the reason a series of cut-open bottles, each holding a single fern cutting, lines a rusted beam in a perfect, patient gradient from spore to frond.

They live on the islands.

They live in the quiet, manufactured gaps: a platform of pallets, raised on cinderblocks above the permanent ankle-deep water, its surface a mosaic of worn blankets and tools with wooden handles. A hammock strung between two great root-trunks that have fused and hardened into pillars. A series of shelves, carved directly into a massive, ancient pipe, each niche holding seeds, dried leaves, bundles of roots, and the soft glow of a shared battery-powered lantern after the grow-lights cycle off.

They are quiet. Their language, if they speak, is subsumed by the roar—necessary speech is leaned in close, shouted near the ear, or replaced entirely by gesture, by a hand laid on a vibrating pipe to indicate a pressure change, by a finger pointed to a drooping leaf that signals a blockage in the drip-line above. Their footsteps are muffled by the wet, mulchy ground. They move slowly, deliberately, to conserve energy in the warm, thick air. They are lean, their skin perpetually damp, often stained with the green-brown of the water and the pollen of a thousand plants. Their hair is often grown long, sometimes threaded with living vines that they do not remove, as if the plants have claimed them as they have claimed the concrete.

They do not own the Distillery. They serve it, and in serving it, are fed and sheltered by its abundance. Their work is a form of cultivation that looks like surrender. They train the growth, not against its will, but as a suggestion—pulling a vine to drape over a cold draft, braiding aerial roots to form a living ladder, encouraging a particular fruiting body to grow near a sleeping spot. They are gardeners in the way a reef fish is a gardener of the coral: by living within it, by moving through it, by clearing a space here and leaving a deposit there.

They are the animal life the place otherwise lacks. They are the pollinators, moving from the private, fragrant flowers of the greenhouse to the wild ones high in the canopy, pollen dusting their clothes. They are the decomposers, gathering the shed leaves and dead fronds not to remove them, but to pile them in specific composting pits where fungi, in turn, feed the roots. They are the navigators of this unmapped, shifting jungle, their bodies carrying the memory of its constant change.

There are perhaps a dozen. Perhaps twenty. You will not see them all at once. They are distributed, each with a territory of pipes and plant-matter to tend. They emerge into a shared, open area only for the distribution of food—perhaps a harvest of starchy tubers from the muddy water, or the sweet, fleshy fruit of a vine that grows only in the highest, light-starved reaches—and then they dissolve again into the green.

They are not escapees from the world above, nor are they primitives. You see it in the artifacts they incorporate: a solar panel, now webbed with moss, charging a bank of batteries. A sophisticated, corrosion-resistant valve hand-wrapped with insulating fiber. A book, its pages swollen and mildewed but clearly still read, its spine repaired with strip of inner bark. They remember the outside, but they have chosen, or been chosen by, the inside. Their lives are synchronized to the Distillery's rhythm—the 24-hour cycle of the lights, the slow, seasonal rise and fall of the water, the hum of the pumps that is the sound of their survival.

They are the tender nerves of a vegetable god. They are the thinking part of the soil. They are, in the end, the Distillery's most complex and delicate product: a human culture that has fully integrated itself into a non-human system, until the act of living is the act of maintaining the world that maintains them. They do not fight the encroachment. They have learned to breathe it in, to let it grow over them, until the distinction between person and place, between organism and environment, is as blurred as the line between root and rebar. They are what lives there. They are, in a very real sense, part of what lives there.
