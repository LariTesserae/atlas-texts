What you have found is the city of Vael, a wound in the world’s skin that has learned to fester with a kind of desperate purpose. It is not so much a home as it is a frantic negotiation with existence itself. And the things that live here are not so much residents as they are components in a vast, grim machine built to endure the impossible.

The people of Vael are not born; they are forged. They are the Vaelborn, and their bodies tell the story of their city. Their skin is a cartographer’s map of their home, a pale, translucent grey etched with faint, dark veins that mirror the patterns of the city’s pipes and girders. Their eyes are adapted to the gloom, the pupils vast and dark, but they do not see light so much as they see its absence. They are masters of shadow and nuance, able to read the minute fraying of a step’s edge or the desynchronization of a shadow and know that a shift in the mountain’s fabric is coming. They move with a deliberate slowness that outsiders mistake for lethargy, but is in fact a form of hyper-awareness. To rush is to be torn by a reality that is not yet settled. Their patience is not a virtue; it is a vital organ.

Their industry is not one of creation, but of coercion. They do not build; they *bind*. The great engines that groan in the mountain’s heart do not power dynamos or pistons in a conventional sense. They generate a constant, low-frequency resonance, a hum that travels through the stone and the pipes, serving as a kind of sonic mortar. It is this hum that holds the fleeting, unformed reality of the mountain in a state of tense stasis. The city’s scaffolding is not just for climbing; it is a lattice of conductive metal, humming with the same frequency, a cage to contain the nascent chaos. The workers in the forges do not smith steel; they temper reality-forged alloys, hammering out temporal dissonances and welding causal fractures shut with carefully focused sound. They are not engineers; they are reality’s scab-pickers, constantly re-knitting the fabric of their world.

They cultivate the luminescent fungi not just for light, but for stability. The mycelial networks of these pale green growths weave through the rock, drawing nourishment from the mountain’s unstable geology. In return, they emit a faint, constant radiation that has a narcotic, slowing effect on local time. To live in a building laced with this fungus is to exist in a pocket of slightly thicker, more syrupy seconds. It is a buffer against the worst of the shimmers. The eyeless lizards, which they call *twitchers*, are their early-warning system. Sensitive to the subtle vibrations and temporal eddies, a colony of lizards falling silent or scurrying in unison can signal a coming reality-slip minutes before it is perceptible to Vael-kin. The great web-spinning spiders, which they name *loom-weavers*, are not pests. Their silk, spun from proteins harvested from the city’s strange fungi, has an innate property of temporal cohesion. It is used to reinforce the city’s most critical structures—the great cisterns, the resonance engines, the lift-chains—acting as a biological caulk against the void.

The Vaelborn do not have families in the way of other peoples. They have work-crews, guilds of shared function bound by the necessity of their task. A resonance-tuner, a fungus-tender, a scaffold-welder—these are not just professions, but identities, castes, and bloodlines. They speak in a clipped, efficient argot filled with technical jargon borrowed from their industry. There is little art, save for the intricate, geometric patterns they carve into their tools and doorways—wards and harmonics designed to keep their immediate space from unraveling. Their children are not raised, but apprenticed from the moment they can walk, taught to read the shivers in the air and to move without disturbing the fragile peace.

They are a people defined by the struggle. Their religion, if it can be called that, is a form of pragmatic fatalism. They do not worship gods, but *anchors*—the resonance engines, the oldest fungal colonies, the sheer, unyielding basalt of the mountain’s core. Their great fear is not death, but *unraveling*—a state where a body or a mind can no longer maintain its cohesion with the hum of the city and is lost to the shimmering chaos. Their greatest hope is not heaven, but *settling*—the belief that one day, through their endless, patient labor, the mountain will finish being born, and their work will be done. Reality will become solid. The hum can cease. But they do not expect to see this day. They are the midwives to a geological event that will last a millennium. Their purpose is to hold the world together, one hammer-fall at a time, in the damp, humming, and deeply uncertain heart of a mountain that has not yet decided what it wants to be.
