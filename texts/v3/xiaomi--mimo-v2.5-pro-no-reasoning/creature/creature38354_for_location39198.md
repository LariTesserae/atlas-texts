# What Lives in the Mineral Threshold

It does not arrive. It is not the kind of thing that arrives anywhere. It has always been here, or always *becoming* here — which in the grammar of this place is the same word.

You might try to name it by its parts. The insects — and they are the closest thing to a visible will — are small, dark, carapaced in a chitin so fine-grained it looks likeite, likeite made living. No single one means anything. They are a millimeter. They are a centimeter. They carry a chip of basalt the size of their own head across a vertical surface and deposit it at the edge of a growing wall and turn back for another. Their mandibles are translucent. Their legs move in a rhythm that has no syncopation, no rest, no variation — not mechanical, but the way water moves over stone, finding the path of least resistance and never deviating once found. They do not communicate in any sense you could prove. They do not need to. They are all doing the same thing at all times. They are building.

But to say "they are building" is to use a word that belongs to your world. In their world, the distinction between building and growing and being has not yet been invented. The nest-hemisphere they are adding to is made of stone and resin and fungal fiber and the shed casings of larvae and the calcium-ite walls of their own dead. It is not a structure *for* them. It *is* them. A colony of forty thousand insects and their constructions are a single entity the way your body is a single entity — the cells do not know they are cells. They do not need to know.

This entity — this thing that lives here — has a shape, though you would need to see it from above and from outside to know that. From inside it looks like the world. The shape is roughly ovoid, conforming to the caldera's floor, built upward in terraced tiers that climb the chimneys and spill across the terraces and bridge the arches in continuous sheets of nest-material and moss and lichen and silk. From above — if a hawk circled overhead and looked down through the rectangle of open sky — it would look like a living crust, dark and textured, breathing with such slowness that the word *breathing* fails. Expanding. That is the better word. It expands in every direction at once, adding a millimeter here, a centimeter there, a new chamber fused into the face of a growing chimney, a new sheet of lichen permitted to colonize a horizontal surface that has been — over years — gently flattened by the passage of small bodies.

The spiders are part of it. They are not parasites, not guests, not predators exactly. They are the perimeter. Their webs, strung in precise geometries across the chimneys at the caldera's rim, catch the insects that come in from the plateau — small flies, winged things drawn by the warmth. These captives are not eaten by the webs. They die, and they dry, and their bodies are slowly enveloped by the fungal filaments that colonize every silk strand, and their nutrients are drawn downward into the network, into the body of the caldera. The spiders sit at the edges of this web-work and eat what they catch directly — the larger moths, the beetles that blunder in. But the smaller things, the vast majority, they give to the filaments. Or the filaments take them. The distinction does not matter. The system is not negotiated. It is emergent. It has no center and no intention and no memory, and yet it has been doing the same thing — or rather, something that becomes increasingly the same thing — for two hundred years.

The succulents are its skin. Vertical, translucent, oozing their gel — a substance that is not water but holds water inside it, that keeps the tissues turgid against the desert air — they cling to the western wall in a dense patchwork, and their gel runs down the faces of the chimneys in slow threads that collect in crevices where mites gather to drink. The mites are its microbiome. The beetles in the lichen sheets are its grazers, keeping the fungal surface from overgrowing, preventing it from choking the mosses. The larvae curled in their amber cases in the warm crevices of the terraces are its reserves — biomass in storage, developing on timescales of months and years, emerging as adults into a world that will barely have changed since they were eggs.

And the fungal network — that is its nerve. Not nervous in the way you understand nerves. There are no signals, no decisions, no awareness. But the filaments connect every living surface to every other living surface. A moss cushion dies in a shaded zone and its nutrients travel, filament by filament, across six meters of open air, to feed a lichen on a sunlit chimney. A spider's abandoned web, stiff with age, is colonized by fungal threads that penetrate the silk and then the nest-resin and then the mineral itself, binding biological to geological to chemical in a single strand. The network does not think. It does not need to. It is the medium through which the caldera becomes one thing instead of many. It is the reason that the words *habitat* and *organism* begin to mean the same thing here.

---

What lives here is a city that does not know it is a city. Or perhaps it is an organism that does not know it is an organism. The insect-colonies build and the spiders hunt and the mosses grow and the succulents ooze and the beetles graze and the mites scatter and the larvae sleep, and all of it is joined by the gossamer threads of the mycelium into a single loose fabric that hangs in the bowl of the caldera like a living web caught in a cup made of stone and heat.

It is ancient and infant both. Two hundred years — nothing in the language of stone, barely anything in the language of moss. The chimneys are still sharp. The nest-materials are still expanding. The fungal network has not yet reached the caldera's full extent. There are raw zones — the northeast, near the freshest vent — where the first hemispheres are just appearing on newly deposited mineral, where the lichen has not yet arrived, where the mosses are still years away. These are the frontiers. The city-organism is colonizing them, slowly, without hurry, without strategy, by the simple accumulation of small lives lived in warm places.

It has no enemy. Nothing here preys destructively on anything else. The beetles eat fungal filaments and are eaten by mites that are too small to significantly diminish them. The spiders catch what falls into their webs and do not overhunt because the web-geometry limits their take to what arrives from outside. The insects outpace all predation by sheer reproductive fecundity — a queen in the deepest chamber of the tallest nest-compound lays eggs continuously, and the colony grows at a rate that absorbs every loss. Decay happens — a moss cushion dies, a lichen sheet thins, a nest-chamber cracks and is abandoned — but it happens slower than growth, and the fungal network scavenges every death so thoroughly that nothing is wasted, nothing is lost, everything is converted into filament and then into moss and then into structure.

It is, in its way, the most patient thing alive. Not patient the way you are patient — enduring discomfort while waiting for change. Patient the way a crystal is patient: existing in the state of growth without experiencing the passage of time. The insects do not hurry because they have no concept of hurry. The mosses do not compete because there is enough light and warmth for all. The spiders do not scheme because their geometry is already complete. The whole system runs on a fuel so slow-burning that it will last longer than your species, longer than the memory of your species, perhaps longer than the plateau itself.

---

If you sat in the plaza — in the center, where the fungal canopy catches the amber light and holds it in a haze of particulate warmth — the insects would flow around you. Not avoiding you. Not investigating you. Simply incorporating you into their landscape the way a stream incorporates a stone: flowing around, over, past. They would not bite. They would not sting. They would build on your boots if you sat still long enough, and the moss would find the creases of your clothing, and the filaments would come — given days, given weeks — to connect you to the network, to make you part of the fabric.

You might mistake this for indifference. It is not. Indifference is a decision. This is something prior to decision: the condition of a system so complete in itself that the concept of *other* has not arisen. You are not other. You are not same. You are a surface, and surfaces are what this place knows. It will colonize you. It will use you. It will, in its slow and voiceless and chemical way, build you into itself — not because it wants to, but because building is what it does, and it has never encountered a surface it did not eventually, patiently, inexhaustibly, claim.

This is what lives here. Not an insect. Not a colony. Not a garden. A *process* — a living, growing, mineral-veined, filament-threaded, silent, warm, amber-lit process that has been unfolding since the earth buckled and the heat came up and the first chimney grew its first millimeter, and that will continue unfolding long after you have stood and brushed the dust from your clothes and climbed back up the raw black walls to the indifferent plateau above.

It will not miss you. It does not know how. But it will remember you — in the way that a wall remembers a brick, in the way that a nest remembers a chip of stone. You will have been here. You will have been, for a moment, part of it. And the filaments will have found you, even in that moment, and they will remember you in the only language this place speaks: the language of connection, slow and gossamer, stretching from where you sat to where the moss grows thick on the chimney to the west, a thread so thin you would never see it, a thread so patient it could outlast the stone itself.
