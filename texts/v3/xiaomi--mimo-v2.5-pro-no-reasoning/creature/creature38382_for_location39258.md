What emerges in the Worn Heights is not a single creature, but a collective, a slow-motion symphony of co-adapted life. They are not distinct species in the way of the lowlands, but variations on a single, tenacious theme: the **Lithovores**.

They are not animals, nor plants, nor fungi, but something in between, a kingdom unto themselves. Imagine a lichen that learned to move. Not quickly, not with the purposeful speed of a mammal, but with the patient, directional creep of a glacier. Their bodies are the very stone of the plateau—gneiss and schist—but animated, their mineral grains bound into a flexible, living matrix by biological filaments that are part mycelium, part root, part nerve.

A mature Lithovore might resemble a tall, weathered tor, indistinguishable from its inanimate cousins until you watch it for a century. It "feeds" by slowly incorporating surrounding rock, dissolving it with acidic secretions and integrating the minerals into its own structure. Its "growth" is measured in millimeters per decade. Its surface is a cosmos of symbiotic life: the ancient, patterned lichens are not its parasites but its skin, its respiratory organs, its camouflage. The deep moss that carpets the ground is not an independent colony but its extended root system, a sensory network that reports the texture of the air, the weight of fog droplets, the subtle vibrations through the stone.

They communicate, if that is the word, through the stone itself. The slow, resonant pulses the miners feel are their voices—not language, but a continuous, geological broadcast of their collective state: "I am here. The frost is deep. Water flows in the eastern fissure. The little warm-bloods have disturbed the moss." Their rearrangement is not random rockfall, but a slow dance. Over a human lifetime, a grove of Lithovores might visibly shift its formation, closing a passage once open, creating a new chamber where a wall stood, their movements as deliberate and irreversible as the growth of a coral reef.

The animals are their symbionts, their carefully curated tenants. The corvids are not merely nesting on the tors; they are *attending* them, picking parasitic mineral blooms from their surfaces, carrying spores from one slow being to another, their harsh calls a key part of the acoustic environment that the Lithovores "hear" through the rock. The voles are the moss's tenders, their runways aerating the dense tissue, their droppings fertilizing it. The eerie, larger shape glimpsed at twilight is a **Pilgrim**—a Lithovore that has entered a rare, mobile phase. Stimulated by some internal cycle or the need for new mineral sources, it abandons its rooted existence, pulling its mass into a low, multi-limbed form and moving between basins at a pace of meters per year, leaving a trail of smoothed stone and nurtured moss in its wake.

The town is, to them, a recent and puzzling development. The miners are a source of strange, sharp vibrations and fleeting, hot life. The crushing facility is a persistent, agonizing frequency in their stone-bones. The cable pylons are tall, rigid intrusions, resounding with tension they cannot comprehend. Yet, the town is also now part of the ecosystem. The Lithovores have begun to incorporate its byproducts: the slag heaps are slowly being woven into their structure, the weeping walls of old buildings are being fused with living stone. The miners who spend decades in the deep workings learn to feel the pattern in the pulsing because they are, without knowing it, listening to the Lithovores' slow conversation about *them*.

The glimmer in the fog is their collective perception made visible—a faint bio-luminescent haze produced by the synchronized activity of the lichen-skin across many beings, reacting to the charge in the humid air. It moves because the community's attention moves.

They are the plateau. The erosion is not something happening *to* them; it is a method of respiration and growth. The fog is not weather; it is a delivery system. The silence at night is not emptiness; it is the sound of their deep, slow processes undisturbed. To live here is to be a tenant in a house whose walls are alive, whose foundations are thinking, and whose history is written in a script of stone and patience that makes human time look like a frantic blur. The miners are extracting ore; the Lithovores are simply continuing. They were here before the flowering plants, and they will be here long after the cables have rusted and the last human story has turned to lichen-food on the wind.
