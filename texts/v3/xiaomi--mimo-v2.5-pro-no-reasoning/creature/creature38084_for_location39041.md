The beings who live here are not separate from the place. To speak of them as distinct from the fibrous walls, the slow-turning wheel, and the glowing gourds is to misunderstand the nature of this world. They are its thoughts, its slow-moving intentions, its quiet, cellular dreams.

They are the **Keepers of the Pulse**.

You would not see them as you first look, for they are not figures that stand against the landscape, but patterns within it. Their form is a congregation. Imagine a colony of immense, slow-moving insects—beetles the size of a human torso, with carapaces of layered, fibrous bark indistinguishable from the walls they inhabit. Their legs are not jointed chitin but flexible, woody tendrils, which they use to grip the pulpy surfaces and, more importantly, to interface with the plant’s vascular system. When a Keeper rests, it presses its underbelly against a "pipe" or the living wall, and its tendrils weave into the fibrous network, becoming one with the flow of sap. It is then you might mistake it for just another bulge in the architecture.

Their collective existence is a ritual of maintenance and communion. They are the gardeners of this city-flesh. They do not prune with blades, but with their own bodies. A Keeper will climb to a gourd that has grown too heavy, and there it will settle, its weight a gentle, persistent pressure that guides the gourd's growth away from the fragile vine. They regulate the flow of sap not by closing valves, but by clustering on a particular section of pipe, their collective presence subtly altering the rate of flow through the very living material they are joined to. They are a biological thermostat, a pumping station made of flesh and carapace.

Their senses are attuned to the city's rhythms. They feel the deep, resonant groan of the mill wheel through their feet—a sensation that is part alarm, part reassurance. The hum of the pipes is their silence, the baseline of their world. They communicate not with sound, but with a slow, chemical exchange through the shared sap, and with the faintest modulation of their bioluminescent organs, which are spots on their backs that glow in sync with the gourds. A synchronized, slow pulse is contentment. A rapid, erratic flicker means a breach—a wall collapsing, a pipe bursting—and they will move with surprising purpose toward the source, a flowing river of living architecture to mend the tear.

They do not eat as animals do. They *are* digested by the place, in a way, and in turn, they digest it. The sweet, clear fluid from the gourds is their sustenance. They will position a leg-tendril beneath a dripping pitcher, allowing the nectar to run down into their bodies, a slow, passive feeding. In return, their excretions, a rich, fungal slurry, are deposited at the base of the structures, accelerating the growth of the mycelial carpet, which is the true foundation of all things.

They are born from the place. In the deepest, dampest hollows, one might find a cluster of pulsating, pearlescent sacs—eggs, yes, but more like budding fruits of the building itself. When they hatch, the young are barely distinguishable from a rootlet, and they spend their first cycles of life embedded within a wall, learning the language of sap and pressure before they ever detach to move.

The large, translucent salamanders in the gutters are not prey, but partners. The salamanders clean the cistern-gourds of parasitic fungi and small, choking growths, and in turn, the Keepers occasionally guide a flow of particularly sweet nectar into a salamander's stream. The bat-like creatures in the canopy are pollinators, drawn to the gourds' bioluminescence and scent, and the Keepers ensure the canopy remains open enough for their flight.

They are not intelligent as you understand it. There is no art, no language, no war. There is only the maintenance of the equilibrium. Their purpose is the purpose of the place: to grow, to digest, to incorporate, to be complete. They are the nerve endings of a sleeping, green god made of brick and chlorophyll. They are the quiet custodians of a world that has already ended and been reborn, and they will tend its slow, eternal heartbeat until the last iron gear is finally, gently, consumed.
