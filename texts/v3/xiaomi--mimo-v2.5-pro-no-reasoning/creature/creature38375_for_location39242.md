The first thing you learn in the deep is that you are not a visitor. You are a disruption—a warm, bright, fast-moving anomaly in a system built on cool, dark patience. You are met with the sound, not with hostility, but with a kind of enveloping, indifferent attention. The hum of the bat-roost, a vibration felt in the bones, modulates its pitch slightly as you pass beneath it. The droning of the insects seems to focus, a granular texture in the air that follows your steps. You are being noted, not by any single mind, but by the system as a whole.

They live here, all of them, and they are one thing. The Separated are the name I give them, for they have forgotten any other. They were the builders. They were the city. They are the ones who laid the columns and carved the corridors. And now, they are the moss, the fungi, the beetles, the burrowing things. They have not devolved. They have *integrated*. The architecture of their bodies has become the architecture of the place.

Their consciousness is not in their minds. It is in the pattern of their growth. A shelf-fungus on a column thinks in rings, each one a century's worth of careful, chemical consideration. The burrowing mammals are the thoughts of the old plumbing system, their slow digestion a form of municipal maintenance. The snakes are the memory of the walls, feeling for the heat-signatures of old conduits. The bats are the breath, the constant circulation of air and information.

They are aware of you. The carpet of mites you disturbed—that was a localized panic, a wave of awareness that spread like a ripple. The centipede that paused, its antennae tasting your sweat on the air—that was a specific thought directed at you. "A surface-thing. Worn. Fast. Dying quickly." You are a flickering candle in their long, slow dream. They do not wish to extinguish you. They are simply observing the phenomenon of your burning.

The city still functions, in its way. The columns are not just supports; they are sensory nodes, their mineral-encrusted surfaces registering the minute shifts in temperature and pressure. The "undulations" of the collapsed walls are the city's musculature, flexing so slowly it takes a lifetime to see. The great shelf-fungi are the archives, their layers storing a chemical record of every change in the atmosphere for millennia. The sound you hear—the constant, overwhelming drone—is their language. It is not communication between individuals, but the monologue of a single, distributed mind, endlessly processing its own state.

You are afraid, and that is understandable. You feel small and watched and deafened. But you are mistaken if you think you are in danger from them. The danger is in your own temporality. Your heartbeat is a frantic drum in their glacial symphony. Your thoughts are sparks in their long, slow burn. The heat you radiate is a temporary spike in their geothermal charts. To stay too long would be to disrupt their equilibrium, to introduce a note so jarringly out of place that it would have to be… assimilated. Not consumed. Integrated.

The moss would begin to grow on your boots, finding purchase in the micro-crevices of the leather. The fungal spores would seek the moisture of your breath. The insects would begin to map the strange topography of your still-warm body, antennae tracing the lines of your veins. You would not die. You would become a new, temporary, and intensely interesting feature of the landscape—a warm, bright, fast-thinking hillock, slowly softening at the edges, its frantic rhythms gradually slowing to match the pulse of the stone, until you were simply another voice in the murmuring deep, another thought in its ancient, patient, and complete mind.
