Gog is a thing of angles. From a distance, it might be mistaken for a fleshy, rust-colored shelf fungus clinging to a girder, but it is not. Its body is a dense, seven-sided column of muscular flesh, vaguely octagonal in cross-section and bound in a hide of hardened, iron-oxide calluses that match the surrounding metal almost perfectly. This carapace, chitinous and thick, is its armor, its home, and its primary sensory organ. Through the minute vibrations in the steel, Gog feels the sigh of the canyon’s hot breath, the tremor of its structurally unsound neighbors, and the distant, fungal scrabble of the Rift-Climbers.

It has no true head. Its top surface is a shallow, concave dish of leathery skin that collects the rare, sulfurous dew. Seven arms radiate from its central column, each a multi-jointed, iron-hard limb ending in a cluster of blades—not claws, but sharpened, molting segments of its own exoskeleton. These are its hands, its weapons, and its tools for etching its history onto the canyon walls. It is a creature of extreme verticality, having no concept of 'ground' or 'sky,' only of 'up' and 'down,' 'stable' and 'unstable.'

Gog is a hermit, a pathfinder, and a scavenger. It moves with a slow, deliberate gravity that is the antithesis of the frantic, light-footed Rift-Climbers. It traverses the chasms not by leaping, but by reshaping them. A sharp blade-hand will extend, punching a finger-deep hole into rusted I-beam, then another, then another, creating a ladder of perfect, uniform holds. Where a path is blocked by a collapsed section, it will spend days patiently chipping away at a supporting strut until the entire mass falls away into the abyss. It has brought down entire sections of the city like this, not out of malice, but out of a desire for a more direct route. It is a creature of profound, patient sabotage.

Its diet is the city itself. It scrapes the rust-colored lichen from the north-facing walls with dextrous, saw-like motions. It cracks open the hydrothermal vents to sip the mineral-rich steam, which it filters through a complex internal network of filters andpouches, expelling pure water vapor from its dorsal slits and retaining the metallic and mineral compounds to strengthen its shell. Sometimes, it will find a dead Rift-Climber, mummified by the heat, and will meticulously dismantle it, absorbing the calcium and iron into its own structure, leaving only a brittle, empty husk.

Its only true relationships are with the deep, thrumming machinery. Down in its core, which is less a stomach and more a geothermal processing plant, it has internalized a small version of the earth-engines. Through a lifetime of study—of feeling the carefully timed *thud-hiss-thud* and placing a limb against a resonant pipe—Gog has learned the rhythm. It does not control the machinery, but it understands its pulse. It uses the deep, predictable vibrations to disorient its rivals, the Climbers, and to sense the structural fatigue of its world. The canyon is a massive tuning fork, and Gog is its most attentive listener.

It communicates in a language of slow, resonant impacts. A three-tap rhythm on a gong-like sheet of metal means the south-eastern pathway is collapsing. A single, long, grinding stroke means it has found a new steam-vent cache. These messages drift down the canyons, not as sound, but as vibration, picked up by others of its kind kilometers below or above. They are a slow, silent society, a guild of structural engineers and pathfinders, maintaining the world through controlled decay, mapping the corpse as they live inside it, marking its slow, geologic demise with the blade-tips of their own bodies.
