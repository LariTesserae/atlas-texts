The ones who live here are small, and numerous, and speak in pulses. They are the voice of the stone bowl.

First, there are the **Singers**. Their proper name, if such a thing were known, is buried in their own throat-song. They are the frogs, thousands of them, each a knuckle-sized jewel of damp life. They do not merely inhabit the basin; they are its nervous system. By day, they are mottled grey-green shadows clinging to the weeping walls, indistinguishable from lichen until a shutter blinks. By night, and in the deep gloom of mist, they begin. It is not a chirp or a croak, but a deep, resonant *throb*—a single note held and released in unison, a vibration that seems to come from the wet stone itself. They sing to claim territory, to call mates, to keep the rhythm of the world. They are the first and most fundamental inhabitants, the baseline hum upon which all other life is orchestrated.

Their song calls the **Shining Labourers**. The iridescent beetles, their carapaces like oil on dark water, move through the spongey moss on a constant, implacable quest. They are the processors, the alchemists. They consume the decaying ferns, the fallen leaves, the things that die in the damp, and they break them down, returning them to the soil. Their movements are deliberate, unhurried, a counterpoint to the frantic song of the Singers. When the mist thickens, they pause, their shells collecting droplets that quiver like perfect globes. They are the patient engine of the place.

The song also calls the **Piercing Whistles**. These are the mountain thrushes, sleek and dark as water-worn obsidian. They are the hunters of the vertical world. With feathers that shed water like oiled cloth, they are never damp, never weighed down. They flit from rock to rock, their eyes fixed on the moss. When a Singer or a Labourer moves, they strike—a quick, precise dive, a sharp *pip!* of a call that cuts through the drone like a sliver of ice. They are not plentiful, but they are ever-present, their sharp notes the punctuation in the basin’s endless sentence.

Watching it all, seen only in flickers of motion at the edge of the moss, is the **Dark Worry**. It is a mustelid, perhaps, or something like it—a sleek, elongated shadow that flows between stones. It is the song’s consequence, the pressure that keeps the Singers vigilant and the Whistles wary. It moves with a liquid patience, its fur dark and likely water-resistant, its eyes bright. It does not sing. It listens. It listens to the throb of the frogs, the trundle of the beetles, the dive of the birds. It is the silent space between the notes, the predator that gives the frantic chorus its purpose. It takes the foolish, the slow, the distracted, and in doing so, it sharpens the song, makes it truer.

And beneath and behind them all, are the **Forgotten Walkers**. They are not here now, not in flesh. They are the memory in the stones. Their presence is in the low, circular windbreaks that are slowly becoming part of the landscape again, the slate melting back into piles. It is in the blackened, empty firepits where rain has done the final cleaning. And it is in the shrine—or watchtower—on the high shelf, a deliberate mark on the cliff face. The Walkers who built it are gone, their shepherds or travelers long turned to soil that feeds the Singers’ moss. But the structure remains, a silent testament. It is a place the Dark Worry might use as a den, where the Whistles might perch, where the Singers’ chorus rises and echoes, absorbed and reflected by the very walls the Walkers raised. They lived here, humbly and briefly, and their greatest works are now just another kind of rock, another surface for the lichen and the water and the small, vibrant lives that have always owned this high, wet bowl.
