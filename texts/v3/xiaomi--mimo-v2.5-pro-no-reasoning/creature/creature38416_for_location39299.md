# The People of Mirell

They are not tall. Taller would be impractical in a place where every doorway is framed by living root and every ceiling is a negotiation with the trunk-width scaffold of something older than the building it holds up. The adults average perhaps a hundred and fifty centimeters, and even that is misleading — they are disproportionately long in the torso, with short, bowed legs developed over generations of constant climbing on slick stone. Their center of gravity sits low. They do not stumble. Even the children, who are round-faced and damp-skinned and quiet as anyone in Mirell is quiet, move through the terraced rings with a sureness that suggests the architecture itself is guiding their feet. It is. They have shaped it, and it has shaped them, and neither quite remembers which came first.

Their skin is the color of limestone underwater — pale grey-brown, sometimes with a faintly greenish cast that comes from the fungal gardens, from the water, from a lifetime of being touched by bioluminescent surfaces in the dark. It is not an unhealthy color. It is the color of a thing that has adapted. Their skin is notably smooth, almost slick, and secretes a thin mucus layer that they do not notice any more than you notice the oil on your own face. It serves them: it repels the fungal films that would otherwise colonize every surface that stays damp, and it allows them to slide through tight root-passages without tearing. Infants are almost eel-smooth. The elderly become rougher, the mucus thinning, and they compensate with rubbed pastes of clay and moss-resin that they apply each morning with the absent-mindedness of combing hair.

Their eyes are large. Not dramatically so — not the saucers of deep-cave organisms that have abandoned vision entirely — but noticeably, disproportionately large relative to their small faces, with wide dark irises and very small whites. They see well in dim light. They see color differently than you do: their vision extends slightly further into the blue-green range, and they are correspondingly less sensitive to red, which in Mirell is the color of nothing important. The fungal gardens produce blue-white light; the water-channel shrimps glow faintly green; the mosses range from pale jade to deep teal. Red exists here only in the clay. It is the color of walls, of ground, of things that do not move. When they speak of warmth, they do not use red as its metaphor. They use white.

Their hands are extraordinary. The fingers are long and slightly webbed — not dramatically, not like a frog's foot, but enough that the webbing is visible when they spread their fingers wide, which they do constantly. They grip wet stone. They braid fiber in near-darkness. They read the surface of root-wood by touch alone, feeling for the micro-textures that tell them whether a root is alive or dying, whether the wood beneath has begun to soften, whether the fungal strain eating into it is the useful kind or the kind that will compromise the wall it threads through. A thresh-hand can identify thirty species of mold by running a thumb across them. This is not unusual. This is childhood education.

Their feet are broad, flat, and splayed, with thickened soles and a gripping quality to the skin that comes from the same mucus layer that covers the rest of them. They go barefoot. Footwear in Mirell is reserved for the deepest thresh-house floors, where the fermented plant juices are caustic enough to irritate even adapted skin, and even then the footwear is a sandal of braided root-fiber that is essentially disposable, lasting two or three wearings before it dissolves and is composted. Shoes are not possessions. They are biological events.

## How They Sound

Quiet. I keep saying this, but it must be understood: Mirell's people are not quiet by temperament. They are quiet by *necessity*, and the necessity has become so old and so total that it has rewired their communication.

Their spoken language exists. It has grammar, tense, metaphor, poetry. But it is spoken at a volume that a visitor would barely register as speech. Consonants are muted. Vowels are drawn out and breathy. The language has evolved an enormous inventory of hand-signs and facial micro-expressions that carry as much semantic weight as the vocal component — sometimes more. A conversation between two thresh-hands can be conducted entirely in gestures of the fingers and slight movements of the head, with no sound at all, and be as nuanced as any shouted debate in a surface city.

When they do vocalize at length — in the innermost rings, where the fungal gardens are thickest and the acoustic damping is greatest — their speech has a liquid quality. It sounds like water moving over stone. Syllables blur into one another. A listener unfamiliar with the language might mistake it for a single continuous exhalation, modulated but unbroken, and would be startled to realize that the two people speaking are conducting a complex argument about the tensile strength of a particular root-rope braid.

They sing. This surprises visitors, who expect silence to mean austerity. They sing in the *eye* — the great gap in the root-ceiling where the sound can escape upward without bouncing off the curved walls and amplifying in ways that disturb the insect colonies. The songs are low, droning, communal, with a harmonic structure that takes advantage of the natural resonance of the sinkhole itself. The bowl amplifies certain frequencies. Their music is written around those frequencies the way another culture's music might be written around a particular scale. It is not music you could replicate above ground. It belongs to the architecture.

## How They Eat

The fiber-forests are not primarily for rope and building. They are food.

The cultivated root-wood that climbs the walls and holds the stonework together produces, at certain nodes where root meets root, swollen tuber-like growths that are harvested on a careful rotation. These are the staple — starchy, faintly sweet when young, increasingly bitter and fibrous as they age, until at their oldest they are useful only for fiber extraction. The youngest tubers are eaten raw or, more commonly, fermented in the thermal channels. The geothermal water is mineral-rich and maintains a steady temperature that certain bacterial cultures find ideal, and Mirell's fermentation pits — carved into the lowest stone terraces, fed by angled conduits of warm water — are as old as the settlement itself. The resulting food is soft, tangy, and dense. It is the first thing infants are fed after weaning. It never changes flavor, and the people of Mirell consider it to have no flavor at all — it is simply *food*, the baseline against which all other tastes are measured.

The water-channels provide the rest. The blind salamanders and translucent shrimp are harvested in small baskets woven from root-fiber, cooked not by fire but by immersion in the hottest thermal conduits — water that comes from the aquifer at nearly eighty degrees and is directed through stone channels precisely angled to maintain that temperature for the length of time required to cook flesh. The toads that sit on the thresh-house thresholds are not eaten but *kept*, deliberately encouraged, because they consume the insect species that damage the fiber crops. The eels are caught, smoked — not with fire, never with fire, but with a fungal fermentation process that takes weeks and produces a preserved fish that is dark, dense, and smells powerfully of iron. Children love it. Adults eat it with the matter-of-factness of eating bread.

Mushrooms — or rather, the fruiting bodies of the fungal gardens — are the luxury food. The mycelial mats that light the settlement are not the cultivated eating strains; those are grown in separate chambers, in total darkness, on substrates of precisely aged wood. The edible fungi are pale, delicate, and have a texture that the people of Mirell describe with a word that translates roughly as *the feeling of water holding still*. They are the food of celebrations, of the naming of children, of the annual pruning of the eye-root when the builders go up into the canopy with their stone tools and hack back the growth that would close the sky.

## How They Organize

There is no king. There is no council, exactly. What there is, is *depth*.

Social status in Mirell is measured in concentric rings — but inversely to what an outsider might expect. The outermost ring, the wettest, the lowest, where the thresh-houses stand in their permanent shallow water, is where the most respected work happens. The thresh-hands who process the fiber, who manage the fermentation, who channel the water and maintain the drainage systems that keep the entire settlement from becoming an uninhabitable swamp — these are the people whose judgment is sought first. Not because they are the rulers. Because they are closest to the fundamental problem: water, and the living matter that must be kept from rotting in it.

The inner rings are where the weavers, the braiders, the rope-testers work. The innermost ring is where the elderly sit and examine what the outer rings have produced, and it is their assessment — is this rope sound, is this wall growing true, is this root still living — that determines whether work continues or begins again. But they do not command. They *assess*. The distinction matters. A thresh-hand who receives a bad assessment does not argue. She returns to her basin and begins again. The elder who gave the assessment does not follow up. The system runs on reputation, and reputation in Mirell is a substance as tangible as limestone — built slowly, layered like root into stone, and capable of crumbling all at once if the work beneath it was false.

Children belong to the settlement, not to their parents. This is not a political statement. It is a practical reality: in a space this dense, this wet, this dependent on collective maintenance, the idea that a child could be raised by two people alone is absurd. Children are raised by whoever is working the ring they happen to be in. They learn by watching, then by doing, then by being corrected, then by doing again. By the age of ten, every child in Mirell can braid rope, identify a dying root from a living one, and navigate the terraced rings in total darkness. By twelve, they have usually found their ring — the depth of work that suits their hands and temperament — and begin to specialize. They are not forced. The settlement is small enough that every child's aptitudes are obvious to everyone. There is no wasted talent in Mirell. There is no room for it.

## How They Die

They die the way everything in Mirell dies — wet, and slowly, and into the system.

The very old, when they sense their work ending, move to the lowest ring. Not the thresh-houses — those are for the living — but a series of shallow stone alcoves built into the outer wall of the lowest terrace, open to the water channels, where the current is slow and the clay is rich. They sit or lie there. They are brought food. They watch the water. The mucus on their skin thins and the fungal films begin to colonize them — gently, harmlessly, the same blue-green glow that lights the walls. This is not seen as death. It is seen as *becoming surface*. The body is left where it rests. The roots find it within weeks. The stone accepts it within months. Within a year, there is a new green patch on the outer wall, and the children are told: *that is your grandmother, keep that root alive, she is holding the wall together*.

This is not metaphor. It is structural engineering. The dead are the most reliable mortar. Everyone knows this. No one finds it morbid. The alternative — burial, burning, removal — would waste the body's calcium and phosphorus, would weaken a wall, would leave a gap in the living system that someone would have to fill with labor. The dead work. They have always worked. It would be strange to stop.

## What They Think About

They think about water. Not abstractly — they are not philosophers of hydrology — but constantly, practically, the way you think about air. Where is it pooling. Where is it flowing too fast. Where has it found a crack in the stone and begun to widen it. Where has a root died and left a channel open that will now let water into a chamber that should be dry. Every person in Mirell thinks about water at least once every few minutes. It is the baseline cognition of the place, the hum beneath every other thought.

They think about roots. Which are growing. Which are resting. Which have been pruned too aggressively and might be signaling stress to the canopy above. Which have found a new path through a wall and need to be guided before they split the stone. Root-thinking is slower than water-thinking. It operates on the scale of seasons and years. The people who think most about roots are the elders in the inner ring, and they carry in their memory a map of the root system that is detailed beyond anything a visitor could comprehend — a four-dimensional map that includes not just position but history, growth rate, species, health, and relationship to every other root in the bowl. They update this map constantly. They die with it. A new elder must rebuild it, and this is considered the most difficult and important work in Mirell — not the building of new structures but the *understanding* of the living structure that already exists and will outlast every wall.

They think about silence. Not as an absence but as a medium — something with texture and density and acoustic properties that can be shaped and used. They can hear, in the silence, the difference between a healthy insect colony and a stressed one. They can hear water running inside a wall. They can hear a root growing, if they press their ear to the stone and hold still long enough. The silence of Mirell is not empty. It is a diagnostic instrument, and everyone is trained to read it.

They think about the surface, sometimes. The thin, wind-scoured limestone plateau above the root-ceiling where nothing grows and the sun is visible as a full disc rather than a constellation of dappled points. Some of the younger builders go up, through the shafts, into the roots, and emerge onto the grey rock and the grey sky and the horizon — the *horizon*, that impossible line that a bowl-dwelling people have no daily experience of — and they come back quiet and strange and needing a day or two before they can settle back into the curved walls and the dripping ceiling and the feeling of being held.

They do not envy the surface. But they are curious about it, the way a fish might be curious about the sky — aware that it exists, aware that it is different, aware that visiting it is technically possible, and unable to imagine wanting to stay.
