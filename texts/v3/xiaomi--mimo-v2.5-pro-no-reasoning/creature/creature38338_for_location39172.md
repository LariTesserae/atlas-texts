They live in the quiet between the stones.

You have to understand — they are not new. That is the thing the geologists on the Terraces never quite grasp, perhaps because they are so rightly amazed by the newness of the rock itself. But the people were here before the last buckle of the shelf. They were here when the vents first breathed. They watched the first seepage pools form, watched the first mosses creep green across the black basalt, and they said nothing, because there was nothing yet to say.

They call themselves — in the closest translation available — the Listeners.

There are perhaps two thousand of them. Maybe fewer. They have never counted, and the question strikes them as faintly absurd, like counting the number of times your heart beats. They live scattered across the plateau in small family groups, moving between shelters that are not buildings — not in any way the Terrace engineers would recognize. Their shelters are the land itself: overhangs in the cliff faces, hollows beneath basalt shelves where thermal warmth seeps upward, shallow caves where the stone weeps slow and constant and the water collects in natural basins they have shaped, over centuries, with patient erosion and careful placement of heat-stones.

The heat-stones are their one great tool. They are ordinary basalt, taken from near the vents, where the rock holds deep warmth for days after it is cut. The Listeners carry them to cold hollows and arrange them in patterns that — to an outsider — look random, but that create currents of warmth in the enclosed air, holding back the frost, keeping the shelters habitable through the long winters. They renew them every few days, walking the distances between shelter and vent in silence, sometimes for hours, carrying stones that are almost too hot to hold but that they cradle against their bodies as though they are children.

They are small people. Not dwarfish — proportioned normally, but compact, thick-bodied, with wide hands and feet and a low center of gravity that speaks of ten thousand years of walking on wet stone. Their skin is the color of the basalt — not metaphorically; it is a dark, blue-grey that would let you mistake them for rock if they stood still, which they often do. They can stand motionless for an extraordinary long time. Their hair is black and coarse and worn long, braided with fibers from the woody ground-shrubs, and their eyes are dark and wide and reflect light in the way that the eyes of nocturnal animals do — a faint, amber sheen in low light that comes from a tapetum they share with the plateau's canids, with whom, it must be said, they have a complicated relationship.

They are not primitive. This is the second misunderstanding, and the more dangerous one. They have no cities, no written language (or none that the Terrace researchers have found), no visible technology beyond heat-stones and simple cordage and stone blades sharper than any manufactured edge. But they have something else: a knowledge of the plateau so deep and so comprehensive that it constitutes a kind of science the Terrace researchers can only envy from a distance.

They know the rhythms of the rock. They feel the seismic precursors through their feet — not metaphorically; their bare soles are sensitive to vibrations that instruments struggle to detect, and they have a vocabulary for the different tremors that runs to hundreds of distinct terms. They know which vents will shift, which pools will freeze, which channels will run. They know the migration patterns of the herds not as data but as something closer to family history — they have names for the great herd-lines, names that have been passed down for so long that they predate any language the researchers can parse, and they follow the herds' movements with an intimacy that suggests, to the more careful observers, that the relationship is not one of predation but of partnership.

They do not hunt the herds often. A few animals per group per year — always old ones, always ones that the Listeners identify by posture and gait as being ready. The killing is done with a single cut, precise, at a point on the throat that the Listeners can show you on themselves, pressing their fingers into the hollow below the jaw where the pulse beats. The herd does not flee when this happens. The other animals stand at a distance and watch, and when it is done, they move on. The Terrace researchers have argued for decades about whether this constitutes domestication, mutualism, or something for which there is no existing term.

The canids — the grey, slate-colored wolves that are not wolves — are the Listeners' closest companions, though "companion" may be too warm a word. They are not pets. They are not tame. But the Listeners and the canids share the plateau in a state of mutual recognition that predates any human presence on the world. The canids follow the Listeners at a distance. The Listeners leave food — scraps of the killed ungulates, portions of the bitter berries — at specific points along the ridgelines. The canids, in turn, drive the herds in directions that the Listeners, through their deep reading of the land, know will bring the herds to the richest grazing. Neither party has agreed to this. Neither party has ever, as far as anyone can tell, discussed it. It is simply how things are done.

The Listeners do not speak often. When they do, their language is quiet, almost subvocal — a series of low tones and clicks that carry poorly in the thin air, which may be by design. They communicate primarily through posture, through the angle of the head, the set of the shoulders, the speed and placement of footsteps on stone. To the Terrace researchers, trained in languages that fill the air with words, the Listeners seem eerily silent. But to watch them move — to watch a group of six or seven cross a fog-wrapped basin, each one reading the others' bodies as clearly as you might read a page — is to understand that they are in constant, intricate conversation. They simply do not speak it aloud.

They are aware of the Terrace settlements. They have always been aware. They watch the researchers from a distance with what the researchers interpret as curiosity and what the Listeners, if pressed (and they are never pressed, because they never allow themselves to be pressed), would call something closer to concern. Not fear — they are not afraid. But they watch the Terraces the way you might watch a child playing near a cliff edge: with attention, with a readiness to intervene, and with a patience that suggests they have seen this before.

They have, in fact, seen this before.

There is a story — one of the few the Listeners have shared, and they shared it not in words but in gesture, over the course of a week of patient, silent demonstration — about a previous people who came to the plateau. Not recently. Not in any timeframe the geologists would recognize. But the Listeners' sense of time is deep, anchored not in human memory but in stone memory, in the way the basalt holds heat, in the patterns of erosion, in the slow grammar of geological formation. The previous people — the Listeners have a name for them that translates, roughly, as "the loud ones" — came to the plateau and built things and studied things and then, at some point, left. Or stopped. The Listeners are unclear on the mechanism. What they are clear on is the result: the stone swallowed it all. The new rock rose over the old works. The marshes filled the old channels. The mosses grew over the old scars. The plateau healed itself, because the plateau is always healing itself, because it is always becoming.

The Listeners stayed.

They stayed because they understood something that the loud ones, for all their cleverness, did not: that you do not impose order on a world that is still being built. You do not build permanent structures on rock that is still rising. You do not chart permanent channels for water that does not yet know where it wants to go. You do not name the herds and pin them to fixed locations on a map when the herds are following a migration pattern that has been shifting, slowly, for millennia, as the plateau's shape changes, as the vents migrate, as the water table rises and falls.

You listen. You watch. You move with the land. You carry your warmth-stones and you follow the herds and you stand on the high ridges at night and you read the sky, and you wait, because the plateau is not finished, and neither are you, and the only wisdom is to remain unfinished alongside it.

This is what the Listeners do. This is who lives here, in the hush between the stones and the mist and the slow exhalation of new rock: a people so old they have forgotten the concept of age, so patient they have forgotten the concept of time, so quiet they have become indistinguishable from the land itself. They are the plateau's memory. They are its conscience. They are its voice, though they speak so softly you have to press your ear to the stone to hear them.

And on clear nights, when the herds stop and stand in the moonlight and stare upward with their dark eyes, the Listeners stand among them — small, grey, motionless — and stare upward too. And they are reading something in the sky that no instrument on the Terraces has ever been designed to detect: the slow, patient language of a world still learning to speak.
