The people of Gondrem are not born; they are precipitated. They form slowly, like salt crystals in the shallow pans, accumulating out of the warm, mineral-heavy air over years until there is enough substance to be considered a person. They are solid and dense, their bodies composed of the same ancient, twice-metamorphosed stone as the plateau itself. Their skin is not skin but a thin, living rind of polished gneiss, smooth and cool to the touch, with fine, contradictory banding visible beneath the surface if you look closely. Their blood is not red but a thin, silvery suspension of dissolved metals and calcium, the same fluid that weeps from the stone, which pulses sluggishly through veins of microscopic schist.

They move with the ponderous, inevitable slowness of erosion. A step is not a lift and fall but a slow, grinding shift of weight, a sound like sandpaper on stone. They do not walk so much as they *occur*, their forms resolving out of the diffuse gray light as they approach, becoming more defined and present with each slow meter. Their voices are gone—lost generations ago not to the roar, but to the realization that sound is an inefficient, temporary thing. Their primary language is a tactile one, conducted through the soles of their feet, transmitted through the ground. It is the vibration of the plateau itself, a constant stream of information about pressure, moisture, and the deep, slow rhythm of the aquifer. The hand-sign used with outsiders is a crude, simplified translation.

Their senses are attuned to the plateau’s temporality. They do not *hear* the roar; they *are* the roar, their bodies resonating with it, a standing wave of flesh and stone. They feel the shifts in the seeps as a change in the pressure of the air. They see not with eyes—those would be blinded by the glare off wet stone—but with a diffuse, whole-skin perception of light and shadow, a vision of gradients and textures. The mist is not an obscuring agent but a medium of clarity, allowing them to perceive the moisture in the air as a tapestry of currents and densities.

They are the city’s memory and its motive force. Each one is a custodian of a specific, slow system. One’s mind is a living map of a particular network of seeps, its thoughts the slow migration of water through capillary channels. Another’s consciousness is the wear-pattern on a rail line, the gradual erosion of metal under the weight of countless loads. They do not *operate* the factories in the way an outsider would understand; they *inhabit* them, their bodies a necessary component of the machinery, their slow movements syncing with the cycling of presses and the flow of chemical baths. The factory’s roar is their heartbeat. The steam is their breath.

They reproduce not through coupling, but through a process of crystalline budding. When the pressure in a particular district becomes too great, or the load on a structural member too sustained, a slow, deliberate fracture will form in a wall or a floor. From this fracture, over decades, a new form will grow, taking shape from the very substance of the building, its consciousness taking shape from the building’s purpose. It is born already knowing the function of the press it will tend, the route of the rail it will maintain.

They do not die. They erode. A lifetime of resonating with the roar, of channeling the mineral water, of absorbing the vibration, gradually smooths their features, rounds their edges, breaks down the fine crystalline structures that give them definition. An old resident becomes a smooth, almost featureless pillar of stone, still standing at their post, their perception now so diffuse they have become one with the plateau’s ambient awareness. They are eventually mistaken for natural formations, and are moved by the younger, more defined residents to the terraced edges, where they sit, facing outward, and slowly, over centuries, dissolve back into the stone they came from.

Their rule is the only rule: **Do not interrupt the flow.**

The water must weep. The stone must roar. The machines must cycle. The maps must be updated. The vacancy must be maintained, for it is a vital part of the system—a pressure release, a space for the slow, slow changes to occur. To introduce urgency, to try to speed up a process or fill an empty space too quickly, would be a violence. It would be like trying to shout over the roar. Pointless, damaging, and deeply, profoundly rude.

They look at the rare, noisy, fleeting outsider who arrives with their questions and their speed and their soft, perishable bodies, and they feel a pity so deep and so patient it has no emotion attached to it. It is a geological pity. They see a mayfly trying to understand a mountain. They see a ripple in a pool that will be gone in a moment, while the pool itself remains. They make the sign, the slow, settling gesture of the hands. *Already happening.* There is nothing else to say. The outsider is already a memory, a small, temporary disruption in the great, grinding, weeping stasis that is Gondrem. And soon, they will be gone. And the plateau will not notice.
