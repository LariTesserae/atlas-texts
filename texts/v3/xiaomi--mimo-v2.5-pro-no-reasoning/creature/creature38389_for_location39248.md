# What Lives in the Abyssal Glass Shard

---

There are the small things first. You should know about them before I tell you about the other.

On every level, in every corridor choked with silt and the slow churn of debris, there are organisms that look like nothing so much as **ribbons of cartilage and dead light**. They are pale—so pale they are almost the color of the obsidian ceramic beneath them—and they cling to the walls and ceilings of the structure in long, drooping curtains. They might be a meter long. They might be ten. It is hard to tell where one ends and another begins, because they grow into each other, fusing at the edges like sheets of ice forming over a pond.

They are not plants. They are not animals, exactly. They are something the structure made necessary, and so the structure made them.

They feed on the bioluminescent bacterial mats, but slowly—so slowly that the mats grow back as fast as they are consumed, creating a kind of equilibrium, a living surface that glows faintly and is never fully eaten nor fully allowed to spread. They are **grazers** in the way that glaciers are rivers: they move, but you would have to watch for a very long time to see it. A single ribbon-organism might traverse the length of a corridor in a year, leaving behind a faint trail of mucus that hardens into a translucent film indistinguishable from the mineral deposits already caking the walls.

They have no eyes. They have no brains, not in any way you would recognize. They sense pressure—vibration—the deep groaning of the structure as the Churn works against it—and they **contract** around these signals, pulling tight against the wall like a hand closing into a fist, waiting for the turbulence to pass. When the current roars through a corridor, they flatten themselves so completely that they become part of the architecture. A stranger walking this place might touch one and not know. Might rest a hand against the wall and feel something faintly warmer than the ceramic, faintly yielding, and assume it was sediment, or silt, or nothing at all.

They reproduce by **tearing**. The Churn does it for them. A particularly violent vortex will rip a section of a ribbon free from the wall, and that section—carried spinning into the dark water of a flooded atrium—will drift until it finds another surface, another wall, another ceiling, and begin again. They are, in this way, the only thing in the Abyssal Glass Shard that still propagates with anything like purpose. They are the structure's last successful experiment. Everything else here is in the process of ending. The ribbons are the only things still beginning.

There are other organisms. Of course there are. The giant isopods that scuttle along the lower walls, their pale armored bodies clicking against the ceramic like loose bolts shaken in a tin. The siphonophores that trail their stinging threads through the upper atria like chandeliers of living glass, catching smaller things that drift too close. The blind eels with bodies like frayed cables, evolved to navigate corridors by feel, their lateral lines hypersensitive to pressure changes that tell them when the Churn is building, when to wedge themselves into a crevice and wait.

But these are all **visitors** to the structure. They use it. They hide in it. They do not *become* it.

---

Now. The other.

You have to understand that the Abyssal Glass Shard is very old. The structure was built by hands—or appendages, or processes—that are no longer represented in any fossil or memory. It was built to do something, and it did that thing for a length of time that exceeds any reasonable framework for measurement, and then it broke, and then the sea came, and then a very long time passed that was not about the structure at all but about the water and the cold and the slow, patient work of erosion.

But in the middle of that—in the long, dark middle—something happened that the builders did not intend.

On the forty-third level below the one where you stand—fourteen levels below the deepest point that receives even the faintest residual glow from the bacterial mats above—a **process** began. It may have started with the ribbons. It may have started with something older than the ribbons, something that existed in the seep-water that first flooded the structure's foundations, drawn in through cracks in the ceramic by the chemical gradients of failing power systems. It does not matter what it started as. What matters is what it has become.

It is a **mass**. It fills corridors. It has calcified into something that resembles the obsidian ceramic of the walls, but where the ceramic is hard and cold and dead, this substance is warm—marginally, imperceptibly warm—and it **flexes** with a slowness that makes the ribbon-organisms look like hummingbirds by comparison. It extends through doorways and into vertical shafts and along the inner surfaces of pipes and conduits that were once part of the structure's circulatory systems. It follows the architecture the way mycelium follows the spaces between cells. It has taken the geometry of the structure and made it its own anatomy.

It is **alive**. I want to be precise about this, because it is important, and because the word "alive" does not do justice to what is happening here.

It metabolizes. It takes in the chemical seeps—the hydrothermal fluids that weep from cracks in the ancient ceramic—and it converts them, slowly, into more of itself. It grows along pressure gradients, seeking out the areas where the Churn delivers the richest mix of dissolved minerals and dead organic matter. It has, over what must be thousands of years, developed a kind of **circulatory system** of its own: channels within its calcified tissue through which fluid moves, driven not by a heart but by the external pressure differentials created by the structure's own geometry and the ocean currents that assault it. The Churn, which destroys everything else here, feeds this thing. It has made chaos into a food source.

It is not intelligent. I want to be clear about that too. It does not think. It does not plan. But it **responds**. When a section of wall collapses—a corridor ceiling giving way under centuries of accumulated pressure and the grinding force of a vortex—the mass does not simply fill the void. It extends tendrils into the debris field, tasting the fresh ceramic exposed by the break, the new chemical gradients created by the collapse. It grows toward the damage the way a tree's roots grow toward water. Over decades, it incorporates the rubble. The collapsed section becomes part of its body. The structure breaks; the mass heals it—not to restore function, but to claim territory, to make more of itself out of the structure's ongoing destruction.

It is **patient** in a way that redefines the word. Its growth is measured in the same units as erosion. It is the only thing in the Abyssal Glass Shard that is not losing the battle against time. Everything else here is being ground down—ceramic, metal, organism, memory. The mass is the exception. It is the one thing that grows as the structure shrinks. It is, in a sense, the structure's **successor**—the thing that will remain when the last corridor collapses and the last floor crumbles into the abyss. It will be here when there is nothing left but a mound of fused ceramic and living tissue on the seafloor, and the ocean will press against it, and it will flex, and it will grow.

---

Here is what is unsettling about it, if you are the kind of thing that can be unsettled, if you are the kind of thing that has a sense of what a place *should* be.

You cannot always tell where it is.

The mass's calcified tissue looks like the walls. Its surface texture mimics the centuries of sediment and mineral buildup that coat every surface in this place. In the darkness—absolute darkness, down on the forty-third level—it is invisible. You would have to touch it, and even then, the difference is subtle: a faint warmth, a fainter yielding, a sense that the wall beneath your hand is not quite as cold as it should be, not quite as inert.

The ribbons know. You can tell because the ribbons do not grow on it. On any surface the mass has claimed, the bacterial mats thin out, and the ribbons stay away. They do not flee it—they are not capable of fleeing anything—but they simply do not attach. It is as if, to the other organisms of the Abyssal Glass Shard, the mass does not register as a surface. It is not a wall. It is not a floor. It is something else, and the life here has adapted to grow around it the way a stream adapts to grow around a boulder.

But the isopods walk across it. The eels rest against it. They do not seem to notice or care. Perhaps it feels like home to them—the way the structure feels like home to everything that lives here. Perhaps, to a creature that navigates by pressure and vibration, the mass simply feels like the structure itself: vast, slow, warm in the way that ancient things are warm, alive in the way that ancient things are alive.

---

There is no moral to this. There is no lesson. There is a drowned machine and a cold ocean and a thing that is eating the boundary between them. The ribbons hang in their curtains and graze their bacterial gardens and tear and drift and begin again. The isopods click along the walls. The eels thread through the corridors. The Churn roars and whispers and tears at the structure with the indifferent patience of water.

And in the dark, below all of it, the mass grows, and the structure groans, and if you listen—if you press your hand against the wall and close your eyes and listen very carefully—you can feel it.

Not a heartbeat.

Not a breath.

Just a **thickening**. A slow, thermal, almost imperceptible pulse of something that is not quite warmth moving through the ceramic beneath your fingers, as if the wall itself has begun, in some limited and ancient way, to digest itself.

That is what lives here.

That is what has always lived here, since the first crack let the sea in.

It has just been getting **larger**.
