They do not name themselves, for names are for things that are fixed, and they are not. They are the Carvers, the Listeners, the Keepers of the Mist. To an outsider, they might look like gaunt, patient herons, their bodies wrapped in layers of a dark, oilcloth-like material woven from the fibers of the Weeping Ironwood. This fabric is slick with a permanent damp, shedding the constant condensation rather than absorbing it. Beneath these layers, their skin is pale and smooth, almost translucent, like the belly of a cave-dwelling fish, with a faint, blue-green web of veins visible at the wrist and throat.

Their most striking feature is their mode of locomotion. Their legs are long and sinewy, and they are masters of the stilts that are, to them, as natural as feet. These are not crude poles, but intricate tools of a dark, resilient wood, shod with a cup of hammered copper that makes soft, resonant contact with the porous rock. They walk with a swaying, deliberate gait, their balance perfect, their passage a series of quiet, sucking lifts and gentle placements. They see the world from a slightly elevated perspective, a constant, gentle sway above the mist-clogged streets and weeping walls.

They do not speak with their mouths in the open air. Their language is one of resonance and texture. They communicate through the rock itself. A hand pressed flat to a wall can send a vibration, a message of warning or welcome, that travels through the mountain’s newborn structure. They tap complex rhythms on their copper-shod stilts—*tap-tap-pause-tap*—a language of patterns that speaks of the mountain’s mood: a deep, slow hum for contentment, a sharp, staccato crack for a coming growth-spurt, a high, thin whistle for a fissure opening to the sky. They listen more than they speak, their heads often tilted, their eyes half-closed, feeling the mountain’s breath in their bones.

Their work is their existence. With tools of sharpened obsidian, whose edges can be knapped to a fineness that carves the living rock like cheese, and soft copper chisels that are constantly being bent, hammered straight, and bent again on small portable anvils, they do not fight the mountain’s form. They guide it. A new spire pushing from a terrace is not an obstacle; it is a gift. They will spend weeks, sometimes seasons, carefully sculpting it into a new archway, a drainage channel for the mist, a perch for the luminous lichen they cultivate. They see the deep, grinding cracks not as destruction, but as a change in the conversation, a new word from the stone.

They tend their mosses and ferns with a forager’s patience, not a farmer’s haste. The great Weeping Ironwood is their kin, its slow, hardening sap the only sealant for their weeping homes. They are a quiet people, their faces unlined by worry but weathered by a permanent, soft-focus attention. Their eyes are large and dark, adapted to the grey, diffuse light, and they seem to see not just the shape of things, but their moisture, their potential, their slow, geologic time.

To them, the world is not solid. It is a process. They are midwives to a mountain, living in the wet, resonant moment of its becoming. They are as fluid as the rock is soft, as patient as the time it takes for a droplet to hang, to gleam, and finally, after a week, to fall. They are the people of the bruised-plum peak, the gardeners of air and stone, and they are home.
