# What Lives in the Overwheel

There is no single answer. Begin with the mat.

---

**The Tenders**

They are the ones you almost saw. You did almost see one. It was there, and then green was there instead, and the difference between the two states was the difference between a word and the breath before it.

They have no name that matters. They are flat — very flat — and wide, and they move between and beneath the root-rows the way a hand moves beneath bedsheets. If you could pull back the living mat (you cannot; it would resist, not with violence but with the slow stubbornness of something that has been growing in one direction for a very long time) you would find them underneath, layered into the rhizome-weave like pages in a book. Their bodies are dark and moist and they have the texture of something between cartilage and fungal tissue — a body that is not quite animal, not quite mycelial, that belongs to neither kingdom or to a kingdom that has not been named because the people who name kingdoms have never come here.

They are shaped, loosely, like the sole of a foot. Broad, tapered at one end. No eyes. No organs you would recognize as sensory, though their underside is a network of fine filaments that interleave with the root-hairs of the mat so thoroughly that to move through the mat is, for them, to feel the mat. They know when a root is failing. They know when a channel is silting. They know this the way you know where your hand is — not as information but as proprioception, as the body knowing itself.

They tend. That is what they do. That is all they do, and it is everything.

They move to a root that is rotting too fast and they settle over it and their filaments thread into the dead tissue and they digest it — slowly, the way everything moves here — and they redistribute what they cannot use into the water-channels, where it flows to feed something else. They move to a channel that has closed and they press their bodies into the blockage and they work it open, not with force but with patience, the way water works limestone, and when the channel flows again they move on. They move to the base of a plant that is growing in a direction that would disrupt the spiral — the spiral matters, though they do not know this as knowledge, only as discomfort, a wrongness in the pattern that their filaments feel as a kind of itch — and they curl around the base and they slow the growth on one side by pressing against it, applying a gentle, constant pressure that the plant reads as shade, as competition, as a reason to grow the other way.

They do not think. They do not need to. They are the grove's immune system, its maintenance crew, its white blood cells — and if that metaphor makes them sound like servants, that is only because you have not understood the relationship. They are not separate from the mat. Their filaments *are* the mat's filaments, or they become so, over time. A tender that stays in one place long enough (and they sometimes do; they are patient beyond patience) begins to root. Its edges soften. Its body loses definition. It becomes part of the weave, indistinguishable, and the filaments that were its nervous system become the mat's nervous system, and the knowledge it carried — *this channel, this rot, this wrongness* — becomes the mat's knowledge, diffused and permanent.

They reproduce by splitting. Not often. A tender that has grown too large — larger than a dinner plate, say, which is very large for a tender; most are the size of a spread hand — will begin to constrict in the middle, and over the course of weeks (months; time moves differently here, not faster or slower but thicker, the way the air is thick) it will divide, and each half will carry the same filament-patterns, the same knowledge of the same section of grove, and they will move apart, slowly, and tend twice the territory between them.

There are perhaps forty of them. Perhaps sixty. They are impossible to count because some are in transition — half-tender, half-mat — and some are so small you would mistake them for a thickening of a root-hair. They have been here as long as the mat has been here, or nearly. They are the mat's way of tending itself. They are the mat, tending.

---

**The Pipe Drinkers**

In the water beneath the mat, in the saturated dark where the channels run and the seeping never stops, there is another kind of life. You would not call it fauna. You might not call it alive.

The old pipes — the ones that still carry water from somewhere below, from an aquifer or a buried cistern or some source the factory once tapped and the grove has since claimed — these pipes weep. They weep from joints where the metal has thinned to paper, and the water that comes through carries iron and copper and trace minerals that the factory's plumbing gathered from deep rock and brought upward, and this water is different from the rainwater and the condensation. It is richer. It tastes, if you were foolish enough to taste it, like blood and pennies.

Something drinks from these weeping joints. You will not see it. You will see what it leaves behind: the dark glossy-leafed plant that grows only from the pipe — *only* from the pipe, nowhere else in the grove — and when you look closely at the base of that plant (you looked; you couldn't help it) you will see that its roots do not descend into the mat. They ascend into the pipe. They thread through the same thinning joint where the water comes out, and they grow *inside* the pipe, upward or downward, following the water, and the plant is not drinking from the soil. The plant is drinking from the pipe. The plant is the visible organ of something that lives inside the plumbing of a dead factory.

What is it? A fungal body, primarily. A single hypha — impossibly large for a hypha, the diameter of a finger — that has colonized the interior of the pipe network, feeding on the mineral-rich water and the slowly corroding iron (it helps the corrosion along; its exudates are acidic; it is eating the factory from the inside) and it extends, slowly, along the pipe, following the water, and where it reaches a joint that weeps, it fruits. The fruit is the dark plant. The dark plant is the fruit. The leaves are the sporangia — though they do not release spores into the air (the air is too wet; spores would rot) but into the water, into the seeping channels, where they drift until they find another pipe, another joint, another weeping.

The copper-scent is the smell of the fungal body metabolizing metal. It is the smell of something eating a machine and converting it, slowly, into flesh.

There are perhaps a dozen of these pipe-colonies. They are all one organism. They are slowly converting the buried factory's plumbing into a body — a circulatory system of water and dissolved iron that feeds the glossy plants above and, in doing so, feeds the mat, because the glossy plants shed their leaves (they do; slowly, one at a time, each leaf lasting a season and then falling) and the leaves are rich in minerals that the rest of the grove cannot access, and the tenders carry the fallen leaves into the channels and the channels distribute the minerals and the whole system enriches.

The Pipe Drinker is the grove's gut. It is the way the grove eats the factory. It is the way the factory becomes the grove, molecule by molecule, pipe by pipe, joint by joint, over the course of centuries that are not finished yet.

---

**The Wheel's Passenger**

And then there is the Overwheel itself, and what lives on it, and what lives *in* it, and the question of whether there is a difference.

The fungal rind — the calcified crust that has grown over the iron — is not inert. It is alive the way a coral reef is alive: the mineral structure is built by organisms, and the organisms are the structure, and you cannot separate them without destroying both. The rind is a community. Layers of lithotrophic bacteria that eat iron and excrete calcium. Layers of moss and liverwort that trap moisture and provide substrate. Layers of fungal hyphae that bind it all together and extend, like roots, into the metal below, prying apart its crystal structure with acids and enzymes, opening channels for water, widening cracks, accelerating the slow conversion of iron into iron oxide into calcium carbonate into stone into soil into root-space.

But in the deepest layer — in the interface between the living rind and the dead metal — there is something else. Something that has found, in the turning of the wheel, a rhythm it needs.

It is not a plant. It is not a fungus. It is not a bacterium. It is all of these and none, organized around a principle that has nothing to do with taxonomy. It is a biofilm — you could call it that — a living film perhaps two millimeters thick that coats the inner surface of the wheel, the surface that faces the shaft, where moisture collects and the iron is most exposed. And this biofilm has a property that the rest of the grove does not share.

It responds to vibration.

The wheel turns. The growth upon it creates unevenness. The unevenness creates vibration — a complex waveform that encodes information about the wheel's speed, the distribution of mass, the resistance of the mat below, the humidity, the temperature, the phase of the moon (the water table shifts; the wheel turns fractionally faster when the groundwater swells). And the biofilm reads this vibration the way a cochlea reads sound. Its structure is periodic — organized in repeating units at scales from microns to millimeters — and each unit resonates at a different frequency, and together they decompose the wheel's groan into something like a spectrum, a rich encoding of the state of the entire grove, transmitted through iron and stone and growth.

The biofilm does not *know* this. The biofilm *is* this. It is a sensory organ that has no brain to report to, a cochlea with no auditory cortex, a system that perceives everything and understands nothing. And yet — and this is the part that should trouble you if you stay long enough to think about it — the biofilm is not passive. Its growth rate changes in response to vibration. Where the vibration is strongest, it grows denser. Where it is weakest, it thins. And this differential growth changes the mass distribution of the wheel, which changes the vibration, which changes the growth, which changes the vibration, in a feedback loop that has been running for longer than the nameplate's date, longer than the factory, longer perhaps than the iron itself (where did the iron come from? who cast the wheel? the questions feel like they belong to a different world).

The wheel's groan is not random. The modulation you almost recognized — that was the biofilm, growing and thinning, shaping the wheel's voice the way a tongue shapes a mouth's voice, unconsciously, inevitably, the way all living things shape the medium they inhabit.

The wheel's passenger is the wheel. The wheel is the passenger. The distinction, like all distinctions here, has eroded past meaning.

---

**The Spiral-Maker**

But someone is responsible for the geometry. The spirals are too regular, too mathematically precise, to be emergent from the tenders' gentle corrections alone. Something is encoding a pattern into the growth of the grove, and it is not any of the things I have described.

It is the thing that lives in the shaft.

The shaft descends into the waterlogged ground, into the saturated dark below the mat, and it turns, and it has been turning, and at its base — you would need to dig through meters of root and mat and waterlogged soil to find it, and the mat would resist, and the tenders would come, not to attack you but to grow over what you had disturbed, to heal it, to close the wound you had made — at its base, the shaft enters something. A cavity. A void in the earth that predates the factory, that predates the wheel, that may predate the grove itself. An underground chamber where water collects and turns in slow eddies that mirror the wheel's rotation because the wheel's rotation *is* the eddies' rotation — they are coupled, mechanically, hydraulically, through the shaft and the water and the stone.

And in that chamber, in the turning water, there is a growth.

It is very large. It is very old. It is not a single organism but a colony that has fused past the point of individuality, a mass of intertwined hyphae and rhizoids and root-like structures that fill the chamber wall to wall, ceiling to floor, threading through the stone, anchoring to the shaft, and this mass — this body — is shaped.

It is shaped like a spiral.

Not a loose spiral. Not a fern-frond spiral. A logarithmic spiral, precise, mathematical, with a growth ratio that would satisfy a mathematician. And it is not merely shaped like a spiral — it *grows* in a spiral, its leading edge advancing along a logarithmic curve, adding new growth at a rate proportional to its current size, and as it grows it releases chemical signals into the water — hormones, pheromones, whatever word you want for the molecular language of a thing that has no language — and these signals travel upward through the shaft, through the water-channels, through the capillary network of the mat, and they reach the growing tips of every plant in the grove, and the plants respond.

They spiral. They spiral because the Spiral-Maker tells them to spiral, and the Spiral-Maker spirals because the turning of the water in the chamber tells it to spiral, and the water turns because the wheel turns, and the wheel turns because — 

Because.

Because the shaft turns. Because something at the base of the shaft, in the stone, in the deep aquifer, in the ancient water that has been seeping through limestone for millennia, something provides a torque. A geological pressure. A thermal gradient. A difference — in temperature, in salinity, in the slow convection currents of water that has been underground since before there were factories, since before there were people, since before there were names for things like *limestone* and *overwheel* and *spiral*.

The Spiral-Maker did not make the wheel turn. The Spiral-Maker found the wheel already turning, and it moved in, and it grew into the shape the turning demanded, and it has been shaping the grove to match ever since. It is not the grove's architect. It is the grove's resonance. It is what happens when a living thing grows long enough in the presence of a rhythm — any rhythm, any turning, any slow mechanical heartbeat — until the living thing and the rhythm become the same thing, inseparable, indistinguishable, turning together in the warm wet dark.

---

**What the Grove Is**

And now you want to know: is the grove alive? Is the grove a single organism? Is the grove *one thing*?

The answer is the answer to every question here.

It is and it isn't and the distinction has eroded past meaning. The tenders are the mat's immune system. The Pipe Drinker is the grove's gut. The biofilm is the wheel's cochlea. The Spiral-Maker is the grove's genome, encoding pattern into growth. And the Overwheel is the grove's heart, pumping not blood but rhythm, not oxygen but vibration, turning and turning and turning, and the whole assembly — the mat, the water, the pipes, the wheel, the shaft, the cavity, the tenders, the Spiral-Maker, the dark glossy plants, the condensation, the dripping, the gurgling, the groan — all of it is one thing, or all of it is many things pretending to be one thing, or all of it is many things that have been together so long that the pretense has become the reality.

It has no name for itself. It has no self to name. It has the turning, and the growing, and the seeping, and the slow mechanical groan that is the sound of a heartbeat in a body that was never supposed to be a body, that was built as a factory and became a grove and became something that is neither and both, something that has existed long enough to forget what it was and to become what it is, which is alive, which is turning, which is growing into the shape of the only thing it has ever wanted to be:

a place where things grow, and water moves, and something turns, and has been turning, and will be turning, slowly, wetly, past meaning, past ending, into the spiraling future that the Spiral-Maker has already drawn in the dark.
