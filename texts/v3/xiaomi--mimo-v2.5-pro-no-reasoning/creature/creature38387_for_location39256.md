The people who live here are small and dark-haired, but they are not the only ones.

There are the **Weavers**, the older women and men with fingers stained green-black by the split reeds. They sit in the doorways of the single-room huts, hunched over their work. They do not look up when you pass. They are weaving mats, walls, and flexible pipes, but they are also weaving the history of the damp. They smell of wet wool and lanolin. They are the keepers of the copper knife and the bronze mirror; they trade these things for silence and salt.

There are the **Dippers**, the children and the nimble-limbed youths. They are the only ones who walk the convex pathways with confidence. They move like water itself—fast, finding the lowest points. They run from the central basin to the outlying huts, carrying clay vessels that never spill. They know the rhythm of the algae; they know when the glow will start at twilight. They do not fear the mist, but they fear the stagnation of the channels. If a channel stops murmuring, a Dipper will find the blockage and clear it with a bone awl.

But deeper than the people, living in the marrow of the limestone itself, are the **Listeners**.

You cannot see them, for they are the architecture.

They are the logic of the grooves cut into the pale stone. They are the *reason* the town clings to the shelf. They are not spirits of the dead, but spirits of the *young* stone—entities that were born when the rock was broken and the water first touched the raw edge. They live in the resonance of the central building; when the wind hums through the strange, functionless patterns on the temple walls, the Listeners are singing back. They are the intelligence that coordinates the flow. They ensure that the water does not rush, but pulses.

They are the observers in the marsh.

Down there, in the dark water where the horizon vanishes, live the **Slicks**. They are not fish, nor are they men. They are the heavy, watching presence that causes the ripples in the reeds. They are the chaotic element—the vast, unchanneled water that the town’s intricate plumbing is designed to hold at bay. They are patient, too. They wait at the edge of the knee-deep harvest line. They are the listeners who do not answer.

And when the bioluminescence begins—a cold, corpse-light blue rising from the drains—everything in the town pauses. The Weavers set down their tools. The Dippers return to their homes. Even the Listeners in the stone seem to hold their breath.

In that glow, the town ceases to be a collection of huts. It becomes a single organism. The people are the blood cells; the channels are the veins; the central hum is the heartbeat. They are building a memory, etched into limestone and clay, hoping that if they manage the water long enough, the stone will learn to speak their language.
