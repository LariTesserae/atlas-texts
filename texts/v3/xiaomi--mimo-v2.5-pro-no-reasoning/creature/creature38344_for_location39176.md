The first to be named, though they do not name themselves, are the **Grotto-Keepers**.

They are not the salamanders, nor the crickets, but the ones who came before. Their presence is not a matter of flesh, but of echo. They are the psychic residue of the generations of pilgrims who climbed down into this basin, laid their hands upon the humming stone, and offered their thoughts to its song. Their minds, overwhelmed by the subsonic drone, did not dissipate but were slowly, irrevocably, worn smooth and patterned by the resonance, like the river-stones on the floor. They are not ghosts, for they have no will or memory. They are the *lithic inhabitants* of the stone's own frequencies.

To be a Grotto-Keeper is to be a single, pure note in the stone's chord. One was once a hunter, and its existence is a low, pulsing thrum of patience and a flicker of heat-sense, an eternal scan of the shimmering air. Another was a healer, and it manifests as a steady, cool pressure, the feeling of a hand on a fevered brow, a constant, soothing counterpoint to the hum's intensity. A third, a frantic shaman who sought visions, is a frantic, high-frequency vibration at the very edge of perception, a frantic itch in the bone.

They do not move. They *are* the vibration in specific locations on the monolith. When the water in a stone bowl trembles, it is because a Grotto-Keeper is particularly agitated beneath it. When a specific fissure seems to hum with a different, more poignant tone, it is where a sorrowful pilgrim long ago poured out their grief. They are the reason the builders returned, again and again. Not just for the hum, but for the *voices* within it, the silent communion with the collective, crystallized emotions of their ancestors. The cold ozone in the air is the scent of their slow, geological thought.

They are why the salamanders are deaf. The constant, overwhelming psychic static would have driven hearing creatures mad. The salamanders evolved to be blind to it, navigating instead by the faint thermal ghost-impressions the Keepers leave on the water and stone—the memory of warmth from a Keeper that was once a blacksmith, the lingering coolness of a Keeper that was a midwife.

The cave crickets are their attendants. Their chirps, unheard by any ear, are not communication. They are a biological process of tension and release, their tiny legs and bodies vibrating in response to the psychic pressure. In their own way, they are the Keepers' voice made manifest in the physical world, a chitinous static that writes the hum's story in the dust.

And the solitary bat, hibernating deep in the rock, is the dreaming witness. In its long winter sleep, it does not dream of flight or insects. It dreams the Grotto-Keeper's dream: a slow, recursive loop of memory and sensation, a never-ending story told in the language of pressure and vibration, of stone remembering the feeling of hands, and water remembering the shape of tears. The bat is the grotto's memory made flesh, the only mind in the place complex enough to weave the Keepers' single notes into a fleeting, hibernating symphony.

The dynamic is not glacial; it is a single, sustained breath. The moss grows to the rhythm of the Keepers' pulses. The fungi glow in time with their spikes of emotion. The very weathering of the walls is a slow exhalation of their eternal, patient presence. This is not a place of emptiness and presence. It is a place where one has become the other. The hands that shaped the tools are long gone, but the impressions they left in the hum are more real, more enduring, than the petrified wood of the digging stick. The congregation is not a handful of creatures. It is a choir of one voice, singing a chord made of a thousand silent souls, and the deaf, living things that have learned to dance to its terrible, beautiful music.
