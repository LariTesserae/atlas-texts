The ones who live here are not many, and they are patient.

**The Stewards:** They call themselves the Custodes of the Seventh Hum. Their ancestors were engineers and terraformers; they are now something closer to high-tech gardeners, or monks of a mechanical faith. They live in modular habitats—sleek, insulated pods bolted to the inner faces of the terraces, clustered around the warm vents of the great air circulators. Their skin is often pale from generations of diffused light, but their movements are sure-footed and calm. They wear lightweight exo-suits not for strength, but for stability against the slick surfaces and for integrated tool-arms for delicate work. They are the reason the pumps still hum and the lifts still groan. Their lives are measured in maintenance schedules and the growth cycles of their hydroponic orchids, which they prize for their fleeting, brilliant blooms in the unchanging light. They speak in low voices, because sound carries strangely in the hollow spaces between the terraces, and they listen more than they speak. They are not masters of this place. They are its shepherds.

**The Surface Dwellers:**
The true citizens are the life that has colonized the metal.
*   **Lattice-Skinks:** The six-limbed, rust-patterned lizards are everywhere. They are ectothermic, basking on the warm steel plating, their metabolisms synchronized to the faint thermal cycles of the machinery below. They are masters of adhesion, their feet tipped with microscopic van der Waals pads that grip the damp, moss-slick surfaces. They hunt the slow-moving grubs in the fern-thickets.
*   **Rust-Wasps:** Large, iridescent insects with a wingspan that cuts the humid air. They are not truly wasps, but a colonial hymenopteran that builds its nests from chewed moss and their own metallic-hued saliva, creating papery, rust-colored combs that hang from conduit junctions. They are pollinators for the waxy-leaved plants and fiercely territorial of their aerial nurseries.
*   **Condor-Gliders:** The gliding birds are silent, soaring kings of the space between tiers. Broad, almost triangular wings with a secondary set of smaller, stabilizing fins at the tail. They ride the thermals and updrafts created by the immense thermal mass of the terraces and the venting of the circulators. They nest on the highest, most wind-scoured ledges, far from the thickets, their nests lined with shed snake-skin and polished metal chips.

**The Aerial Life:**
*   **Spire-Darts:** Smaller than the condor-gliders, these creatures are more insect-like in their maneuverability. They dart between the fern fronds, feeding on aerial plankton—minuscule insects and fungal spores suspended in the eternal humidity. Their wings hum with a higher pitch, a sound constantly woven into the drone of the machines.
*   **Tier-Kites:** The apex of the engineered fauna. They are rarely seen up close. When they glide overhead, silhouetted against the bright white ceiling, they are spectral shapes—vast wings of a fabric that shifts between matte grey and glinting silver, catching and shedding moisture. There is a whirr, barely audible, from their bodies—the sound of nutrient filters extracting sustenance from the air itself. They are scavengers, cleaners, and symbols. Some Custodes believe they are the original maintenance drones, given over to evolution and purpose.

**The Deep Dwellers:**
In the shadows of the great buttresses, in the pools of collected condensation in drainage basins, other things live.
*   **Iron-Root Networks:** Not plants, but a fungal mycelium that has adapted to the substrate. It sends out pale, root-like hyphae deep into the crevices and corrosion pits of the metal, slowly dissolving and incorporating trace minerals. Its fruiting bodies are delicate, translucent mushrooms that glow with a faint bioluminescence, creating tiny galaxies in the dark.
*   **Vein-Moss:** On the lower, wetter tiers, this moss grows in thick, rope-like strands, following the lines of pipes and conduits as if they were its own vascular system. It filters heavy metals from the dripping water, its tissues laced with safe, inert oxides, giving it a beautiful, dark variegation.

The humans and the fauna exist in a state of mutual, careful disregard. The skinks skitter over the Custodes’ boots; the wasps build their nests on the outsides of habitat pods. The great gliders cast their shadows over the hydroponic bays. It is a community without intimacy, a shared space governed by the deep, slow rhythms of the structure itself: the throb of the pumps, the sigh of the circulators, the eternal drip of water from leaf to leaf to warm, pitted steel. They are all inhabitants of the same vertical jungle, breathing the same thick, green air, living at the pace of corroding metal and unfolding fern fronds. They are the life that has answered the call of the perpetual, humid noon.
