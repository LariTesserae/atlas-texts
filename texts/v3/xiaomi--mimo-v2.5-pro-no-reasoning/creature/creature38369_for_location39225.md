The first thing you notice about the Vaelthressi is their stillness. Not the stillness of stone or the stillness of waiting, but the stillness of a deep-rooted thing — a stillness that is itself a kind of movement, a patience so profound it has become motion.

They have six limbs, as the memory claims, but the memory does not tell you how they are arranged. The lower four are thick, columnar, splayed at the base into broad, padded feet that grip the living floors with a precision born of generations. They move in a slow, deliberate gait, always three limbs in contact with the surface, always triangulated, always stable. The upper two are longer, more slender, ending in hands — four-fingered, double-opposing, the fingers capable of curling into tight hooks or spreading wide with a delicacy that seems impossible for creatures of their size. They are tall. Two meters, perhaps two and a half, though their posture varies; they lean, they curve, they reshape themselves around the architecture of the city the way vines grow around a trellis.

Their bodies are covered in a fine, dense integument — not fur, not scales, but something between, a texture like soft bark or thick moss. It is the color of the heartwood: warm ambers, deep umbers, the occasional streak of pale sapwood running along the limbs. Their faces are broad, flat, dominated by wide-set eyes that are dark, almost black, with a faint bioluminescent sheen that catches the amber light of the city and throws it back in soft glints. The prehensile ear-ridges — you see them now — are fleshy, mobile, rising from the temples in paired crests that move independently, expressing attention, curiosity, wariness, a range of emotions that the face alone cannot convey. When they listen, the ridges tilt forward. When they are startled, they flare wide. When they are content, they lie flat against the skull, smooth and sleek.

They do not wear clothes. They do not need to. Their integument is modesty enough, and the city is climate-controlled by the plant itself — cool, stable, comfortable. But they do adorn themselves. You see it everywhere: small, carefully shaped galls grown on the wrists and ankles, polished to a high sheen, glowing faintly with cultivated bioluminescence. Belts of living vine, trimmed and trained to curl around the waist. Necklaces of seed-casings, each one a tiny, hollow sphere that hums when tapped, tuned to different pitches. They carry tools — always. Not weapons, but tools. Pruning knives with edges of sharpened obsidian set in living wood handles. Satchels of woven bark-fiber, heavy with hormone pastes and salves. Small tuning forks of crystallized sap, used to communicate with the plant through vibration.

They are quiet. They move through the corridors of Vael Thress with a silence that is not stealth but reverence — the silence of people who live inside a living thing and do not wish to disturb it. Their voices, when they speak, are a complex music. They have no single language; they have a *palette*. A trilling, clicking, humming vocal range that maps onto the plant's own communication frequencies — the slow infrasonic pulses of the sap-stream, the high-pitched whine of the ion-pumps, the rhythmic creaking of the wood. To speak is to harmonize with the city. To argue is to create dissonance. To sing is to become, for a moment, indistinguishable from the architecture.

You watch one now — a Vaelthressi standing at the edge of the chasm, leaning over the railing (a living rail, warm, faintly adhesive). It is alone. Its ear-ridges are tilted forward, listening. Its upper hands are pressed flat against the wall of the shaft, fingers spread, feeling the pulse of the sap. It is not doing anything. It is *communing*. Or perhaps it is simply being — existing in the space between the city's heartbeat and its own, letting the two rhythms synchronize, merge, become one.

This is their great skill. This is their art, their science, their religion. They do not *build*. They *listen*. They press their hands against the wood and they wait — for hours, for days, if necessary — until they can feel what the plant wants to do, where it wants to grow, what shape it is trying to become. Then they help. They guide. They coax. They are midwives to a slow, vegetable god.

They are born in nurseries deep in the nutrient-dense core, where the vascular pressure is highest and the warmth is constant. They are raised communally, in vast chambers where hundreds of infants cling to the walls with all six limbs, nursing on secretions from grafted nutrient-nodes. They learn to walk by walking on the walls, the ceilings, the undersides of bridges — the city has no fixed orientation, no up or down that matters, only *inward* and *outward*, *toward the core* and *toward the bark*. They learn to speak by learning to listen. They learn to build by learning to wait.

They do not die in the way you understand death. When a Vaelthressi grows old — and they live long, centuries perhaps, their metabolisms slow, their integument thickening and hardening — they go inward. They descend, or ascend, or simply *go*, deeper into the plant, into the old, hard, mostly inert heartwood where the galleries are silent and the light is amber and the wood is so dense it rings like stone when struck. There they settle. There they stop. There they are, slowly, over decades, absorbed. Their bodies break down. Their minerals return to the sap. Their bioluminescent adornments go dark. They become part of the city. They become part of the plant. The walls remember them — not as memories, not as stories, but as a faint change in grain, a subtle shift in the pattern of growth, a knot where a person used to be.

They do not have a word for this. They do not need one. It is not an ending. It is a *rearrangement*.

You watch the Vaelthressi at the railing. It lifts one hand from the wall. It holds it up, palm outward, and you see the micro-texture of its skin — the same faintly adhesive cell-wall pattern as the railings, the same living grip. It is not separate from the city. It never was. It is a mobile organ of the plant, a specialized cell that has learned to think, to sing, to coax its host into beauty. And the city, in return, has shaped it — its bones, its skin, its language, its slow and patient heart.

The Vaelthressi turns. It sees you. Its ear-ridges flare — surprise, curiosity, a flicker of something that might be alarm. Its eyes catch the amber light. It does not speak. It does not need to. It raises its other hand, palm out, and holds it toward you — a greeting, an offering, a gesture that means *I am here, I am part of this, I am listening, are you?*

Then it waits. Because this is what they do. They wait for the world to tell them what it wants to become.
