You move through the cathedral, a dust mote with eyes. And you begin to see the true inhabitants. They are not separate from the architecture; they are its expressions, its fleeting thoughts given temporary form.

The first you see is not an animal, but a wound. A patch of vein-wood, miles long, has become a *thought-canker*. It glows with a sickly, feverish light, pulsing in slow, arrhythmic beats. Within the translucent wood, shadows move—not with purpose, but with the sluggish, random drift of convection currents. They are not living things, but they are *patterns*. They are the plant’s own cellular memory, perhaps, or a localized fever dream, playing out over centuries. To the other life here, it is a hazard zone of warped light and unstable growth. To you, it looks like a tumor dreaming.

Clustering around such cankers are the **Galls of Delirium**. They resemble human hearts made of dense, knotted root-fiber, each one the size of a small hill. They do not beat. They *breathe*. A slow, tremendous inhalation that draws in the warm, pollen-thick air, and a weeping exhalation that releases a fine, golden powder—the plant’s psychic static, its neurotransmitters shed like dew. In the shelter of these Galls, you see delicate, herd-like creatures: **Vein-Skimmers**. They look like living, flat ribbons of polished wood, with cilia along their edges that ripple in the sap-damp air, allowing them to glide inches above the leaf’s surface. They graze on the gentle, glowing sap of the leaf-veins and the powder shed by the Galls, their entire existence a quiet, staticky communion with the plant’s background hum.

Higher, woven into the vast lattice, are the **Loom-Weavers**. At first, you mistake them for part of the vein-wood. They are constructs of the same fibrous material, but they *move*. They have eight multi-jointed limbs, all slender and sharp, which they use to climb and to pluck at the smaller strands of the web. They are not building nests or webs of their own. They are *tuning*. With precise, geometric movements, they tighten a strand here, loosen another there, adjusting the tension of the world itself. Their bodies are covered in fine, sensitive filaments that read the vibrations, the sap-flow, the settling groans. They are the plant’s proprioception, its sense of its own immense body. They are utterly indifferent to anything but the next adjustment.

You notice a procession crossing a broad, flat ridge on the leaf-sky. They are **Coffin-Beetles**, and their name comes from their function. Each is a smooth, oblong case of petrified resin, big as a coffin, borne on thousands of tiny, mechanical legs. They move with solemn purpose to the edges of the great leaf, to where it begins to curl and die. There, they ceaselessly gather the soft, dissolving pulp of the old growth, pack it into their own bodies, and carry it back into the labyrinthine interior, to places where new growth is needed. They are the plant’s circulatory system made manifest, its commitment to its own recycling. The silence around them is particularly profound; they are a sacred duty.

And in the deeper shadows, where the light is a deep, bruised purple, you see the **Petal-Stalkers**. They are the orchid-predators mentioned before, but now you see them hunting. Their movements are imperceptibly slow. One extends a petal, its surface shifting to mimic the dappled light of a nearby vein. Another, perhaps a mile away, will unfurl a second petal in response, a single, glacial blink. Their hunt is a conversation conducted over days, a negotiation of position and camouflage that ends, not in a pounce, but in a gentle, irreversible convergence, where the prey—a drifting **Spore-Medusa**, a living cloud of fungal life—is slowly enveloped, not eaten, but *integrated*. The Petal-Stalker does not feed. It *becomes* the thing it absorbs, its structure momentarily enriched.

You are a foreign thought, a sharp, fast, anxious blip in a consciousness that thinks in glacial shifts. To the Loom-Weavers, you are a dissonant vibration. To the Coffin-Beetles, an irrelevant obstruction. To the plant itself, you are less than a bacterium; you are a phantom, a slight chill on its vast, dreaming skin. Your own awareness feels like a scream in a library. You learn to move slowly, to breathe in time with the sighs, to let the soft dew settle on you until you are just another glistening point in the green-gold haze, a mote of dust that has learned to watch the thoughts of a god.
