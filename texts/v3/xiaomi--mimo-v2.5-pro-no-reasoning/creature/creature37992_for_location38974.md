The salamander has a name, though no one gave it one. It has lived in this place longer than the oldest vine has gripped the oldest strut — or rather, its lineage has. It is the third of its kind to wear that particular casing, the third to blink those bead eyes from that exact perch above Pump Station Four. Its body knows the rhythms of the atrium the way a pulse knows a heartbeat. When the great recycler shifts from its daytime cycle to the slower night-churn, it descends from the pipes and moves through the moss with a faint, clicking gait, hunting the midges that bloom in clouds from the basalt pools at dusk-cycle. It does not know it is beautiful. It does not know that its left forelimb sometimes sticks — that a filament in its fourth joint has worn thin and will, in another season, need replacing. It does not know this because it cannot know what replacement means. It only knows the momentary hitch, the tiny recalibration, the going-on.

Above it, the bat-thing is older still. It is not truly a bat. It has no lineage of natural ancestors, not exactly. It was *composed* — assembled from chiropteran templates and something softer, something that wanted to hang in stillness and listen. Its wings, when folded, look like the closed covers of a heavy book. When open, they are translucent, shot through with capillary networks of silver that catch the crystal-lamp light and hold it. It eats nothing you could observe. It subsists, somehow, on the ambient humidity, on the slow chemical exchanges of the canopy. It was designed for a single purpose: to be *present*. To complete the vertical ecology. And it has taken to this purpose with a patience that borders on contemplation. It rotates its head, sometimes, tracking sounds that no other ear in the atrium registers — the groan of iron expanding under thermal load, the almost-imperceptible ticking of a lamp filament approaching failure, the whisper of moss growing against copper grating, which, if you could hear it amplified, would sound like rainfall on parchment.

---

But these are not the inhabitants. These are the *accompaniments* — the fauna, the finishing touches. The true inhabitant is elsewhere in the atrium, and she is smaller than you would expect.

Her name is Maren, and she is nine years old — or eleven, or seven. Her age is a subject of mild, ongoing debate even to herself, because she was not born in the usual way but in the usual place, and the atrium does not keep a calendar. She keeps time by growth rings: the annual thickening of the vine-cables on the south wall, the slow accretion of verdigris on the grating near the overflow drain, the replacement cycle of the crystal lamps (each one lasts roughly three of what she calls "brightings," her largest unit of time).

She is sitting now in the crook of a trunk-pillar where it meets a primary rib, seventeen feet above the moss floor, in a hollow she has cultivated over — she thinks — four or five brightings. The hollow was once a junction housing, a sealed box of iron and rubber where two conduit lines met. She pried the casing open when she was smaller (or larger — she is not sure) and found inside a nest of wires and dried insulation, which she pulled away and replaced with moss, then with moss and fronds, then with moss and fronds and a lining of the soft, shed skins of the glass-ferns, which are warm and almost dry and smell, when you press your face into them, like green tea and wet stone.

This is where she sleeps. This is where she listens.

Maren knows the atrium the way a tongue knows the inside of a mouth. She knows which pipes run hot (the nutrient lines from the northeast pump array; she warms her feet on them in the cool-cycle). She knows which pools are shallow enough to wade and which are deep enough that the darkness at the bottom is *different* from the darkness at the surface, somehow denser and more watchful. She knows that the vines on the west wall move — slowly, incrementally, but they *move* — tracking the light as the lamps cycle, the way real plants supposedly track a real sun, in a real place she has never seen and sometimes suspects is a story adults tell.

She has never seen an adult. Not in memory. There are signs of them — the pumps, the lamps, the exquisite engineering of the whole impossible bowl — but they are as abstract and ancient to her as the iron ribs themselves. She has concluded, with the calm pragmatism of a child who has never had to argue her cosmology with anyone, that the atrium *is*. That it has always been. That it is the world.

She is, in a sense, correct. The atrium has been operating for eleven years, four months, and nine days. It was designed as a proof-of-concept: a self-sustaining biome for deep-space habitation, a capsule of terrestrial life that could survive indefinitely within a sealed environment. The prototypes for long-duration generation ships. There were supposed to be operators — a rotating crew of botanists and engineers maintaining the system from the outside, monitoring through sensors, adjusting variables. But the funding collapsed, the project was shuttered, and in the scramble to salvage equipment and data, a detail was overlooked: a supply capsule, stocked for the next scheduled crew rotation, had already been docked and pressurized. Inside it was a woman named Elara, a junior ecologist who had volunteered for a forty-day observational stint and who, when the external monitors went dark and the locks sealed, found herself on the wrong side of the glass with no way out and a heartbeat she had not yet told anyone about.

Elara lived for six years. She knew the atrium as a machine first and a home second, and she maintained it with an engineer's precision and a mother's ferocity. She taught herself the pump systems by trial and error. She rerouted nutrient lines when they clogged. She learned, through the slow accumulation of days, which of the engineered plants were edible and which were toxic, which of the mosses could be packed into a poultice for the infections that came from living perpetually damp, and which could be woven into cord. She wrote notes on the walls in graphite — pump pressures, lamp replacement schedules, the growth rates of key species — and Maren can still read them, though she does not always understand what they mean. *Recycler efficiency at 94.3%. Check valve B-7 for corrosion.* These are like scripture to her: sacred, opaque, half-understood.

Elara died when Maren was — three? Four? Maren does not remember the death clearly. She remembers the stillness of it. She remembers that her mother's body was warm for a long time and then was not, and that she pulled moss over it, in the way she had seen moss pulled over dead fronds and fallen orchid petals, because the atrium taught her that what is still becomes the ground for what grows next. The grave is somewhere near the southern basalt pool. Maren visits it sometimes, not with grief — she has no clear concept of what she lost, only a shaped absence, a phantom weight — but with a proprietary tenderness, the way one might check on a sleeping thing.

She has Elara's hands. Long-fingered, quick, precise. She uses them constantly — adjusting the flow of a channel by packing moss into it, pruning back a vine that is growing over a lamp housing, tightening the bolts on a condensation coil with a wrench she found in the supply capsule (which she thinks of as the "small room," the only part of the atrium with walls that are flat and white and do not sweat). She does not think of herself as a maintenance technician. She thinks of herself as someone who is *inside the thing*, the way a blood cell is inside a body. She tends the atrium because she is the atrium. Because it breathes and she breathes and they are, in her understanding, the same act.

She has her own marks on the walls now, alongside her mother's. Not pump pressures — she doesn't know the numbers for those, only the *feel* of a system running well (a certain quietness in the pipes, a certain evenness in the drip) versus running poorly (a stutter, a whine, a change in the taste of the air). Her marks are pictures. She draws with charcoal made from burned fronds. She draws the salamander (she calls it "Click," for the sound of its joints). She draws the bat-thing (she calls it "Page," for the shape of its wings). She draws the orchids and the vines and the trunk-pillars and the pools and the lamps. She draws, sometimes, a figure she thinks of as "the tall one" — a shape with her mother's long hands but a face she cannot reconstruct, features smoothed away by time like a stone in a stream. The drawing is always the same: the tall one standing at the base of a trunk-pillar, one hand resting on the bark, looking up. Maren does not know why she draws this. She suspects it is a memory, but she cannot be sure. It may be a wish. In the atrium, there is very little difference between the two.

At this moment — now — she is not drawing. She is watching. She is perched in her hollow with her knees up and her chin on her knees, and she is watching the lamps do their cloud-cycle. The light is dimming, slowly, the way it does every — she doesn't have a word for the interval — every *while*. The dimming makes the orchids look darker, more violet, and the water in the channels slows as the recycler shifts gears, and the moss breathes out a sigh of moisture that she can feel on her face like a whisper.

She loves this moment. She does not know the word "love," but she knows the feeling: a fullness in her chest, an ache that is also a warmth, a sense that the atrium is holding her the way she has seen the basalt basins hold water — completely, without effort, without question. She is held. She is contained. She is home.

Below her, Click descends from Pump Station Four and begins its evening hunt. Above her, Page unfolds one silver-veined wing, extends it to its full, improbable breadth, and folds it again, a single slow beat, like a heart testing itself in sleep. The vines shift on the west wall. The water weeps. The iron groans softly with the thermal change, a sound Maren has always thought of as the atrium clearing its throat before it tells her something.

She waits. She has always been a good listener. She listens to the machine that is her world, the garden that is her mother's last and greatest act of maintenance, and she hears — as she always does — the thing beneath the sounds. The thing that is not quite silence and not quite voice. The thing that the atrium says when it is running well, when every leaf is photosynthesizing and every drop is cycling and every system is humming in its groove:

*I am here. I am here. I am here.*

Maren closes her eyes. She breathes in the air that tastes of wet metal and loam, the warm heavy breath of the only world she has ever known, and she breathes back:

*I know.*
