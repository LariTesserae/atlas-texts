It breathes. Not with lungs, but with a million porous sacs and pulsating tubes. The plateau itself is the primary resident, a single, slow-thinking entity of mineral and patience. Its thoughts are not in language, but in the geothermal hum that vibrates up from its core, in the tidal pull of mineral-rich brines deep beneath the salt. The frantic, noisy life upon its skin is not separate from it; it is its nervous system, its digestive tract, its dreams given frantic, chitinous form.

The builders of the domed structures are its last, fleeting memory. They were quick, clever things, children of its own surface who learned to listen to the deep hum, to mimic the shapes of its claws and wings, to harvest the bladder-growths without rupturing the plateau’s skin. They did not last. Their short, hot lives of flint and curiosity were a burst of static in the plateau’s long, slow song. They are not gone; they are *recalibrated*. Their bones are now part of the crust, their tools smoothed into pebbles, their knowledge a faint echo in the pattern of the lichen that grows where their hearths once were. The doorway framed with iridescent shell is a fossil of intent, a hollow in the plateau’s memory.

So who, or what, truly lives here?

In the air, the gliding manta-ray creatures are its idle thoughts. They ride the warm updrafts that are the plateau’s exhalations, their bioluminescent patterns a silent, shifting language of appetite and drift. They are not hunting; they are *considering* the state of the lichen fields, the density of the grazer herds below.

The six-legged grazers with pickaxe mandibles are its hunger. They are the instrument of its constant, noisy metamorphosis. Their work is a vital, painful massage, scraping away the old and stimulated growth to make way for the new. Their chitinous bodies are a direct extension of the plateau’s will to *recalibrate*, to never let any one pattern become too dominant or too still. The deafening click of their mandibles on salt-spire is the plateau grinding its teeth in its sleep.

The pulsating vein-moss is its circulation, its lymphatic system. It moves nutrients—mineral salts, trace geothermal compounds, the recycled essence of spent grazers—through the plateau’s body. The bladder-growths are its sweat glands, swelling with viscous fluid that regulates the mineral balance of its skin. The bioluminescent lichen is its skin’s pigment, its camouflage and its memory, glowing in patterns that map out old wounds, rich veins, and the ghosts of settlements.

The builder-hollows are its scars. The cleared grinding circles are its calluses. The whole plateau is in a state of constant, hyperactive repair and growth because it is its own ecosystem. It is a single organism playing host to a million smaller, faster organisms that are all, in fact, aspects of itself.

To stand here is to stand on the living skin of something vast, ancient, and deeply absorbed in its own noisy, mineral-based existence. It does not notice you as a separate entity. You are, at best, a new pressure on its crust, a new sound in its air, a potential source of interesting salts. Its only rule is the rule of its own body: to grow, to be worn down, to be recalibrated, and to hum, constantly, with the deep, resonant vibration of a world without silence.
