The Drowned Hamlet breathes with layered life, each occupant woven into its submerged and emergent architecture:

1.  **The Architects of Stone and Timber (Remembered):** Though gone, their presence lingers in the very bones of the place – the mortise-and-peg joinery, the thick foundations defining submerged rooms, the geometry of the paths now serving as waterways. Their craftsmanship is the scaffold for the new world.

2.  **The First Wave: The Colonizers (Below the Waterline):**
    *   **The Wall Weavers:** Rushes, thick-stemmed and dense, have grown through and around the submerged timber frames. They are no longer mere plants; they are structural reinforcement, living mats forming the new lower walls, holding back the silt and the pressure of the water.
    *   **The Canopy Makers:** Pondweeds with broad, translucent leaves form layered underwater forests, filtering light into the drowned rooms. Water moss clings to every stone, softening edges into velvet. Duckweed and frogbit paint calm surfaces in floating green skins.
    *   **The Ceiling Weavers:** Where thatch rotted, lily pads and water-lettuce formed dense, floating mats. From below, they are stained-glass canopies veined with light; from above, they shudder with the passage of unseen things. Rain drips through them, slow and persistent.
    *   **The Drifters & Dwellers:** Silt, leaf-litter, and dislodged duckweed pads travel the gentle currents flowing along the old stone paths. Mosquitoes and midges cloud the air above, their hum a physical vibration. Dragonflies, iridescent and enormous, hunt them with whirring wings.

3.  **The Tenants of the Deep (Submerged Rooms):**
    *   **The Patrollers:** Perch claim the larger chambers – dark shapes drifting in loose schools through the green murk, hanging in doorways, circling the old hearthstones.
    *   **The Residents of Light:** Minnows occupy shallower, brighter rooms near the waterline.
    *   **The Transients:** Trout pass through on their circuits of the basin, following the spring-fed current, pausing in the larger rooms before moving on.
    *   **The Bottom Feeders:** In the deepest, darkest cellars, stone loaches and bullheads nose through the accumulated silt – the ground-down sediment of the old hamlet.
    *   **The Sentinels:** Newts cling motionless to submerged timbers for hours, their vivid orange bellies a startling contrast against the dark wood.
    *   **The Scrapers & Clickers:** Snails rasp on stone surfaces. Freshwater shrimps click against submerged wood. Fish bump muffledly against old walls in the dimness.
    *   **The Air Seekers:** Trapped air pockets shift and groan within the timbers, eventually escaping in sudden, effervescent bursts of bubbles that ripple the surface.

4.  **The Tenants of the Air (Dry Upper Storeys & Trees):**
    *   **The Rulers of the Roof:** Herons stand statuesque on peak-roofs and colonize the basin-edge trees, their deep, hoarse croaks echoing constantly.
    *   **The Diving Jewels:** Kingfishers flash from exposed roofbeams, plunging into the water below.
    *   **The Wing-Spreaders:** Cormorants perch on rooftops, wings outstretched to dry, punctuating the air with guttural grunts.
    *   **The Chorus Layers:** Small birds – finches, warblers – fill the trees above the waterline with urgent, continuous song.
    *   **The Chittering Shadows:** Bats inhabit the roof-space of the dry upper rooms, emerging at dusk with a faint chittering, leaving droppings on the floorboards.
    *   **The Web Spinners:** Spiders have built webs across every gap, every window frame, catching the insects that rise from the water.

5.  **The Lords of the Shore (Basin's Edge):**
    *   **The Sliding Families:** An otter family has denned where trees meet water. Their muddy slides scar the banks. They move through the submerged rooms with fluid ease, fishing and playing – permanent, confident residents of this waterlogged architecture.

6.  **The Current Inhabitants (The People):**
    *   **The Adapters:** Fifteen to twenty souls, two or three families. Descendants of those who stayed or returned. Living in the dry upper storeys, reached by ladders or boats tied to window-frames.
    *   **The Skilled Hands:** Wielding the same tools as the original builders – axes, adzes, awls, hooks, lines – the tradition unbroken, merely repurposed. Axes shape boats now too. Hooks fish the flooded rooms. Lines map depths, currents, shifting air pockets.
    *   **The Navigators:** They fish the submerged rooms, harvest rushes for weaving and fuel, gather duckweed. They keep a few ducks nesting on the floating mats. Every submerged wall, every cellar drop, every current and eddy is mapped in their memory, passed down like breathing.
    *   **The Learners:** Children master the waterways in stages – first the dry rooms, then the shallow margins, then deeper water, finally the full circuit by boat.
    *   **The Settled:** Not sad, not mourning. This *is* their world – high, cool, green, loud, and slow. The water is not an invader but the medium of life. They plan in generations: which rooms will flood next, where to build the next dry space, how to train the future navigators. Their smoke rises, thin and white, smelling of wet green rushes burning.

**The Collective:** What lives here is not a list of species, but a *system*. The humans are not masters, but participants. The walls breathe, the ceilings shudder, the floors teem. The old paths carry water, the old rooms house fish, the old roofs shelter birds and people alike. The sound – heron croaks, frog thrums, insect hums, water laps, timber groans, human voices – is the constant breath of this slow, settled, profoundly adapted community. Life hasn't reclaimed the ruins; it has *become* the architecture, and the remaining people are part of its living structure.
