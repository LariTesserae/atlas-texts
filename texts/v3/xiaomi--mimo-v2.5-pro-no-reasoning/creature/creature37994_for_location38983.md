What lives in the Vatry is not *in* it so much as *of* it. The distinction is crucial. These are not colonists; they are continuations.

**The Verdure.** The primary life is the plant life, and it is not merely vegetation—it is a fungal-plant symbiosis that has long since merged with the building's own systems. The mosses are not just moss; they are hyphae-rich, mycorrhizal mats that feed on the mineral runoff from the corroding iron, weaving through rust and rivet in a slow-motion digestion. The ferns are epiphytes that have abandoned soil entirely; their roots grip directly into the condensation-slick walls, drawing nutrients from the biofilm of algae and bacteria that coats every damp surface. The woody vines, which some might mistake for straggling ivy, are in fact a climbing fig relative that has evolved, over centuries, to etch its tendrils directly into the oxidized metal. It does not damage the structure; it reinforces it, its vascular network forming a secondary lattice of living wood that patches rust-holes and holds decaying plates together. This symbiosis is so complete that the plants regulate the building’s interior climate: transpiration adds humidity, which condenses and is recirculated by the pumps, which is warmed by the turbines, which provides the vapor the plants need. They are the building’s lungs and sweat glands.

**The Fauna, by Stratum.** The animal life exists in strict vertical and functional layers, each adapted to a specific zone of the Vatry’s anatomy.

*In the Ceiling Void (Upper Glass & Iron Canopy):*
Here, in the perpetual twilight above the catwalks, live the **Glass-Weavers**. They are a type of caddisfly, but their larvae have adapted to a unique niche. They spin their cases not from sand or twigs, but from crystallized mineral deposits harvested from the inner surfaces of the clouded roof panes. Their larval homes look like tiny, twisted glass beads. They feed on the algae that grow in the condensation films, and their slow, scraping movement is one of the few sounds in the upper air besides the wind and the hum. The adult moths are their pollinators and dispersers, but also a primary food source for the birds.

*Among the Catwalk Jungle (The Central Vegetated Layer):*
The small, dun-colored birds—the **Rivet-Finch** and the **Vent-Hatcher**—are the most visible fauna. They are silent singers; their vocalizations are outside the range of human hearing, pitched above the constant low hum that fills the structure. Their nests are architectural marvels of the place: woven from the tough, fibrous stems of the epiphytic ferns, lined with downy seed tufts, and anchored deep within the tangles of vine or the hollow of a corroded pipe. They are insectivores and occasional frugivores, eating the tiny, hard berries produced by a few of the woody plants. Their evolutionary path has been shaped by the Vatry’s light arrays. They are not crepuscular or diurnal, but **machinal**—they are most active during the long “day” phase of the ceiling lights, and roost during the “night” phase, regardless of the sun outside. Their skeletons in the summit volume are a testament to a final instinct: an urge to reach the highest point of their world, perhaps to die in the most direct light.

*The Insect Armies:*
The insects are the true workforce of the Vatry. Beyond the moths and flies, there are vast colonies of **Oxide-Beetles** (a sleek, black species of darkling beetle) that slowly consume the fungal growth on the machinery, their mandibles adapted to scraping bracket fungi off iron. There are **Glass-Mites**, near-microscopic, that live in the condensation films on every pane, grazing on bacterial blooms. And there are the **Pond-Skaters** of the lower vats, long-legged water striders that skim the surface of the black water pools, feeding on the detritus that falls from above.

*In the Depths (The Wet and Warm Lower Floors):*
Here, where the air is hot and saturated, the ecosystem simplifies. The dominant organisms are fungi and the **Pale Crawlers**—translucent, eyeless amphibians. They are neotenic, retaining their larval gills into adulthood, breathing through the dense, moist air and skin. Their translucent bodies allow a view of their simple organs and the slow movement of their meals—fungi and the occasional drowned insect—through their gut. They are scavengers and fungivores, and their only predator is the **Condensation Snake**, a slender, damp-skinned relative of the blindsnake, which hunts by sensing the minute vibrations of their movement in the water through its lower jaw. These snakes also eat the eggs of the beetles.

**The True Inhabitants: The Rhythms.**
But to say these creatures “live in” the Vatry misses the point. They are expressions of its continuity. Their breeding cycles are not tied to seasons, but to the **long cycle**—a machine-year, a period of about 13 months that the turbines and light arrays complete before repeating. Birds nest when the warming conduits in their chosen wall reach a specific temperature. Amphibians breed when the water pumps create a particular pattern of currents in the lower vats. The insects emerge in waves synchronized with the flickering of the light arrays.

Nothing here fights to survive. Everything here is in tune. The subsonic hum is not background noise; it is the base frequency of their world, the metronome to which their metabolisms are set. The warmth and damp are not conditions they endure; they are the very substance of their environment, as fundamental as soil and sun are to creatures outside. The Vatry is not their home. It is their biome, their weather, their geology. They have not adapted to a building. The building, over eight centuries, has become a habitat—a warm, damp, humming, and entirely self-consistent world—and they are its native, and only, children. They live in the space between the rust and the condensation, in the pause between the turbine's turn, and they do not know that outside, there is a sky of sharp blue and a wind that can kill. This is all. This is everything.
