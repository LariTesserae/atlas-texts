On the **Argentum Steppe**, life is a slow, geological process. The primary inhabitants are the **Argentum Grazers**, colossal, multi-legged arthropods with segmented bodies of dark, metallic chitin. They move in sparse herds across the vast basalt plains, their broad, flat-padded legs making only a soft, rhythmic *scrape* on the ancient stone.

Their heads are low-slung, equipped with mandible-like appendages that methodically scrape the black moss and armored lichens from the rock. This grazing is not merely feeding; it is the engine of the steppe's ecology. Their metabolism is a quiet industry: they consume the rock-bound life and, within their colossal bodies, process minerals and moisture to produce the calcium-like substance that forms the **Chitin Domes**. These vast, hollow, bleached structures, scattered like the discarded shells of impossible insects, are their generational handiwork, slowly added to over centuries with secretions mixed with wind-blown dust. The Grazers' waste products chemically alter the condensation pools, and their respiration contributes a faint, persistent ozone taste to the thin air.

The ecosystem revolves entirely around them. The sparse vegetation exists only in the shadows they do not constantly tread, and the water cycle is influenced by their presence. They are living factories, their internal processes the only true technology. Their sounds are the steppe's punctuation: the scrape of grazing, the soft settling of a leg on frost, the infrequent *tink* of cracking mineral crust. In the immense, silent scale of the plateau, they are patient, moving on cycles that measure in years, their lives measured not in heartbeats but in the slow accumulation of their own chitin and the imperceptible growth of their domes. They are the slow, metallic heartbeat of a stone world.
