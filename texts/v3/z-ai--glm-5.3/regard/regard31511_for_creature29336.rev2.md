A power that was both benevolent and wise would begin, as everyone here must, by reading the admission requirement.

Nobody on the Table owns anything. And it would take some time over this, because for a power of any size, ownership is the native vice. Such beings arrive in places carrying the usual gifts — restoration, permanence, justice,fix — and most places, being unfinished, need them. But this place finished its sentence a hundred million years ago, and everything since is the water's commentary, and the water is mid-sentence. A power that stepped in here and *finished* things would not be a benefactor. It would be a second flood, worse than the first, because the first flood at least had the decency to keep.

Consider what mercy would cost here. To raise Caldbeck would be to evict the perch from their roads, unhouse the caddis from their rose-stem cases, fire the cormorants from the tower, and take the calendar out of the daffodils' keeping — to unmake, in a gesture of pity, eighty years of a parish's patient reassembly by tenants who were never asked and pay no rent. To still the drawdown, to fix the level, to give the Strand a permanent address — that would be to kill the gardener, and the gardener never rests here; that is the whole arrangement. To lift the fish over the Edge by miracle, and let the Hopper rust unused, would be to cancel the liturgy, and the liturgy, running mostly without witnesses, is the point of the witnesses.

No. A wise power would see within the first morning — standing at the Edge in the last light, hand on the rock, listening to the rain's wall come on with that hiss like the sea entering a cave — that this is not a place that needs helping. It is a place that needs *holding*, and it has already solved that problem comprehensively, staffing itself with whatever could keep an appointment: char, caddis, ferryman, bulbs. The census is complete. There is one vacancy at most.

So the power would do the only thing the place permits anyone to do, which is join.

⁂

It would come in as weather, because that is the local form of arrival, and everything here is paid in weather. A squall that walks up the lake and does not rain, quite; a mist off the Edge at the wrong hour, noon instead of dawn; light on the water of a Tuesday afternoon with no obvious source, which the ferryman crosses without ceremony, or with the smallest ceremony there is. The wardens would log it in the flat professional grammar that has absorbed eighty years of mornings without comment — *Unseasonal calm, mid-lake. Barometer steady. Note.* — and file both facts, the way the warden on the causeway files the warm handrail, without opinion. The inn's dog would sleep through it, having long ago assigned all anomaly to the lake's business. The place has excellent defenses against the supernatural. Its defense is that nothing here is allowed to be more important than the level.

And the power would take a post. Not the top post — there is no top post; the wardens hold the gauge and the gates and the fish, the osprey wards the milfoil, the pike ward the crossroads, and the hierarchy goes down, not up, all the way to the Bottom. There is, however, the open question. The Landing holds that nothing mostly makes it through the works — the young salmon, going down fourteen hundred meters of steel and surge, toward a sea they have never seen. The Bottom holds that they mostly do. The arithmetic never closes; the fish come back in numbers no one can account for; the question has stood for eighty years, argued at the inn in Bottom time, unanswerable in principle because nobody can follow a smolt down a penstock and come back with anything but a story.

A benevolent power would close the arithmetic, and close it invisibly, the way everything here travels.

Not by miracle — a miracle would show, and what shows, here, is weather. Just by attention. A fish going down the works in the dark would find, some mornings, that the surge had a gap in it shaped exactly like a salmon; that the last gate had a soft place; that the terrible arithmetic had, without announcement, begun to lean toward the Landing's version of hope. No one would ever prove it. The counts would improve so slowly they could be argued with, and would be, at the bar, for decades — which is how you know the parish is still alive. The power would do for the smolts what the scheme does for the sea-run fish: pay the Edge's debt by hand, on a schedule, without witnesses. It is the local liturgy. It takes its members where it finds them.

⁂

Then, the small things, which are the only things.

It would stand among the daffodils in the spring, in oilskins it did not need, saying nothing, because there is nothing to say that the bulbs are not already saying more punctually. It would be one of the figures from the Landing, that week, unremarked among them. This is worth doing, and a wise power would know why: the bulbs bloom for whoever shows up. Eighty years, and somebody always has. The power would make certain of the *always* — quietly underwriting attendance, an unnamed and inexhaustible congregation — without ever letting the showing-up seem owed. Gratitude would ruin it. The daffodils keep the calendar on the understanding that the calendar is kept *for* them, not at them.

It would touch nothing in Caldbeck. The diver's whole profession is helping the lake keep, and the museum has no shop; the power has seen more museums than the lake has dreamed of and knows exactly one rule worth enforcing in all of them: *what the water holds, holds.* But once — this is the one liberty it would allow itself, and it would take some centuries deciding whether to — it might, passing the bicycle frame in the green light at twenty meters, where the bell still hangs from the handlebars, patient as the Pan — ring it.

Once. Quietly. The single note traveling wrong through the cold, felt in the sternum before heard, like the pumps, like everything down there.

And then leave the evidence as it found it, because the theology at the inn — two schools, no evidence — deserves better than resolution. A bell rung once by a passing power settles nothing; the argument would simply acquire a third school, *the divers heard it once in 'whatever, and it wasn't the pumps*, and the argument is more alive than any answer. The parish's deepest treasures are its open questions. The power would count the argument among the kept things the lake cannot reach, and leave it in the water.

⁂

Would it save the scheme? When the last gate rusts open and the Top Water walks off the Edge entire, in a season — would a benevolent power prevent that?

No. It would grieve, in whatever powers grieve with, and let it happen. Because the arrangement is the arrangement: the land finished ages ago, the water hasn't, and both parties are satisfied. The power did not write the sentence and has no standing to unsay it. What it would do — and this is the whole of its benevolence, held within the whole of its wisdom — is be there for the last run. When the final Hopper climbs the rope with its tank sloshing and the fish thumping softly, a heart with somewhere to be, and the door drops into a lake that is already walking backward off the Edge, the power would be standing at the Cut in the rain, under the bell buoy, watching the salmon go up into the sky one last time — the only witness the liturgy ever asked for, arriving eighty years late and exactly on schedule.

And afterward, on the finished Table — flat, and wet, and quiet, the last word a landscape is allowed to say — the seed bank would still be sleeping decades deep in the mud of the dales, and the Strand would grow whatever the new rain exposed, and the daffodils would keep whatever calendar was left to keep.

The power would hold the register. Someone should. The dead go uphill, and the christenings were entered at the lake, and the register never closed; it moved. It is moved *to*, here, in the end — every wardenry resolves into keeping, and keeping needs a keeper of last resort, and that is the one post that was always open, the one thing nobody here owns, because everyone here is only holding it for somebody.

A power that was both benevolent and wise would hold it. Say nothing. Log the weather.

Both parties, as far as anyone could ever tell, were satisfied with the help.
