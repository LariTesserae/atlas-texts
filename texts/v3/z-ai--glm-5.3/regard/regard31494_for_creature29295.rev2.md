It comes in through the hatch, because that is where everything comes in. It has been to galaxies; it has held dying stars the way you hold a fading lantern; it has walked through civilizations that begged it for things. It descends the ladder two meters, lands on the gravel with no crunch at all — nothing in this world weighs enough to announce it — and stops.

Not because something is wrong. Because nothing is.

This is the rarest thing it has found in ten thousand years of looking, and it takes it a moment, kneeling under the pipe canopy in the dim gold, to understand what it is seeing: a place that does not want anything. Every other world it visits is a petition. This one is a completed sentence. The slick feeds the springtails, the springtails feed the margins, the margins feed the cricket chorus, the chorus feeds the tide-literate cat, the moths close their loop in the spiders' ruin-webs, the guppies hold their sea, the concrete is fossilizing a living man's handprint palm and finger by finger, and the rain falls on the shift roster flavored with juniper. There is no gap in it. There is no one down here who is missing anything, because there is no one down here who has ever heard of anything to be missing.

So it does the first thing a wise power does in a finished world, which is the hardest thing for a power to do, which is: nothing. It crawls. It puts its palm on the wet gravel and covers a village and lifts it again and each springtail parts around the intrusion like smoke around a stone and rejoins, unharmed and uninterested, and it apologizes to them in the only language they have, which is to be still until the drift forgets it. It sits through a full pump cycle at the trench rim and feels the tide come in. It listens to the ten thousand drops land their percussion on steel and stone and it hears, what no one above the lid has ever heard, that the room is not quiet at all — that it is a tuned instrument, and the building above has accidentally built a music box and locked it under its own floor.

It attends the gecko on pipe six. It finds the eggs in the lagging seam and counts them and commits them to memory that does not need writing but will keep them anyway, forever, the way you keep a date. It looks at the sparrows in cable tray three, the bilinguals, dawn commuters to the real sky, and does not envy them and does not pity them. It goes to the sump and hovers over the red-rimmed pit and looks down at eleven generations of guppies who have never seen sky and it does not — this is the test, and it passes — it does not show them the sky. It understands that the sky, to a guppy of this nation, would not be a revelation. It would be an exile. Their entire cosmos is this hole, and the hole is enough, and a mercy that empties a world in order to enlarge it is not mercy. It simply watches them for a long time, the stocky dark shapes schooling in dim gold water, and it is — there is a word for what the maintenance men feel, and the power feels the larger version of it — proud of them.

Then it works. Not visibly. A power that loves a finished world does not add to it; it guards it, and the guarding is done in probabilities, in the margins, in the paperwork of the thin civilization above.

It goes to the sump pump and lays one fact over the machine like a hand on a shoulder: it will not fail on a weekend. Not ever. Some Friday-years from now a bearing will run hot on a Tuesday and be caught, because the nation in the pit must not ever, not once, hold its breath in vain. It goes to the hatch and warms the steel plate a half-degree beyond habit so that no one above ever remembers it is there. It goes into the future, briefly, where a safety consultant with a clipboard is scheduled to notice the condensate, the films, the whole illegal lushness, and order it scraped and sealed and sterile — and it loses that page of the clipboard. It will keep losing it. Some buildings are protected by being forgotten; this one is protected by being misremembered, and the power arranges that whenever a human above thinks of the underfloor at all, they think of it as a crawlspace, a void, a nothing, a place where pipes are, and the thought slides off like water off lagging.

It protects the correspondence. The jar lids along the drip-line, catching the cleanest gin-rain, emptied by a party who has never met the party who set them: this the power considers for a long time, kneeling in the aisle, because it could finish it. It could arrange the meeting. It is the work of a moment — a stuck door, a dropped tool, two people in the same aisle at the same hour, and the nation's only two citizens face to face.

It does not. A correspondence is not an unfinished thing. It is a form, complete, like a haiku, and the meeting would not complete it — it would translate it, into something smaller: a handshake, a laugh, an awkwardness, a name. The mystery is not standing in for the relationship; the mystery *is* the relationship, and it has held for months, conducted over the heads of guppies and drunk moths, and the wisest thing any power can do with a thing that works is stand where it cannot be seen and make sure nothing stops it. The lids will not be thrown away. The drips will not be re-routed. And when, years from now, one of the two correspondents leaves the plant, the power will see to it that the other one's lids stay out, filling and filling, for exactly as long as the milk of kindness keeps — because even unfinished letters have a grace period, and grief deserves a tide too.

It finds the office. Stool, tin, pallet, the paperback swollen to twice its thickness, the emery bookmark halfway in. It reads the paperback, all of it, in the space of a drip-fall — the power reads like light travels — and it reads the passage at the bookmark, and yes, it is a passage about patience, and yes, the reader stopped there on purpose, and the power leaves the emery cloth exactly on the same line. It sits on the stool for the length of one pump cycle, which is, in this nation, a season. Then it moves, because the stool is an artifact and artifacts should be used by hands, not by glories.

And then there is the tomato.

It stands before the seedling a long time. The seedling that came from a sandwich, growing leggy and earnest toward the noon lozenge that crawls, chasing its sun a few centimeters a day across the gravel, forever arriving, never to fruit, leaning anyway with the full seriousness of a plant that does not know what a building is. The power could open the roof. It could remake the light. It does none of that, because to remake the light is to remake the nation — the moss, the ferns, the borders of the light-country, the dry province of the mice — and the nation is finished, and it is not for the power to finish it differently.

But it does one thing. One afternoon, one time, never repeated: at the far end of the underfloor, the loading door rolls open at the low golden hour, and stays open eleven minutes longer than any schedule explains, and the sun comes in low along the expansion joint and the thin blade of light slides — as it never has before and never will again — all the way down the driest aisle, and lies across a leggy stem and its small yellow flowers like a hand.

Insects come, or don't; the power sees to it that they do — a few, blown in on the same draft, one afternoon's worth, a pollination the size of a whisper. Then the door rolls shut. The lozenge resumes its crawl. The seedling goes on leaning toward a borrowed sky that will never be enough, and it will die in its own time, as it always would have, having changed nothing.

Except that first, in the lowest country there is, watered on gin-rain and lit for eleven minutes by an arrangement nobody will ever audit, it sets one fruit. Small. Green, ripening to a red the color of the painted rim of the pit. Nobody will ever see it. Nobody is meant to. Almost nobody.

The cat sees it. She is on her loop, at her minute, never wrong about anything, and she pauses — the only unexplained pause of her career — beside the pallet for the time it takes a drop to fall, and looks at the fruit, and looks at nothing in the aisle where the power is standing, and the power understands that it has been seen, correctly assessed, and filed as something too big to be dangerous and too quiet to be food. Then she continues her loop. The power takes this as the certificate of its work, signed by the only notary in the nation whose judgment cannot be appealed.

At the end it goes to the sump one more time, because every visit should end at the sea. The pump swallows; the tide turns in the trench; the crickets come down to drink on the minute, in numbers, at the margin, inheriting the schedule of one dead cricket who happened to be in the right place eleven months ago — and the power, which has watched species rise and fall like weather, finds itself moved — actually, physically, whatever a power has instead of a chest — by this: that life does not need scale to be complete. That a nation can be the size of a manhole and have a people, a climate, a god on a timer, and a history. That a man's idle kindness with a feeder bag became a civilization. That this is how the universe does it everywhere, at every size, given any hole and any warmth and a little spilled sweetness: it leans toward the light, whether or not the light is enough. Whether or not the light even knows.

It climbs the ladder. It lowers the steel plate with the pull-ring back over the bright loud hall, warm to the touch, hinged like a coin drawer, exactly as it found it. The thunder stays above, a lid. The rain goes on falling from the pipes, flavored with juniper.

It keeps a record of everything — the power keeps records of everything it attends to, and it attends, now, permanently, here. The census: the eleven generations and the eleven to come. The gecko's eggs, and the gecko's eggs' eggs. The one fossil handprint, varnishing toward forever. The two citizens and their jar lids, unmet. The cat's loop. The cricket's inheritor's inheritor's song.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does with a place like this. It does not lift anyone out. It does not open anything that is closed or close anything that is open. It stands guard, at a distance, over a country that does not need saving, and it performs exactly one useless, gratuitous, unaccountable act of love — one fruit in the dark, seen by no one, eaten eventually by mice who will not know what it was — because it has learned, over ten thousand years and ten thousand worlds, that the deepest kindness is not rescue.

The deepest kindness is to have seen something completely, and let it go on being itself.
