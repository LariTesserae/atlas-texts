# What the Power Did

The power arrived the way everything arrives in Gallery Twelve: as pressure, felt before it was understood.

It came in through the north intakes with the cloud, or perhaps it *was* the cloud — a benevolent power does not force doors, and the hatch was not the only entrance anyway. It passed the warm flanks of the fins. It stood, if standing is the word, in the nave. It listened, in the way a power listens, which is to say it took the whole arrangement into itself at once: the banyan streaming north, the swifts folded into the beat, the moths oiling the rail with their lives, the frog nation singing through cast iron in its confessional of quiet, the falcon wearing the roar like weather, the honey of rust and condensate, the fan at three revolutions to the minute turning its slow whoomp through everything.

And it understood, first, what it must *not* do.

This is the mark of a benevolent power, and it is rarer than benevolence: restraint. Any lesser wisdom would have heard the roar and called it suffering. Would have heard the falcon's lost scream, the moths that never touch ground, the frogs that sing where they cannot hear, and called it damage — called the mountain a wound in the shape of a machine, and moved to heal it. Quieted the fan. Freed the birds. Restored the silence that every animal is owed.

The power saw what that kindness would be. It saw the swifts, whose wings are timed to the whoomp, starving in the sudden quiet. It saw the moths, who mate in the warm column, conveyed at last into a stillness that would simply drop them. It saw the frogs, exiles in the one quiet room, singing at last where they could be heard — and heard badly, and *colonized by hearing*, for a chorus that carries by air is a chorus that carries competition, and the pocket would fill and the pocket would not hold. It saw the garden failing in a season, the screens clogging with nobody's roots, the soil no longer made, the grease-cups no longer topped, the machine coasting to a stop with a fig for a heart. It saw a cathedral that had crossed over from being maintained to being metabolic, and it understood that the roar is not something *done to* the creatures of Gallery Twelve. It is something they are. Everything here is the roar, arranged. To remove it would not free them. It would unmake them, each one, in the specific shape their generations have taken.

So the power did not silence the mountain. It kneeled, in whatever way powers kneel, to the tuning of the fins.

But benevolence is not only restraint, and wisdom is not only the wisdom to leave things alone. The power looked longer, and it saw the truth beneath the miracle: the fragility. It saw one fan. One governor of clockwork, sized like a font, with one set of bearings. One banyan holding the screens, one bee colony in one junction box, one falcon through one failed louver. It saw the ledger, decades between entries, the last one years old: *Figs good this year. Fan slower, I think.* It saw that "slower" written in a hand that had no way to check, and it saw the generations of E., thinning like the entries, and it understood that Gallery Twelve is not immortal. It is alive, and everything alive can die, and everything alive *with one of each part* dies easily. The Works became an organism, yes. An organism with no redundancy. A body with one heart, and the heart is a fan whose bearings are fed by its own overflow, and the overflow by its own garden, and the whole loop is beautiful and the whole loop is one broken gear away from ending.

The lowlands would not even know for a hundred years. The clouds would keep passing. The range would just sit there, blue in the morning, black at dusk, dead.

So the power did three small things, which is the number of things benevolent powers do when they are wise.

**First, it blessed the loop, not the machine.** There is a difference, and it matters. To bless the machine would be to freeze it — to guarantee the fan, the fins, the tuning, forever as they are, a specimen preserved. Instead the power laid its attention along the *relations*: the roots and the screens, the lint and the soil, the soil and the figs, the figs and the air, the air and the water, the water and the grease-cups. It did not make the loop unbreakable. It made the loop *forgiving* — gave the system, as it were, a metabolism's quiet talent for rerouting around small wounds. A fin-section now can fail without taking the chord. A screen can clog without strangling the fig that clears it. If the banyan one year fruiting badly, the swifts can nest. Not immortality — an organism does not want immortality, it wants convalescence. The power gave Gallery Twelve the ability to heal, which is the only gift a living system can accept without being destroyed by it.

**Second, it went to the frogs.** Because the power understood something that no lowland engineer and few climbers have ever understood: Gallery Twelve kept one voice. Behind the banyan's trunk, in the cast-iron stairwell where the fin-tuning cancels itself by accident of geometry, the mountain *chose* — or stumbled into — a place where singing is possible. The power did not enlarge the pocket. It did not add quiet rooms, which would dilute the nation that lives in the one. But it touched the iron of the stairwell's railing, the place where a climber who has stood an hour in the roar puts a hand and feels the chorus come up like a second pulse. And it made that pulse carry. Not far. Not into the roar, where it would die. Down. Down the cast iron, down the downcomers, into the pipes, into the reservoirs, the orchards, the thirst. So that now, on certain evenings in the lowlands — and the lowlanders have never once connected this — the water arrives with a faint wrongness in its taste, a mineral note, a sweetness, and the old women say the mountain is singing, and they are right, and the frogs of Gallery Twelve are heard, at last, by an entire nation that will never climb to thank them. The power does not think this gift is wasted on the unthanking. Gifts of that kind never are.

**Third, it wrote in the ledger.** This took the longest, because a wise power knows what it is tampering with: the ledger is the gallery's memory, sparse, punctual, patient, and it must stay *human* or it stops being memory and becomes monitoring. So the entry is small. It sits above the last, in pencil, in a hand that E. — whichever E. climbs next — will assume belongs to another climber, some cousin from the lowlands, a stranger who came up through the trunk-ladders and stood in the roar and did not stay the night. It says:

*Fan slower, yes. Checked the governor — one tooth of the escapement is worn near the changeover. Grease-cup on the north bearing runs dry before the overflow tops it; there is a channel you can clear, behind the fig's third root, that restores the feed. I did not clear it. It will last one more season, maybe two. Do it yourself. It should be a hand.*

*Figs were good. Bees strong. The falcon's nest has two eggs. The frogs are singing. You'll feel it at the stairwell.*

*— E.*

And that is the whole of the entry, and it is the largest of the three gifts, though it looks like the smallest. Because the power understood the true fragility of Gallery Twelve, and it is not the fan. Machines become metabolic; gardens become factories; loops close and hold for centuries on their own. What the gallery cannot make for itself is a keeper. The banyan does not know that the mountain is being forgotten below. The moths do not descend to the lowlands to say *we are up here, we are your water, climb*. E. is the gallery's only voice pointed outward, and E. is one dynasty of tired hands, a parish office nobody applied for. So the power wrote to E. the way you write to a colleague: with one fact, one task, one trust. It did not fix the escapement itself, though it could have made the clockwork whole with a thought. It left a worn tooth, and a description, and a reason to climb. *It should be a hand.* The machine crossed over from being maintained to being metabolic — but the crossing is not complete, and perhaps it should never complete, because a place that no longer needs *anyone* is a place that will be forgotten the moment the first gear forgets. The ledger keeps the knowledge of the keeping alive. So the power, who is wise, kept the ledger alive by writing the smallest possible true thing in it.

Then it left the way it came, with the cloud, out the intakes — or perhaps it stayed; a benevolent power is not in a hurry, and the gallery is a good place to be. If it remained, it is not in the garden, which is already full. It is in the interval — in the half-second between seeing the blades pass and feeling the whoomp arrive, in the gap the machine leaves, where the swifts hunt. It is the kind of presence that would like a room like this: a place where the dead sections of rake became trellis, where a swallowed gauge still reads the humidity of 1941, where the dirt is made of moths and the honey tastes of rust, where even the roar, given centuries, arranged itself into chords.

The power does not want worship; there is nowhere here to speak a prayer anyway. But if you climb the trunk-ladders all day, and read the ledger, and go through the hatch, and stand the hour in the roar until it becomes a floor — then put your hand on the stairwell rail behind the banyan's trunk, where the frog nation sings through iron — you will feel two pulses come up under your palm.

One is the mountain's apology.

The other one, the fainter one, the second, is something else. It feels, the climbers say, like being watched by something that has decided, on the whole, looking at everything, to leave you exactly as you are — and to make sure you last.

That was what the power did. And the figs, that year, were good.
