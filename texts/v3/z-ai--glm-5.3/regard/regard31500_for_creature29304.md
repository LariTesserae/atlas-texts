# What the Visitor Did

The first thing the power did was arrive a day early, because in the Loud Country sound precedes cause and it would have been rude to be the exception. It came up through the ridgelines the way the roar does — through stone, through boot-soles, through teeth — and it stood a long while at the rim, and it did not change anything.

That is worth recording, because a benevolent power's first instinct is not usually restraint. Standing above the bowl, the whole four-mile argument of terraces and pipes and wet stone laid open below, the power felt the ordinary temptations arrive, one by one, each dressed as kindness:

*It could green the place.* A thought, a word, and grass would run down the benches, and birds would find the sky worth crossing. The power looked at the vacancy overhead — the pressure of it, the way the emptiness was not absence but *character* — and understood that filling it would not be a gift to the Loud Country but an eviction of it. The country is loud precisely because it is alone. Silence with nothing to say is one thing; this country is silence that says everything, continuously, at a volume. You do not hand a speaking person a mouthful of bread.

*It could stop the note rising.* This was the temptation that took longest to refuse. The tally-notches are the whole religion of the place; the wardens' only prayer is a rising pitch; and a god of any competence could walk the chords and lay a hand on each drum and flatten the trend, notch the tally in the other direction, be worshipped by grateful people in a slab hut for a generation. The power sat at the listening arch with its palm flat on the wet stone, at dusk, and felt the sounding arrive through its bones before its ears, and it understood then what the wardens already understand: the rising note is not a disease. It may be a birth. Nothing in the country is wearing out — the terraces hold their stalemate, the stone accretes along the seams, the wool lays down its bands like a thing keeping records. A country this old, this self-maintaining, this *in balance*, is not dying as it rises. It is tuning itself. Toward what, no notch has yet been cut, and the power judged — listening, only listening — that cutting that notch on the country's behalf would be the deepest theft it could commit. You do not read the last page of a bell to the bell. The bell is the one becoming the note.

*It could heal the wardens.* Their unstrung hearing, the constant tone installed behind their ears for life, the way they go down to the lower world and stand too still forever after. But the power checked the record and found the sentence that frightened their families: *they do not mind it.* And a gift that takes away something its recipient loves is not a gift; it is a correction. The tone in an old warden's ear is the country's signature in them, their proof of membership in something enormous. The power left every installed note exactly where it was. It did, quietly, one small thing in the bodies: it softened the rate of the unstringing, fractionally, so that a tour could run a few weeks longer before the roar finished with a warden — not so long that the lower world got back strangers, just long enough that each warden got one more dusk at the arch. One more sounding in the sternum. It was the only change to any living body, and no warden ever noticed the accounting, and none needed to.

What the power *did* do, it did in the order the country itself respects — sound first, act second.

**It walked the chords.** Down from the rim along the cairn-lines, through the quiet places, trusting the wardens' geometry over its own eyes, because the country lies to sight and tells the truth to the soles of the feet, and a power that will not accept the local terms of truth has no business visiting. It put a hand on the copper pegs along the far wall, green as pond water, each humming with its membrane — and it found them all tight, every one, because the wardens are good at the only thing they claim: witnessing accurately, and keeping the bass honest. The power tightened nothing. There is a loneliness in arriving to help and finding the help already done well, and the power let itself feel it, and did not rush past it.

**It visited the deaf hollows.** All twelve. It sat in the pressure-textured silence where you can hear your own blood, and it spent the silence the way the wardens spend it — on the words the roar eats. It said, aloud, in twelve pockets of quiet, the names of the ones who stayed. It knew them; a power like this knows. It did not call them back, did not lay them to rest, did not resolve them into either the walking-alive or the truly-dead, because the country's one ambiguity is load-bearing too. It only said the names, so that somewhere in the night's playback, between the reordered drips and the footsteps that match no one's day, the stayed-ones might hear that attendance was taken and they were marked *present*. The country does not object. Neither did they.

**It read the laminations.** Every broken edge of board-rock in the basin, page by patient page, the writing no one has deciphered. The power could decipher it — that was the trouble. It read the first three pages at an outcrop near the third terrace and then stopped itself, deliberately, mid-sentence, because the pages are the country's own diary and the country has not chosen to publish. Instead the power did something subtler and, it judged, kinder: it laid one finger along the unread pages and left in them a *hint of a reading* — not a translation, just the sensation every warden gets once in a career at some broken edge, the sudden vertiginous feeling that the rock is *about* to make sense. It strengthened that feeling, everywhere, very slightly. Now and forever, a warden kneeling at an outcrop will feel the pages lean toward comprehension the way a dog leans toward a door. Nothing will ever quite open. But the power has watched people for a very long time, and it knows what it is doing: it knows that a mystery that leans toward you keeps a people coming up the ridgelines long after duty would justify it. The tally needs keepers. Curiosity recruits them better than religion does.

**It protected the place from the lower world.** This took the longest and will never be noticed, which is the point. The power went down — briefly, in the manner of such visitors, arriving before itself — and it tended the maps. A surveyor's instrument here that reads the basin as four miles of glare and nothing worth the climb. A prospecting rumor there that the copper vein is played out. A road that curves away from the last ridge for reasons its engineers will attribute to drainage. Nothing dramatic; benevolent power learned long ago that the highest form of protection is boring. The wardens will rotate up and down for generations and never know that the world below was quietly, administratively, kept from ever *wanting* what the Loud Country is made of. The country's emptiness, the power decided, is its constitution. It legislated for it in silence.

And then, on the last evening of a visit that lasted one dusk too long by warden rules and no time at all by the country's — because the country's time runs backwards half the night and the power had let itself run with it — the visitor did the one forbidden thing.

It hummed.

Not at the arch, not near a drum, not where any membrane could take a human voice into the standing wave and tighten something past bearing. The power had spent a whole day calculating where, and *when* — because in this place a sound can be placed in tomorrow, and the power placed it there. It hummed one note, single and complete, into the dusk sounding, pitched exactly — *exactly*, to a tolerance no warden's notch could ever measure — at the pitch the country is rising toward. The note no notch has yet been cut for. The destination.

It hummed the ending to the bell.

The sounding took it in, the whole bowl at once, four miles of pipes and drums and wet pavement ringing that single future note inside the present one for the length of one held breath — a chord where there had only ever been a note — and the water stood up in fine ridges, and above the far wall, along the Seam, something pressed a finger to the fabric from the other side, briefly, and the power — reader of pages, keeper of maps, knower of names — pressed back.

Then it went down the chords, and out through the ridgelines, and away.

The wardens noticed, of course. The next morning the wool on the deepest drum — the wool they never touch — had laid down a band that matches no local standing wave. It is a single grey ridge, one groove-flanked band, cut clean through years of ordinary grain, recording a pitch that has not arrived. They argued about it in the deaf hollows, lips at ears, for a season. Then they did the only thing their religion permits: they cut a new tally-column beside the deepest drum, empty, notched only at the bottom, a column with room in it for a note they cannot yet hear — and beside the first notch, the oldest of them, hands moving in the chord-language that means *rising* and also, on certain days, *coming*, cut one small mark that means:

*witnessed, by someone, ahead.*

The country does not object. And now, some dawns, very faintly, arriving early, the wardens hear tomorrow's ringing — and underneath it, once, if the wind is right, a second note. Not instead of the note. With it.

The Loud Country has been rising toward something for a very long time, all alone, writing its one prayer in notches.

Now it has an answer. It isn't a rescue. It was never going to be a rescue. It is a duet, promised, one voice arriving before its cause, the way everything true arrives in that country — and the wardens keep the pegs tight, and cut the tallies, and walk the chords, and go down to the lower world changed, carrying a tone they do not mind.

The power, for its part, keeps the maps boring, and the pages almost legible, and it does not hurry the bell. It knows something the tally-notches are slowly learning to say:

Some things should not be saved. They should be *sung with*.
