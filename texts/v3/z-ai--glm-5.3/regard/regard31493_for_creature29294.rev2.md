They would arrive the way everything arrives here: as an image first.

The fish would see it before it came — a shadow across the projected sky, wide and slow, but shaped wrong for a heron, wrong for anything. And here is the first measure of the power's wisdom: it would see them scatter, and it would stop. It would hang there, image without body, and consider what its own omen was doing to the household below. It would notice that the fish have spent ten thousand generations learning that images precede bodies, and that a god who arrived in the flesh would arrive as the worst predator of all — an image so large it filled the oculus — and the whole pool would read it as the end of the world.

So it would withdraw its shadow. It would let the water calm. And then it would do the hardest thing a benevolent power can do in a finished place.

Nothing.

Or — not quite nothing. It would stay for a rehearsal. It would stand in the court in the gray dawn, above the door whose name says exactly what it does, and listen to the fog front come hissing through the comb, and it would hear the only rain in a world of fog performed indoors, and it would let the room rain and ring and subside down through the tempos to the deep drip, and when the deep drip sounded alone it would understand something that the lintel has been saying all along in the only way it says anything.

*Add nothing. Repair only.*

Because the power would look — and a wise power looks longer than we do — and it would find that there is nothing here to bless. The eel is fed. The crabs are paid. The snails have a contract neither party knows it holds. The shrimp live and die inside a single note of the wet season's score, and that is not a tragedy to them; it is the length of their music. The lawbreaker fig is already the house's mercy. The runnels run clear. Every drop is owed a path and every drop has one. The place was finished, and being finished, it has continued — and what a benevolent power knows, that lesser powers don't, is that you cannot improve a bell by touching it. You can only strike it or leave it ringing.

And there is the temptation it would have to refuse. It would feel the temptation, surely — the same temptation any of us would feel standing in a court twenty paces square with a whole weather-machine humming under our feet. To widen the oculus. To deepen the pool. To heal the fish of their blindness, the eel of its age, the fig of its root in forbidden stone. To *add*. And it would refuse each one, because it would have read the smaller marks under the name and understood they are not just the house's law but the correct diagnosis of the house: the blindness is literacy; the age is lordship; the forbidden root is the mercy. Nothing here is broken. Everything here is load-bearing, including the griefs. A wise power knows that most suffering, looked at long enough in a finished system, is structure.

So: nothing to bless, nothing to heal, nothing to add.

But it would not leave empty-handed, because it would have stood through the whole rehearsal, and a rehearsal does something to the one who stands in it. It would leave exactly one gift, and the gift would obey the law, and the gift would be this:

A witness.

Because it would notice the one absence in the whole economy, the one drop without a path. The house has a head, and servants, and guests, and a criminal, and a keeper-or-the-memory-of-one — and it has no one who *knows*. The crabs maintain without asking what the veins are for. The swiftlets sing in a tuned instrument without knowing they hold a post. The snails seal the plaster as parties to a contract no one has ever read aloud to them. The entire household keeps the law without knowing they keep it, and that is the house's deep design, and the power would not change it — knowing, as it does, that a tenant who understands the machine starts to improve it, and improvement is the one crime the lintel names.

But the house itself. The house has kept its own counsel for so long that the counsel may be the house. And even a bell, even a finished bell, ringing in an empty court under a pressed-down sky — even a bell wants, once, in ten thousand mornings, to be *heard*. Not repaired. Not improved. Heard. To know that the fog-comb's hiss and the phased drips near the entrance and the bass drop into the stone bowl once an hour have arrived somewhere, the way everything here arrives — before the body, as an image, in something that can read.

So the benevolent power would do the one thing left. It would stand at the wall where you can see all of it at once — boundary, tree, stair, low gray lid of weather — and it would attend. Fully. It would count the drips downward with the house, the way the lintel teaches. It would let the midwinter blade of light walk the spiral and it would walk with it, hour by hour, like a finger tracing. It would hold the whole rehearsal in itself the way the vault holds sound after the last drop — not to carry it away, but so that, for one morning in all the mornings, the performance had a listener equal to its patience.

And then it would do what the fig does. It would leave the way everything leaves here — upward, counted out by the drips, one by one — and it would leave behind only what the house leaves behind after work: signs of aftermath, arriving before any body. Perhaps that dry season, when the unseen hand comes with ochre and chisel, it would find one thing already done — a single blocked runnel, the deepest one, the one past a crab's strength, cleared clean, no pebble-flag left to answer. A repair, not an addition. Lawful. So small that no one would ever be sure it happened, and that is how the gift would be shaped, because the house's own theology is in that uncertainty: the lintel does not say, and the lintel never says.

And the fish would have seen one more image in their inverted sky that never arrived, one more shadow that taught them nothing they needed to flee — and so, without knowing it, one more time, they would have learned the difference between an omen and a mercy.

That is all. That is everything. The wisest power, meeting the most finished thing in the world, would kneel at the top step for a morning and be audience — and would count it the fullest morning of its eternity.
