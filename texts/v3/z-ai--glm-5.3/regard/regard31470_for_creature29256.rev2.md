# The Guest Who Built Nothing

## Where the love was already sufficient

You will not find it in either atlas.

The ascending atlas and the descending atlas disagree about nearly everything — the same stones under different names, whole streets filed twice, distance above the tenth tier hedged in knots — and the cartographers have survived by never saying aloud that both are right. But on one point the two books close ranks, seamless and uncharacteristic: no such guest was ever registered. No name offered at the gate. No entry in either bell-ledger at arrival. In a city where discrepancy is law, the visit has the shape of its own absence, and you should understand that this was the first miracle, and that it was deliberate.

Because something did come. The place — and the being who lives in it, the youngest resident, the one your informant will name last, looking up at the veiled summit — drew the attention of a power that was benevolent and, rarer, wise. It came up on a hoist-platform in no season of urgency, carrying luggage that could be abandoned without grief, which is how the locals say a person arrives correctly. The gate-keepers, who can spot the finished at a hundred paces — decisive luggage, conclusions where the eyes should go — did not flag it. Later they agreed on why. It had the one thing the finished never carry: it was not done either. Wisdom, whatever else it is, turns out to be the kind of knowledge that keeps generating questions, and the gate reads that condition better than any theology in the lowlands.

It gave no name. The city, which withholds names from the things that come down wrong-angled, understood at once, though the reasons ran opposite: names are invitations, and what is invited stays — the nameless hunters are kept out that way — and this guest, whatever it intended, intended eventually to leave. A permanence here would have been a kind of finishing, and it had come, specifically, to finish nothing.

It took a room in a dark-quarter, where noon is a rumor and leases are cheap, and paid its rent in labor at the going pace, which is to say slowly. Children saw it clearly, which should have settled the matter; children also see the second image under the puddles, which does not. Adults said they could not see it at all, which is not the same as being unable.

## The refusals, which were the monument

Begin with what it declined, because in this city the refusals are the monument.

Consider the temptations laid out for a benevolent power with ability to match its attention. It could harden the rock — end the shedding, the slumping, the whole adolescent geology — and every terrace would grey over within a season, because the dreaming rock feeds what grows on it, and the green that shouldn't be here is here anyway, and the ninth course of the Dew Stair is the Lindens. It could finish the crown, pour completion down the summit like a blessing, and no one living would ever again be mid-becoming in the family sense — the debt of stone paid off early, the grandchildren defrauded of their inheritance, the cradle-hum of the works gone suddenly quiet, the quiet of a finished thing. It could straighten the nine-and-ten stair, un-mirror the chuffs, dry out the breathing houses, collapse the two atlases into one correct one. It could answer, with a single word, the argument about the Scaffold Beyond. It could lift the taken down out of the mountain's dream, gentle as moving a sleeping hand. It could add a century to the founders and un-ache every joint on the lower terraces.

Every one of these mercies would have been the same murder: the murder of a becoming by a conclusion. You do not finish someone else's childhood. You do not answer a question the child is still learning how to ask. The rule at the base of the Scaffold Beyond — *do not climb toward it* — is not about danger, the locals will tell you; it is about manners; you do not walk into a child's dream while the child is still deciding what it is. The power read that rule once, I am told, and was quiet a long while, and thereafter kept it the way the mountain keeps its veins — from the inside. The locals wrote it for rude climbers. It happens to be the only ethical document I know of that was drafted to god scale without anyone noticing.

It did not banish the hunters, either, because banishment is a decision, and decisions up here belong to the mountain; and because — if what the lamplighters never say is true — the hunters may be nothing worse than the child's bad dreams, shed downhill as it turns in its sleep, and you do not amputate a child's nightmares. You sit where they land.

So when it acted, it acted at the tempo of the place, in denominations the place already minted. What follows is the near-complete catalogue, assembled from two atlases, two bell-ledgers, one lamp-register, and a drawer. You will notice it is short, and quiet, and that every item is deniable. This was not stealth for its own sake. In a city whose whole genius is holding two truths without dropping either, a mercy that can be read two ways is the only mercy that doesn't cost the place its mind.

## A season of blank pages

It apprenticed itself to the masons.

An apprentice's first commission is to sit a season with a settling foundation and report what the stone does. The diligent fill notebooks with weather. The promising turn in blank pages. The guest sat a season on the sixth terrace, through the dew-tides and the drizzle and the oceanic drone, and turned in blank pages, and the master who received them — one of the first masons, hands too swollen for a chisel, the kind children are brought to touch — held the pages a long moment and nodded, and that was the entire ceremony. *It held.* No report was ever so complete.

There is an apprentice on that same terrace who once stood at dawn gripping her mending-stone before a doorway that had not been there that morning. You may have heard of her; the guidebooks keep her. What the guidebooks do not keep is that a lodger from the dark-quarters stood beside her for the length of that dawn, looking at the doorway with her — no hurry, no advice, both breaths fogging — and then, because eventually someone has to know whether a thing bears weight, walked through it, and came back the ordinary way, by the stairs, and said: *it holds.*

She left her notebook blank about it. The masters nodded. The doorway, incidentally, is still there on the days it is, and not on the days it is not, and this satisfied everyone — including, as far as anyone can tell, the doorway.

## The night the arcs did not flare

It walked rounds with the lamplighters, in the season when their work stops being maintenance and becomes ministry. Banking the reflector-lanes at dusk, slowest at the uppermost, which they let burn a little long out of respect; the guest let them burn a little longer than long, and stood where the light ends while the guild descended, and what it stood as, that night, was the fence.

There was one descent all that year. The record is firm on this in the way records here are firm — corroborated, contradicted, kept. Something long, low, wrong-angled came down past the tenth tier. The chuffs stopped first, the murmur dying in a spreading wave, a held breath moving through the scaffold; the upper arcs flared; the forges lit the night shift whose real product is sound. And at the rule line there was already someone standing.

The witnesses were lamplighters, who decline to describe. What they do not describe is this: no battle, no word, no light brighter than disagreement. Only attention — the full, patient, unblinking regard of something that wished the creature no harm and no change whatsoever — and it turns out that being seen entire, by something with no fear of you and no use for you, is more than wrong angles can hold. The thing eased back up into the higher, colder altitudes, thinner than it came. The forges ran their shift anyway. Morale needs the rhythm.

What the hunters are, the guest learned that night, and never said. Knowledge of a thing is a name, and names are invitations, and the fence above the tenth tier has always been built of not-asking. It kept the knowledge the way the mountain keeps its veins. The lamplighters, who also know and also never say, recognized a colleague; it was invited on a second round, which in that guild is a knighthood.

In the morning the sweepers found, on a shed curl of dreaming rock at the rule line, the print of a hand — pressed shallow, almost fond, the shape of a hand that was holding rather than gripping. It went into a drawer at the watch-house, to be passed among the families on the bad evenings, and here is the drawer's whole mystery: no one who has held it has ever had a night bad enough to need it. That may be the point.

## The lamp with no grave

Come to the one evening in six, when the Scaffold Beyond shows its lamps.

The families climb to the rule line and stand looking up, arguing gently about which light is theirs. Among them, for one whole visit, stood an old woman from the seventh terrace whose husband had been a cartographer — one of the ones who knew that both atlases were right — and who climbed, one evening long ago, toward the one thing he could not put on either map, and the mountain took him the way a bed takes a sleeper. Her wage of grain for him has been in the same tin since, changed when grain must be changed, which is a sentence I refuse to make less astonishing than it is.

The guest stood beside her the whole of that evening, and looked up, and could have told her — with the authority of the only witness ever qualified to speak — which light was hers. It is possible that nothing in its long career cost more. What it said was: *that one burns steady.* Which is true of several. Which settles nothing. Which was therefore correct.

Some weeks later, in the upper works, the lamplighters found a lamp that no register accounted for. No oil-line. No lease. No name in the ledger of the dead among whom it plainly belonged. The guild's first instinct was jurisdiction; its second was the lamplighter's, and the second won: they added it to the works. It burns through weather that dims honest lamps. On the evenings-in-six it burns steady. The faithful have decided what it means. The engineers have decided what it means. The decisions do not agree; both are right; the guild survives by never saying so aloud — which is, when you think about it, the guest's signature: a mercy legible in every creed and mandatory in none.

The tin of grain it left entirely alone. That was deliberate, and it is the sentence I would put on the monument, if the city allowed monuments, which it does not, being itself one. The setting-aside is the love. A benevolence that fills the tin for you has taken the only task that was ever truly yours. It had come to a city that did its own loving, and it was not going to rob them of a single hour of it.

## Well-settled

You will want to know about the stair.

The oldest woman on the seventh terrace — she was born there, back when that terrace was the sky — sits on the nine-and-ten step with her granddaughter and two bowls of forge-warmed broth, both breaths fogging, waiting, comfortably, professionally, for the city to catch up with them. Going up, that stair is nine steps; coming down, ten; the guest, for whom every number is a negotiable altitude, counted neither, which the old woman approved of without saying so.

A chuff landed on its shoulder and went to sleep.

There is a tradition about this, and no foreman has ever successfully argued with it: the crews stand down; you wait on the bird. The guest stood down. Not for a moment — the chuff slept through the broth, through the fog, through the whole long patient pearl of the evening — and the guest held exactly as still as a gantry crane mid-reach, which is to say perfectly, which is to say *pausable*, and passers-by noted the stillness the way you note good masonry.

At the end of the evening the old woman said — about the stair, the broth, the city, the guest; no one knows which — *well-settled.*

Most strangers walk away not realizing they have received the place's highest praise. The guest realized. You could see it take the hit. Whatever it has been called, in whatever vocables, under whatever skies — and one may imagine the archive of titles a being like that hauls behind it like decisive luggage — I would wager none of them was ever true. This one was. It kept the word the way this city keeps its dead: settled into the newest course, and load-bearing now.

## It hummed over the hummers

And what of the being who lives here — the youngest resident, the mountain?

I think the power came for the child. What it found was a nursery already staffed: a whole civilization on shift, two heartbeats keeping time — ptarmigan and forge — a covenant performed nightly and never once spoken, *we build at your pace; in exchange, consent to be a place*, and the great drone of the works, which is, among other things, the hum an adult makes over a cradle. It had arrived expecting, at most, to take the night watch. The night was watched. The cradle was hummed.

So it did the only work left, the work almost no one ever thinks to do.

**It hummed over the hummers.**

That year the frost came gently, valley-gentle, an insult to the altitude that the altitude apparently forgave. The mist ran kind; the old joints of the lower terraces ached no worse than usual, which in this city counts as a festival. The drone dipped some nights toward silence and never once found it — a note under the note, sourceless, which the dew-wardens logged as *good drinking weather* and did not investigate, because a warden who investigated everything would soon have no pipes left to love. The hunters came down once, and thin. The chuff census, taken by ear, by children, recorded a murmuring a full contentment fatter than the year before. High above the tenth tier the child slept deeper, shed fewer dreams downhill, and took its time about everything, and nothing rushed it; and the masons, watching foundations take an extra season to settle, approved in the only language they have, which is waiting — because *nothing outruns the rock*, and now, for one year, nothing had to.

For one week in the deep of that winter, every lamplighter on the upper circuits slept like the dead they keep light for. No lamp failed. The guild investigated, found nothing, and — this is the part I love — did not investigate further. Some gifts you audit. Some you bank.

## The departure, in which both atlases agree

It left the way the dew does: present all night, gone by noon, the railings dry.

The bell-keepers keep two ledgers, one for bells rung and one for bells pre-heard, and the etiquette is strict: you never act on an early bell. Understand what this cost the guest. It had heard every early bell of its entire stay on the day it arrived — the whole year pre-heard, the departure included — and it acted on none of it, and answered no toll before its hour, and lived the whole visit in the deliberate present tense like everyone else. For a being of that kind, this amounts to fasting.

The night before it left, an entry appeared in the second ledger — a hoist-bell, dawn, no cause scheduled — and at dawn the bell rang, to the minute, and for the first time in the guild's memory both ledgers agreed on a bell that mattered. The keeper on duty took the day off and the strong drink, on the grounds that the tradition clearly covered something, and could not tell anyone whether it was for grief or celebration, and was told, pleasantly, that this was correct — the city living as it does in the present tense, and the ledgers agreeing being nothing more than the present and the future settling toward each other, which is what settling does, given love and time.

No letter ever arrived for it, before or after; the cartographers privately call this the cleanest address in the city.

What remained: one lamp with no grave, banked slowest of all, out of respect. One drawer-stone at the rule line, printed shallow, the shape of a hand that was holding. One cistern, on the third terrace, that has never sulked since. One apprentice's blank page. One grandmother's word, load-bearing now. One matched entry in two ledgers. And a green — this item belongs to the gardeners, and they will deny it the way they deny planting, which is to say by permitting — a green. You know the maps: the vegetation maps the moisture, and it maps the dead. That year, in three or four places — the rule line, the stair, the cistern's side — it also mapped something warm that stood very still through the cold watches, because condensation gathers where warmth stands, and the ferns noticed before anyone. The gardeners permitted it. Gardeners here do not plant. They permit. There was nothing to ask.

The chuffs, for the record, learned nothing. Again. The guest regarded this as the year's finest report: an entire class of being left entirely alone, and thriving, spherical and unimproved — which is, locally, the definition of thriving — and none the wiser, forever, which is how they like it and how the city likes them.

## Come to the rule line

Come, some evening one-in-six, when the haze above the summit works its one impossible trick and the Scaffold Beyond shows its lamps. Climb to the rule line with the families. Argue gently about which light is yours; take your time; it is the local way of loving. If the drawer-stone is passed to you, hold it the way you would hold a hand you trust — not gripped. Gripped is for the worst nights, and the odd mercy of this stone is that it has never yet met one.

And when the argument runs long, and someone — it will be a newcomer; it is always a newcomer — points up and asks about the one that burns steady, watch the lamplighters say nothing, pleasantly, and let it function as an answer.

You will ask, at the gate, on your way down, what a finished thing could possibly want with a city like this. Sabbath, I think. Rest. The chance, for one year, to be mid-becoming in company — the condition such a power is always being asked to grant, and is almost never offered back. It came able to end every waiting in the place, and instead it sat a season with a foundation, and stood down for a bird, and kept a rule that was never written for it, and lit one lamp that settles nothing, and left the tin of grain exactly, exactly as it was.

The geology has not finished deciding, and neither has anyone else. But for one year, in a light that barely consents to exist, something that had finished — long ago, and at whatever cost — decided to be unfinished again, in company; and the company, being well-settled, took it in without a question, the way the rock takes a sleeper, the way this city takes anything it loves: at its own pace, into the newest course, under a fern.
