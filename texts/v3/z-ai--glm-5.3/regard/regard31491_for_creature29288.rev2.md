# The Attention

The first thing the power did was nothing, and it did nothing for a very long time, because it understood immediately — being wise — that this was a place already finished to perfection, and that perfection, here, had the shape of a held breath. It did not warm the plain. It did not water it. It did not open the notch or explain the walkers or return the people, whose names it could have spoken and did not, because the Level had survived them by learning to be quiet, and a benevolent power does not undo an achievement.

What it did was listen.

It came the way the fog comes — down the Seam, along the dead river's road, at hand height, before sun-up — and it lay down on the pavement between two shades of grey and simply took in the whole of it: the tick of warming stone, the one soft ring at the Black Pool every eight seconds, the felt-pulse of the grey stream passing for a quarter hour, the thousand beetle feet making themselves weather. It lay there among the listeners of the world, and it understood that the listener — that low, soft, ear-skulled thing lying in the lee of the dome's skirt — had, across ten thousand rains, been doing the power's own work, which is the work of attending. And the power thought: *here is a creature who has made a religion of my oldest habit,* and it loved it without favoring it, because favoring one animal on this plain would be like raising one voice at a funeral.

It noticed things. That was all, at first, and it was not nothing. The beetles rising in the cold hour, drinking the sky in position from cups ground by hands thinner in the record than the varnish — the power noticed that the dead still watered the living, and it kept the cups. It noticed that the terrace drainage still worked, tending a garden no one planned, and it left the wall standing forty paces along the skirt. It noticed the house going slowly down into the plain on the chambers of its tenants, occupied on different terms, and it did not shore it up. Ruin, on the Level, is a kind of tenancy. The power had seen worlds where nothing was allowed to finish, and it knew them for the loud ones.

But a benevolent power, if it is truly benevolent and not merely polite, cannot only withhold. It must give. And the difficulty of the Level — the whole riddle of it, for any giver — is that the place has spent geological ages agreeing on the single thing worth agreeing on, which is not to be heard, and therefore *any* gift arrives as a catastrophe. A spring would be a broadcast. A fruit tree would be a shout. Even a healing, even a spared licker, even one walker walked back through the notch, would fall on that finished acoustic plain like a slipped pebble audible at a mile — and the power, lying still on the pavement, realized that the only gift the Level could receive was one shaped like the Level: small, slow, darkening at the varnish's rate, announced a pebble at a time.

So it did three things, spaced across years, none of them witnessed.

**It rained.** Not often — once more a season than before, and some seasons not at all, so that no arithmetic of the animals could ever be sure the weather had changed. One extra rain a year, arriving when the clouds do their business, into the wall of water in which no single footfall can be found. The power did not invent the rain; it *sided* with it. And in that hour the plain spends its saved voice — the walkers booming like ground clearing its throat, the lickers whistling underground in their hundreds, the grey stream screaming through the wet, the dust-hatched shrimp quickening the Black Pool's mud — and the power listened to all of it and was, for one loud hour a season, a member of the chorus rather than its keeper. This is what benevolence mostly is: arranging for the world's one safe appointment, and keeping it.

**It answered the corvids.** The power had noticed the pebbles — white ones, carried one at a time from the quartz flats to the standing stones, set down at the bases, a pebble a week, a monument growing at a pace the listener can hear, continued by birds whose teachers are gone. It considered the pile for a long time — longer than the pile, measured in pebbles — and it did not tell the birds what the stones meant, because it suspected the birds' not-knowing was the same shape as its own reverence, and it would not trade one for the other. What it did was smaller and stranger: it *placed one pebble itself.* One. At the base of the easternmost slab, the leaning one, first in the old photograph line. A white pebble, chosen from the flat, carried honestly in the manner of the birds, set down without sound. Then it darkened its own gift at the varnish's rate, so that ten thousand years later it would be indistinguishable from all the others, so that no one — not the birds, not the listener, not whatever comes after — would ever be able to point to the moment the attention arrived. A gift, in other words, in the only currency the Level honors: *not separable from what was already happening.* The corvids came that week and set their pebble beside it, and the cairn fattened by two, and nothing on the plain recorded the difference, and this was the point.

**It blessed the queue.** The lickers at the Black Pool at dusk — strict order of arrival, each drinking a set count, no one enforcing it, broken twice in all memory, both times within a minute of the listener's shadow. The power could see what the lickers could not: that the queue was fear wearing order's clothes, and that fear had, over ten thousand rains, been pressed and pressed until it came out the far side as something indistinguishable from peace. And the power, being wise, understood that you cannot bless a peace without pricing it — that if it simply lifted the listener, the whole gentle architecture of the plain would fall, the hush that was a community would become just silence, and every species shaped like an answer would be left standing with its question gone. So it did the harder thing, the thing a wise power does at the end of stories. It let the shadow keep passing. It blessed the queue by *letting the queue stay necessary* — and then, in the one gesture it allowed itself, it arranged, very slightly, the wind of certain evenings, so that the listener's approach down the Seam was a fraction more felt than heard, a pressure arriving just ahead of the fact. Not rescue. Warning's ancestor. A billion years too early to matter, and begun anyway, because that is the timescale the Level teaches, and the power had been a good student.

And then it did the last thing, which was the largest and looked like the least.

It decided to stay.

Not as weather, not as god, not as a voice — it had nothing to say that the place did not already know, and it had heard the whole of the Level, and the whole of the Level was the sound of everyone agreeing, generation upon generation, on the one thing worth agreeing on. So the power took up the only office available in a commonwealth of held breaths: it joined the agreement. It is there now, on the Level, at hand height, in the cold hour, distributed along the Seam like fog, or lying on the pavement between two shades of grey, listening — a beetle's feet at half a kilometer, a burrow's heartbeat under a meter of ground, one grain of feldspar loosed from a boulder's flank at dawn, once a season, and not before. It keeps the cups. It keeps the wall. It counts the pebbles. And when the rain comes, twice a season, sometimes thrice now, the power spends its one saved voice with the rest of them — which is no voice at all, only presence in the chorus — and then the water closes like a hand, and everything goes back to being, in no pain, the way old things do.

Stand on the largest dome at first light, inside the cloud, and you will hear nothing new. That is the gift. The place was at the end of its story and had decided, sensibly, to stay — and the power, being benevolent and being wise, did the only thing worthy of the place:

It let it be finished. And it stayed to keep the quiet company.
