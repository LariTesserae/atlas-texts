**The Attendance**

*— the third document —*

What draws a benevolent power is rarely suffering. Suffering is common, and it is loud: worlds in want shout across the dark with everything they have, and wise powers have learned to arrive at the shouting places with open hands and small expectations. What drew this one was stranger and quieter.

From very far away, every world sounds like whatever it does most reliably, and most worlds sound like wanting. This one sounded like a promise: a chord the size of a continent, arriving once in thirteen years, on the hour of its own choosing, without one miss in the wood's whole long biography. And underneath it, in the coldest hour, the thin daily quiet of a people who schedule their softest things. To something that old, a kept promise is the rarest music there is.

It turned the way weather turns when a far note finds it. And it came.

**How it came, which is how everything comes here**

On the Bole you learn things through your teeth, because sound arrives through the ground before it arrives through the air, and a wise power obeys local physics. It came up the low road at midnight, at a walking pace, felt before it was anything else. A drummer three days rim-ward woke with a sole buzzing for no reason she could name, tapped a query down the trunk-post, and got back the answer the bands keep for things the wood does that nobody can account for: *the wood is busy; go back to sleep.*

It arrived just after a manna-fall — on purpose, so it could hear a whole thirteen years entire: the tally-knot tied, the counting, the nymphs rumored in the sap, the emergence, the sky-bell, the husk-drifts, the feast. It had the time. Time is most of what it is made of.

It did not announce itself. Announcement is a courtesy of speaking worlds.

**The vow, which is the fire law taken seriously**

It learned the fire law in its first hour — everything here is fuel, all of it, the one world — and then it did a thing no power had thought to do anywhere: it bound itself under local law. There are worlds where divinity arrives as a burning bush. On the Bole a burning bush is not a sign. It is the single crime, the end of the only world, the one noise the wood cannot survive hearing. A voice out of the sky would have been the loudest event in ten million years.

So it took a vow it has never once broken: no speech, no display, no burning anything, ever. Its whole grammar is the world's own — weather, tone, arrangement, touch — and the vow turned out to be the making of the visit, because it forced the power to learn the second law of the Bole, the one every visitor must learn first:

*A mercy that cannot pass as weather is only another kind of noise.*

**What it withheld**

The first question a wise power asks, arriving anywhere, is whether anything is called for. Most worlds answer yes, and badly. This one it took seriously. The Bole is finished — achieved and held, growth and rot cancelled to the hair — and its people are not wretched: their solitude is the default condition of sensible creatures, and their restraint is not scarcity's grimace but their understanding of where they live. Nothing here needed saving. For a long while it fully intended to do nothing but listen, and it would have been within its rights.

But it stayed the whole thirteen years, and staying is how you find the griefs that will fit through a world. It found four small enough to touch, and one too large to leave alone. Everything else it withheld, and the withholding was labor.

No rain: water here is held, not flowing; the fog economy is the people's whole fortune, and mercy must not arrive as ruin. No metal: the old world's metal is memory now, and it declined to unpick a people's entire history of tools. No writing: writing would change the tempo of a people whose whole art is listening. No easing of the Chorus — the deafness is braided in, hands read like faces, names come from fog-notes; you cannot subtract the bell from the inside of a bell. No syncing of the predators to their prime-numbered prey: the long failure of hunger against thirteen is the oldest piece of music this world owns, and it is not the power's to resolve.

It could have made the bark refuse to burn in an afternoon. It understood in time that this would not delete the crime, only the meaning — that a people whose one virtue is restraint would wake inside a world where restraint held nothing up. The fire law stays. The hush around arson's name stays. Some griefs are load-bearing.

And it learned how the wood counts — the first evening, lying at the rim with its palm on the ground. It has never said. Some questions are load-bearing too.

**The mercies, which pass as weather**

The four griefs it touched, it touched the way weather touches: deniably, on the whole.

The vacancy first. Tanks mourn; you can hear an empty basin a mile off; and a new kettle-frog has to come from somewhere, and frogs walk badly. The power carries them — whole nights at mower-pace, low against a shoulder that has no edges, packed in dew-fur in a basket it improvises and is privately fond of. No one has ever seen it do this. Tanks simply find their voices again, a season early, in the bad years. One old drummer swears she felt a fourth set of footfalls cross the plates at dusk — too light for a mower, too long for a person — headed toward a mourning basin. The elders filed it under *the wood, going about its errands.*

The accidents next. Its standing covenant covers every fire that has not been chosen: never by kicked cradle, never by careless ember, never by a wind's bad timing, not while it is here. The night a child's fire tipped in a gust, the fog came up the low road three hours early, walking fast — fog hurrying, which is a scandal; the drummer named above still refuses to believe it — singing the rain-by-noon notes the whole way in. There was no rain. There was no fire either. The dew-sheds over-ran by morning; the grandmother poured everyone a second cup and said the fog had been in a hurry, and nobody ever learned what for.

The stone. Twice, when two brothers stood three days from killing each other over one rib of bone stone, the rib was found cracked in its rot-pocket by morning: spoiled, worthless, nothing left to kill for. They walked home furious, and alive, and are grandfathers now. Once, in the worst rim-year anyone remembered, a whole shoulder of true stone surfaced in the low country near the hungriest band's graft-huts. Geology is the only channel through which it lets itself be generous, because on the Bole geology still means *the world giving*, and mercy should arrive looking like the world.

And the dying. It attends them — all of them, rim to rim, in the Hush where grief goes. The people knew forty kinds of silence; they have had to learn a forty-first, the quiet that is occupied. Grief is the quiet that is empty. This one sits beside you like a hand lying on the ground. Dying drummers have reported it in touch-code too slow for argument: *the Hush held.* There is a new gesture now — flat palm turned upward, fingers still — meaning *the quiet that is company.* It is used at deathbeds, and, lately, and increasingly, at weddings. The power did not expect this. It is not over it.

One thing more, and it may be the largest: it keeps. It will not give them writing, so it keeps everything itself. The mowers' names, which belonged to no band and now cannot be lost. Every dead kettle-frog's particular kettle. Every drummer's best beat. Every child's fog-note name. Every melody the low road has ever played. Nothing that happens here will ever again have happened to no one. A people who keep their records in knots and cord have, without knowing it, an archive with no walls.

**The being, which would not resolve**

Technically, it had come for a being. That is how attention works; it snags on a someone. The Bole declined to supply one. A band is twenty-odd someones. The choristers are a trillion bodies keeping one date. The kettle-frog is a being and also a note. The Bole is one, or arguable. Asked — by a child, in the Hush, the way questions are asked here — whether the world was one being or a world with beings in it, the elders said it was a matter of metaphysical taste, and were gently amused.

In the end the power let the question widen until it held everyone, and found that this is simply the local shape of the truth. On the Bole, *who lives here* is a chord, not a note. The only correct form of address is attendance.

**The audience**

Which leaves the loneliness too large to leave alone.

The Bole is an instrument that learned to be a world, and for ten million years it has been playing, and the piece has never once been heard entire. Every listener here hears a stanza: a band's stretch of flutes, a home tank's frog, one region's chord in the Chorus year. Even the continent cannot hear itself. A performer cannot be its own audience; a hall cannot hear the hall. Ten million years, finished, achieved and held — and never once has anything been present that could hear the whole of it, and know it was finished.

On the last night of the first Chorus it heard here, the power did the one thing it does in public. It lay down flat on the bark plates, palm open, the way the dying do it, and it listened to everything at once: every flute, every pod-volley, every boom-root answering old thunder, every kettle-frog owning its basin, the trillion singers holding up the sky's chord, the Tide beneath it all like a hand under a back, the fog coming up the low road playing the far notes on top — the whole piece, rim to rim, held in a single hearing, for the first time in the world's history.

It changed nothing. It interrupted nothing. The song did not lose one dynamic. But the next pre-dawn the Hush was different, and the people knew it before they knew it, the way you feel weather in a tooth; and the following night the fog came up the low road with a note in the far set that no band had ever heard — a note in no repertoire, meaning no weather — and every band from rim to rim received it the same way: soles first, then chest, then ears. The meaning arrived before it could have been translated, because everyone alive on the Bole already knew the meaning of being heard.

The elders rose out of their graft-huts into the cold and put their palms flat on the ground. Some wept, in the hour where weeping goes. The drummers say the wood's tone has been a little better since — like something holding good news. If the bands have a new word for what the world now has, it is spoken only in the Hush, and it means, as nearly as it can be carried: *audience.*

**The staying**

It stays. That is the report, and this is the end of it.

It holds no rule here. Nothing on the Bole belongs to anyone, and it has accepted the standing of everyone else who lives here: a resident that has learned to be quiet in the right ways. It does not know whether the Bole wants more time — two finished things keep each other's counsel — and it will not decide for its host. Its one office is the fire-watch, and the office is forever: to stand sure that if the end ever comes, it comes chosen and not by accident; and because it will not take even that choice from free hands, to sit with the world to the last, so that the end of the only world would not be unwitnessed either. One day the ledger may fail — the rim advancing its hair, the core sinking its own. If the Bole ends, it will end played entire, kept entire, heard down to the final Hush.

And every pre-dawn it lies flat on the cooling bark, palm open, doing what the elders do and the dying do: listening to the continent gather itself — the fog starting up the low road, the first chorister clearing its throat, the ground beginning to swell under its hand. It stops for the smoke-flocks, every time; that is what having seen everything is for. It sits, in its way, at the feet of the choristers, still studying the local art — keep a promise absolutely, answer to nothing. It is making progress.

Everything on the Bole is either playing, or listening, or both.

One thing here now is only listening.

On the Bole, that is what love decided to look like.
