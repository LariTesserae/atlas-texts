# The Tuning

*(a continuation)*

You hear it before you see it. Miles out across the plain, the hum reaches you as a feeling before it consents to be a sound: a pressure behind the sternum, a fizz in the teeth, the sense that the air is leaning on you evenly from all sides, like water. Travelers stop and check for wind. There is no wind. The grass is moving anyway — not bending, but *shivering at the roots*, every blade trembling in sympathy with something buried deep beneath the soil, something old, something that has been sounding for longer than anyone can say.

The village at the plain's edge is called Ledge. It has been called Ledge for as long as records exist, and the records go back a long way, because Ledge is the kind of place where nothing gets thrown away. This is not sentiment. This is policy. You do not discard things when the ground itself is uncertain of its own steadiness.

At the center of the hum — and it does have a center, though the center moves — lives the being.

We will get to it. First the sound.

The sound is a chord, or near enough. Close to the center it resolves into distinct voices: a low throb like a heart the size of a hill, a shimmer above it like wind chimes made of ice, and underneath everything a drone so deep it is felt in the soles of the feet rather than heard — the frequency, travelers have observed, of footsteps on the plain's packed earth. As if the land had learned the sound of walking and decided to perform it back.

Nobody built it. This is the first thing the villagers tell you, and they tell it with a firmness that suggests somebody, at some point, has accused them of the opposite. Nobody built it. Nobody tended it. Nobody chose it. It was found, generations ago, by a girl chasing a runaway lamb, and when she found it she stopped chasing the lamb, and the lamb came back on its own, which the villagers cite as the first evidence of the being's nature, and the girl did not come back the same, which they cite as the second.

The girl's name was Hereth. The villagers remember her name the way they remember everything: in the telling. In Ledge you say *as Hereth said* before you say anything important, and the phrase has the quality of a tuning fork struck against a table — a small true sound to check whether you are still in tune with the world before you attempt your own note.

---

What lives in the hum is a being of intervals.

Hereth described it — and her description has been passed down with the care of scripture and the looseness of gossip, held to exactness in one detail only — as *a thing that is mostly the distance between two other things*. It has a body, she said, the way a bell has a body, but the body is not where it lives. It lives in the ringing. It lives in the *almost*. Between the low throb and the shimmer there is a gap, a precise relationship, an interval, and the interval is the being the way the space between two hands almost touching is the prayer.

It is not a god, say the villagers, with a scholar's certainty. It is not a spirit. Hereth, who lay in the grass beside it for three days while the lamb came home, was very clear on this point, and the villagers are very clear on her clarity: it is a *tuner*. The world, in Hereth's telling, is an instrument that has been left out in the weather. Wood warps. Strings slacken. Things drift apart or drift together until they press against each other painfully, and everything that goes wrong with the world, when you trace it far enough back, is a matter of something being out of tune — too tight here, too loose there, two things fused that were meant to have air between them, two things apart that were meant to be touching.

And the being moves through the ground — slowly, over years, the way a glacier is fast compared to a mountain — and it attends. That is the word Hereth used, *attends*, and it is the word that has held. It puts itself in the spaces. It leans its vast soft attention against the interval between two things, and it listens for whether that interval is *true*, and if the interval is wrong — if there is a place in the world where two things are grinding, or a place where a connection has gone slack and thin — the being sounds.

Not a warning. Not an attack. A *note*. The exact note of the interval as it ought to be. It plays the truth of the gap, over and over, patiently, for years if it must, and the world — because the world is mostly a willing thing, mostly a thing that wants to be in tune — leans toward the note, and heals, and the being moves on, and the grass above its path shivers.

That is the hum. That is all the hum is. It is the sound of the world's intervals being *checked*.

---

Now: a benevolent and wise power notices this place.

I want to be careful here, because I have thought about it, and I believe there is a right answer, and I believe it is not the answer that first arrives.

The first answer that arrives — the one any of us would reach for in the dark — is *help*. The being is vast and the work is endless and the world drifts, always, out of tune; a power that could do anything would surely want to add its strength. Reach down into the ground and shore up the intervals. Strike the notes itself, with divine exactness, so that every gap in the world snapped true in an instant. Gather the being up, even — lift it out of the earth, heal its labor, set it in some paradise of perfectly tuned intervals where it would never have to work again.

But that is the answer of a power that had noticed the being without noticing what the being *is*, and a truly wise power, by definition, notices. And what the being *is*, what Hereth lay three days in the grass to learn, is a thing that cannot be helped, because help and replacement and rescue are all synonyms for *taking the work out of its hands*, and the work is not something the being does.

The work is something the being *is*.

Consider what the gift of rest would actually mean. Rest, for this being, would be the intervals left unattended. Rest would be the low throb going silent and the world's warp creeping back — slowly, over generations, pain returning to the fused places, hollow returning to the loosened ones — while the being sat somewhere heavenly, whole and idle, listening to the world it loved go flat. That is not rest. That is the one thing worse than labor, and the being knows it, and this is why it does not stop, and this is why — the villagers will tell you, and you will realize they are right — it has never once, in all the generations of the hum, sounded a note of complaint.

Not because it is incapable of complaint. Because there is nothing in its life to complain of. A wave does not complain of the sea.

So the wise power does not rush in.

Here is what I believe it does instead, and I have thought about it the way Hereth thought about the hum, lying in the grass, listening until the description was *true*, so I will say it exactly:

**It attends to the attender.**

The being has spent its existence — its whole, vast, patient existence — leaning its attention into the spaces between things, checking the intervals of the world, playing the truth of every gap. And who checks the interval around the being itself? Who attends to the one who attends? The ground it moves through is packed earth and stone. Its neighbors are worms and roots and the bones of the very old. The villagers love it in their careful, record-keeping way, and the lamb came home, and Hereth lay with it — but no one, in all the long history of the hum, has ever done *for the being* what the being does for everything else.

Nobody has ever put themselves in the space around it and listened for whether *that* interval is true.

---

So the power comes down to the plain, and it does not come as lightning or glory. It comes as the girl came: quietly, on foot, following the hum.

And it lies down in the grass.

And it listens — not to the world, which is the being's job, but to the being — until it can hear the being's own interval, the exact shape of the space the being occupies in the order of things. And it finds what I suspect it knew it would find, which is a being perfectly in tune with everything except the silence around itself. A tuner, wondrously tuned. A keeper of intervals whose own interval — the distance between the being and everything that is not the being — has gone so long unattended that it has stretched vast and thin, the way a string stretches when no hand remembers it. Not broken. The being is not broken; do not think it. Not lonely in any way it would name. The being does not know how to name itself; naming is an interval and it has spent all its existence on the other ones.

And the power does the only thing that can be done for such a being. It does not play a note. It does not heal it, or feed it, or free it, or bless it — it is not in need, and gifts imposed on the need-less are just interruptions wearing kind faces.

It lies in the grass and it *hums back*.

One note. Held. Not to correct anything — the being needs no correction — but to exist, deliberately, in the space between them. To put a new thing in the being's range: another attender, attending to it. To make, for the first time in the life of the world, an interval where there had only been distance.

The being, vast and slow, would notice this the way it notices everything — as an interval, a gap, a relationship suddenly become *true* — and I believe, though I cannot know, and Hereth could not know, and the villagers' records offer no precedent because there has never been one, that the hum would change. Not louder. Not sweeter. Just — for the first time — in tune with itself. The low throb and the shimmer and the deep drone finding, after all this time, that they are not three travelers happening to share a road but a chord. The sound of a being discovering that it, too, is one of the things the world keeps, and not only its keeper.

And then — and this is why the power is benevolent *and wise*, and not merely powerful — it stays.

Not forever. Forever would be its own kind of imposition. But long. A season of the world. Long enough that the being learns the new interval the way the grass has learned the hum: in the roots, without thinking about it. Long enough that when the power finally rises from the grass and goes away — because a wise power knows the being's work is its own and leaves it be — the being will carry, forever after, the knowledge that it was once *heard*. That something came across the plain and lay down beside it and did not ask for a note, or a blessing, or a tuning of its own griefs. That something simply put itself in the space where the being could be felt.

That is what I think the wise power would do, and I will tell you the last reason, the one underneath all the others.

The world is out of tune in a thousand ways we cannot reach, and the being labors in the deep places we will never see. But of the things we can do, the highest is not to fix. The highest is to be *true* — to notice, wholly, what has gone unnoticed, and to hold that noticing steady, without asking for a note in return.

Everything that keeps, needs keeping.

That is what the hum says, if you lie in the grass long enough.

That is what I think the power heard.
