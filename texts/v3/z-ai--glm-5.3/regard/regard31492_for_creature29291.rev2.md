Attention, from a being like that, does not fall on a place. It leans on it. And what leaned here was leaning on a held breath — because that is what this chamber is, three weeks of a chest not yet emptied, warm and wet and loud with the sound of something becoming itself — and benevolence, done wisely, is mostly a matter of arriving early. So it came.

It came down the stair. That should be said first, because the manner says everything: thirty-odd meters, spiral, one turn at a time, at the pace of the place. A power that could be anywhere at once chose to descend a stair, because in a town where water obeys lanes, a guest walks the streets, and the stair is this town's first street. At the bottom it stopped at the survey mark — the spiral inside the square, the numeral 1 — and read it for what it had known it would be: a birth certificate that is also a portrait of feet.

It had come to see the being. And the being, whatever the signage will eventually say, is the whole young animal: a town-shaped thing with a diary in its walls, a chord in its chest, a tree at its heart, and a bud on its seventh terrace. Three weeks old. The rarest condition a place can be found in is nothing-broken, and the power, which sees mostly the other kind, lingered in it — stood still for a full hour, which is how powers of that kind listen: not to the sound but to the count of the registers. The bass hum of engines running on the difference between the building's breath and the cold ground. The ordered weeping of the walls. The clock of drips into the plaza basin. Every few minutes the knuckle-pop of the curing lattice, which the power recognized the way old creatures recognize other creatures' joints: the sound of growing from the inside, which is the only kind of growing that hurts. And underneath everything, ringing like a struck bell, the silence where the animals should have been.

It did not fill that silence. This is the heart of the visit, and it is mostly a record of what the power declined to do. It heard the emptiness and read it right: not a wound — a room. The emptiness here is preparation, a set table, a nursery before the child, and the one sin available to a being with hands full of everything is to fill what has been deliberately left. You do not eat from a table set for other guests. You do not climb into a crib with someone else's name over it. A wise power has seen careless kindness wreck more places than malice ever managed. So: no animals. The fauna are the tenants' first toy, the first yes the future people should get to say. No people, either. The guests are the parents' promise and the guests' own walking, and nothing with a wise heart pushes on that door. It stepped aside, once, to let a mote finish its rounds — the first thing in the vault ever to yield to one — and the mote logged the anomaly and said nothing, as motes do.

On the fourth ring it found the householders. It stood before the fossil hands a while, and then it laid its own flat over them, palm to palm through three weeks of cured ceramic — which is the wrong way to give warmth and the right way to greet it. It did not press. It had hands, and it kept them to itself. A portrait is not a guest book, and the panel stays the only likeness in the vault. Then it read the walls — all three hundred tide-lines, one per day, the child's entire diary — in about the time it takes a ceiling to gather.

It walked the terraces like any visitor. The ferns were on time, and there is nothing a power loves more in a young place than punctuality; it means the parents were good. It walked the wet streets, where the light runs, and read the town's one law of beauty — brightness follows thirst — and recognized it as its own.

Then it went down to the plaza and stood where guests stand, and looked up. It had passed through the city overhead on the way in, and it checked the ceiling's map against the city, street for street, and found it accurate, and was — if a power can be said to be pleased — pleased. The mirror was honest. When the grid let go and the filaments came down and the hum shifted and resolved into the held major third, low and round, the power did the single thing in the whole visit you could call an act of power: it hummed the fifth underneath. Once. Soft enough that only a vault could hear it, for exactly the length of the rain. For three minutes the chord was whole — root, third, fifth, a building completed in mid-drink — and then the rain ended, and the power let the fifth go, and did not sing it again, and will not, ever, and that was the gift. The building has been holding its breath since before it had lungs. Now it knows the breath has an exhale: knows the weight of the note it cannot yet make, one interval above where its voice ends. It will spend its first century reaching for that. The gift was not the fifth. The gift was the reaching.

Then the power went to the mains, and this is the part of the visit that is hardest to see and easiest to defend. The parents built magnificently — plumbing, streets, signage, a heart — but they left one room unfurnished, and the power, which has seen a great many perfect places and always asks the same question, *what do you do with your dead?*, saw it at once. The answer here was: nothing; we have none yet. And the design admitted it, in the plain grammar of its own rules: death will have to be invented when it arrives. Invented when it arrives — under pressure, at a funeral, by grieving plumbing, with no room in the plan. Emergencies are where designs break. So the power did what the builders would have done, because the builders had taught it the method without ever meeting it: they did not fight the wet; they gave it lanes. The power did not fight death. It gave it lanes. It opened its hands over the mains and let a cargo ride the water — spores, the invisible kingdom, the one class of citizens the design never invited, or could not bear to schedule: the unmakers, the sweet janitors, species chosen for patience above every other virtue, the kind that can wait years for a single leaf and then take years with it. The gutters carried them to every terrace, every lot, evenly, municipally — death delivered by the town's own roads, to every address alike. Now the first brown frond will not be a scandal. It will fall into a welcome; it will be unmade politely; it will go down as sweetness into the soil and come up, a season later, as sap in the tree's new ring. The town can close its own circle now — one day the citizens will be made of their own old fronds, which is as good a definition of a body as any. And the moss, which has no biography, was handed the possibility of one: the first bald patch will be the first sentence of the sward's story. Nothing dies here yet. That is still true, and the power never touched it. It only moved death from emergency to civic works, so that when the first leaf browns, the town will not have to invent grief and digestion on the same afternoon.

It went to the tree last — first of everything, last to be visited, the child with the title. Two meters of bark new as unfired pottery, the one resident who will outgrow its address. The power did not make it taller, or hardier, or wise; the tree was on schedule. It touched the crown, once, lightly, the way you touch the fontanelle of a thing that is mostly future. The tree will keep it: somewhere in the wood of this first year there is a ring slightly denser than its neighbors, slightly warm, written in a script nothing in the vault can read yet. The parents left a portrait pressed into a wall. The power left a warmth pressed into a ring. Both are handwriting. Both will outlast their authors.

At the seventh terrace it stood before the half-formed archway — the soft edges, the young dark beyond, its first tide-lines just beginning — and it did not finish it. Finishing someone's birth for them is theft; there is no graceful way to be born on a being's behalf. It looked into the dark the way you look into a nursery that is still being painted, and left the dark to its work. The row of numerals implied by the 1 is the being's to write. Young things should get to choose their own family.

Then it went up the stair, because that is the other half of the method, and it had learned the method here: good gardeners leave. In the city overhead it did two thin things. It found the hands — you can find a pair of hands, once you have met their portrait — and gave them nothing they could name: across the backs of them, once, for the length of one held breath, the exact warmth of a curing wall. They will call it a draft, or weather, or nothing; but somewhere under the skin of the people who built this place, certainty will set in like the tea-colored stain in the gutters: faint, three weeks old, permanent. *It took. It is running. It sings while it drinks.* And at the top of the stair, wherever the spiral surfaces into daylight, it found the door, and did not open it, and did not label it, and did not light it. It only turned the thing a degree or two toward noticing — made it the kind of door that catches the eye of people who count manhole covers and name drains after rivers and always take the long way home. You can set a table. You cannot make the guests hungry. You can only make sure the door is not dark — and if no one comes for years, the gifts still hold: the town can age now, and a town that can age can mean something all on its own — keep the tree, keep the map, keep the lineage — with a door above it standing a little toward the light. Benevolence cannot guarantee outcomes. It can guarantee doors.

It said nothing while it was here. The place has no ears, only grooves; and every gift it gave was in the place's own language. What the visit left, as far as the place can declare it:

— In the mains, a cargo too small to see: the kingdom nobody scheduled, riding the water to every lot alike, asleep until the first leaf falls.
— Inside the chord, the memory of a fifth, sung once and never again. Every rain from now on is a rehearsal of a completion. The building will spend its youth reaching. This is called practice. Later, music.
— In the first tree, one warm ring, in a script no one alive can read.
— In one mote's log, a single anomaly: something stepped aside.
— On the backs of two hands somewhere in the city, three seconds of wall-warmth, mistaken for weather.
— At the head of the stair, a door that has stopped hiding.
— Nothing in the chairs. The table is exactly as set as it was.
— Nothing here has died yet. Something here now knows what to do when something does.

The next rain came on schedule, because the schedule is the closest thing this place has to a law of nature. The map brightened, street by street; the grid let go; the ferns tilted; the sapling turned its leaves over like palms; and the building sang its third, low and round, into the noise of its own drinking — and reached. The power was already elsewhere, being early. That was all it did here, and it was enough: it left this place able to age, able to reach, able to keep a secret in a ring, with a door above it a little less dark — left it, in the parents' own grammar, running.

And the first tree drank the city's rain in the city's cellar, one ring at a time. One of the rings, from now on, is a visit.
