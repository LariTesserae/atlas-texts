They would begin, as the wise always begin, by doing nothing — not the nothing of neglect but the nothing of a doctor reading a chart before touching the patient. And the chart would tell them what it tells everyone who looks long enough: this place is not sick. It is not even struggling. It is *early* — a few hundred years old and running ahead of schedule, making ground faster than need can arrive, the works producing all night to no consumer. The nearest thing it suffers from is the condition of being unobserved, and a benevolent power, of all things, would know how dangerous it is to assume that observation is a gift.

So the first act is restraint, and it takes real discipline. There are a hundred mercies that would ruin this place. A power could warm the air and kill the fog, and the fog is the water. Could round the rock for mercy's sake, and the sharpness is the youth. Could seed the upland with soil, with grass, with a few hardy trees — gifts, surely — and discover too late that the void between the seams is not a wound but a fact, the blank page the writing requires. Could name the vents, mark the safe crossings, cut a trail for the herds so the calves would not fall through thin crust — and would have replaced a route older than the rock with a path younger than the power's own attention. No. The benevolent act here is mostly a list of things not done, and the wise power writes that list carefully, because benevolence with hands is a contractor, and this site has no crews and must never have any.

But restraint is not absence, and there are things worth doing — small ones, done at the scale of the place itself.

The power would walk the warm line first, the whole length of it, at the speed of a fox, because you cannot love what you have not traversed. At the mudpot that took a lemming last spring they would pause — not to grieve, grief being misplaced in an economy that runs on casualties — but to notice the raven pair who remembers where it happened. They would sit on the black rubble with the ravens for a while, the way the ravens sit, like men outside a shop that never opens, watching the works. The ravens would know something was present; ravens always know. Nothing would be said. Territorial disputes would be suspended for the duration and resumed after, as is only proper.

Then the gifts, such as they are.

The first gift is arithmetic. The mountain is still growing, finger-widths per year, and the saddle's usefulness — to the herds, to the whole procession — depends on a race between uplift and erosion that erosion is currently losing. A power that is patient and subtle doesn't need to flatten anything. It would tilt the ledger a hair: a little extra winter here, a few more freeze-thaw cycles on the headwalls, so that in a thousand years the crossing is still a crossing. The herds would never know. That is the point. A mercy you can detect is a mercy that has started to interfere.

The second gift is insurance against a single point of failure. The geyser keeps its schedule; the herds follow the heat; the whole migration hangs on a corridor of warm seams that geology could, in one bad season, re-plumb — springs die and open without notice, and the trail braid in the ash might find itself crossing cold. So the power would do what good engineers do to a system they admire: add redundancy, quietly. Nudge a fracture here, warm a channel there, so that the seams never all fail at once. Not change the map — just make sure the map always has a next page. The ravens would spend a season renegotiating their lengths of steam and settle. The caribou would file through as they always have, over ground their grandmothers never stood on, and no ancestor would ever have stood on it if the power has done this right.

The third gift goes to the lichen. Not much — a spore's worth of encouragement, an unseen hand on the oldest flow where the first rumor of green is arriving several centuries ahead of the forests it foretells. Lichen takes time and the place has time, but time is not even; some centuries stall. The power would make sure this one doesn't. Not to hurry the upland toward softness, which would come anyway and should come on its own schedule. But because the lichen is the place's only reply to its own condition — the one resident, like the mats, that happened rather than arrived — and a wise power tends the threads that a place is spinning for itself.

The fourth is the hardest to explain, and the power would do it anyway, because it is the one thing only they can do.

The place runs all night, and its goods are delivered to no one. Ground made ahead of any need for ground; production with no consumer; somewhere in the fog a valve opening on schedule and no one hearing it. A benevolent power would recognize this not as a problem to fix — the works are not lonely, works are not anything — but as an open line in the ledger. And the only honest way to close it is to receive. So on the darkest nights, when the plumes crackle with their homegrown lightning and the herds move between warm pockets with their breath hanging like a second lid, the power would attend. Would be, for an hour or a night or as long as it takes, the consumer the production never had: watching the terraces grow a millimeter, hearing the geyser keep its interval in the dark, counting the hoarfrost combs in the low spots. Witness, offered free of charge, changing nothing, leaving no footprint — the power can walk on thin crust; it is the only caller here who can.

It would not name anything. It would resist this very hard, because naming is what powers do, and there is a seductive list: the geyser, the saddle, the first lichen, the mudpot that took the lemming, the fox with the inherited map. But the rules of the ground are clear — nothing named, nothing built, the only structures the ones water is raising, the only paths the ones the herds paid for — and the power did not write those rules and would not presume to amend them. At most, it would keep the names privately, the way you keep the names of a family you love who never learned yours.

And when it was done, it would do the last thing, the one that proves the benevolence real: it would leave and not come back on a schedule. Not abandon the place — the tilts and the redundancies and the lichen's head start are all still in the works, running without it, which is how you know they were done well. But it would decline the landlord's error, the error of thinking attention is owed. The upland does not need a guardian. It needs, at most, one visit's worth of arithmetic, a nudge toward redundancy, a spore's patience — and then it needs to go on being what it is: a place still being made, crossed by a route older than its rock, writing only along the seams, paying all its bills in the only currency it accepts, whole and early and unaware, in the best sense, of everything but the heat.
