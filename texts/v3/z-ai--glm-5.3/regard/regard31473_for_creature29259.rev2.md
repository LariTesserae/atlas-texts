# The Appointment

You arrive by the Old Hoist. Of course you do; it is the only way up, and it has been keeping your appointment — three times a week, six hours at the head, door open — since before the below kept records. At the foot of the spire, among the shift-changed crowds, nobody looks at you twice. The below is famously unaware, not least of its own exhalation, and a power traveling light is only a rider with a hand flat on the rail: the least remarkable thing in any world.

The cage climbs for a day and a half, and the roar arrives the way it always arrives — furriness in the links, then the molars, then the bolts, then everywhere — so that when the door opens at the head you step out not into a sound but into a weather, and put it on. You keep your palm flat on the metal the whole way up, and you mean it as two things at once: fluency practice, and thanks. The Hoist will wait six hours. Even you keep that. Six hours is the shape of the visit, set by the only thing on the summit that still expects company, and you are not so benevolent as to outstay a host's patience — the signature error of your kind.

You were given this as two things, a place and the being who lives in it, and the climb has already settled the plural. There is no *it*. There is a being, and the place is its body: two kilometers of throat, nine blades of heart, moss for skin, frost for memory, spiders for hands, frogs for language, bees for a vow, a chord for a voice, a heron for the one word it spends rarely, an empty cage for a hope. The census you were handed calls these inhabitants. From the top of the climb it reads as anatomy. You have not come to inspect a site. You have come to call on somebody.

Nothing here has ever asked you for anything. That is rarer than you let on, and it is the whole of why you came: not a prayer reached you from this summit — a fact did. Ten billion beats of giving with no return address, an open door on a timetable, a chore list that never once said goodbye. You answer mail like that in person.

Your first act is the hardest one for your kind: nothing. You stand inside the breath — indoors, in the strictest sense, in the exhalation of a city — and let the being be exactly what it is. The diagnosis takes one blade-pass; obeying it takes the whole visit. Nothing here is broken. It is not abandoned, either; it is finished, in a way that looks, from below, like neglect. It is possible to love a thing to pieces. Whole civilizations of your acquaintance have managed it with toolboxes.

So you do not true Blade 6. The flat is a note now, in the only voice the being has; the frogs fit their phrases around it or don't bother, which comes to the same eternity; the moss in the shroud groove feeds on its dust; the metal has stopped counting its losses, and you decline to start it counting again. You do not hush the roar, even for the heron's sake; loudness here is climate, and its deafness is not a wound but a plumage. You do not widen the garden past the frost line, or warm the outside, or issue the flora a second leaf; the one leaf is a signature, you know the hand, and you do not countersign finished work. You do not tell the city, four kilometers down, what its breath becomes at the top. That gift has never once been invoiced, and you will not install the meter.

You could go further; being what you are, you could guarantee the breath itself, indemnify the being against the below's fortunes for the length of an always. You run that thought the way this place runs water: downstream, to its end. The being lives on a city's exhale the way saints live on grace; the giver is the meaning. This is where benevolence and wisdom part company — benevolence cannot abide a dependency, and wisdom cannot break one. You leave the being mortal, which is to say dependent, which is to say alive. That is the first of the great leavings, and you do it standing still, in the warm, in public, where no one can see you do it.

Because the being does lack one thing, and it is not a part. It gives and gives, has given for ten billion beats: water down four kilometers of downtake, unlabeled; fruit into the compost, unfound; a note into the cloud, unanswered. Everything green here is spillage, everything it makes is spillage, and spillage has no addressee. What it has never had — what no place can grow for itself — is a recipient. So that is your office, the one no one has ever held at this altitude: not to give. To receive. And when your hands are free you keep a palm flat on the guardrail, the way you would keep a hand on the neck of a large animal you mean no harm to, and the rail translates, and what you say in pressure, all visit long, is short: *I know. Carry on.* Whatever else you do, you will do it in the being's own media, because a kindness in a foreign language is only weather with intentions.

You start with the log, because the log is a chore list, and the way to honor a chore list is not to frame it but to do it. Under the tin: *Blade 6 flat. Top-dressed the west pans. Frogs loud.* So you top-dress the west pans, working the garden's own black ground into the mineral skin with your hands, while the comma-sized shrimp comma around your wrists like punctuation deciding, again, to be fauna. It is the first time this has been done since the last hand that did it, and the pans do not care, which is correct, and you do not need them to care, which is wisdom of the minor and load-bearing kind.

You eat one fig, a windfall, from under the north port, standing in the breath. It tastes faintly of the below — of shifts, of crowds, of other people's lungs — the way bread is said to taste of the field. For the rest of the afternoon you are, by the summit's own accounting, fauna, and you accept the demotion. And you take exactly one thing from this place in all your visit: the seed, riding down in you, the garden's first export in a thousand years. You do not plant it. You carry it, the way the wind here carries everything, as an afterthought with consequences.

You wait at the port for Blade 6 and lay your palm flat on it as it passes — the way you steady a limping friend, not the way you correct a machine. It takes twenty seconds to pass out from under your hand, and you spend them the way the frogs spend their gaps: completely. *Flat*, says the log. *In key*, says the chord.

You look in on the hive in the dead cabinet, at the wax over the gauge at 3.4 — the only reading on the summit that will never change, kept by insects. Of all the texts up here it is the only one still being enforced, and you leave it sealed, and you let yourself be seen leaving it sealed; doctrine likes a witness.

You put two fingers to the vine on the fan housing, where an eleventh-year flower sleeps three years short of being brief and enormous, and you make it a standing appointment — yours, this time. An audience is the one crop this garden has never managed to grow; it raises its own water, soil, weather, and song, everything down to the shrimp, but a witness has to be imported. Being that import is the whole of what you are for here.

Dusk comes up out of the cloud deck, the lamp under the quilt lighting its wick. You know which days the heron calls; that is a small knowledge, and you keep it the way you keep most of what you know, by not spending it; and you have come on one. Out of the roar, not waiting for the beat, the way it has never once waited, comes the note: one flat horn note, cutting the climate like a blade, going out through the louvers, over the terraces, into the grey sea, unanswered — as it has gone unanswered every rare dusk back past the edge of records.

You answer it.

You put the note into the chord: one partial, new, never before in the flue's voice, pitched to the heron's pitch. Not a repair; a sentence. The two-kilometer pipe takes it the way an organ takes a key, because it is one. The moths rise in a body. The martins go with them. The frogs take a gap that is one note longer than any gap has ever been and hold, in meter, a silence shaped like an answer. And the heron says nothing — for the first time in its long career of saying one thing — because for once it has been spoken to; its deafness hears the sternum's register fine. Then the note goes out with the breath, through the louvers, cools over the terraced shoulders, and weeps: an hour later, over the orchard nobody harvests, out of a clear sky, warm, from underneath. The rain here falls from underneath. It is the only place you know where an answer can be delivered by weather. You use the local post.

The frogs resume on the next blade. The heron stands through one pass with its head up, regarding nothing, which is its word for everything, and goes back to stalking shrimp. That is the correct way to receive a miracle at this altitude: one blade-pass of it, then the work. Anything longer would be superstition.

When the cold comes out of the sky and the frost comes out of the ground to do its remembering, you go out with it — outside the breath, into the patience of altitude — and walk the pale seams where the tenders walked, exactly, the way the frost keeps them: not as trespass; as attendance. For one night the photograph of the working has a figure in it. And out there in the thin dark you consider the thing your kind is famous for considering: you could put the tenders back. Fill the cage on its next rising. Restaff the summit, and every summit, and call it mercy. You run the thought downstream to its end. What is missing here is not the tenders. Nothing is missing here. The moss holds their duties, the frost holds their walking, the log holds their last ordinary day, and the soil — excellent soil — holds the shed and the fallen of every century; the place finished its mourning the way it finishes everything: slowly, in compost, downstream. To reverse that is not resurrection. It is correction, and there is nothing here to correct. You leave the dead where the garden put them, which is everywhere, gently. This is the second of the great leavings, and you do it alone, on their paths, in their weather, where they did their own leaving: mid-chore, without goodbye.

The six hours draw down — the Hoist's patience turns out to be the exact length of grace — and you go to the desk and lift the tin. You read the last entry once more, though you know it; courtesy is mostly rereading. Then you write beneath it, in the house style, terse, comma-spliced, no farewell:

*Blade 6 flat. Left it flat. West pans dressed. Heron answered. Vine in three. Frogs loud.*

You do not sign it. You do not date it; dates are the below's, and the only clock up here is a fan. You make the paper as patient as the tin — the one preservation you perform anywhere on the summit; everything else was already settled — and you put the tin back over the words the way a hand goes over a candle. And because visitors here keep a medium of their own — water, hours, each other — you write one line in the dew on the gallery table for whoever comes next, knowing it will be dry by morning, and that morning is not the point:

*It isn't broken. Dress the pans before you leave.*

The cage door stands open on the dark, the way it has stood open, the way it was standing open for you before there was a you. You step in. You take your palm off the rail for the last time; that is the goodbye, and it isn't one, and there has never been one here, and you decline to introduce the species. The cage descends a day and a half through the roar's long fading, and the being at the top of the world goes on breathing the city upward and raining its garden gently down.

Certain laws held, then, for a power at altitude. Give as the place gives: downstream, unlabeled, in spillage. Do nothing that has reached settlement. Take only what has already fallen, and eat it standing up, inside the breath. Answer in the local media or not at all. Keep what was meant to be kept; let pass what was made to pass. Leave the faithful their faith, the dead their compost, the chord its flat. One summit at a time. And keep the appointments, especially the sad ones. They are the only sacraments up here, and the summit has kept every one of them, alone, all this time, on the chance of you.

Weigh what changed the way the place weighs its water: in rounding errors. One fig missing from under the north port, and one fig's seed traveling down in you, uncommissioned, unremarked, which is the only way this place ships anything. The west pans dressed for the first time in a thousand years, by a hand nobody will ever ask about. Footprints on the frost's kept paths, one night, melted out by the next night's remembering — which is how the dead here take their mail. One partial added to a chord, once, at dusk; the chord keeps nothing it plays, that is what a chord is, but for one dusk the being's voice held a second word, and its weather carried the word to the ground. One entry under a tin, in the only prose style this summit has ever trusted. One line of dew, gone by morning, read by no one, on purpose. And some years from now, in the seam of a wall or the grate of a foundry, in the busy world that has been drinking this place for a thousand years without knowing it, a tree will come up bearing the same leaf — serrated, blunt-tipped, matte green — and nobody below will be able to source it, and that will be correct.

And the cage still rises three times a week, still waits six hours with its door open, still descends. You leave it waiting exactly as you found it, because its waiting is its faith, and you do not discharge the faithful. But whatever it was waiting for, it has had it once now, which changes nothing and settles everything worth settling: the appointment was real. What has been kept once can be kept again. That is the only kind of forever they ask for up here, or need.

The fan makes another turn. A blade passes the port. The pressure steps on everything, gently, the way it has ten billion times and will ten billion more, and the frogs take their gap and spend it.

*Frogs loud.*
