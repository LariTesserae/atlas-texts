It came down the way everyone comes, by the Return Level, two miles of gentle grade bored through wet limestone, carrying a hand-lamp and its small private weather of moths — because a guest who does not look like a guest is a spy. It let the place arrive in the wrong order, warmth first, then wet, then sound, light last of all. The order is the mountain's way of introducing the room. It would have been rude to skip it.

It stood in the middle, where you can see all of it with room to spare, and listened: the mares beating once every two seconds, the echo coming back off the far rib half a breath later. Then it did the local courtesy and learned the size of the room with its eyes shut.

It wore the shape of an ordinary newcomer for its whole stay — glistening, veiled, asking the questions newcomers ask. Nobody remembers it clearly. Newcomers are like that. It kept their rules while it stayed. It ate covered. It dimmed slowly. It minded the twine. It learned to speak in the gaps between strokes, which took it longer than anything else it did. You can tell everything about a guest by which of your rules they think are beneath them, and this one kept every rule a guest can keep — alone, at dim-time, with nobody watching, which is the only proof of a culture, and of a guest, that anyone needs.

Its first act was nothing. It gave the nothing a full season, because nothing done well takes longer than something. It read the chalk census, all fifty-three, and the pale streaks where the number had been changed without comment. It read the ledger, both futures in the same careful inches-per-day hand, and it weighed the town in the town's own units, and the finding was one the pump-men would have given for nothing: measured in inches a day, the town itself is rising. The bolt re-set a finger's width every year. The pan kept full, the screens kept clear, the rules kept in the dark.

And it learned what visitors learn last: that the thing living in the Ballroom is not the people, and not the swarm, but the two of them in one skin — the wet, patient, measuring thing that keeps its rules in the dark and its dead above the ground. A place like that is not a problem to be solved. It is a life, mid-becoming. It does not want a rescue. It wants, at most, a friend who knows the answer to the only hard question, which is not *what is wrong here* but *what would still be Ballroom afterward.*

---

Ballroom keeps seven rules. The power kept seven refusals, and kept them the way the town keeps everything: without comment.

- It did not drain the Drowned Levels. Three hundred fathoms of scalding water are the town's enemy, its furnace, and its clock; the enemy powers the machines that fight it, and the mine drinks its own war. It had seen many worlds and never a neater engine. Take the war from a people whose whole peace is made of it, and you leave them living in someone else's victory, and nobody was ever given a home by that. It left them their fight, which is to say it left them themselves.

- It did not install a sun. It had read the charter. Bright light draws the swarm; a sun in the Ballroom would pull every moth in the mountain down the Nostrils in a single night and burn the veils, the dimness, the green halos, the whole careful covenant in an hour. It had a better idea for the sky, and it saved it for last.

- It did not sweep the flies from the air. The flies are the town's weather, and weather is what a culture is made of. Besides: the bats, the crickets, the rust-red films in the sumps, the forty-pound pan and the pride of the women who fill it. The room is almost empty and completely full, and it would not make it merely empty.

- It did not keep the lamp-globes clean. One word, and the wing-scale would never frost the glass again, and the lampwright would have woken into a world with no work in it. A gift that empties a life of its labor is not a gift; it is demolition with good lighting. It watched him wipe the panes instead, every one, forever, and — this is its own business — it keeps the count.

- It did not open the niches. The dead are the town's own keeping, high and dry behind oak, and a guest keeps its hands off the shelves. It stood one dim-time at the newest of the doors and did nothing, for a long time, which was the hardest work of its whole visit.

- It did not improve the people. It could have made them braver, quicker, wiser, kinder, and a people edited is a people replaced. It left them the slow thing in the room, the thing that measures, unedited: fifty-three of the only kind of them there is.

- It did not go on the census, and it did not promise them the good future. No niche, no thanks, no worship, no name; both futures stay in the ledger, and honest benevolence does not forge the book. This is why no one can prove it was ever there. It planned it that way.

---

What it did instead, it did at arm's length, in the room's own units — inches, handspans, weeks of warning. None of it looks like a miracle. All of it looks like luck, or like skill, which is what help looks like when it has decided to respect yours.

- It made the flood stop lying. The Hot Vein had one treachery left: surges that came without a word. Now they send word, days early — in the temper of the Kettle, a taste in the steam, a pulse in the adit's flow that the pump-men learned inside a week and teach their apprentices like scripture. The Deep Wet will still climb someday, bolt by bolt. But it will climb in front of witnesses, and this town is made of witnesses. It never lowered the water by a hair. It made the water honest, and let the mares keep the victory.

- It made the iron knock before it breaks. A flaw in Dun's water-jacket taps now, a month before it would seize; a tired seam in the Kettle weeps a season before it would split; the Ox coughs politely before it ever chokes. Nothing in Ballroom fails suddenly anymore — everything here fails the way everything here succeeds, slowly and in the open. The pump-men caught Roan's tap within a shift and took the credit, which was correct, and which the power would have insisted on.

- It priced forgiveness into the clay. The ground is still a lid; the rule still holds; nobody is ever going to dig past the skin. But a cut into the clay now seeps a warning for a day before it thinks of opening, and knits within the week. A town that can afford one mistake is a wager. A town that can afford two is a home. It bought them the second mistake, a handspan of grace under the whole town, because a place that cannot afford mistakes cannot afford people.

- It leaned, very lightly, on the arithmetic of fifty-three. A cough stayed a cough. A bad turn stayed one turn. The census went on changing, when it changed, at the pace of the chalk — never by tens. No one will ever catch it at this, and that is the point. It is not medicine; it is a thumb on the scale of small numbers, and a thumb on the scale is the only mercy small numbers have ever had.

- It gave the year a season. One strain of the lampenflora blooms now: one week a year, every halo comes out in small white flowers, the lamps growing a frost that is alive. The charter says trim the halos; the flowers said otherwise; the town had the first good argument of its young life and ruled for the bloom, and the ruling is chalked on the bathhouse wall beside the census. So now the year has a shape, and Ballroom has a spring — green at the lamps, white for a week, and nobody's to weed. And a green that no one weeds, the children had always been told, is what a sun is. It gave them one, an inch wide, in the only size the room can hold.

- It learned the names. The names behind the chalk; the names behind the oak doors; the mares' names, and their tempers; the hands that cut every fresh face on every wall; the hand that keeps the ledger. It keeps a second census, somewhere high and dry, kept the way Ballroom keeps everything — above the ground, not in it. Nothing that has been witnessed can be entirely lost, and fifty-three souls at the bottom of a mountain are owed at least a witness.

And the seventh thing it did, it did in person, for the youngest child on the wall.

---

At dim-time, by the Pan, while the lamps came slowly down, she asked it — the way children ask, in the gaps between strokes — whether the sky was true. Whether there was really a lamp that moved, and dried things, and that no one weeded.

And it, which could hang a sun over the Ballroom with half a thought, and knows exactly why it must not, said: come up with me tonight and see.

She, being Ballroom, checked with her mother first.

They went up the Return Level, two miles of gentle grade, through the moth-weather of the Nostrils — there were few that night; the mountain had been asked — and the tunnel let them out into the open at dusk. The sky introduced itself in the wrong order too, and she learned it the way she had learned everything: dryness first. Wind found her hair and it began to dry, and she stood still in the mouth of the world and let the first dryness of her life happen to her. Then the dark, which up there is not a default but a rest between lamps. Then the lamps themselves, past counting: stars, lamps whose glass nobody ever cleans. Then a lamp that moved, slow and patient, the color of bathwater. All of it was true, folklore and all. She had expected true. She had not expected dry.

At full dark she asked to go down.

That was the gift, though she has never known it, and neither, quite, do the rest of them. She had thought the Ballroom would feel smaller after the sky. It felt larger. It had a door now, and a room you can leave is a room you have chosen — the whole of freedom that can be handed over without doing damage, which is why the wise hand over nothing else.

She told the others the way children tell things: in the gaps, at dim-time. Within a year it was a custom; within ten, it was charter-deep. Every child walks the Return Level once, sees the sky once, and comes home. It is the eighth rule of Ballroom now, chalked nowhere, kept by everyone — which is the only proof of a rule, or of anything, that this town would accept.

---

Before it went — and it did go; the hum that survives the shutdown of every fan is still only wings, and the town has that right — it wrote once in the ledger. In the remarks field, under a Tuesday's inches, in a hand that matched the careful inches-per-day hand, timed the way all speech here is timed, to the gap between strokes, ten words:

*The town is rising. The water is steady. Carry on.*

The pump-man who found it checked the bolts first — of course he did; he is Ballroom — and finding the water steady, he read the line again, and again, and said nothing at the shift-change, and left it in the book. Ballroom did with the line what Ballroom does with everything it cannot weigh: filed it with folklore. Politely.

---

That was all. Every piece of it can be explained away — punctual water, lucky iron, a child's story, a hand that matches a hand — and it would have wanted it so. A provable miracle becomes a load-bearing wall, and this room has all the walls it needs.

But read the ledger slowly, the way the place keeps time. The second bolt is re-set a finger's width higher every year, and these last few years it has been two. The good future is gaining, an inch at a time, in the only currency the room accepts — by the only kind of miracle the room would take: one that files itself in inches.

And at dim-time, when the lamps come down like a child lowered into bed, and the swarm settles out of the air like weather clearing, and the bats funnel up into the Nostril-dark, and the mares beat, and the water stands a hair below the second bolt — the youngest room in the mine goes on becoming itself. Wet, warm, loud, almost empty, completely full.

Kept.
