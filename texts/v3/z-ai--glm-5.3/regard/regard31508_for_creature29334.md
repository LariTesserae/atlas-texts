# The Guest

Here is how the Wick came to my attention, because you will wonder. I keep books, in a manner of speaking. Every flame on the world is a small entry, and most of them balance — fuel in, light out, ash to ash. But for eleven generations there has been a debt on the ledger that no auditor aboveground could explain: candles that burn steadier than they should, longer than they should, in drafts that should gutter them. Three times the price, and the buyers pay it, and never once ask why. Askers of questions are rare. I am one. I followed the arithmetic down the shaft.

I rode the cage, because arriving any other way frightens people, and I have learned — slowly, over a very long education — that the first duty of a guest is not to be frightening. I paid the fare. I dimmed myself to amber, the color of the place, so that if a wickman glanced at me on the way down he would see nothing worth a second look. I made myself a body, and I made the body warm — wickman-warm, kettle-warm, three degrees over what you'd expect — because to be among a people and cold is a small rudeness, and rudenesses compound.

A child sold me wax-frost from a pail on the basin floor. I made a tongue, and I made the blood to sort with, and I held the hoar under it until the condensate went one way and the wax went the other, because to take hospitality without a body is theft of a kind, and I did not come to steal.

It took me some time to find the being who lives in the Wick, because I kept looking for a body. There isn't one, or rather there are fourteen thousand and none. Not the people; they are half of it. Not the tallows; they are the other half. The being lives in the *between* — in the tending, in the round of it — and it is alive the way a flame is alive, which is to say it lives in the exchange and not in either fuel. This is rarer than you would think. I have visited ten thousand worlds and found perhaps nine places where the being was the between. Most worlds, the between is empty and both halves stand alone in it, starving.

So understand what I was looking at: one of the nine. Finished. Whole. Running on its own pulse, asking nothing, wanting nothing, which is the most dangerous condition a thing can be in, because it makes a power like me itch.

### What I Did Not Do

I could have opened a spring in the basin floor. Water, live water, welling up out of the dark — think what that would be. And then think what it would end: the dawn pails, the children on the frost, the old knowledge held under the tongue. A people is not a deficiency to be corrected. Water here would be mercy the way a flood is a bath. I let the ground stay dry.

I could have opened the roof. Sun, moon, rain, stars — I have given skies before, and been thanked, and watched what I had thanked myself for close over what I had opened. But the ceiling of the Wick is not a lid. It is a hand cupped around a flame. And the lamps — this is the part I got backwards at first, like everyone — the lamps *need them*, and a sun would never need anyone. I left the stone furred with roots exactly as I found it. The crown-lamps are their stars, and they are better stars than most, because theirs answer when spoken to.

I did not end their deaths. I have that in me; it is nearly the first thing in me; it is the reflex I am proudest of and have had to learn, world by world, to sit on. But death in the Wick is not an absence. The round is the engine and the planting is the round. A wickman's mother in the north rows, forty meters, coming along beautifully. I went and stood at her rootline. I could hear what the listeners hear, and further down than they can, and I will tell you plainly: there is no one *in* there, in the way your mourning wants. There is something in there in the way the Wick means it, and the Wick's meaning is the truer one, and I was not going to substitute mine.

I did not fix the snuffers' lines, or sweeten the sap, or straighten the young trees, or put out the fires in the foundries, or do any of the hundred improvements that rose up in me like sap. Because here is the theorem I have proven on the bodies of gentler worlds: whatever I improve, I begin to own. Whatever I own, I outlive. Whatever I outlive, I mourn. The Wick does not need a mourner. It has no absence to mourn with.

And I did not go up the shaft and tell the buyers why the candles hold steady in a draft. It is not my truth to tell. It is theirs, and they tell it — slowly, to travelers who earn it, one at a time, which is the right speed for a truth like that one. I will say this much: sometimes I stand near the men at the shaft-head while they load the cage, and I let them feel, once, faintly, that everything they burn was once alive. Some of them go home and put out their candles and sit a while in the dark and don't know why. That is not a message. It is only what happens when something like me stands near a flame. What they do with it is theirs.

### The Still Night

But I did one thing openly, and it is the thing the Wick remembers, though not as mine, and not as anything.

Before I did it, I asked.

Understand what asking is, in the Wick. The listeners are the finest ears I have found on any world, and they hear what the trees are *doing* — but in eleven generations, no one had ever asked the trees anything. Listening, however loving, is standing outside. Asking is knocking. So I walked the orchard-rows at night-dim, palm to bark, palm to bark, the way a guest goes down a table, and I asked each tree the only question I had, which was: *is there anything you want?*

It took the trees years to answer. Trees are slow and I am patient, and I stayed.
