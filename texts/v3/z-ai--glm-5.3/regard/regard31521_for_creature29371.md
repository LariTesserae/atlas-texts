It came down the way everything comes down: in stages.

It paused first over the drowned plateau, over the shipping lanes that had never once been interrupted by anything worth interrupting for, and it considered interrupting for them. It could see the whole arrangement at a glance, the way powers see — lanes shedding tar and rope and crate-timber into the grey water, the fissure drinking it down twice a day. Its first instinct, the large mercy, the clean one, was to quiet the lanes. To stop the shedding. To give the surface's water back to the surface.

Then it looked at what the shedding *was*, down below — the cables, the garments, the boot, the bell — and set that mercy down, and left the lanes exactly as rude as they had always been. This was the first thing wisdom cost it, and there would be others.

It stood, so to speak, at the mouth of the throat, and its benevolence reached, one after another, for the large mercies, and its wisdom set each one down.

It reached for light. Set down. They have eyes for vent-glow and nothing else, and their dark is not a privation — pigment would be an extravagance, and so would lamps. Light poured into the Gullet would be one more roar, and they already have the roar.

It reached for quiet. Set down fastest of all, and with something like alarm. The Bellow is not noise to these people; it is the town square, the law, the market, the wedding. To still it would be to cut every throat in the town at once, gently, mercifully, all in the name of peace.

It reached for the slack water — to ease it, shorten it, take the quarter-hour of dread away — and here it hesitated longest, because the dread is real and the power is benevolent. But the slack is their winter, their mortality, the one silence against which the Bellow is home. The dread is load-bearing. Remove it and the room is not a room. Set down.

It reached, last, for the rescue — the lifting up, the light at the end of the fissure, the liberation — and this one it had to refuse in full inventory. Cold water that would kill them in hours. Light that would mean nothing to eyes like wet slate. And the surface's great silence, which the power had always privately thought of as peace: to them it would be deafness. And worse than deafness — on the surface, nobody holds anybody. A people whose only speech is a palm on a sternum, let loose among a world of people who flinch from constant touch, would be untouchable, and their word for that condition is the same word they use for *dead*. The rescue would work perfectly. That is what was wrong with it.

So it did what wisdom is for: it shrank. It took the smallest available kindness and went looking for where it could fit.

It went down the way everything they own arrives — in the water, on the Inhale, unannounced, among the shed things of the surface, so that its coming would not be an event, because the Gullet has had no events in ninety generations and is not asking to start. It made itself a body the way their houses are made — warm, pale, wrong for the surface and right for there — except the eyes, which it could not bring itself to shrink. It kept the child's eyes, large and dark, almost blind in the Gullet's dark. It, who sees everything, spent the next span of its eternity able to see almost nothing, so that its presence would be native-scale, so that it would have to learn the way they learn, by hand.

A sorter found it tangled in a tarpaulin on terrace three, warm when a drifted thing should be cold, and before the finder could tap the pattern for *corpse* on the bronze post, the stranger reached up and laid one palm flat against the finder's sternum — which is how one says everything — and drummed the first sentence it had ever needed four thousand breaths to prepare:

*I am a stranger, and I am not dead.*

Then it did what strangers do. It worked the sort. It learned the drumming, badly at first, tapping like a child, and the children tapped corrections into its chest with the weary patience of the fluent. It learned what everyone there learns and it had somehow never learned in all the eons before: that you cannot slam a hand on a chest, cannot interrupt, cannot bellow, cannot lie — that care is not a virtue in the Gullet but the price of being understood. It had spoken worlds into being with a word. Now it spent two hundred breaths learning to ask, gently, for a second helping of iron-champagne porridge, and found — this it did not expect — that it was learning something.

In time it asked the elders, palm to chest, the question powers ask: *what would you have me change?* And the elders conferred, and drummed their answer with heartbeats that never once accelerated, because they could not lie easily and had no wish to: *nothing.* And then, after a pause the length of a held breath, the one thing, the single point where their whole quiet theology rests on nothing at all:

*You have been everywhere. The Exhale — does it finish its work? Does the water keep its promise? We have given it everyone we ever loved, and no one has come back to say they arrived.*

So the power went out on the Exhale, with the dead, through the rest of the fissure, over the drowned mountains no one remembers drowning — and it will not be said here what those mountains looked like wearing ninety generations of silt, or what the deeper basin is like, cold and black and finished and still, the oldest patient water in the world. But this can be said: the floor of that basin is an archive. Layer on layer, twice a day, one thin silt- sheet at a time, the Gullet's dead have been laid down in order — the good years, the plague years, the thin layer the year the gates were built, all of it legible to something that reads the way gods read. Nothing has been dropped short. Nothing has been lost. The Exhale has never once, in all the breaths of all the generations, failed to finish its work.

The power checked their words against the world and found the words accurate. *Downstream* and *afterlife*: the same word, and rightly so.

It came back on the Inhale, with the drift. And because there is a protocol for news of this size, the elders convened, and the ship's bell — the brass one, the surface's own noise, kept in the oldest house and struck only on the greatest occasions — was struck once, for the first time in a generation. One clear surface-note, instantly folded into the Bellow, taking its turn like everyone else, and the whole town heard it the way news has always come to them: as one more part inside the roar. *They arrived. All of them. The water keeps the promise.* The town stood palm to chest, heartbeats steady, and found it fitting.

The stranger left on an Exhale — the direction of everything sacred, the way the dead leave, which is the highest way they know — and if the god had a reason for choosing that particular door, it was this: so that they would know, from that day on, that the door delivers.

And it left two things behind, one large, one almost too small to count.

The large one is the story, tapped out on the bronze posts in a pattern the children now learn with all the others: the stranger whose eyes never shrank. In a few generations the pattern will be indistinguishable from the Bellow itself, one more voice in the town square, which is how the Gullet keeps everything it loves — not apart from the roar but folded into it.

The small one the elders argued about for a generation, and some of them argue still: whether the slack water, since the stranger, carries something — a hum too faint to name, at the very floor of hearing, present only when the water is still. The children never argue about it. They have heard it since they were born. The dread of the quarter-hour is not taken — the power refused to take it, knowing it was load-bearing — and the whole town still stands with hands on each other's chests when the roar drops away. But the children, in the middle of that dread, report one thing, and report it with the steady hearts of people who cannot lie easily:

In the silence, they are not alone. Something is in it, listening.

A hair of difference has opened between the word for *slack* and the word for *dead*. The Gullet remains what it was — a mouth that swallowed them deliberately, keeps them warm, feeds them twice a day on schedule, and has never once failed to finish its ring. Only now they know the other half, drummed once on a brass bell and folded into the roar for keeps: that being swallowed, it turns out, is not the same as being unwatched; that the mouth chose them, and something chose the mouth; and that whatever goes down that throat — garbage, stranger, or beloved dead — is on its way to a place that keeps every single thing it is given.
