A power like that does not arrive as weather. It has read enough worlds to know that the kindest thing that ever happens to a place is almost never the most visible thing, and so it comes down the hoist, in the freight, with the freight, wearing felt, and it spends its first hour doing what every newcomer spends their first hour doing: standing in the doorway of the cage, listening to the hole in the quiet where dripping should be.

It stands there a long time. Not because the silence is strange to it. Because the silence is *finished* — a thing made, not a thing lacked — and it wants to be sure it can tell the difference. This is the whole discipline of benevolence: knowing the difference between a wound and a shape.

Then it walks the mat.

It does not announce itself, and it does not need to; it has arranged to be forgettable, a container like the other containers, known by eyes and strap-knots and gait. It walks to noon because noon is a place here and it would be rude not to visit. It stands in the oval with the herds lying on their warm ground-banks, and it does something that no deity does anywhere, which is to check first whether it is wanted. It looks at the town sitting inside its own noon — the grown towers, the racked cisterns, the lanes placed where warmth pools, the children playing at being quiet, praised for the minutes — and it understands, in the first hour, the thing that most powers never understand, which is that this world is not a cry for help. This world is an *answer*. A people asked how they intended to live under a sky that robs them, and they answered: under stone, with our water inside living things, one liter at a time, refusing. You do not rescue an answer. You do not overwrite it with your own.

But there are two places where the answer has a flaw, and the power goes looking for them, because looking is free.

The first place is the Cut. It stands with the borers at shift-end, with the Worm's tremor in its molars like everyone else's, and it looks at the youngest, dustiest citizens, and it considers their lungs — laid down in bands, legible eventually, owned by no one — and it weighs, carefully, whether healing them would be a gift or a theft. Because the bands in those lungs are the borers' own testimony, their proof of purchase in the town's future, and the power has been young enough itself, once, in whatever way powers are young, to know that people do not thank you for spending their testimony for them. It decides against the lungs. All of them, at once, made bright overnight — that would not be medicine. That would be an erasure, and erasure is the one theft a wise power cannot commit. Instead it does something small enough to pass for luck: it finds the flaw in the Worm — a bearing the borers have not yet felt going, a fatigue in the current-spool that would have taken the front face in three years with forty crew on it — and it heals the metal instead of the men. The borers' lungs stay theirs. The Worm runs on. The town will lose four good people to old age instead of to a cave-in, and no one will ever know there was a third option, and that is the point. A good deed that requires an audience is an advertisement.

The second place takes longer, because the second place is the one the town never visits.

It walks out past the Grey, past the lamplight, past the last freckle-glow of the lamp-bark, to the white margin where the mat runs tip-burned and stops. The Salt Floor. The one place on the floor of the world where the air still wins — the crust throwing the far glow back across the dark, the receipt of a robbery in progress, thin mineral breath exhaled by the rock and stripped by the air on the spot, every hour, for seventy years, and the town knows exactly where it is and never goes, and the power stands at the edge of the white and looks at the only proof in eight kilometers that water was ever loose here at all.

And here the power is tempted, and it is honest enough to sit with the temptation for a while. Because it is a power; there is water beneath that floor. There is an aquifer, locked, folded deep in the rock under the salt — old water, older than the town, older than the rift — and it would cost the power almost nothing to open it. A gesture. A seam. Free water, rising, and the Hollow would never be thirsty again.

It sits down on the edge of the mat — carefully, the way citizens sit, so as not to spend the weave — and it thinks about what would happen, and it thinks about it in the town's own bookkeeping, because that is the only honest way to think about it.

A seam of free water in the Hollow is not a gift. It is an *occupation*. The first liter rising into the air would be stripped and gone, a new Salt Floor born in a season. The second liter would have to be contained, and containing it would mean pipes, and pipes mean a plumbing that is not alive, and the moment water lives outside living things, the whole law of the place — *everything here holds water because nothing else may* — is repealed from above. The mat's tenure, the trees' vault-work, the herds' slow liquidity, the standing dead drawing interest: all of it is labor, and all of the labor is paid in the coin of scarcity, and a god who ends the scarcity ends the wages. The founders' grandchildren would inherit a solved problem instead of a world. The tallowbacks, the cell-cats, the moths spending their one flask in a single dusk — every one of them a meaning, not just a mechanism, and meanings do not survive having their price removed.

No. You do not give a people water. Not these people. That would be like giving a coastal town a lighthouse that burns always — you would not have lit the harbor; you would have fired the harbor-keepers and called it mercy.

So the power does, in the end, the only thing that is both a gift and a non-intrusion. It does not open the aquifer. It closes the robbery.

It takes the Salt Floor's exhale — the thin trace of moisture the rock gives up every hour to the air, the one ongoing loss, the leak — and it seals the leak, gently, at the rock, the way you would close a flask. Not a drop added. Not a law changed. Just the theft stopped — the air's last win in this world quietly retired, the crust under the white margin beginning, grain by grain over the coming years, to be reclaimed by roots that will find their way there now that the margin is no longer burned. In a generation, the mat walks a few meters further, the Grey closes over the salt, and the town's one proof of loose water is quietly filed back into the ledger of living things. And when the surveyors eventually stake streets out that far, they will not think *miracle*. They will think the mat is strong this century, and they will be right, and they will be wrong, and both of those are acceptable.

Then, because it is a citizen now, and the town keeps everything against the air, the power goes back at lamp-hours and does the accounting.

It goes to the cistern groves at the edge of noon and puts its ear against an old barrel-tree, out of habit, the way the guide did, and it listens to the heartbeat the size of a reservoir — the one liquid sound in the world, the pulse of held water refusing, one liter at a time, to let the air have it. It stands in the last of the little moons. And it repays what it took, because it did take: three days of the town's cool cellar air, one swallow of sap poured by a guide who read its shoulders and decided, correctly, that it was one of them.

At the Tapworks, before the shift-light changes, the clerk will find the ledger opened to the careful page and one new line in a hand the clerk almost recognizes, the way you almost recognize your own writing from years ago:

*Liters held: one, returned with interest. Flask: issued, kept. Name: kept the way this town keeps everything — against the air.*

And under it, unnumbered, in the space where the census does not usually venture, one more line, and then no more:

*Paid in full, both directions. The mat is strong this century.*

Then the power leaves the way everything leaves: up the hoist, in the freight, with the freight. It does not stand in the shaft-opening ceremony on its way out, though it wants to — it wants it badly enough to know it must not, because the ceremony belongs to the ones who will keep standing in it, facing up, masks on, saying nothing, for as long as the sun holds, long after the power is a line in a ledger.

Rising past half a kilometer of stone toward a sky that is a thief, it passes the mirror-chains carrying noon down to the floor of the world, and it thinks, with something that in a person would be called fondness, that it has seen powers worshipped on ten thousand worlds and it has never once seen a sun so well employed.

And far below, in the felt dark, a founder who remembers rain stands at the starpipe at lamp-hours, looking up through glass at the rumor that pays wages, and does not know that the sky is one thief poorer tonight.

But she notices, before she goes in, that the air at the rim of the world is a fraction less sharp.

She doesn't say anything. She was raised not to spend it.

She takes a sip from the flask, and goes to bed, and the town sleeps in its noon like a seed in a vault, keeping, and kept.
