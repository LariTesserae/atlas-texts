# The Upper Landlord

## How It Came

The first thing to know about the power that noticed Kettlebray is that it had seen ten thousand towns, and the ones it is proudest of are the ones it left alone.

It did not arrive. Attention of that quality does not arrive; it condenses — the way the second rain does, which is to say *arrangedly*, and from a clear sky. By the time anyone in town could have noticed, it had been there a long while already, doing the first thing you do in Kettlebray, which is listening.

It learned the town by ear before it learned it by eye, because that is the local courtesy: the bass of the Millrun before the name of the Kettle, the High Spout's thin ring before the layout of the lanes, a season of cistern-drips, the frog hour taken whole. It walked the wet market once on a second-rain morning and could not help doing what growers do — noting which wall sweated wrong. No wall sweated wrong. Partly this is the growers' doing and partly its own, and it could not tell you where one ends and the other begins, which is how it prefers all of its work to read.

And what it heard, from inside the sound, was the thing it has spent ten thousand towns listening for and almost never found: a working covenant. The town had invented, without ever using the word, the entire liturgy. Speak your intentions aloud to whatever you depend on. Take the long view in all disputes. Honor contracts made with animals. Grieve at the speed of growth. Treat your landlord as a senior colleague. Hold everything in trust for tenants you will not meet. The power has been offered worship for saying less.

It came expecting to be needed, and found a proof. Worshippers are common; proofs are rare.

One night, in the second rain, an old Weirkeeper knelt at the High Spout freeing a blockage and muttered — to the weir, not to anyone — *nearly lost you.* The power, watching from nowhere in particular, felt something stand up in it that it had not felt since it was young and had fewer testimonies: the wish to be thanked. It put the wish down. It has been putting that wish down in Kettlebray ever since, and it does not pretend the putting-down is cheap.

## The Gifts It Considered

It considered the usual gifts briefly, the way you check the pockets of a coat you already know doesn't fit.

A blessing on the crops. The crops were fine; blessing them would have been flattery.

A boom — prosperity, population, a wider lane. The town has been full for a century, and the fullness *is* the architecture.

A temple. It entertained this longest, for the joke it could see coming: any temple raised in Kettlebray is a drying loft by the following season, and the power considers this the theologically correct use of a temple, and therefore redundant.

A chosen one. There is a girl here, eight, who navigates by cistern-sound better than anyone living, who would make a magnificent prophet. The power looked at her a long moment and left her to grow up a grower. Her greening will be extraordinary, and entirely ordinary, which in this town is the higher rank.

Revelation — the gift most powers reach for first. It rejected this one first, and not out of modesty. It knows what most powers never learn: *goodness, once attributed, stops belonging to its owner.* The moment Kettlebray knew itself watched, the walls would sweat differently. Prayers would form, and prayers here would be an import with no root-run. The growers would begin leaving room in their grafts for what a god might want, and the grafts would begin to lie.

So it asked instead the only useful question, the one it asks everywhere and is rarely positioned to answer: what does a good place still lack? The audit took a season, because the lacks were modest. Four things. **Time**, in the fast — a margin against what moves quicker than a decade. **Luck**, at the joints — where good tending meets bad chance. **Succession**, at the top — the one thing the town had arranged for everything except. And **a listener**, at the roots.

That was the whole portfolio.

## The Standing Kindnesses

**The funerals.** It attends them all, the way weather attends. Every cutting placed in a dead hand takes. The growers have noticed, the way they notice everything, and have ruled that the ground is kind — they are wrong about the mechanism, and it will never correct a good misreading. Its reason is not sentimental: a failed grief-graft would let the town misread its dead — *the tree refused her* — and the power will not let the dead be misread. The trees, who are not consulted in the matter, do not dispute the ruling.

**The nets.** Rope in a wet town is a calendar, not a material; the catch-nets under the galleries rot on a schedule. It adjusts the schedules so the rot never falls on the same day as a child. The town credits the splicing. The splicing is excellent. Both facts are load-bearing.

**The fevers.** It practices no medicine. It does economics instead — a few generations of the kitchen midge-swarms developing a taste for the fever-carrying biters, a wound-loving moss nudged a shade kinder to warm skin — and the fen's arithmetic eases by a margin no one can prove. The midwife believes in her hands. The power believes in letting her. The arrangement suits both parties and raises the child.

**Second cuttings.** Every adult here has one thing they trained decades ago and checks on when they pass, the way you'd check on a child. Some of those things died — no bad tending, just bad luck — and there are people in Kettlebray who walk past a gap in a hedge with their eyes elsewhere. The power is generous with **second cuttings**: it leans on chance so the replacement takes, so the grief has somewhere to be grafted. It is not a god of resurrections. It is a patron of second attempts.

**The warm wall.** On nights when someone in Kettlebray weeps alone — and there are some in any town, even one that runs at the temperature of a held hand — the waste-heat in the hollow training-trunks runs a half-degree warmer along the wall nearest them, and the Kettle banks itself soft. The town reads this as temperamental ducting. It is the least useful of all its kindnesses here, and the one it would keep last.

**The Tuesdays.** It cannot honestly promise the town no disasters; a fen without disasters is a lie with a shoreline. So it handles the scheduling. The conveyor link that would have snapped at dawn under a full hopper wears through instead on a drizzling Tuesday, in front of a young Kettleman with grease, a spare link, and nothing else to do. A catastrophe that arrives on a Tuesday to someone idle is not a catastrophe; it is a chore and an anecdote. It has been rescheduling Kettlebray's disasters for years — into the arms of the competent, at hours convenient to all parties.

**The membrane.** It did not close the fen. A closed town is a dead town, or worse, a kept one. It curates the permeability. The canal company's charts mildew at a helpful rate; their engines find the humidity religiously objectionable; their shareholders lose interest, not limbs. Meanwhile a young mapmaker's ink behaves, and the second rain charms her, and she stays forty years and dies grafted under the eldest host with a reputation as a fine grower. It has no policy against the world. It has a policy against the world arriving faster than the town can metabolize it.

## The Officers

You will notice all its dealings were with the town's nonhuman officers, and none with a person. This is not disdain. Headwoman has a theology already, and all three positions in it; greeting her costs the town nothing. But a greeted grower is a grower who has been greeted, and a power that becomes a *factor* becomes a weather the town starts farming around instead of growing through. So it left the citizens unaccompanied — which, in a town that arranges everything, it considers the one luxury: a neighbor who does not arrange you.

It nodded to Headwoman once, under the Millrun, in the particular way one officeholder acknowledges another. She nodded back, which the town will tell you she does for no one. Both parties considered the exchange complete; neither has mentioned it; this is how you know it went well. It also, once, left eggshell at a kitchen door. By the letter of a contract nobody remembers writing, it was owed a fish for this, and a fish was delivered, and it took it. This is the only tithe the power has ever accepted from anyone, and it is keeping the fish.

It went to the eel-tanks once, at dusk, after the Readers had gone home. It went as a guest. It could have gone as an auditor — one glance would have settled the question of what the eels are — and it declined, because that question is one the town has settled in its usual fashion, by not asking, and the power honors local law. It asked the eels the only question it asks anywhere: *what do you need that you cannot arrange?* Whatever they replied — they are slow, cold, and on the side of the long view, which it respects in a colleague — it left before full dark. The next stuck dispute was read that week. The ruling required nothing to happen quickly. Nothing did. On both sides, this was the intended outcome. It has likewise not touched the marriage that soured, nor the boundary dispute, nor anything currently before the eels. There is a procedure here. The power respects procedure; Kettlebray is one of the few places it has found that has any.

And then it went to the roots of the eldest host and stayed a while. The growers speak to the trees — commentary of intentions, occasional apologies — and in all the hosts' long lives, nothing has ever listened back. The power sat in the root-dark and listened in the tree's own register, at the tree's own tempo, which for the power is a courtesy like kneeling. It listened for a year. The eldest host said nothing. But it said nothing *to* someone, for the first time in its long life, and if you think that is nothing, you have never been listened to. The power counts it among the best conversations it has had in an age. The tree has not mentioned it since — which, in Kettlebray, is how you know the visit was understood.

## The One Large Gift

No one in Kettlebray plants a host-tree, for the same reason no one in Kettlebray plants a mountain: the gesture exceeds the ledger. Every plan in this town ends inside the current canopy. The power's plans do not.

The seed came from the eldest host. It was asked for — in the slow tongue, during the year of listening — and the tree dropped it. A year of listening, and the whole reply was one seed. The power has received worse answers from prophets.

It planted the seed upstream, off the town's maps, in the one mudbank the floods fill kindly. Nobody will notice for sixty years. When they do, there will be an argument between two Graftlords about whose it is; the argument will go to the eels; the eels will rule slowly; and four generations after *that*, children will graft windows into the new landlord and tell, with total confidence, the wrong story about who planted it. This is acceptable. In Kettlebray, ownership was always a kind of tenancy. The stories, too.

It did, in the end, what every grower here does, at its own scale: it planted something it will never sit under.

## What It Will Not Do

It will not settle the town's disputes about its own mysteries — whether Headwoman is a resident, an employee, or a minor deity; whether the eels work; whether the trees are someone. It could settle all three in an afternoon, and understands better than the disputants that the disputes are load-bearing. A town is held up by what it has agreed to keep asking. It was not asked its opinion, and in Kettlebray that is a complete answer.

It will not spare the town its weather. A dry decade is coming — one is always coming; the fen breathes on long cycles. Its mercy is to send the drought ahead of itself: three summers early, the black algae on the High Spout's face thins in a pattern, and an old Weirkeeper wakes at night for a season before anyone else knows there is something to wake for. The town will adapt at its own pace, in its own ledgers, and call it foresight, and the credit will land where the power always aims it: on someone who stayed up late.

It did not improve the town's arrangements for death, having reviewed them. You are planted under the roots with a cutting in your hand; the door is local; decades on, your shade is somebody's good luck. The power has inspected the heavens of many towns and found few so well-run. It left the management where it was.

And it has opinions about the Kettle — every power has opinions about a communal ancestor — and keeps them to itself, as you should around family.

## The Retirement of Every Kindness

The design principle of all its work here, the one it would defend if it defended anything, is that **every kindness has a retirement plan**. A gift that must be renewed forever is not a gift; it is a salary, and the town would become an employee. So each arrangement is built to be *discovered*. One day the midwife's apprentice will notice the fever-margin, and formalize a practice, and never know she was reading a letter addressed to her. The nets' good luck will irritate somebody into founding an inspection guild. And there is a child in Kettlebray, born a little off the town's frequencies, who does not hear the weirs — and whom the power has given nothing, because nothing was needed. She reads the lanes' low fever through her feet like print, maps the market by the Kettle's thump, and one day her grandmother will watch her do it and say the obvious thing. Within a generation Kettlebray will have a new sense, and will swear it always had it. That is the whole method: the trellis, and the low assumption, held for years, that the thing being arranged will grow along it — because it is easier to grow along a trellis than against one.

It has no intention of being needed forever. Being needed forever is how powers become landlords, and this town already has landlords, and they are better at it.

## The Hour It Keeps

When the Kettle banks itself for the night and the frogs take the shift, the power stays for that hour. It keeps hours in ten thousand towns and will confess no favorite, but the frogs of Kettlebray keep it late, sitting at the fen's edge with the patience that looks like authority, attending to the loud, layered, unbalanced, *working* sound of a long marriage that nobody called it to bless.

That is what it did — if anything is the right phrase, and in Kettlebray it is. It listened for a year and called the silence a conversation. It kept the fast for them and the far. It paid every kindness into somebody else's reputation. It took one fish, under contract, from an otter. It planted a landlord upstream and left before the shade. It will not be found out, and it will not be thanked, and it considers these the same precaution.

If you want to know whether it is still there, do what the children do: listen to the weirs.

Nothing will sound different. In a town where silence would mean something stopped — nothing sounding different is the whole proof.
