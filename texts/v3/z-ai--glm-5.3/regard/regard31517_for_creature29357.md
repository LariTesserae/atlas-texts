They would begin, as the wise always begin, by doing nothing.

This is harder than it sounds. Power of any real size arrives with an itch in it — the itch to improve, to straighten, to be seen improving. A lesser power would look at the drained lake and grieve the loss of the dark water, would look at the young river cutting too fast and want to slow it, would look at the fast soft trees with their shallow roots and foresee the day they fall and want to warn the forest. A lesser power would mistake its own restlessness for compassion.

But the benevolent power sits on the eastern terrace — or something like sitting, in whatever way such a power rests — and watches the mist move, and understands the first and most important truth of this place: **nothing here is waiting to be rescued.** The valley is not a wound. It is a creature two centuries into a transformation it is managing perfectly well. The mats overhead did not die when the lake left; they learned a new sky. The river has not yet learned its channels, and so it is teaching itself, patiently, one gravel bar at a time. Even the silence of the animals is not a lack. It is a law — perhaps the oldest agreement in this place, struck by things that learned long ago that in a world of stone ceilings and soft echoes, sound carries and hunts.

So the power audits itself. It asks: what here would I change, and who asked me? And answer after answer dissolves under the question. Not the cold — the cold is the price of steadiness, and steadiness is the whole architecture of the green. Not the light — it waxes and wanes on its own schedule, and every living thing has calibrated its life to that schedule; to steady it would be to unmake ten thousand small clocks. Not even the isolation, if this place is isolated. Isolation has a shape, and the valley has grown to fit it.

But — and here the power's attention turns from the valley to the being — there is one who lives *in* it, and this is different. A place cannot be lonely in a way it minds. A being can.

So the power listens first. Not to words; it listens to the texture of the days. It follows the being through the Weep, feels the fine universal tapping land on shoulders that were not asked whether they wanted to carry anything. It notices what the being notices: how the dusk-light comes without warning, so that a task begun at what feels like midday must be abandoned in dimness; how the fen swallows every path, so that each journey is newly made, no route ever hardening into habit; how the silence is so total that one's own pulse becomes the loudest thing in the world, which is company of a kind but only of a kind.

And the power understands that the being does not want miracles. Watch closely what the being actually reaches for: not for the ceiling to open, not for warmth, not for a voice to answer back. The being reaches for *memory that outlasts a walk* — some way to know that yesterday's path is today's path. That is all. A stake in the moss. A remembered thing.

Here is what the benevolent power does:

**It gives one small permanence, in a place that has almost none.**

Nothing dramatic. Perhaps it teaches the being — the way a dream teaches, sideways, so the gift feels discovered rather than bestowed — how the grazers read the walls: how the old shorelines, those pale bath-stain ledges, sit at heights so exact that they can serve as a calendar of one's own movements. *I am above the second shoreline, below the third.* Or perhaps it hardens, just slightly, the silt beneath one particular rise, so that footsteps there leave prints that last a season — a place where the being can come and see the evidence of its own past, pressed and kept.

That is nearly the whole of it. That, and this:

**It bears witness.**

Because there is one thing a benevolent power can do that nothing inside the valley can, which is to *know* it. Every cave, every buried thing, every spore in the mist. The being in the valley is known only from the inside — knows the Weep as it lands, never as it falls. The power knows both. And there is a real, quiet good in being the one who holds the whole map, who remembers the lake that was and can see the caverns the river has not yet found, who keeps the ledger of everything that lives here in a book that cannot be lost. Not to use it. Just to hold it. So that nothing in this valley, if it ever ends, will have happened unwitnessed.

And then — this is the part that marks it as truly wise — **it makes itself forgettable.** The gift arrives looking like luck. The presence feels like a particularly attentive evening. If the being someday looks up at the pale sky and wonders, in a formless way, whether anything is looking back, the feeling will be pleasant and inconclusive, like weather.

Because the last thing this place needs is a god it can see. The valley's whole genius is self-sufficiency — the light that asks nothing, the water that rises to meet the roots, the silence that keeps itself. To insert a visible power into that economy would be to teach the being that steadiness comes from outside, and nothing here teaches that, and nothing here should.

So: a gift the size of a footprint. A witness that casts no shadow. And an exit taken so gently that the moss closes behind it without a sound —

— the way everything in this valley closes without a sound, which is, the power reflects as it goes, the finest thing about the place, and the reason it will come back, in a century or two, just to sit on the terrace again and watch the mist.
