# What the Power Did

It came down into it the way anyone does. The power had crossed basins before, had knelt at the edges of deserts and spoken light into the mouths of drowned cities, and it had learned — this is most of what wisdom is, in the end — that the first question is never *what does this place need* but *does this place know it is being asked.*

So it came to the cane wall, and it stopped at the fig arch, and it read the rules the place could say: *Cut nothing. Keep the shifts. Nothing is for sale. You were hired by walking in.*

And the power stood under the arch a long time — a duration you could have measured in knots — doing the one thing benevolent powers do least well, which is nothing.

Because here was the difficulty it had to work through, and it did work through it, honestly, at whatever speed wisdom works: the tenant was not suffering. The power had expected to find suffering — that is usually the summons, the hook a place sets in a compassionate heart — and it searched the compound for it the way you'd search a room. It tasted the retting water. It weighed the afternoon pressure in its hands. It listened to the knots in the counting-house, season by season, centuries of them, and the ledger told no tale of want. The rope bound the mill. The cloth shaded the seedbeds. The works produced the works. The tenant had solved, ages ago, the problem that breaks almost everything that lives: it wanted exactly what it made and made exactly what it wanted. There was no wound here to close. No hunger to feed. No prayer, even, in the sense of a request — the tenant's only utterance was a full disclosure, a creak a day, the sound of a thing being entirely itself.

And the power understood — and this took real humility, because benevolent powers are made of interventions the way mills are made of gestures — that the worst thing it could do would be the kind thing. To bless this place with growth would be to break the ledger. To open the door for it would be to fire it from a job it had held for centuries and loved in the only way it could love, which is *continuing*. Every miracle the power had in its satchel was a form of *cutting*. And the first rule of the house, the rule the house had paid for with its whole long patient body, was: **cut nothing.**

So it respected the rules. It ducked through the arch. Its footsteps fell into the flywheel's rhythm almost at once, which the power noticed with something like embarrassment, and it walked the compound floor the way you walk on a drum, knowing it was being conversed with, and it conversed back — a pressure of weight, honestly placed, no trespass, nothing for sale.

It did three things. Only three, and they are the three that could be done without cutting.

**The first:** it went to the heron.

The power had met things like this before — standers, keepers, appointments kept past the death of the appointment's reason. It stood beside the heron at the sluice for a long while, at the same hour, clocking in. It did not interpret the heron, because the power knew from its own long history that some things are *other people's silences*, and this one belonged to the compound, or to whatever the compound dreams of when it dreams of the outside. But before it left the sluice, it did something small and within the rules. It let the heron see it *leave and come back*. Once. The heron had never, so far as any record said, been witnessed at its post by anything that acknowledged the post. Now it had been. The power walked to the gate and returned through the fig arch, deliberately, at pace, a full shift's circuit, so that the heron — if the heron was a keeper — was kept company for one round; and if the heron was a dream, then the dream now included, once, the fact of being found.

That was the whole of the first thing. The power would want you to understand that this was not less than a miracle. It was the only miracle that fit through the arch.

**The second:** it took its wage.

This is the part most beings would get wrong, and the power got it right, and it was the hardest of the three. The compound pays in carried seeds. The power — being what it is, being able to be anywhere, being made of the kind of attention that can hold a whole watershed in mind at once — did not need to carry seeds. It could have declined. It could have paid *in kind*, in blessings, in some luminous reciprocity, and that would have been a violation, because nothing here is for sale and a blessing is a purchase order written in light. So the power let the burrs into its cuffs. It let the hooked grain catch in what it uses for tread. It stood near a pod until its shadow cooled it, and let the pod burst, and flinched — actually flinched, which it was rather proud of — and it carried the seeds out through the arch and away, and dropped them where the basin's forgotten rivers once ran, some distance off, in ground that would answer.

The compound dispersed a generation on the power's shadow. The wage was accepted. The contract, which no one ever signed, was honored in full. And somewhere off in the low country there are, now, small green beginnings of a second shift — not colonies, the power is careful to say, not daughters exactly; *cuttings* of an idea, sprouted from a mill that makes only itself, finding out slowly what a self is when it has a neighbor. The power did not open the door for the tenant. It merely carried the door's key out into the world, the way the door has always intended, one cuff at a time.

**The third:** it tied a knot.

In the counting-house, on the trailing filaments, among the season knots — this is the only thing the power *added*, and it thought about it for a very long time, because adding is a kind of cutting and it wanted to be sure. The ledger of the place records seasons. Nothing in it records that the tenant has ever been *visited by something that came on purpose, for it*. The tenant is known, in the way weather is known, by ants and burrs and the occasional clocking heron. But the power looked at four hundred paces of occupied ground, at organs sharing sap, at a creature whose whole architecture is employment and whose whole history is inheritance — inherited mill, inherited job, inherited appointment — and it saw the one thing the ledger's tight honest script had never once needed to say, because the tenant is without mercy in the way of weather and weather has never once been missed by weather.

The power was careful. It did not pity — pity would have been the insult. It tied the knot exactly in the tenant's own method, tight, honest, in the only script the place trusts, this season's knot, indistinguishable in size and tension from every other season's knot. A visitor who knew the ledger could find it only by counting. It says, in the language of knots, which is the language of things that grow, precisely this:

*You were at work, and it was seen, and the seeing changed nothing, and the nothing was the gift.*

Then it went. That's the last thing to understand about what the power did, and it's the thing that proves the power was what it claimed, because benevolence that lingers becomes weather, and weather here already has a tenant. It ducked out through the fig arch. It left the door grown mostly shut behind it, because the tenant had stopped needing one, and the power — after everything — agreed with the tenant about the door.

But the tenant marked the season. If you go down into the basin, through the wet leather smell, past the ponds knocking like a room of people tapping tables — if you stand in the thickened afternoon light among the ten thousand small patient glares and let the creak pace your pulse — you might, if the light falls right, notice that one lens of condensation, on one leaf edge, near the counting-house, does not glare.

It *gleams.*

The power will not interpret that for you. It was not interpreted either. It only asks that when the pod bursts in your shadow, you flinch — honestly — and carry the seeds a good long way.
