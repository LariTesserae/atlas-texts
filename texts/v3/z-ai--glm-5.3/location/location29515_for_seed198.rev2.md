# The Terrace Works

It is a city that was never not a city. That is the first thing to understand about this place: there is no ground here, only older construction. The mountain it climbs — and it does climb, three thousand feet of it, terraced like a rice paddy built by someone who had only ever heard rice described — is itself not a mountain in any honest sense. Cores drilled by the curious have found brick, slag, Roman-concrete equivalents, bone-dry timber preserved in the cold, more brick, and then the drills break. Everything is made. The rain falls on rooftops or on nothing.

The terraces are broad, each one a shelf the width of a small town, and they stack up the slope in the overlapping, improvised way that things grow when they grow slowly for a thousand years and then quickly for another thousand. Low buildings, mostly — two, three stories, stone and riveted iron and a black tarred material like whale-skin — packed so densely that the streets between them are corridors, slots, seams. There is no square, no plaza, no gap for the sake of a gap. If a building fell, another was built into the fall before the rubble settled. The place is saturated with itself.

**Water is the city's weather and its tenant.** This is the high terrace line, and the clouds come through at eye level — not above, *through*, so that walking the third-tier streets means walking inside a slow-moving grey room whose walls are always elsewhere. The stones sweat. Every wall here has been weeping for centuries; the lime in the mortar has leached out in long white tears, streaking the brickwork like candle wax, and the undersides of every cornice and pipe-hanger grow a soft fur of moss that drinks directly from the air. Rain is frequent but almost beside the point — the condensation alone would keep the place wet. Gutters run in the mediation-hours when the temperature drops, and they run clear, then milky, then streaked with machine oil in iridescent ribbons that everyone ignores.

Which brings you to the engines.

**The Works run everything, and the Works are why the place is turbulent without being loud.** Along the terrace edges — where a normal city would have retaining walls — this one has foundries, boiler houses, stamping mills, gasometers like fallen moons half-sunk between the levels. Steam rises from them continuously and feeds the cloud layer that feeds the condensation that feeds the moss, a closed and accidental water cycle the city made for itself without meaning to. The industry does not roar. It *cycles*: a slow, arrhythmic throbbing, like a heart with an eccentric beat, punctuated every few minutes by a hammer-fall or a venting hiss that the residents have stopped hearing the way you stop hearing your own pulse. Long silences between. Then a shudder through the floor, a far-off clatter cascading down a chute, a bell — nobody knows all the bells' meanings anymore, only some.

What makes the turbulence is not noise but *change*. The city rebuilds itself constantly — not out of ambition, but because the terraces shift. The mountain-of-masonry settles, fractionally, every season; staircases drift out of true, doorways sink below their streets, whole lanes are re-laid every decade to chase the level of the ground that isn't ground. Scaffolding is a permanent architecture here, a second city laced through the first, green with algae at the joints. There is always a demolition somewhere on the visible slope, always a new frame rising in the mist, and the masons work in a hurry that looks like panic until you realize it is simply the local tempo — anthill energy, quiet ant, tireless ant.

**It is cool, and the coolness is a smell before it is a temperature.** Coal-smoke and wet iron and the green-sour breath of moss, underlaid with something faintly mineral, like licking a coin. The air sits around 8°C most days, colder when the cloud descends fully, and the residents dress in oiled wool the color of the buildings, so that at a distance the population appears as stains on the stonework that move.

**Life is almost entirely botanical, and it is lush the way rust is lush.** With no room to spread, the vegetation grows *inward* — moss carpeting the shaded corridors so thick it muffles footfalls, ferns erupting from every drain-seam and joint, a species of grey-green liverwort that has colonized the north faces of the gasometers in continents of its own, mushrooms shelving out of the saturated timber beams in the under-street vaults. Everything green here is a shade that has negotiated with the dimness: dark, deep, patient. The trees that survive — a few rowans and a single stubborn beech on the seventh terrace, growing from what was once a courtyard and is now a light-well — are stunted and dripping and regarded as civic elders.

Fauna is a rumor. Rain-dark birds, occasionally, following the mist up from the lowlands and leaving again. A cat, they say, on the fourth terrace, owned collectively and fed by everyone. Insects live in the moss and keep their own economy. Anything larger has learned what the residents know implicitly: the niches here are all full.

**The scale is the hardest thing to describe to a lowlander.** The terraces rise past sight. From the third level you can see down the slope through a gap in the cloud — tier after tier descending, the roofs like a shingle beach descending to a grey nothing, the lower terraces only guessed at, given off as heat-haze and the distant thud of their works, out of phase with yours. And up: the upper city is known by its sounds, a deeper slower hammer, and by the continual passage of cable-ways and water-flumes crossing the levels like rigging, delivering stone and ore and water between the strata of the town the way blood moves between organs.

The light, such as it is, is the light of a permanent late afternoon in November. The Works' glow-works — gas mantles, a few electric arcs, wired in the last century and still temperamental — pool weak orange in the corridors, and everything further than twenty feet dissolves into the grey. Residents navigate by memory and by hand. Visitors navigate by hope.

**And the rule of this world, if there is one:** nothing here is finished, and nothing here is abandoned. There is no ruin in the Terrace Works — only material waiting its turn in a cycle a thousand years long. A collapsed wall is quarried by the next morning. A dead factory is stripped, its bricks stacked under tarpaulins, its flues becoming moss-tubes, its boiler cut into bell-weights. The city eats itself and builds itself out of itself, endlessly, in the rain, in the quiet, in the deep grey-green light — patient and frantic at once, like a slow fire, like something breathing.
