The ground is warm. That is the first fact, learned through the boots before the eyes understand anything: an even, patient heat rising out of the stone, the way a stove holds warmth long after the fire. And yet the light is poor — a dim, directionless pearl-gray luminance falling from a ceiling of haze so low you feel you could touch it. Warmth from below, dimness from above. Everything about this place is arranged upside down from how a landscape is supposed to be, and the longer you stand here, the more the arrangement seems deliberate.

You are high. The air carries that thinness your lungs keep noticing — clouds sometimes pass below you, or drift past at knee height — but there is no cold. That mismatch is the plateau's signature, and the whole vast country unfolds from it like a mechanism unfolding from a case. The plateau is young beyond argument. Every edge is a knife. Canyons drop away with walls so fresh they still show the striations of their parting, and there is no rubble at their feet because there has not been time for rubble. Nothing here has been argued with yet by water or wind. The stone arrives finished — flats that are planar to a tolerance, bores that are round, chamfers where two faces meet as if someone had taken a tool to the join. There are no tool marks. There is nothing made here at all. And yet.

Stand at the rim of one of the vents at the dim hour and watch the glow come up. Deep in the throat of the rock there is a kiln-orange radiance, steady, breathing with a slow rhythm like a sleeping thing's furnace, and out of the opening rolls a warm exhale of steam that rises in a column until it feeds the haze-deck overhead. The vents are everywhere — you pass one every few hundred meters, sometimes a bare bore, sometimes a cluster like a battery of stacks, and from each one the same patient exhalation. That is when the word arrives, and once it arrives it will not leave: *works*. This is a works. A foundry, a plant, a floor of operations the size of a small sea. But walk a week in any direction and you will not find a rivet, a weld, a foundation, a wrapper, a tool dropped by a hand. There are no hands. The machinery is not installed in the land. The machinery *is* the land — geology run as industry, a factory with no one on the floor and no product going out the door, or else the product is the plateau itself, still being made.

The water arrives on a schedule. There is no rain to speak of, no rivers, no lakes — the gorges are dry, but they run fog: each dim-shift the valleys fill bank to bank with a slow grey current that moves like water remembering being water. And every surface sweats. Stone wears a film of condensation, beading silver in the weak light, sheeting on the polished flats. If you press cloth to a boulder and wring it into your mouth you get a taste of hot flint and green — drinkable, faintly mineral, warm as breath. Dew is the currency here. Everything that lives is in the business of wringing it out of the air.

And the air is crowded. That is what the dimness hides at first: how little of this place is empty. Fog in the gorges. Spores riding the updrafts in visible grain, like dust in a shaft of light, except the light is everywhere and so is the grain. Steam threading between the stacks. Birds stitched to the vents. And on every surface that holds still for more than a day, the felt.

The felt is the vegetation, and it is not slow. A fresh rock face — and every rock face here is fresh — goes grey-soft within a day, furred within three, and inside a week it carries a mat of pale succulent growth thick as a thumb, drinking the condensation before it can bead. From the mat comes everything else: climbers that run the ridgelines like conduit, laying their pale stems along the sharp edges as if following printed lines; forests in the hollows of soft-pillared trees whose trunks are barely wood — more cork than timber, warm to the touch, pale as uncooked dough. Cut one and you find no rings. Instead there are bands — dozens of fine growth-bands to a trunk — and if you sit with them long enough you learn what they mean: not years, because there are no years here, but *shifts*. The trees keep the engine's calendar. They flower when the vent pressure changes, all of them at once across a hundred kilometers, opening in a single soft exhalation of pale blooms that the fog then carries off within hours. It is lush, but it is a young lushness — not the cathedral hush of old forest, but the packed, hurried, weed-energy green of something covering ground because ground has just become available.

It is also, all of it, quiet in a way that becomes unnerving. The felt takes sound. The fog takes sound. The soft ground takes sound. And the great rearrangements of the place — and they are constant, the land revising itself hour by hour — happen in near silence, so that you see a hillside shrug loose and re-form, a whole slope of it, stone rising and folding with the slow terrible assurance of water, and hear nothing but your own pulse. Storms of steam roll across the plateau and re-arrange the horizon and pass you like a held breath. The only constant note is not a note at all: a sub-bass idle, felt in the sternum and the teeth rather than heard, the floor-frequency of the deep machinery, always on. Against that silence the small sounds become events. A single drop falling from a felt-tip into a dew pool lands like a struck bell. When the vent-birds call to each other up and down the stack-columns — a chittering, metallic, like a wrench dropped on a workbench in another room — it is the only speech in the country.

The animals are not residents. That is the strangest thing to accept, and the plateau gives you no choice about accepting it. They arrive on shift. In the bright-dim of the opening hours the grazers come up out of the low fog in loose crews — slab-bodied, low-slung things with keratin scrapers instead of teeth, equipment they were born wearing, and they move along the dew-sheets licking the stone in lines, unhurried, thorough, leaving licked-clean flats behind them like finished work. The vent-birds live inside the exhaust columns, riding the updrafts, feeding on whatever the vents exhale — some warm particulate the air is dense with — and they never land anywhere but the stack-rims. And there are the ones still being finished. In the herds you will see the same animal at different revisions: three jaw-shapes grazing within sight of each other, none of them the final version, some with legs that end unfinished, smooth as fired clay, faintly steaming in the cool of the hollows, being cured. Once, at a kiln-vent, you watch one come out — new, warm, damp with condensate, walking within minutes, blinking in the pearl light. Nothing here is born. Things are *issued*. And when a term runs out, an animal does not die; it walks down into the fog-filled low places, lies down in the warm steam, and is rained upward — loosened back into mist over hours, returned to the stock. Death, here, is reclamation and restocking, and the herds sync to it like crews changing shifts.

Then there is the drafting, which is the plateau's deepest business, and the reason nothing here can be mapped. Landforms condense. Out of the denser hazes — the grey banks that pool in the low country — ridges precipitate, assembling from the fog itself the way frost assembles on glass, and they run their term sharp-edged and new, and then dissolve again into the air that made them. You can watch a hill be made. You can watch an arch condense out of nothing over the course of an hour — and once you watched the arch take proportions that stopped you: the exact proportions of a doorway, lintel-sharp, sills squared, sized for something your shape. There is no doorway anywhere here. There is no builder anywhere here. The land had simply *practiced* one — one of its almost-artifacts, its rehearsals of the made: steps in stone that no feet shaped, a basin with a poured lip, a groove that wants to be a channel, each one produced, held for a shift or a season, and then re-absorbed. The plateau keeps almost inventing tools and never keeps any. Whatever it is for, none of it is for keeping.

The rules, as well as anyone has drawn them:

- The heat comes from below and the light from above, and the two have nothing to do with each other.
- Water is delivered by condensation, on the engine's schedule, and everything alive is a dew-harvester of one kind or another.
- Stone arrives finished and hard; life arrives soft and fast; and both are provisional.
- Motion is mostly mute here — the felt, the fog, and the ground itself take the sound out of the air, so that cataclysm looks like weather.
- Nothing built persists. The land can produce the *shapes* of made things, but it dissolves every artifact before the artifact can mean.
- Time does not run level. Some hollows are slow — steam hangs mid-swirl for hours there, dew takes half a shift to fall — and the fauna use those pools or avoid them by some knowledge you don't have.
- The fog revises what passes through it. Walk through a dense bank and you may come out elsewhere, or later, or very slightly different. Your boots may not match afterward.
- Maps go stale within a day. Do not trust a map, including the one you are making in your head, including this one.

At shift-change — the plateau's own dusk, when the vents all exhale together and the haze-deck swells and the light dims toward the ember-dark — you can stand at the rim of a canyon and watch the undersides of the clouds go kiln-orange from a thousand glowing throats, and the herds file down into the low fog to be rained or to rest, and the felt-forests close their flowers in one long soft wave rolling away to the horizon like a workforce going off the clock. Nothing watches this happen. That may be the final, hardest fact of the place: it is a works with no one observing the output, a production with no product going anywhere, an engine the size of a country running for no audience at all. You are not part of the process. You are, at most, a draft of a visitor, standing on ground that is already being revised behind your heels — your footprints dissolving, gently, before you have finished making them.

You leave with dew in your cuffs, the idle hum still in your ribs, and the strong suspicion that the plateau has already re-used everything you touched.
