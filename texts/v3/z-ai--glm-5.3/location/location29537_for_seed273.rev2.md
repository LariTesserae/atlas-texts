You come up the last of the stair-cuts in early light, and the country opens all at once: a green highland standing so far above the world that the clouds are below you, hazing out the lower countries entirely. Kilometers up. The air is thin and very bright, and the land rolls away for miles — a forest of enormous red chimneys rising like old-growth trunks, ranges of glasshouse roofs blazing, wooded hills, a stitched line of bright water running down a valley that has clearly been a valley for centuries. It looks, at first, like a lush warm province that happens to have industry in it. Only after you have walked an hour does the order of things reverse: the industry has the province in it. There is no ground here. There has never been ground here.

The cliffs at the rim are brick. Not brick-clad — brick through. Lay a hand on the crag and your fingers find courses, headers and stretchers, weathered until they read like sedimentary strata; a band of kiln ash between one era of building and the next sits like tuff between lava flows. Erosion has had centuries to be kind, and it has been. Edges are rounded like river stone. Steps are dished by feet. Iron has gone to soft umber velvet, and concrete to something streaked and gentle, streaked the way old men are lined. Lichen borders on the walls have not moved within living memory; the keepers use them the way sailors use charts.

And the crag is warm. That is the first thing your body learns here: warmth at height. The whole Table holds heat the way the back of a kiln does. Warmth comes up through every floor, out of every flue, off every duct, and the thin bright air sits on it like a lid. Frost touches only the very highest masts, only at dawn, and is gone by mid-morning. Nights cool slowly — the masonry gives back the day for hours, and the gardens never know winter.

The works sweat. That is where the water comes from, mostly. In the cool hours dew stands on every rail, every leaf, every cable, and the Table shines wet from rim to rim in the first light. Then it runs. Every walkway has a gutter cut into it, every gutter feeds a weir-ladder, every weir-ladder a chain that drips with the patience of a metronome, and all of it gathers into the Rill — the slow bright river stitched down the middle of the valley, its bed not stone but cast ceramic, molded in imitation of rapids so faithful that herons fish it without complaint. Between the wet gardens there are dry terraces where the wind keeps house; the water lives in pockets — cisterns under the plazas, black and full of eels; boiler ponds warm as baths; troughs along the foundry walls. Real rain comes rarely, a few times a year, and when it does the whole region becomes an instrument for a day. People stand outside to hear it.

Light is not subtle here. Thin air, hard glare, whitewashed cistern walls, glass by the hectare — noon is a white roar. On the high masts, mirror arrays creep a degree an hour, pitching sun down through gratings onto the under-decks, and the light that falls through them arrives in leaves, in bars, in coins. Moss grows on every face, including the north ones. Brightness is so freely spent it comes back as a property of the air itself.

It is loud, but loud the way a river is loud — a saturation, not an assault, made entirely of slow things summed. The flywheels thump somewhere below, patient as surf. The drip-chorus follows the sweat-run. A million swifts scream around the chimney mouths at the shift of breath. Mason bees work the worn hollows of the mortar by the thousands, an audible fuzz. When wind crosses the Chimneys, the openings sound long organ chords, different every hour, and the flue forest plays until the weather changes. Underneath all of it is the hum — flywheels, fans, current in the wires, water in pipes — at a pitch you stop hearing by the second day. You notice it only when you leave, and everywhere else sounds like a tooth missing.

The vegetation is equipment, and is treated as such. The great filter gardens were built to scrub and cool the works' air, and they do, and they have grown lush past all intention: fig trees rooted in compost beds ten meters deep, gourds climbing the trellis-cables, ferns furred along every gutter, epiphytes hung on the gantries like washing, orchids established in the warm throats of the chimneys where the exhaust breathes through them all day. The keepers service a fig tree the way they'd service a pump — check the drip line, clear the graft, note the yield. Pruning here is maintenance, not gardening. A moss bank is a filter with a maintenance schedule. The southern hills, wooded now, are old slag heaps — fig and bamboo on fired spoil — and even their soil is laid: crushed brick, kiln ash, compost, bedding down in strata like everything else.

For the animals, the Table is simply habitat, and a generous one. Swifts by the million in the Chimneys. Cats patrolling the mill floors on schedules of their own invention. Goats on the cable-highs, grazing the gantry grasses. Eels and fat carp in the cisterns; frogs in the gutter-runs; herons at the weir-ladders at the same turns every day. Monkeys work the fig terraces, and have learned the keepers' parts drawers, and are tolerated, because their sorting is accurate. Fireflies rise in the flue shade at dusk. Everything living is timed to the machinery — the dawn chorus detonates exactly at the breath-shift, as if the works had promised it.

The engines themselves are the reason none of this hurries. The Slow Engines — nobody calls them anything else — are beam-and-flywheel things of riveted iron, turning a few times a minute, always turning, warm to the touch, oiled by hand. Time here is told in turns; the day has a number of them, and the Rill's schedule, the market hours, the ferry across the boiler pond, all run by the count. Hammers rise and fall at the foundry every forty seconds, an unhurried heartbeat. Fans the size of millwheels move the garden air at a walking pace. Electricity exists, humbly — lamps in the under-decks, small motors, the mirror-masts — but nothing here is ever in a rush to finish, because nothing here is going anywhere.

The keepers make that literal. There are moss-cutters and gutter-walkers, flue-sweepers, condensate measurers, seed librarians, and their whole economy is balance: repair matches wear, exactly, forever. Nothing is rebuilt before its time and nothing is left past it. A wall gets its new courses the season its old ones go soft. The rules are few and old: water belongs to the Works until it reaches the Rill, and then it belongs to everyone. Heat is never wasted; every flue's breath is ducted through something living. Wear is a schedule, not a failure. Nothing new, nothing lost.

As for what the Works make — that is the quiet joke of the place. Brick. Tile, glass, castings, glazed pipe. Ordinary, excellent, endless. And all of it is consumed as ground. Dig anywhere — not that anyone digs; digging here is demolition — and you find floors under floors, and between the floors the Works' own output laid down in strata: coarse early brick, then finer, then tile, then the modern courses. The deepest bricks anyone has reached are glazed and blank except for a single stamped word in no script the keepers can read. And at the rim, quietly, kilns cast the next terrace — the Table grows a hand's width a year, a millimeter a season, the world's slowest construction project extending into the sky. The Works make the ground they stand on. They have been standing on themselves, and growing, for longer than the records run, which is not long, because records here are kept on brick.

Evening comes slowly, like everything else. The sweat-run begins; the Rill drops to its night schedule; the Chimneys start their long exhale, hours of it, the whole region breathing out. The mirror-masts creep home to their dawn positions. If you stand at the rim, you can feel the warmth rising out of the country for miles behind you and see the lights of the lower countries far below, going about their fast business. The hum continues. This place has been mid-breath for centuries and is in no hurry to finish.
