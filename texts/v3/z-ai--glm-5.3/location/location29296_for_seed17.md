You hear it before you see anything — though *hear* is the wrong word, because sound in this water is not a thing received by ears. It arrives through your sternum. Through your teeth. Through the fluid behind your eyes. It is a roar with no edge and no beginning, so constant that after a while your body files it under *gravity* and stops noticing, the way it stopped noticing weight.

Then the light sorts itself out, and you understand where you are.

You are two hundred meters down on a drowned highland, and the water above you is the clearest you will ever stand inside — a cold, mineral, almost drinking-water clarity that carries sunlight all the way down as a strong silver-blue, banded and shifting, cathedral light. The surface is a ceiling far overhead, a trembling sheet of wind-bright metal, so far up that its storms, if it ever has storms, arrive here as nothing but a slow softening of the glow. The water is one degree above freezing. It takes heat from you the way a machine takes fuel. Whatever you are, however you're breathing, the cold is the first rule and the second rule both.

The reason the water is so clear is the same reason the word *green* means nothing here. There are no plants. Not drowned gardens, not drifting spores, not a single filament of leaf or stem or alga anywhere on the highland or in the water column above it. Nothing photosynthesizes. The sea over the Table is an unfiltered pane, and everything in it is visible from an unreasonable distance, which mostly means you can see exactly how empty it is.

Because it is empty. The drowned country beneath you is continental — ridgelines running off past the reach of sight, a whole province of hills and benches and fault-scarps a thousand kilometers across — and it holds almost nothing. You can travel for days over it. A gravel ridge, sharp as the day it was dumped. Then nothing. A pylon. Then nothing. A shoal of pale animals like a sudden weather system. Then nothing again, an hour of nothing, the mineral floor sliding by under the blue light with the composure of a place that has never needed to be interesting.

And the terrain is *new*. That's the strangest gift of the flood: it drowned the highland before the highland could grow old. The scarps still have edges like table knives. The gravel beds are unrolled, unwashed, unsorted. There are blast benches cut into the ridges — quarry work, machine-cut steps with the tool marks still crisp — and no frost, no roots, no rain ever came afterward to soften them, because the water took over the work of preservation the moment it arrived. There is no soil anywhere. There never was. Soil is something plants make, and plants never made it here. The flood did not erode this country. It canned it.

The roar, when you finally trace it to its source, is the rim.

The Table ends at a cliff, and the cliff runs for four hundred kilometers, and over its lip the cold dense water of the plateau pours continuously into the deep basin beyond — a cataract falling three kilometers, water falling through water, too slow and too heavy to look like a waterfall, more like a slow blue avalanche of the sea sliding down its own staircase. It doesn't taper, doesn't surge, doesn't rest. It has been pouring at this rate for longer than anything here can remember, and it will be pouring when you leave. At the base, the fall shatters into a permanent fog of suspended sediment, a standing cloud kilometers wide, and around the cloud the water organizes itself into slow fixed gyres and internal waves that never break and never rebuild — a weather system made entirely of density and pressure. If you want to know what weather is like in a place that has no sky, it is this. The roar is the weather.

The machines are what keep it all steady, and this takes a while to understand.

Along the rim, set into the lip like dentures, are weirs and gates and great metering-housings — pump halls straddling the notches, their intakes drinking the plateau water, their outfalls delivering it over the edge in governed volume. The pour is constant because the pour is *managed*. Back from the rim, a cable-way marches across the empty country, pylon after pylon at kilometer spacing, each mast wearing a lamp, and every lamp burns the same strong industrial white, and once a day — you are told, or you measure it, because it's too slow to watch — the light runs a slow chase down the entire line and returns to its origin, a heartbeat a thousand kilometers long. There is a transformer yard on a shelf, humming. There is a stack venting a rope of mineral-saturated water that holds its shape for thirty kilometers downstream, a standing plume. Stenciled numbers, arrows, depth-plates, warnings — all still legible under their thin amber film. Everything runs. Nothing wears. Nothing has needed a hand in as long as the counters count, and no one is here to count. The Works do not appear to be making anything. They appear to be *holding* something — metering the pour, balancing the gyres, feeding the lamps — a maintenance of an equilibrium so complete that the maintenance itself is invisible unless you stand still for days and watch the water not change.

And the machines, it turns out, are the soil.

The base of all life here is a film — a chemotrophic varnish, faintly sulfur-yellow, that grows on bare rock, on cut stone, on pipe and plate and the insides of flooded rooms, living on the chemistry of the young rock itself and on the leaks and warmth the Works exude. Where the varnish grows, the animals come, and around the outfalls and the lamps and the plume they come in absurd abundance: swarms of comb-animals thick enough to read a stencil through, drifting like slow syrup in the cold; armored grazers the size of doors that mow the varnish off the cable-way in tidy overlapping lanes; dredgers, huge and unhurried, that work the sediment fog at the base of the falls. And needles — sleek hunting things that have learned the one secret of the loud world: silence is real estate.
