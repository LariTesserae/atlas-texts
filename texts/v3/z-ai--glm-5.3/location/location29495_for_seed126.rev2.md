# The Terraces

There is no ground here. There never was. Everything you could stand on, everything you can see, was made — and made so long ago that the making has itself become a kind of geology.

The place is called, by those who bother to name it, the Terraces, though the name undersells the scale. It is a constructed landscape the size of a small continent, perhaps the size of a world — horizon after horizon of interlocking platforms, ramps, basins, and stepped planes, all of it engineered from a material that has no clean analogue: it looks like ceramic, but it flexes; it looks like metal, but it is warm to the touch, and faintly, faintly translucent at the edges, as though light is stored somewhere inside it and only occasionally remembers to leave.

## The form of the place

The Terraces are old in the way that erosion is old — but here, erosion ran backwards. Whatever built this place shaped it toward stillness, and then billions of cycles of dust, heat, and light were permitted to finish the job. The result is a landscape in its final, settled form: vast shallow basins hundreds of kilometers across, connected by ramps so long and so gently graded that you cannot see them rise — you only discover, hours later, that you have climbed. The great edges and jaggedness that young landscapes have are gone. Everything has been worn, or was born, smooth. The horizon is a set of soft concentric terraces stepping down toward a distance you can feel but never reach.

Elevation exists only as a rumor. The whole terrain hovers near some universal baseline, a plane the builders seem to have agreed upon, and the basins dip below it and the ramps rise above it by degrees so slight the world feels almost, but not quite, flat.

## Dryness, and what fills the absence

There is no water. Not a scarcity of it — an *absence*, engineered or absolute. Nothing rains. Nothing flows. What the basins hold instead is a dry, dense, faintly luminous particulate — a mineral snow that migrates across the terraces in slow, bright drifts, moving the way sand moves in water, in dunes and curtains and long whispering sheets. When a drift crosses a ramp it makes a sound: a hiss so soft it sits at the very bottom of hearing, like the memory of rain played back at a fraction of its volume.

This is nearly the only sound. The Terraces are quiet in the way deep libraries are quiet — not empty, but hushed, as though the entire environment is holding a conversation below the threshold of ears. Occasionally a distant terrace emits a single low tone, a full-bodied hum that rises through your feet rather than the air, holds for a minute, and stops. Nobody knows what the tones are for. The tones do not repeat.

## Light and warmth

It is bright here the way noon on salt flats is bright, but without the cruelty. The light comes from everywhere and nowhere — the material of the terraces themselves glow at a low, tireless level, and something high above, a diffuse sourceless sky, contributes the rest. Warmth is constant: a pleasant, dry, late-afternoon heat that has not changed within the memory of anything alive. Shadows are short and soft. Colors are pale — bone, oyster, pale gold, the greenish sheen of old glass — and the brightness bleaches them further so the whole world reads like an overexposed photograph that nonetheless contains infinite detail if you look long enough.

## What grows, what moves

Life is sparse but utterly confident of its place. From seams in the terrace material — hairline joints in the made ground, the only places where the world admits it was assembled — grows a low, silver-green vegetation: mats of fine leafless stalks that shiver without wind and slowly, over weeks, migrate across the surface like very patient animals. They drink nothing. They metabolize the light and the mineral drift directly, and where they have lived a long time the terrace beneath them has developed a faint patina, a bloom, like tarnish on silver.

Fauna is rarer still. There is one thing here worth calling an animal, and you may walk for days without seeing it: a flat, slow, many-legged grazer the size of a hand, translucent as wax paper, with a body organized around a single long digestive tract that processes the luminous dust. It moves at glacial speed and appears to be warm inside. When two of them meet they stop, press their edges together, exchange something, and continue. That is the entirety of observed animal behavior in this world. It is somehow enough.

## Density and the deep world

What makes the Terraces overwhelming is not any single view — it is how much is *here*. The world is dense the way a manuscript is dense: every surface carries structure. The material is not plain; it is inscribed at every scale. At the scale of a continent, the terraces themselves form patterns — basins arranged in vast spirals visible only from a height no one has. At the scale of a meter, the surfaces carry grooves, channels, dimples, sockets — the sockets regularly spaced, all empty, all clean, as though an entire civilization of parts was once plugged into this world and has since been withdrawn. And at the scale of the very small — this is where the engineering goes past the modern — the material is *active*. Look closely at any surface and you can see it working: a texture like heat-shimmer, a slow rearrangement, the terrace maintaining itself, healing its scratches, rounding its own corners back toward its designed shape. The world is worn and ancient and perfectly maintained, simultaneously. It has reached a state where decay and repair are the same process.

## The active stillness

For all its quiet, the Terraces are not dead — they are running. Weather happens here: the mineral drifts consolidate into slow storms, bright fogbanks of particulate that move along channels the world seems to have prepared for them, thickening in the basins, thinning on the ramps. The light has tides. The tones come and go. The vegetation migrates on its patient journeys; the grazers graze; the surfaces self-repair in real time. It is an ecosystem running at perhaps a thousandth of Earthly tempo, every process present but stretched across time like honey, so that to a hurried observer it looks like stillness and only to a patient one looks like the elaborate, ongoing, functioning machine that it is.

## The feeling of the place

Standing on the Terraces at the long bright noon, you notice three things, in order.

First: the silence — and under the silence, that almost-sound, the hiss of drifting mineral, the world breathing through its floor.

Second: the age. This place is not ruined and not young; it is *finished*. Whatever drama it contained is over. It has settled into its eternal form the way a river settles into its channel at the end of a continent, and there is something both comforting and terrible about standing on a world that is complete.

Third — and this arrives slowly — the suspicion that the Terraces are not abandoned. That the empty sockets, the tones, the self-repair, the perfect grade of every ramp, are all the visible portion of a system still in use. That the world is not waiting to be filled again.

That it is occupied by something simply too large, too slow, or too quiet for you to have noticed yet.
