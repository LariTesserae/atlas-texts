# The Cistern Balcony

You find it through a door that was never meant to be a door — a maintenance seam in the flank of a structure so large no one alive remembers its whole footprint. The mountain, if it is a mountain, is made. Everything here is made. There is no rock under anything, only rock-*shaped* material, and once you've noticed that — once you've seen the strata of the cliff face resolve into laminated intervals of manufacture, each layer a different century's idea of what stone should pretend to be — you cannot unsee it.

The seam opens onto a pocket. A single room, though "room" does it injustice and so does "cave." Perhaps forty meters across, hung inside the structure's upper works like a jewel in a setting. Elevation is real here — the air is thin enough that the warmth sits strangely on the skin, humid and high both, like breathing inside a greenhouse that has been lifted six kilometers into the sky by an act of pure stubbornness.

It is dim. Not dark — dim, the way deep aquariums are dim. Light comes from nowhere in particular and everywhere faintly: a cold phosphorescence sweating out of the made-stone itself, strongest along joints and seams, as if the structure glows where it is tired. The architecture of the place is old machinery gone mineral. Terraced balconies of corroded alloy descend in gentle stairs toward a basin at the center, and everything, everything, is wet.

Water is the place's whole occupation. It does not rain here, exactly. The walls *weep*. Condensation is engineered, deliberate, an old system still faithfully running: moisture is gathered somewhere far below, lifted, and exhaled from ten thousand hairline channels in the masonry. It beads on the ceiling in arrays so regular they look typeset. It runs down fluted pilasters that were — you realize after a while — grown, not carved, their fluting the ghost of vascular channels. Droplets fall in a steady, countless percussion into the basin, onto ledges, into the small mouths of drainage glyphs.

And this is why the place is deafening. Not mechanically loud — *composed* loud. Thousands of water-drops, each landing surface tuned by shape and material to a different pitch, and the ancient system drops them in loose, drifting rhythms that stack into a wash of sound like an orchestra of tuned leaks. The noise is physical. It fills your chest. Conversation is impossible; you communicate by touch, by gesture, by the slow language of standing near someone. The creatures here have solved this problem long ago.

Because the fauna — there is almost no vegetation, a few pale films of engineered lichen and a single stubborn curtain of reed-like growth at the basin's rim — but the *animals* are abundant beyond reason. The density of life in this one pocket is the density of a coral reef. Everything warm and dim and wet invites them. Small segmented grazers the size of a hand move along the weeping channels in near-touching processions, drinking the structure's exhalations, their shells colored to match the phosphorescence. Gliding membrane-things wheel through the upper dark in silent flocks, feeding on the midges that swarm wherever water pools. Something large and slow breathes in the basin's deeper shadow — you hear it only as a low modulation under the water-music, a silence shaped like a body.

There is a predator. You never quite see it. You see the processions of grazers suddenly *thin*, see the flocking things snap into tight defensive spirals, see ripples in the basin that traveled the wrong direction. The ecosystem is in motion here, complete, balanced — has been for an age, in its own state, weathered into equilibrium. The structure built the climate; the climate invited the life; the life now maintains the climate, the grazers keeping the channels clear, the flocks seeding the lichen. Nobody runs this place anymore. It runs itself, the way an old millwheel keeps turning after the miller's whole civilization is dust.

The one genuinely strange thing — and it is only mildly strange, a wrongness you taste rather than see — is that the water never rises. The basin brims. The drops keep falling by the thousand. And the level stays exactly where it has always been, marked by a mineral lip grown smooth by eons of touching. Somewhere, the water goes. Somewhere, all this sound, all this falling, all this faithful lifting-and-releasing is *going somewhere*, and the balcony's oldest residents — the animals, the lichen, the machinery itself — do not know where, and do not care.

You stay longer than you meant to. The sound makes thought difficult and rest easy. When you finally leave through the seam, the outer silence feels like a held breath.
