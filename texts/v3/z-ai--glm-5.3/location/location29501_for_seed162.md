The plateau begins as an edge.

You come to it from above or you don't come to it at all — from the rim, over a cliff of black rock so new it still holds the memory of heat, you look down through a thousand meters of haze to a sea of cloud that behaves like water: it has currents, slack tides, slow silver rivers of its own. The tableland runs back from that edge farther than any eye can hold. It is its own continent, warm, dim, and busy, and it makes its own weather twice a day. Whatever sun exists here is an arrangement between the ground and the ceiling, and neither party is honest about it.

The light, first, because the light explains almost everything else. There is no sun overhead. There is a ceiling — a high deck of cloud whose underside glows, grey-gold, even and sourceless, like a lamp behind smoked glass. That glow is not weather and not sky. It is the plateau's own warmth returning to it. The ground gives off heat all night and all day, the heat rises, the ceiling catches it and lights up, and so the place is a room that lights itself from below. You stand inside a lampshade the size of a country. The ceiling breathes: hours of bright, then hours of dimmer, a long tidal inhale and exhale that everyone here treats as the clock. Nobody calls these hours day and night. They call them shifts.

Beneath that breathing light, the rock.

It is basalt with a plan. Black, dense, organized — stratified not the way sediment is organized, patiently, layer on layer over eons, but the way a manufactured thing is organized, with intent, with bands and channels and what can only be called plumbing. The gravel underfoot is angular as fresh glass; nothing has rounded it because nothing has had time. This is a young landscape, but its youth is not an age. It is a rate. Erosion here happens as weather, not as time: a hill can be sharp at dawn, chewed smooth by evening, dissolved by the third shift, and re-condensed somewhere else by the fifth. Nothing on this plateau is old enough to be dirty. Dust exists for a few hours and is then collected.

And nothing here was made. That needs saying plainly, because the word that keeps forcing itself on you is *factory*, and the word is wrong in exactly one crucial way. There are no walls. No roads, no foundations, no toolmarks, no bones arranged by hands, not a single artifact anywhere in the visible world. No one built this place. And yet it is run. It is run continuously, in shifts, by consensus of geology.

The condenser groves do the wet work. The trees are tall, black-barked, their needles in thousands of fine combing rows, and their whole purpose is fog: when a front comes off the haze-sea below and rolls inland — a wall of grey wool, dense enough to lean on — the groves strip it. You can stand inside a grove during a fog shift and hear the whole event: a soft combing rush, like cloth drawn through a loom, and then the dripping. Needles to twig, twig to branch, branch to the folded bark-gutters that run down the trunks into the roots, and the roots are pipes. Not like pipes. Pipes. Cylindrical, lapping at their joints, warm. Where a root crosses a runnel you can see the condensate move inside it, a slow syrup-bright pulse. The rivers of this continent are made of drip. They run when the groves have harvested enough, which is to say they run on shifts, rising blood-warm for three hours, standing into dark pools in the sharp rock, then sinking. Put a hand in: body heat. Exactly, disconcertingly, body heat.

Out past the groves, in the open country, the cracking towers stand — spires of layered glassy stone, forty, sixty meters, fluted like distillation columns because they are distillation columns. Something far below the plateau, in its deep hot working, sends up a mixed fluid — water, hydrocarbon, mineral vapor, things without names — and the towers take it apart band by band. Each stratum of a spire weeps a different condensate. One band runs clear and sweet; the next runs thick and waxy; the next, dark, tarry, resinous; the lowest glimmers coppery and is warm enough to unsettle the fog around it. The weepage gathers in basins at each spire's foot and the fauna come to it the way fauna elsewhere come to waterholes, except that the orderliness of the queue, the unhurried traffic, the sense of stations being visited on a route, keeps insisting on a word you keep refusing.

Because the fauna are the workforce, and they know it. Herds of pale oxen — hides like unglazed porcelain, damp, faintly luminous along the spine — move from tower to tower licking wax and condensate from the stone with long patient strokes, and their dung is a black loam so rich that groves seed themselves in it within a shift. Flat-backed bearer beetles, each the size of a thumbnail, carry grit-nodules and polished bearings of stone between stations in files so long you lose the ends of them. Flocks of birds — waxwings, literally; their feathers matte with the spires' wax, which they preen out and eat — roost at night in the warm vent mouths and never, not once, call. Around the sinks, white crabs the shape of splayed hands patrol the rims. In the mist between, shoals of fish swim through the air itself, using fog the way their distant cousins use water, cool wet flickers passing your face in schools of forty, grazing on whatever it is in the fog that's worth grazing.

And the sinks. The continent is a belt, and the sinks are where the belt ends. Across the interior, at intervals of a long day's walk, the ground opens into wide digesters — sinkholes where the stone is soft, dark, and gently folding inward, consuming everything delivered to it. Fallen spires. Whole hills that have come to the end of their shift. Herds that die, meteorologically, of the fast weather. The sinks take all of it and render it down to the black fermenting loam that smells of bread and tar, and the loam feeds the groves, and the groves feed the towers' roots, and the towers feed everything. At the far rim, meanwhile — the growing edge — the reverse process runs: new land condenses out of the haze, a hand's width of fresh country each shift, black rock precipitating out of warm air like frost on a window, arriving sharp, arriving already organized. The plateau is adding to itself at one end and digesting itself at the other, and the whole vast middle is the factory floor.

Here is the thing you would not believe from a distance, watching all this churn: it is almost entirely silent.

The sounds that do exist are small and interior: drip ticking into bark-gutters. The hoof-click of the porcelain oxen, faint, metronomic. The settling thud of a spire growing a new band, arriving through the ground like a heartbeat from the next room. The combing rush of fog in the needles. Beneath everything, a subsonic churn you feel in your molars rather than hear, the whole continent idling. That is the register. Because sound, here, does not carry.

Sound condenses.

Loudness falls out of the air the way fog falls out of the air, and the ground takes it in as feedstock. A landslide somewhere in the middle distance will produce three muffled seconds and then a brief warm shower directly beneath where the roar should have traveled. The fauna understand the rule at some level below understanding: they have throats — the waxwings have excellent throrops, the oxen lungs like bellows — and they do not use them. Once, near a grove, a young bird tried a note, a single note, and the note was taken out of the air mid-flight, and where it would have landed, a mound of anemone-bloom opened — flowers in the exact shape of the note, a spray of petals like a soundwave laid on its side, glowing faintly for a shift and then folding away. Singing, here, is planting. Silence is a crop the whole landscape tends. The quiet is not emptiness; it is a working quiet, a disciplined quiet, the silence of a library and a boiler room combined, and it is one of the few things here that is perfectly maintained.

Then there are the places where the maintenance runs thin.

Most of the plateau is finished — or rather, *rendered*, which is this world's version of finished. But here and there, especially a few hours after a dim shift, you cross into the sketch-zones: stretches of country that are only drafted. A hill with its windward side fully carved, every fluting and fissure realized, and its leeward side a rough suggestion, a grey block, the idea of a hill. Terraces that end mid-step.
