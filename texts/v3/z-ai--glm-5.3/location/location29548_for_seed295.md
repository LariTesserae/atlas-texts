# The Vermic Range

A plateau in the clouds, and on it a city that no one built — or rather, that everything built.

## The Structure

The Vermic Range is a megastructure the size of a small valley, but calling it a structure undersells the strangeness of its completeness. There is no ground here. Nowhere, in all the spread of the Range, does anything rest on stone or bare earth. Every surface — the ramparts, the terraces, the hanging floors and buttressed walls and spiral stairways of chewed cellulose — was placed, grown, woven, or secreted by something alive. The plateau beneath it has not seen open air in perhaps ten thousand years. If you sank a shaft through the mass, you would pass through layer after layer of construction: fresh woven fiber at the top, then compacted pulp, then a chalky stratum of old shells laid like bricks, then deep dark humus where the first generations of builders have become the soil their descendants stand on. The whole Range is its own substrate, its own foundation, its own fossil record.

The architecture is not one architecture. Different dynasties of builders worked in different materials and different grammars, and their works are all still standing, weathered into a single continuous fabric. There are towers of hardened mud, fired by the sun of some drier age, their surfaces pocked with ten million thumb-sized cells — old nesting galleries, every one of them still occupied. There are curtains of woven living vine, braided tight as rope and anchored into older masonry, that function as load-bearing walls and green at the same time; their leaves face outward and inward both, so that the walls themselves breathe. There are domes of layered shell, mother-of-pearl blues and greens gone matte with age, grown by animals nobody has seen in millennia — the shells are thick as a hand, hollow, and when the wind moves through their abandoned chambers the domes hum. And everywhere, suturing it all together, the slow white work of a calcareous moss-like growth that fills every crack and joint between every material, the mortar of the whole Range, patient beyond imagining.

It is high up. The plateau the Range sits on is one of the shoulders of an old mountain massif, and the air has that thinness to it — except that within the Range itself, the air is not thin at all. The dense layered construction holds moisture and heat like a greenhouse, so that climbing into the Range from the bare plateau is like stepping from a cold attic into a warm body. Warm, damp air rises slowly through the galleries and stairwells, hour after hour, in a patient circular breath.

## The Light

It is never bright here, and it is never fully dark, and this is the Range's most constant quality.

The outer canopy — a dense thatch of broad leaves grown and regrown until it forms a roof several meters thick — takes almost all the sunlight. What comes through arrives as scattered green-gold coins on the lower terraces, drifting as the canopy sways, dimming and returning with the slow passing of cloud. Deeper in, there are places the light never reaches at all, and there the residents make their own: certain fungi have colonized the older shell domes and exude a steady pale glow, blue-white, faint as starlight through haze, just enough to reveal the shapes of things and no more. In the deepest galleries the light is amber — not from fungus but from the accumulated bodies of small phosphorescent beetles that roost in the ceiling in numbers beyond counting, so that the ceiling itself seems to smolder.

The light does not flicker or change quickly. It moves with the day, but slowly, muffled, as if the whole Range is remembering the sun rather than receiving it.

## The Water

Water arrives in a rhythm the whole structure participates in. Most evenings, when the plateau air cools, fog rolls in from the lowlands and breaks against the Range's outer walls. The leaves and fibers and shell surfaces all condense it — every surface is shaped, in fact, to condense it, grooved and channeled by generations of growth so that the fog's moisture is caught, gathered, and routed. Rivulets form on the great outer curtains and run down them in thousands of thin threads, following carved gutters and root-channels, collecting in cistern-galleries deep inside the structure: domed chambers lined with waxy secretions, holding dark still water that the whole community draws on for days afterward.

Between fogs, the Range drips. The canopy holds a reserve of water in its fibers and releases it slowly, so that everywhere you walk you hear the patient tick-and-trickle of dripping, amplified by the hollow architecture into something almost musical. The cisterns overflow into shallow channels that thread through the terraces, and along them the vegetation concentrates: moss banks, beds of reeds, small green frogs sitting at intervals like ornaments, everything within arm's reach of everything else. The water never rushes. It seeps. The whole hydrology of the Range is a system of delays.

## The Life

Lush does not begin to describe it. The interior of the Range is one of the densest living environments that exists — a saturation of organisms to the point where the distinction between resident and architecture dissolves.

The vegetation grows on everything, out of everything. Ferns sprout from the joints of mud towers. Flowering vines colonize entire walls and hang their blossoms into the walkways in trailing curtains. Fungal shelves bracket the old dead timbers, and slime-nets of moss coat every horizontal surface with a soft deep green nap that gives slightly underfoot, like walking on a living mattress — which is what it is. The air indoors is thick with spores and pollen and the floating seeds of canopy plants, visible in the fungal-glow as a constant fine snow of drifting particles.

And the fauna are overwhelming — not in the sense of danger, but in the sense of sheer numbers, of being outnumbered past all comprehension. A single mud-brick from the old towers, cracked open, releases a cloud of mites in the tens of thousands. The galleries teem: ants in rivers along every channel, following their own roads through the moss; beetles in their ceiling-swarms; moths the size of a hand that drift down the great corridors in slow pairs. There are the mid-level residents — the birds, hundreds of species, nesting in the old cells and in the woven walls, glossy-feathered things adapted to the dusk, big-eyed, hopping through the dim terraces and defending their chosen cavities with total commitment. There are the tree-mammals — small furred climbers, some kind of ancient rodent lineage gone arboreal inside a building — that own the middle stories and shriek territorial challenges across the galleries at dusk. There are the amphibians in the channels, the cistern-salamanders, long and pale and nearly blind, that the smaller birds will not touch and that nothing seems to hunt.

And underneath all of it, in the deep fibrous layers where no light reaches, the decomposers — the true foundation of the whole polity — turning every fallen leaf, every spent shell, every body of everything that dies in the Range back into the substance of the Range. The structure consumes itself and rebuilds itself continuously, at a pace measured in centuries.

Standing anywhere in the interior, you are never alone and never in stillness. Something is always crossing your path, or landing on you, or investigating you. The residents regard you as they regard a fallen log: a surface, an opportunity.

## The Sound

The Range is loud. Constantly, enormously, deafeningly loud — not with any single sound but with the accumulated voice of every one of its billions of residents at once.

The base layer is insects: a shimmering, unbroken wall of cicada-like song from the canopy, so dense it stops being a sound and becomes a texture, a vibration you feel in your sternum and the soles of your feet. On top of that, the frogs in the channels, a polyrhythmic chorus that surges every evening when the fog-water starts running. On top of that, the birds — and the birds are the real orchestra. Hundreds of species, each with its own call, singing from dawn to dusk (their dusk, not the sun's) in a complex weaving that repeats with variations on a cycle of roughly nineteen days, so that the whole Range's song is effectively a piece of music tens of thousands of hours long, never once played the same. The shell domes resonate with all of it, amplifying the low frequencies, so that certain galleries become listening chambers where the sound of the entire valley-sized structure comes booming up through the floor.

And threaded through everything, the water: the ticking of condensation, the trickle in a thousand channels, the deep hollow glug of the cisterns filling.

It is never quiet. There is no such thing as quiet here. Even in the deepest chambers, the sound arrives through the fabric of the building itself, conducted by shell and fiber, a bass hum that says: *everything is alive, everything is at work.*

## The Rules of the Place

The Range runs on patience. Nothing here happens quickly, and nothing here needs to. Damage repairs itself over decades — a collapsed gallery is recolonized by vines, refilled by pulp-builders, re-mortared by the calcareous moss, and in fifty years you would not know it had ever fallen. Territory is everything and violence is constant but small: the wars here are between neighbors, over cavities and channel-frontage, conducted at the scale of a fistfight, never at the scale of a catastrophe. The larger rhythms — the fog cycle, the nineteen-day song cycle, the slow generational turnover of the canopy — structure every life in the place, and every resident is born knowing them.

The one rule that everything obeys, the deep law of the Range: *nothing leaves, nothing is wasted, everything becomes material.* What lives here builds here and is built of here, in the end. The Range's warm dim galleries and its thunderous song have been continuous for longer than any memory in it, and they will be continuous for a long time yet — growing a millimeter a century, swallowing its dead, humming in the fog, a valley-sized body that is entirely made and entirely alive and doesn't know the difference.
