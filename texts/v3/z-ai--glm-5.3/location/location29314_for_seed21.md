You come up onto it through a pass of black, glassy rock and find, on the far side of the shoulder, a basin hanging in the high air like a held cup of water.

It is small. You could walk its floor in a quarter hour, rim to rim, and yet it doesn't feel small, because the walls rise sheer on three sides — fresh scarps, dark as struck flint, too sharp-edged to have stood through more than a handful of years — and the fourth side opens onto a lip where the whole basin exhales its water downward, in threads and veils, into a country hidden entirely by mist. The shape of the place is the shape of a nave: walls, pillars, an altar of water at the center, a floor of pale mineral terraces. It is not a ruin of a cathedral. It is simply, by accident or intention, the same geometry.

The first thing your body notices is the heat. You are high — the sky is thin, the light has teeth, your breath comes slightly short — and the air at this altitude ought to be cold. It isn't. The stone underfoot is blood-warm, warm like a sleeping animal, and the warmth rises all day as a shimmer above the terraces. This shelf of the world is young and still cooling; it rose recently with its heat still in it, and the heat has nowhere to go but up through your boots and into your face. You sweat. And here is the second thing your body notices: the sweat doesn't dry. The whole basin steams faintly, all day, wherever warm stone meets cold thin air, and the mist is born at the rim and on the cliff faces — not from any weather, which stays far above and indifferent, but from the place itself, making its own climate inward and downward.

The cliffs weep. Every scarp face is seamed with vertical threads of water — hundreds of them, hair-thin to finger-thick, each running in a groove it has stained its own color: green-black, iron gold, a few the startling blue of copper salt. The water doesn't sheet down the stone; it runs in discrete lines, evenly spaced, as if the cliff were an instrument someone had strung. Stand still long enough and you find the strangest regularity in the basin: the drips keep shared time. Where threads gather at pool edges and release their drops, they release together — a pulse across the whole place, one beat every few seconds, subdivided by the smaller lines. The basin is a water clock the size of a cathedral, and everything in it runs on the clock.

The floor is stepped terraces, rims of pale sinter built up like porcelain along each pool's edge. The pools are stacked side by side at different depths and temperatures and they do not mix. One is warm as a bath; the one touching it, separated by a lip of stone only a palm's width, is cool as the underside of your hand. When wind ruffles a surface — or when you trail fingers through it, which you will only do once, out of respect after the first time — the water goes dead flat again almost instantly, faster than gravity has any right to allow. The surfaces want to be level. Down in the deepest pool, at the center, the water is so clear it seems to exaggerate its own depth, and you can make out, far below, a framework — a lattice, a sunken geometry of mineral branches, being grown or being waited on. You will not see the bottom of that pool. No one has.

On the shaded stone, where condensation gathers, the moisture doesn't bead randomly. It forms lattices — hexagonal ferns of water, ordered like glyphs, growing in the heat when frost-logic says they should not exist at all. You can almost read them. That almost is the closest anyone has come.

The vegetation is lush and very spare, and the sparseness is the point. Each plant stands alone, with room around it, the way objects are placed in a garden rather than scattered by a seedbed. The pillars of the nave are plants: tall green columns, two to four meters, each rising from its own little bowl of still water — its own stoup — with leaves of dark wet leather spiraling up the stem like a slow fountain in reverse. Their crowns tilt through the day, tracking the sun, shading their own stoups at noon; at dusk they open slightly, as if exhaling. They grow only where a spring rises, and the springs are evenly spaced, so the plants stand at intervals like columns, twenty meters apart, thirty, forty — each one an island of intense aliveness in the general emptiness. Between them: mineral crust, quiet air, and the green-gold moss that runs only along the weep-lines, in ribbons, like script.

In the threads on the cliffs you can watch the basin's work happening. Seeds travel inside the waterlines — bright, elongated, carried down the vertical grooves at a pace you can follow with your eye. The plants fruit at the top of the scarps, where their roots drink from the emerging water; the threads deliver the seeds to the terraces; and at the outfall lip, the veils of falling water carry more seeds over the edge, down into the mist. Below that mist — you can tell, though you can never see it — there are already terraces going green. The basin is printing a watershed, one seed every few heartbeats, into a country it cannot see. It is a font in both old senses: source of water, and source of copies.

The built traces are few, and quiet, and easy to miss. You will walk the pale slab path from the rim threshold to the center pool before you realize it is a path — fitted stones, mostly swallowed by sinter and moss. At the head of each major weep-line, set into the cliff, is a stud of dark machined alloy, spiraled inside like a drill's ambition, and here is the detail that undoes easy explanations: the fittings are older than the rock around them. The stone grew after the metal. At the pool's edge stands a small frame, knee-high, of the same alloy, and the rising column of water inside it parts around the metal without ever touching it, thread separating and rejoining in a vertical braid. A dozen shallow cups are ground into the rim slabs; by nightfall every one has filled itself with condensate, each exactly full, never overfull. And at the outfall lip, seven steps descend into the white and stop, mid-mist. Whatever used them is either gone or still arriving.

Sound barely exists here. The loudest thing is the hush of the veil-falls, which is less a sound than a texture at the edge of hearing, and the individual drips, spaced by long silences. The scarps do not echo; a shout falls over the lip and does not come back. What the place has instead, deep in your sternum, is a hum — felt, never heard — the resonant note of all that working water, one tone the basin holds the way a bell holds its struck sound. And once each evening, at the moment the light goes amber, the central pool produces an audible note: low, soft, like a wet glass rim touched by a finger, four seconds long, always the same pitch. Then the mist rises off the terraces like a tide coming in from below.

The fauna is rare, and faithful to the rules. In the films of moisture on the stone, droplets the size of peppercorns move with evident intent — the water's smallest citizens, repairing films, ferrying seeds to the threads. Once, at dusk, a gray bird will land beside one of the cups without any sound at all — knee-high, heron-angled, thin as a held thought — drink exactly one cup, and be gone before the mist takes the floor. And once, from far below the outfall, you will hear two thin cries, like something greeting something else in the new green terraces, and then nothing more.

The rules of the place, as far as they can be known: the water never rises above its marks. The drips keep common time. The stone stays young — edges do not dull, cracks do not widen; the basin is held, deliberately, at the very moment of its formation, fresh forever by something that considers erosion a draft not ready for revision. Seeds travel in water and never on wind. The cups fill nightly. The note sounds at dusk. The heat comes from below and the cold from above, and every surface wears condensation by evening, including you.

Stay past nightfall once. The stars come down hard and close through the thin air, the pool's lattice below glimmers with the day's stored warmth, and the mist rises until the whole nave is a held breath — pillars dissolving, walls dissolving, everything gone but the floor you sit on and the clock. The drips continue in their shared tempo, one beat every few seconds, in the white everywhere around you. And somewhere below, in the dark country you cannot see, a seed arrives in a new world, right on time.
