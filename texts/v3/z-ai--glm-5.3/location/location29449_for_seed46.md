# The Green Gallery

The mountain is new — newer than its own shadow — and near its crown, behind a loose flake of slate that hangs like a door nobody has hinged yet, there is a fissure the width of one shoulder. That is all the entrance there is. No path leads here, no clearing announces it, no feature says *look*. The ranges below are finished, settled, assigned their shadows and their names; this mountain is the frontier of the construction, the topmost work-in-progress in a world that is still, in places, being made. The pocket behind the slate is where some of that making happens.

If you stood at the fissure, you would be unscheduled. Nothing here has been told about you.

Squeeze through. The heat arrives before the light does.

You expect a cave. It is more like a workshop, or a nave — a hundred paces long, perhaps thirty wide, roofed with low stone that makes the tall things inside seem to stoop. The stone is green, and *green* is the correct word, the way green timber is green: not the color so much as the condition. Uncured. Set stone is decided stone, patient, finished with deciding. Green stone is still thinking. Every surface in the pocket — floor, walls, the underside of the roof — is still thinking, still a degree or two soft of its final fact, and under your boots it has the faint chalky give of something that has not yet committed to being basalt, and may yet become something else if the margins say so.

The margins do say so. Here and there the strata run sideways in fine dark lines, and the lines are not bedding planes but script — annotations written in the stone itself, the way ore grades are stamped into ore: *WIDEN. TRY WARMER. HOLD.* No hand cut them. They are not inscriptions so much as self-notes, the geology keeping its own minutes.

The light is warm and even and workmanlike, an afternoon that has been going on without interruption since the beginning. It comes from no lamp you can find. Overhead there is a long slot in the roof, and through the slot you can see the sky under construction: pale bands of trial blue, pinned up stripe by stripe, a swatch wall of heavens. Down here the light is that same color with its notes removed — plain, steady, the general illumination of a works where whatever is being made is being made slowly enough that no one needs better light.

And the heat. The heat is the medium of the place, the thing water is in other worlds. It is kiln-heat, oven-door heat, utterly dry — there is no dampness anywhere in the pocket, no humidity, no cool side of anything, no word for *wet* in the local dictionary except as a rumor. The rock glows along one long seam like a stove eye. The air above the floor shimmers in slow standing waves that never break. Hold still and you can feel warmth arrive through your boots, through the air, through the roof, from every direction except above, where the slot leaks a thin line of cooler bright — the only coolness, and it is miles away at the top of the young mountain, high thin finished air, the frontier wind.

Now the machines. Nobody built them. That is the first rule of the pocket, the one everything else is footnoted to: *nothing here was built.* There are no bolts, no maker's marks, no seams of assembly — only seams of strata. The machines precipitated, the way crystals precipitate; they extruded themselves out of the green stone over ages, the way ore bodies do, and they are still growing. There is a crankshaft of hematite, forty paces long, lying in a trough of its own making, meshing with gear-teeth that no file ever touched. It turns. One revolution takes a season. The stamp-mill in the eastern bay has seven columns of feldspar that rise and fall once each per day, so slow that the strike is not sound but weather — a shudder you feel in the sternum, the pocket's only clock, and even that clock runs slow. Along the south wall runs the smelter: a seam where the rock purifies itself into iron, and the iron walks — actually walks, on a tilting stratum no steeper than patience — down toward an exit it will reach in forty years, arriving as ingots for a world that has not yet placed its order.

Because that is the product. Not iron. The iron is a byproduct of the tempering. This factory makes geography. In the back bays lie the jigs — great curved armatures, half-clad in cooling stone, on which the next ridge is being laid up like a hull. On a long bench of native coal that arranged itself into rows without being told to, blanks of hill sit curing.
