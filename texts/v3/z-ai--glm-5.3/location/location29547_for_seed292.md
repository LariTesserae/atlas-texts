You hear it before you see any of it: rain landing on water. Not rain on a roof, which is percussion — this is a roar with no edges, a continuous white bellow made of a million soft impacts per second, every second, and it has been going on long enough that the people here no longer call it weather. It is the ground tone of the world. You are standing four thousand meters above the sea, thin enough air that a kettle boils at eighty-seven degrees and the sun burns your face while the wind freezes it, and you are surrounded by sea.

The land beneath is called the Harrow, named for the tool and not the rite. It is the oldest finished thing you will ever stand on. Three hundred million years ago an ocean spent itself gnawing a mountain range down to a plain — planed it the way a carpenter planes a door, patient past all meaning of patience — and left the surface marked with ripple prints from storms older than insects, lithified, still legible on the rock ledges that break above the waterline. Then the whole plain rose, without wrinkling, lifted level like a table carried upstairs, and nothing has been able to rough it up since. It is terrain in its final form. Every valley that could be cut has been cut. Every hill that could dissolve has mostly done so. The plateau has settled into the shape it will hold for as long as there is a planet to hold it, and the proof of that is what's on top now — not a mountain range, not even low hills, but water. The sea came back to look at its finished work and found it could not leave.

The water is recent; that's the strangeness and there is no other. For forty years the rain has exceeded what the land can pass. The Harrow is a dish — a vast near-perfect flat, rimmed on three sides by the old escarpment, and its single outlet is a gorge called the Slot, a knife-cut the ancient rivers spent ten million years deepening, which turns out to be a few meters too narrow for eternity's weather. So the flood rose. Over the hedgerows first, then the lanes, then the fields, then the village of Broadlaw, until it found its level: two fathoms deep over the old land in the south, nine in the drowned river valleys, a shallow inland sea forty kilometers one way and ninety the other, sitting on top of the world like water spilled on a table that no one has wiped. The drowned country is close enough beneath the surface to read in good light. Roads show as pale lines. Field boundaries show as darker seams. When the silt settles for an hour after a storm, you look down through the whole history of a mountain range ground to flour, brewed into tea, spread thin over its own grave.

The shoreline is redrawn weekly. That is one of the rules. Wind sets up a seiche in the shallow basin — the water leans, all ninety kilometers of it, rising a hand-span at one rim and dropping at the other — and the coasts walk. The fleet-folk read the lean the way sailors read glass: water leaning north means a storm came from the south; water leaning north with birds gone quiet means one is coming. There are also waterspouts, three or four on a bad day, wandering the open water like slow pillars of smoke walking on their hands. And there are the reed fleets themselves — thelush thing, the reason this cold place is so green. A quill-reed grows here that mats its roots into raft-islands, a hundred meters across, deep as a ship's draft, and the floods turned the whole aquatic meadow loose. The fleets drift, break apart in the big blows, re-knit in the sun-hours, drift again. They belong to no one. Everyone harvests them. The drowned fields didn't die either; the old pasture grasses went aquatic and now meadow the shallows in waving acres, and the drowned orchards hold their crowns an inch beneath the surface, moss grown thick on every branch, so that a boat passes over what looks like a green ceiling with a sky below it. All of it grows so fast because it is fertilized by the plateau itself — the silt is the last few meters of the old mountains, and the water carries them into every leaf.

The fauna have written their constitution around the storm. Fish run up the drowned streets of Broadlaw when the water clears, spawning in the church aisles and the schoolroom, in the sheltered eddies behind doorways; the tower stands out of the flood to its shoulders, and when heavy weather works the tower, debris knocks the bell, and everyone within two kilometers knows the direction of the wind by the bell. Coots and grebes nest in the fleets in numbers that make the air above them look like a shaken carpet; gulls colonize every roof the Board owns, so that the pump stations shriek like factories of complaint. In the sun-hours — there are usually two a day, arriving without schedule — the frogs chorus from every floating acre, mayflies rise in visible columns, and the fish rise behind them, and for an hour the loudest place you have ever been becomes a marketplace of living things, layered like the water itself: film, mid-depth, bottom, each saturated, each near-touching its neighbors.

The Board is the human presence, and it is scattered by design: eleven stations, causewayed, corrugated-iron and brick, standing above the floodline on the old high ground, connected by a submerged rail and a live electric line strung on pylons that march into the water and out again, humming, cormorants on every crossarm. Each station holds pumps — diesel, thudding, and at Station Nine a great siphon that uses the plateau's own altitude, the one time four thousand meters has ever worked in anyone's favor. The pumps do not try to drain the Mere. The ledger's first line, taught to every keeper, says: the plain will not be drained; the plain will be kept. What they actually fight for is the Slot, where the dredge works the day through, cutting the silt the storms feed it — a labor exactly, precisely sufficient, which is another way of saying it is exactly and precisely endless. It is anthill work, swarm work, and during the calm windows the small boats come out of every station at once and the whole basin briefly moves like a disturbed nest.
