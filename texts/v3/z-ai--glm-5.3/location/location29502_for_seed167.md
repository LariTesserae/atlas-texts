The water is warm. That is the first fact, and everything else follows from it. Warm the way a held hand is warm, the way bathwater left standing is warm — a blood-heat that has nothing to do with any sun, because the sun ended a long way up. The last light dies perhaps three meters below the surface. Below that, the dark is not an absence. It is the medium. It is what the world is made of here, the way air is the medium of a meadow.

This is a drowned valley, the lowest bowl in a hot, flat country. A river town stood in it once — streets, a market square, a mill, a church with a steeple — and then the water came, slowly or all at once, and stayed. It has been under for so long that "flood" is the wrong word now. The town has been submerged longer than it was ever dry. It has finished collapsing. That is an important thing to understand about the place: it is not falling apart anymore. The silt has found its angle of rest in every room. The mortar leached away decades ago and left walls of lace; the bricks have gone soft as stale bread; the roofs, what remains of them, are reefs. The dissolving and the growing-up have reached an agreement. The town is dissolving into the water at exactly the rate at which living things are building it back.

Come down through it. Above you is the surface — or rather the underside of the surface, which is the town's only sky. On a bright day it hangs up there like a wrung-white ceiling, wrinkling when rain hits it. Rain sounds like static on a roof you can never reach. Storms do not arrive here as weather; they arrive as pressure, as a shoulder of cooler water rolling down the old avenues like a slow nightfall.

The streets are still streets. The current runs down Main Street the way wind once did, a steady two-knot flow that comes from the weir upstream and never, in living memory, has stopped. The weir is the town's horizon sound: a far-off boom, like thunder that never resolves, that has become so constant it functions as silence. There is a hush to deep water that has no equivalent on land — not emptiness, but a crowd holding still. What you hear instead, when you listen: the hum of current drawn across window casements. The soft working suck of catfish reading doorframes with their whiskers. The click of shrimp in the next room, like tiny castanets. Once an hour, maybe, the hinge-creak bite of a turtle.

And under all of it, the springs. The cellars of the town still run warm from below — the ground heats itself, the town keeps its own body temperature — so even in the coldest weeks the water never bites. Warm water is thin water, breathless water; it holds almost no air. Everything down here lives hurried and hushed. The fish move in quick flicks and rest long. Afternoon is siesta. Dusk is rush hour.

There is almost no green anywhere. No light for it. The only plants are a brown-gold skin of film on the upper roof tiles, and the black skeleton of a drowned orchard in the shallows upstream, its branches ghosting with silt. The valley's food does not grow here; it falls here. The river delivers it from the whole world upstream — leaf-rot, pollen, the fine gray snow of other places' living — and it settles through the dark like a slow confetti, and everything below is, in one way or another, a scavenger of the surface's leavings.

The market square is a mussel bed now. Tens of thousands of them, half-buried in the old cobbles, inhaling murk, and here is a small miracle you can only find in black water: the clearest water in the entire valley hangs in a halo around each filter-feeder. Approach a mussel shoal and the dark opens like a doorway. The clearest rooms in town are the ones where someone is working.

The animals have inherited more than the walls. They have inherited the habits. Silver shoals move along Main Street in the evenings with the conviction of commuters, and at dusk they gather — actually gather — in the shapes of the doorways, holding position in the frames of shops they will never buy anything in, the way their ancestors must have held to the reef ledges they once lived on. The electric fish know every wall by its hum, reading the town like braille, and they keep to the lanes. The eels run the drains, a second street-system under the first, quicker and steeper. The steeple is the tall thing, the only structure that reaches toward light, and each dawn the shoals rise along it — a vertical commute, from the black rooms of the town up to the bright wrinkled membrane, grazing the half-light, sinking again by evening. The town keeps a timetable no one set.

Glass lies everywhere on the silt, tumbled round into green and brown gems. The iron went to red bloom long ago, and the bloom to red-brown stone. Nothing made by hands still functions here, and nothing needs to. The church bell lies on its side in the nave, crusted with freshwater sponge, and catfish shelter inside its hollow the way they'd shelter in any good hole; it has been housing them for so long that it is difficult to say whether the bell is an artifact or an address.

There is one old crocodilian — a gray, soft-snouted landlord of enormous patience — who patrols the boulevards at dusk. When it passes, on certain warm nights, the water sparks. A faint, cold-blue dust of light ripples off its scales and off the eddies of its passing, and for ten seconds the street it is swimming down exists in outlines: doorframes, lampless lamp-standards, a shoal scattering like flung coins. It is the only light this town produces — light as a consequence of passage, an illumination made entirely of disturbance — and when it fades, everything that paused resumes, slowly, as if ashamed of having been seen.

In the drowned gardens behind the houses, the caddisfly larvae are repairing the town. Each one builds its little case from whatever the ruins provide — a chip of brick, a sliver of bottle glass, a squared fragment of roof slate — and so, millimeter by millimeter, the city is being rebuilt by insects who have no idea it is a city. On the plaza silt, some days, you can find a perfect tiny staircase, three centimeters tall, made entirely of window glass.

The rules are simple, and the water enforces them. Darkness collapses distance: the whole town is one room, and every sound is a neighbor. Ownership lasts only as long as contact; every surface belongs to whoever is touching it, every doorway has a tenant, and every tenant has a successor. Artifacts become mineral, and mineral becomes surface, and surface becomes home. Time is not kept by light but by flow: by the river's pulse, by the spawning runs that climb the old gutters in spring, by the annual flood when the valley inhales — cooler water scouring the streets for a week, silt-weather thickening until the dark goes grainy — and then the long warm stillness of summer, when the valley holds everything it has caught and the shrimp click in the flooded kitchens like small clocks.

Downstream, past the drowned bridge, the basin deepens and the last houses stand in the full black, where the biggest fish hold their water and the springs run warmest. Upstream, the orchard bones rise and the water grades from black to brown to, at last, in the top half-meter, a green-gold that would hurt, if anything here still remembered what light felt like on its skin.

What this place is not, is a ruin. A ruin is a thing abandoned. Nothing here is abandoned. Every room has a tenant, the walls are under constant repair at the scale of millimeters, the streets are swept nightly by whisker and current, and the market has trading hours. It was a human town once. It is an animal town now, and in all its long submersion it has never once — not for an hour, not for a single afternoon — been empty.
