You come up through the cloud deck rather than over it. Below — far below, and visible only through brief tears in the white — lie terraced fields and the thread of a pipeline stitched down the mountain's flank. Above you the fog thickens into something you can almost lean against, warm as breath, and then the trail cuts through a notch in the rock and you are inside the basin.

The range itself is younger than most of its trees. It rose within living memory, geologically speaking — fast enough that the surveyors' brass plugs set at its birth are now measurably higher than their counterparts on the plain, a few patient millimeters a year, a mountain still being born underfoot. The rock shows its youth everywhere: black and grey faces with edges still sharp from the fracturing, none of that rounded geological politeness, quartz glittering along fresh shear planes, no soil worth the name except where the living things have manufactured it. What the rock has instead is heat. The stone is warm — truly warm, warm to the bare palm at night, warm enough that the young mountain exhales faintly — and that warmth is the secret of everything here. The lowlands send up their humid air against the escarpment, and the air breaks like a wave, and the warm young rock and the cold young height wring the water out of it. The whole basin is a comb set in the cloud's hair.

The basin is small — a hanging shelf you could walk across in ten unhurried minutes, walled at the back by a cliff like the side of a nave, opening at the front onto the drop. Cathedral-scale, and it feels it: the tree ferns are the columns, the mist is the incense, and the pipes — the pipes are the organ.

Because there is industry here. Not sprawling, not smoking — a single waterworks, a Cloudery, built into the grove like a row of harps left by giants. Ranks of vertical copper fins, taller than a man, stand in ordered rows among the ferns, thousands of edges beaded with condensation, every bead sliding down to a gutter, every gutter feeding a downcomer, every downcomer murmuring into a pair of vast stone cisterns sunk into the grove floor. An engine shed with a moss-green corrugated roof houses the heart of it: a great slow piston that runs not on coal but on the mountain's own childhood, its working fluid boiled in pipes sunk into the warm rock itself. The piston draws. The piston discharges. Every eleven seconds, a deep ker-CHUNK rolls around the cirque walls and comes back to you changed — rounder, softer, carrying leaf-hiss with it. The basin's heartbeat. The locals below call this interval the Stroke, and they tell time by it.

The vegetation has made the whole place a single near-touching mass. Moss lies deep enough to sleep in; stepping off the gravel path, you sink to the ankle in green velvet that squeezes warm water up around your boot. Filmy ferns, transparent as green glass, coat every stone face. Liverworts hang in ropes from the cliff. Bromeliads and tank-plants sit in the crotches of the tree ferns, each one a small cistern of its own, and here and there on the cliff wall whole gardens hang vertically — orchids, a climbing fern with sulfur-colored spore-dust, moss cascading in green rapids. Nothing has been here long; everything has grown fast and close, because water is unlimited and the competition is for footing. The forest has annexed the machinery without ceremony: moss furs the copper fins until the racks look like the fins of some enormous fossil fish, ferns are seated in the gutter joints, and one tree fern, germinating in a debris-clogged downcomer, has shouldered the pipe a hand's width out of true and now wears it like a collar.

The built marks are scattered and few. The engine shed. A keeper's hut with an iron stove, its chimney dribbling a thread of almost-unnecessary smoke into the wet. A three-walled shelter near the lip of the drop, roofed in shingles gone soft and lichen-gold. And — half-swallowed at the basin's eastern edge — the ruin of the First Racks, an earlier, failed attempt in zinc, whose fins corroded in a decade and whose frame the climbing ferns now use as a trellis. Its gutters still work, still carry their small honest trickle to the cisterns, which tells you something about the place's relationship to its own failures: nothing here is thrown away, merely overgrown. Elsewhere: brass survey plugs, chiseled arrows, drill scars from the cistern quarry, a slate tally board on the shed wall where drips-per-Stroke have been chalked for decades, the numbers drifting by single digits over the years.

It is loud. Loud in a way that takes an hour to stop fighting. Underfoot the engine's throb; overhead and all around, the ticking of condensation, ten thousand droplets a second striking copper, a bright frying static; the rain — a fine warm rain arrives every hour or so, no violence in it, just the cloud closing its hand — hissing on frond and roof and moss; the main pipeline's continuous throat-clearing as it takes the water down the mountain; and above everything, every eleven seconds, the Stroke, rolling and re-rolling in the amphitheater until the whole basin rings with it. You learn quickly to speak at arm's length or not at all. The keeper, who has been here alone for years, mostly doesn't, and has gone quiet in himself, watchful as a heron.

The fauna is sparse to the point of ceremony. Tank frogs, small and metallic-voiced, live out their entire existences in the bromeliad cisterns — a civilization the size of a teacup. Wick moths with wings like wet tissue paper drink from the beaded edges of the fins, trembling with each tick of falling water. In the moss, a nation of springtails. That is very nearly the whole census. No birds live at this height and this clamor; the altitude is a country no wings have yet settled. Perhaps once a season a lowland bird comes up through the fog, dazed, and shelters overnight in the shed, and the keeper writes it in the log with the date, the way you would record an eclipse.

The light is a warm pearl. The mist glows rather than dims; at midday it thins for an hour and actual shadows appear — the first shadow of a tree fern across a copper rack is an event each day, a dark green hand laid over the shining metal — and then the cloud closes again like water over a dropped coin. At the lip of the drop, gaps come and go: the terraces far below, the pipeline's thread, once in a great while the whole plain with its rivers shining, and then the fog heals over it and you are enclosed again.

And the mild strange thing, the thing the keeper will point at if you stand still long enough: everything here keeps the Stroke's time. When the piston draws, the ten thousand drips along the fins seem to hang, infinitesimally, and fall together on the discharge. The tank frogs call in the gap and never over the beat. The ferns shiver at the moment of discharge on days when there is no wind at all. The keeper will tell you, quietly, that if you cut a tree fern here and read its trunk, the growth rings come in bands of eleven-second rhythm, the forest growing to the tempo of the machine the way a shore creature grows to the tide — and he will offer you a cup of the cistern water, which tastes faintly and unmistakably of hot young metal, like drinking the mountain's childhood.

That is the law of the place, if a place this damp and loud can be said to have law: water is the only fast thing here, and even it moves down and around with a worker's patience — cloud to fin to gutter to cistern to pipe to terrace, and back up as warm fog on the following afternoon. Everything else — the rock still rising, the moss closing over the zinc, the tally numbers crawling, the fern fronds unrolling a season at a time — moves at the speed of geology's apprentices. The loudness is not violence; it is the sound of labor, patient as a mill. Nothing in the basin is finished: not the mountain, not the forest, not the works, and the three infants have grown into one another so thoroughly that no one could now say where the garden ends and the machine begins.

The last entry on the slate board, in a hand gone steadier with the years, says it all: *Still dripping. Still rising.*
