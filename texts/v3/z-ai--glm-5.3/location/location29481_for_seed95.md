# The Kept Plain

Stand anywhere on it and the first thing you're given is the horizon — a clean line drawn all the way around, so far off it fades to guesswork. The second thing is warmth: this high, the air is thin and the sun works close, and by afternoon the flat stones hold heat like bread holds an oven. What there is of wind comes up warm and smells of chalk and dust and something faintly green underneath, like the memory of a leaf.

The mountains that once stood here are finished being mountains. Erosion has been at this country so long it has nothing left to prove; it has worn the range down to its remainder, a level so true that rainwater hesitates on it, unsure which way is down. A few inselbergs remain — gray, whale-backed humps of the last resistant rock, anchored out at the distances of a hard day's walk, standing in the haze like a fleet that sailed here and stopped caring about sailing.

The first impression is emptiness. The emptiness is a rumor.

Look down. The ground is pale gravel, and the pale gravel is shell. This was a seabed once, lifted whole into the sky and left to dry; the sea closed like a book and the book has been open to the same page for an unthinkably long time. Pick up any stone and you'll find a fossil whole in it, unworn, as perfect as the day it stopped — or you'll find that the stone *is* fossils, pressed together so long they've become a white rock. Beneath the stones, the soil is a census. A double handful holds shell fragments, insect egg cases, a vacated pupa, one live beetle, three snail eggs, the detached wing of a moth. Nothing here is barren; it is merely quiet about how occupied it is. Every cubic meter of this plain is claimed several times over — once by the dead sea, once by the lichens, once by whatever is living in it this season. The place looks sparse the way a library looks sparse from the highway.

At night the plain runs its one great machine. The stones, warm at dusk, radiate their heat straight up into a sky too thin to hold it back, and their surfaces fall cold while the air stays mild, and by dawn every flat thing is beaded with water. This is the country's rain, fallen upward. Condensation beads on the gravel, silvering it; it beads on the lichens, waking them; it beads on the flanks of the termite mounds, where it is not wasted.

Because the mounds are what you notice next, once you stop trusting the emptiness. They stand across the whole region, thousands of them, pale as the ground they're built from, from knee-height to taller than a person, spaced like an orchard planted by someone with immense patience. Each one is grooved and fluted, its channels cut to gather the night's dew and run it down into the earth toward the roots the builders tend. Each has chimney mouths that breathe: a faint hiss at dawn as the colony vents the day's first heat, the only evidence that the plain is exhaling. Inside, in the dark, the gray masons grow their fungus gardens on chewed dew-grass, warm and wet where the world above is neither.

They build with the shell-hash itself. Understand what that means: the sea was ground to gravel, and the gravel was pasted back up again, a few grains at a time, by animals who have never seen water deeper than a dewdrop. The mounds are the sea, reassembled. And there is something stranger — stand on an inselberg and read the pattern below. The mounds are not scattered evenly. They crowd and thin in long overlapping bands, the way the spacing looks underfoot in the fossil reefs. Where the old reef was richest, the mounds are thickest. Nothing living here has ever seen that reef. The colony follows the chemistry of the stone, and so the living arrangement echoes the dead one, and the plain carries a shape under its shape, remembered by tenants who don't know they're remembering.

The mounds are the only buildings, and they belong to their builders outright. Around them, the plain's sparse vegetation clusters — flat-topped trees with canopies level as water, tough gray tussock grass — because a mound is an island: it concentrates water, enriches soil, and its steady interior warmth shelters seeds through the cold high nights. Everything that wants to live out here does it in the mounds' lee. The region's architecture is otherwise all internal, all underground, all dark. What look like spires on the horizon are chimneys.

The fauna is the plain's open secret. There are more animals in a square meter of this soil than in a meter of rainforest canopy, but almost none of them are large enough to have a voice. The census is enormous; the volume is near zero. Snails come out with the dew in numbers that reorganize the ground — banded shells, inching along on mica-bright trails, rasping lichen off the stones. Darkling beetles stand on their heads at first light, hindquarters raised, letting the fog condense on their backs and run down to their mouths, and the plain's morning is full of small brown drinkers doing headstands as far as you can see. Moths take the night shift; after dark the air acquires a second population, thick with papery wings, and a lanternless walk passes through them like walking through warm snow. Lizards own the middays, stationed on the mound flanks where the temperature is always fair. Above it all, one or two raptors turn on the thermals, nearly silent, and at dusk a swift will scream exactly once, as if filing the day under a heading.

Sound is rationed. The wind. The dry click of grass seed against stone. The tick of beetle feet. A moth striking a leaf and folding. At dawn, dew gathers on the lips of the mounds until the drops are heavy enough to fall, and in the plain's silence you can hear them land — individual, countable, a slow pulse like the driest rain in the world. On the inselbergs, wind-eroded flutes in the rock occasionally catch a breeze and sound a low hollow note, one tone held for a minute and then gone for a week. Your own footsteps are the loudest thing you bring. Move, and the animals read you as an event — everything that can, goes still or vanishes. Stand still for an hour instead, and the plain stops pretending. The snails resume. The beetle line re-forms. The soil resumes its faint grain-by-grain sound of being worked on, and you realize the quiet was never emptiness, only patience.

Water keeps a few larger theatrics in reserve, but it does not spend them often. A fossil river stitches across the region — a channel of pale cobbles and old snail-script — and runs perhaps once in several years, briefly, brown and celebratory, then goes quiet again. Scattered through the plain are shallow pans of cracked white clay, and in their dust lie eggs: shrimp, frogs, things whose whole strategy is to wait. When real rain finally comes, a pan hatches an entire childhood's worth of creatures in one wet week — a single night of frog sound so astonishing after all that silence that it wakes everything with ears — and then the pan dries and the next generation goes back into the dust, seeded, patient, prepared to wait decades for the next rain. And once in a long while, on a schedule no observer has ever found, the great colony does the thing the old routes call the Turning: every mound on the plain vents at once, and the winged ones rise together, so many that the air looks smoked, and for one afternoon the plain makes a sound like a distant sea. Then it stops. Then it is quiet for another lifetime.

The rules settle out of the land if you stay long enough.

All water arrives as breath; dew is the salary, rain is the festival. Nothing hurries and nothing is rewarded for hurrying; the only sudden things here are death and rain.
