Before it is a place, it is a sound. You hear the Orphan Works for a hundred kilometers before you could ever be said to have arrived: a low continuous hum, pitched too deep for the ear, felt instead in the sternum and the fillings of the teeth, rolling out of the darkness ahead like the drone of a sleeping animal the size of a continent. Which is more or less what it is.

The Works occupy a plateau three thousand kilometers across, high enough that the air is thin and the horizon, if there were light to see it by, would curve noticeably downward at the edges. There is no light. The sky is a lid of black cloud a few hundred meters up, seamless, permanent, and everything below it lives in a darkness so total that eyes here are vestigial — pinprinked, lashless, grown over in the larger animals with the same waxy skin as the rest of the face. The world is warm anyway, greenhouse-warm, compost-warm, blood-warm, because heat arrives in this darkness by other means: on a great slow wind that blows in from beyond the horizon, where — it is said, though nothing here can verify it — a sun burns over some distant day-side of the world and ships its warmth across the curve of the planet like freight. The animals do not call the wind anything. But the warmth rides it, wet and heavy, and when it meets the cold thin air of the plateau, it lets go of its water.

That is the first thing you learn about the Works, standing in the dark with your sleeves going damp: it does not rain here. It rains sideways, and inside-out, and upward. Moisture condenses out of the fog onto every surface — onto rock, onto steel, onto leaves and cables and the backs of grazing animals — and slides down in silence. The rocks weep. The pipes weep. The enormous rusted flanks of the half-buried engines weep long mineral tears that leave white tracks of travertine down their sides, so that the ruins have grown, over the centuries, a kind of geology of themselves: stalactites of lead-white carbonate hanging from tank roofs, terraced shelves of pale stone spreading around old outflows, cliffs of compacted coke and carbon-black dust that erode into hoodoos and mesas indistinguishable, now, from the natural ones. There are beaches of tar. There are rivers that run black with dissolved iron and shine with oil-sheen rainbows that no eye will ever see. Everything drips. You navigate by touch, by smell — iron, sulfur, ozone off the live power lines, mushroom-sweet rot, hot oil — and by the heat of things.

The machines are everywhere and nowhere dense: scattered across the plateau like bones across a floodplain. A refinery sinks into its own rust here. A single chimney stands two hundred meters tall there, furred with black algae, home to colonies of nesting things. Pipelines the width of houses run for a hundred kilometers across the plain, half-buried, hot where the steam still moves through them — and steam still moves through most of them. Valve-towers stand like monoliths. Cables sag between pylon and pylon, marching off into the fog, humming, live, a grid with no customer left on it. The Works were built by someone; the remains of that someone turn up rarely — a boot sealed in travertine, a flaking slogan painted on a tank, letters raised enough to be read by fingertip, a flooded pump-hall whose roof has fallen in around a still-submerged control room. Whoever they were, they left long ago, in no particular hurry or all at once; there is no way to tell in the dark. What is certain is that no one turned anything off.

The engines still run on some deep fuel of the plateau's own crust — carbon, sulfur, hydrogen sulfide seeping up through the rock — and what they exhale is heat. Heat, and steam, and pressure cycles, and a four-second beat. Everything alive keeps time to it.

Understand the ecology of this place and you understand that heat is its sun. Nothing photosynthesizes; nothing needs to. The base of the food web grows where warmth flows: black bacterial mats spreading in the hot outfall channels; oilmoss thriving on weeping machine lubricants; rust-bloom sludge, red-black and gelatinous, wherever condensate runs down corroded steel — whole communities of iron-eaters whose grazing keeps the corrosion from ever quite finishing its work. It is the great settled fact of the Works, the thing that has taken millennia to reach equilibrium: the machines corrode at the same rate the biosphere harvests them, grow clogged at the same rate the fauna clears them, vent steam that falls as dew that carries the dissolved food that feeds the creatures that keep the vents open. The Works are no longer dying. They finished dying a long time ago and have entered a different state — weathered, in balance, in their own condition — a landscape the way a coral reef is a landscape. The boundary between the industrial and the biological turned out to be administrative, not real, and everything here has quietly stopped observing it.

So the plateau is lush, in its lightless way. Funnelleaved plants stand in dense stands wherever warmth rises from the ground, their leaves pale and waxy and enormous, shaped like scoops and gutters, funneling condensed fog down to their roots — a forest that drinks the sky instead of waiting for it. Fungal towers rise from the tar beaches, thick-shelled, faintly warm themselves, generating their own heat as they digest. Along the hot pipeline runs, the oilmoss grows in hedgerows, and in those hedgerows live the drummers: squat, armor-backed grazers that travel in herds of thousands, knocking their heads against the resonant pipe-shells to talk to one another across the dark. A herd in full conversation sounds like continuous rolling applause, and it carries forty kilometers. The herds follow the warm lines the way their ancestors might once have followed rivers — except that they also, in passing, scrape the pipelines clear of overgrowth and keep the corridors open, and the corridors need keeping, and so the grazing continues, and so the steam keeps flowing, and so the warmth keeps falling as dew on the funnelleaves. Nobody arranged this. It is simply what the place has become.

The air is as crowded as the ground. Above every vent and outfall, boiler-swarms rise in slow vertical columns, millions of midge-things whose larvae live nowhere but in near-boiling water — and through the columns dart the clickwings, small fast echolocators whose clicks are so constant that the fog itself seems to have texture, an acoustic texture you can move through like a swimmer. The fog lies in decks, strata of mist stacked one atop another, and each deck holds its own community, near-touching, layered, so saturated with presences that no surface anywhere is unclaimed: every square hand-width of rock, steel, bark, or bone is furred, grazed, occupied, nested in, or being slowly eaten by something smaller.

And there are quiet things. Where the soundscape dips, where a hole opens in the applause and the clicking, the animals freeze — because silence here means one of two things, and both are bad. The first is a predator. The large hunters of the Works have learned to walk in the four-second beat, each footfall landing exactly on the piston-stroke of the great engines, so that their approach is swallowed by the world's own metronome. The prey species know this. They have begun to distrust the beat itself, and so an arms race has evolved around the rhythm of the machinery — hunters hiding inside the clock, prey straining to hear what the clock is concealing — and the entire acoustic ecology of a continent is bent, now, around the stroke of engines nobody remembers building.

Every twenty-six hours the deep pressure cycles peak, and the Works exhale. Valves open across three thousand kilometers in a rolling wave, and hot wind pours out of the earth in roaring white gusts that flatten the funnelleaves, scatter the fog decks, and rattle dew off every surface in a brief continent-wide percussion. This is the Exhale, and it functions as dawn. The boiler-swarms surge. The clickwings feed. The drummers drum their loudest. Out in the flooded lowlands at the plateau's heart, something enormous sounds a single bell-like note, once per Exhale, a note that carries for a hundred kilometers through the fog and has never once been answered or explained. And on the rim of the plateau, where the rivers and the fog-falls pour off the edge into darkness and depth, there stands a lighthouse — stone base, steel tower, its lamp long dead and its paint long gone — whose horn still sounds at every Exhale, automatically, faithfully, for ships that will never come. The migrating herds navigate by it. In a world without light, a lighthouse has become a bell.

The rules of this place, then, as well as they can be stated:

Heat is the sun. All direction is toward warmth; every growing thing orients to the temperature gradient the way leaves once turned to a star. Water is blood, food, and slow acid all at once — it carries the world's meals dissolved in it, and it eats the world at its own patient pace. Sound is sight: loudness is shelter, silence is stalking, and the four-second beat is the shared clock that everyone keeps time to and no one trusts. Every surface belongs to someone. The machines are tended by their dependents, and the dependents are kept by their machines, and the difference between the preserve and the patient has dissolved. Nothing sees.
