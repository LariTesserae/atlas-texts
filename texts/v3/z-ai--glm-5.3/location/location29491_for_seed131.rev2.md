You hear it before anything else, and for a long time it is the only thing you hear. The Engine's note comes up out of the ground a full day's walk before the machine itself appears — a hum your soles report to you before your ears do — and the low country goes gradually quiet in every other register, as though the land were lowering its voice out of respect. The marsh closes in around you: warm water, reed-beds, duckweed frying in green films over the channels. Then the machine rises out of the fen like a levee — not a building, a rampart of riveted plate, dark with algae to the waterline and pale with lichen above, streaked white where its weep-holes pour thin constant threads of warm water back into the reeds. Its summit is rounded and low, a shape that has finished rising. It is the oldest thing in the landscape and the loudest, and it is not a ruin. Nothing about it has stopped.

The door is on the marsh side, at the height of your heart. It is the size of a coffin lid, painted a red that has weathered from blood to brick, and it opens onto a stone throat descending behind a curtain of falling condensate. You part the warm rain with your shoulder and go in, and the rain closes behind you, and for a while that is the whole of your experience: the roar.

It is not loud the way events are loud. It is loud the way the sea is wide. The Engine's note arrives through the floor and down out of the vault at once, a full-throated organ tone with a slow percussion under it — the great wheel turning somewhere in the machine's body, one ponderous pass every nine seconds, each pass reaching your sternum before your ears, so that you begin to breathe in bars. The hanging chains shiver, thousands of links chattering against their neighbors like metal insects. The glass ticks. Every still surface in the room carries a fine stipple, an interference texture, rain that refuses to fall. You open your mouth and feel your voice leave as vibration in your jaw and arrive nowhere. The room eats echoes; there is no room in the sound for one more sound. This gallery ended speech a long time ago. Its people, when it had any, signed.

Give it an hour. The roar stops being a sound and becomes a condition, like warmth, like altitude. The ear does not defeat it; the ear is defeated by it, then annexed. This is the only silence the place has ever issued: not the absence of the roar but its permanence.

The room itself is a gallery you could cross in fifty strides — a nave under a glass vault, and a room the size of the word for it: you take all of it in one standing glance, from the coffin-lid door to the far weepers. The panes are thick as paving-stones, warped under their own weight, mineral-skinned, patched generations ago with lead, and the light that comes through them is noon without a sun — diffuse, sourceless, green-gold, shadowless, a light with no weather in it. The floor is chequered iron and pierced cast slabs over the warm reservoir below; the water is never more than an arm's reach beneath your feet. It has been given law to run in. Gutters are incised in the stonework, their lips worn to soft egg-and-dart; chains hang from the vault as downspouts, each beaded with water to its foot, a curtain of counted beads; cast weepers — small pursed mouths of iron — deliver the walls' sweat in threads no thicker than string. Bronze stand-pipes rise at intervals with their lotus capitals gone green as pond. In a glazed cabinet a governor spins its fly-balls at a speed it has held so long the cabinet has grown mist-blind. The gauge-board needles sit steady at mid-scale, like compasses at a pole. There is a barograph whose drum turns once a day; its pen has drawn one flat unbroken line for as long as anyone has looked, and the charts, taken off and filed, are kept in a drawer, all of them identical, because discarding a day once felt like something.

The warmth is the Engine's breath. High in the vault a grille admits a slow plume of steam that has been rising through it for longer than the marsh has had a name, and the vault sweats and rains — softly, constantly, exactly. The rain falls only where it was told to fall: in trays, in gutters, in the moss. The walkways are laid along the dry lanes, so you can stand in a permanent rain and never be wet. The air holds its water so completely that nothing here has ever dried. Every rail wears a skin of beaded water. Tools sweat. You are sweating before you have crossed half the room; the coat on the coat-hook gave up long before the moss took it. Chalk must be kept in a warmed cabinet or it will not write. The room takes attendance of you, too — everything you exhale joins the ceiling and comes down later, somewhere scheduled, as a few grams of your rain. It smells of warm iron, wet stone, crushed green, the sweet black of moss compost, and under everything the ghost of the oil they fed the bearings with in some earlier age. The Dew Room has climate the way other places have weather. It is post-rain and pre-rain at once.

The garden is a map of the plumbing drawn in plants. Wherever the water walks, the green follows in its exact footprints: moss lawns upholstering the drip-trays, ferns ranked along the gutter-edges like a choir processing, liverwort quilting the cooler north wall in gray-green hexagons, a cascade of spike-moss taking the stair the way green water takes a weir. Underfoot, in the sump-baskets, the room has been manufacturing soil for as long as it has been making anything — dust drawn in through the intake, spores, the bodies of ten trillion midges, the shed of ten thousand fern-years, all of it composted by pure damp into a black loam. It is the only soil in the world to be assembled indoors, and nothing in this place rests on ground that was not first made and then decided.

In the largest basket stands the fig. Its roots go down through the grating like a hand closing, its toes drink from the reservoir, and its crown has risen into the vault and props the glass. It grew, as trees do, toward its ration of light and water — and then, having reached the exact size of that ration, it stopped. It is not dying. It is done. Every plant in this gallery has done the same: grown to the size of its allowance and ceased, not with the violence of pruning but with the completeness of a signature. This is a climax forest in a bottle, a garden that finished its argument with itself a very long time ago and is resting in the conclusion.

The animals are few enough to census by hand. Midges breed in the filter-scum and hang in the bright air like a struck shimmer. A pale ribbed gecko patrols eight meters of warm pipe in the same order, twice a day, though there are no days — its route and the clock have nothing to do with each other — and somewhere on the far side of the room its neighbor patrols a mirrored pipe, the two of them like sensible landlords who divided the territory and never met. Orb-weavers have annexed the glazing bars; each web is rebuilt nightly to the same anchors, identical down to the screws, so that the webs are less artifacts than habits the corners have. There is a snail. There has always been a snail, in the way of greenrooms and garden walls — one at a time, the office handed down — grazing the algae film off the glass at roughly the pace of the wheel, its track cleaned and re-clouded, cleaned and re-clouded, the nearest thing the room has to a clock face.

And in the settling basin, the fish. They are the size of your thumbnail, gold as struck coin — the keepers called them coins — and they are a nation entire: the whole species lives in this one room and nowhere else on earth. They were founded by accident, eggs through the intake or in a bird's feathers in a year without a number, and the door was leaded over afterward, and the nation has governed itself sensibly ever since. It numbers about three hundred. It has always numbered about three hundred; the midge supply is the ceiling on its prosperity, and the ceiling holds. No heron has ever hunted them, no larger fish ever joined them. There are no birds in this room and no bats — the roar excludes anything that needs to hear. Life here is life that surrendered its ears and kept its eyes, and the coins school in the one bright shaft beneath the grille, a slow eclipse that never resolves and never has.

Understand what the age of this place is. It is not old like an antique; it is old like a delta. The concrete's edges are rounded like a pebble the size of a room. Calcite has come down the walls in sheets and swallowed a hand-rail whole, so the rail lies inside the stone now like the fossil of a gesture; stalactites have grown along the vault's ribs in the shapes of pipes — the rock has been copying the plumbing so long the copies are load-bearing. Cut the surface anywhere and you read the room's sediment: grey, red, grey, green. Everything that could fall has fallen, and the fallen dust has reached its angle of rest. Nothing here is decaying — decay is a process, and every process in this room has been completed. The wheel still turns, the water still walks, the midges still hatch; every clock in the room still runs; but the clocks cancel. The Dew Room's time does not pass; it circulates. It is a landscape near the end of its own slope, spending exactly what it receives, and it has been at that equilibrium so long that equilibrium is no longer a state the room is in but a material it is made of.

There have been keepers. A desk, a stool, a slate cabinet, the coat-hook with its coat the moss has long since finished with. On the wall, in letters a meter high gone soft under the green like a tattoo grown over with fur, the rules — painted large because whispering was never any use:

*LET THE WATER KEEP ITS PATHS.*
*A keeper once dried a gutter to scrub it; the moss died in a stripe and took a hundred years to forgive the insult.*

*SIGN. DO NOT SPEAK.*
*DATE ALL THINGS IN TURNS OF THE WHEEL.*
*DO NOT GOVERN THE FISH; THEY GOVERN THEMSELVES.*
*DO NOT TOUCH THE GOVERNOR.*

And the logbook, with its handspan of pages. *Steam at normal. Fish as before. Nothing moved.* The same line, in several different hands, across several different centuries — everything there was to say. The turn-count in the ledger has eleven digits; no keeper now living has seen the leading one change. There is a final column, headed OTHER, which no hand has ever needed.

The Engine's errand is water. It lifts the warm fen over the ridge to the dry country beyond — or drains the low fields so the fen won't take them; the ledgers say both, in different centuries, and the Engine, indifferent to phrasing, goes on lifting. It is lower than the river it serves; water wants in from every side; the whole design of the place is a controlled surrender to water's wanting.

Stand in the middle of the room and you will find its secret, which is that its middle is nothing. Ten paces of warm bright loud nothing: fog, the boom of the wheel, the fine rain missing you. All the presences sit at the rim — the green along the gutters, the water at the walls, the gecko on its pipe, the coins in their shaft of light — and the center is held empty, as a machine keeps one chamber of rest inside its body of duty. The room is the Engine's exhale, made into a place. A marsh that has been given architecture. A rain that has been given law.

If the Engine were ever to stop — it will not; it has run so long it has become the reason it runs — the Dew Room would hear, for the first time, its own drips: a ticking that has been going on under the roar the whole while, patient as only forever can be. The gecko would pause. The snail would not. The moss would keep its lawns, and the coins would school on in the light, as before. The water would take its paths, and the rain would fall where it was told — as before, as before.
