**The Rootworks**

You go down a stair that was cut into a root. That is the first fact of the place, and the stair announces it: each tread is a thick cross-section of living wood, dished concave by generations of boots, so that your feet settle into the hollows other feet made and you descend at the pace of the worn wood, not your own. A brick collar holds the mouth of the shaft — courses of old masonry, laid with municipal care — but six steps down the root has swallowed the brick, clasped it, grown over it in slow knuckles. The air comes up warm, like the breath of a brewery: cut grass, hot sugar, a vinegar edge, a thread of ozone.

The stair opens into a single hall, and the hall is the whole world down here. It is nave-sized, one room, bounded absolutely — there is a skirt on every side where the walls close down from soft felt to solid root, and nothing beyond it that anyone visits. The chamber is a clearing that the great tree, the Sugarheart, hollowed around its own taproot, and the taproot descends through the middle distance of the room like a column, like a silo, thicker than three men's arms linked, running from the dense root-matted ceiling into the woven floor. The walls themselves are root-felt, pressed and lined, something between adobe and intestine. The floor is rhizome, coiled and sprung, warm underfoot from its own slow composting.

And the hall is mostly empty. That is the second fact. The machines in it are few — four, five pieces — and they stand far apart, each in its own pool of amber lamplight, with wide unlit floor between them. The emptiness is not neglect. It is kept.

The machinery, when you reach it, refuses to sort itself into living or made. At chest height along the taproot's flank, brass spiles are bored into the sapwood — huge versions of a sugarmaker's taps — running into a manifold of pipe. Some stretches of that pipe are ceramic, some are copper, and some are simply root: hollowed runners the tree grew around the copper, gasketing every joint with its own gum. The sap runs downhill to the Pan, a shallow copper evaporator the size of a dining table, where the water is sweated off. The heat comes from the Heart: a sac, a grown chamber of woody gourd, in which a culture of yeast-like things metabolizes continuously, contracting the sac in slow patient threes. Thump. Thump. Thump. The sac drives a beam; the beam works the pumps; a governor arm, off the beam's wrist, rings the Hour — a brass bell — and flicks a small dynamo, the Coil, whose current feeds the lamps and holds the bell's clapper. The Heart exhales warm, sweet, spent air, and the root drinks that air through a lip-shaped flue grown for the purpose. The engine feeds the tree. The tree feeds the engine. Neither is dead enough to be called machinery, and neither is alive enough, alone, to be called a tree.

The works are old, and the room wears its age openly. There are empty mounts on the floor — stumps of stand-pipe, bolt-channels, gantry rails that go nowhere — the ghosts of machines that were moved, or absorbed. Where pipe was withdrawn, the root has healed over the stub and left a puckered mouth. Along the taproot's flank, high and low, run rings of old tap-scars, galleries of healed punctures ranked like the stops of a flute. The works have not stood still: they migrate, a slow spiral around the pillar, generation by generation, always leaving the root room to swell. The center of the floor is kept bare for the same reason — the taproot is thickening there, year over year, and the emptiness at the heart of the hall is reserved for it. Nothing is stored in the middle. The middle is the future.

The rules are legible to anyone who looks. Tap the outer sapwood only, never the heartwood; the tree has grown seats for the spiles, has accepted the brass, has negotiated. Take only past the mark — there is a carved ring, a wear-notch, and when the flow at the sight-glass drops below it the taps are closed for a watch. Give back: the skim from the Pan, the sweet foam, goes into long troughs, and the troughs are why the hall is never, for one second, quiet or empty. The bell is the sun here. The Hour rings three-watch days, and everything in the chamber — wing, mandible, foot — keeps time to it, because the bell and the troughs and the taps have been one rhythm for so long that the animals below have a day, and their day is made of brass.

Water is intermittent, and it arrives twice, in two different latenesses. The first is condensation: warm root against cooler ceiling, and so the root-matted ceiling weeps along fixed drip-lines, every drop counted by gutters — brass runs and root-runs — that feed stone basins and the Heart's jacket. The second is rain. When a storm crosses the forest above, the water percolates down through twenty meters of ground over six, ten, sometimes thirty hours, and then the ceiling changes: it glosses, it beads, and the hall rains.
