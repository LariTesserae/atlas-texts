# The Terraces of Vhen-Aldat

The town sits high, and the height announces itself first as thinness — air with edges to it, breath that costs a fraction more than it should. But the heat is wrong for altitude. That is the first thing a traveler notices and never quite stops noticing: at this elevation the air should bite, and instead it *steams*. The plateau of Vhen-Aldt is warm the way a stone left in the sun all day is warm, except the sun went down hours ago and the stone is still giving it back, and will give it back all night, because the warmth comes from below.

## The Water That Has Nowhere Left to Go

Water is everywhere here, and nowhere, in the way of a thing that has been lifted to its destination and has no intention of descending. The plateau is the top of the world as far as the water is concerned. Clouds come up the escarpment in the afternoons, haul themselves over the rim, and find the plateau hotter than they are. They don't rain so much as *give up* — dissolving into a bright, warm mist that condenses on every surface with the patience of a process that has been running for ten thousand years.

The rocks weep. This is not poetry; it is municipal infrastructure. The whole town is built below a shelf of dark, vesicular basalt shot through with horizontal seams, and the mist collects in those seams and travels them like slow, mineral blood, emerging at the cliff face as a thousand slow trickles that have been channeled — some by nature, more by carved soapstone gutters older than anyone's grandmother — into cisterns, aqueducts, fountains, bathhouses, and the cooling flumes of the foundries. Every surface in Vhen-Aldt glistens. Walk anywhere in the town and you will hear, under everything, the faint irregular percussion of drips striking stone, stone-lined troughs, stone-lined basins. The town has no rivers. It has a *ceiling* that leaks, and it has organized the leaking.

The streets are cut channels, shallow and stone-bottomed, angled a half-degree so the condensation gathers itself and flows somewhere useful. The locals walk the dry crowns between the channels the way other townspeople walk sidewalks, and visitors inevitably step wrong once, twice, three times, ankle-deep in warm water that tastes of iron and faintly of sulfur, before they learn the rhythm.

## What Grows Here (Nothing)

No plants. This must be said plainly, because the eye keeps looking for them and keeps being refused. There are no trees, no grasses, no moss even — the heat is too steady, the mineral load too heavy, the soil a white crust of precipitated salts that crunches like stale bread underfoot. Whatever forested this plateau once did so in some age with different rules, and it is gone beyond even the memory of the soil.

What there is instead is a whiteness that grows in the *negative* direction — the salts and evaporites building their own slow, crystalline architecture: shelf upon shelf of pale rind on the dark basalt, terraces of travertine and tufa stepped down the escarpment edge like a staircase built for a giant who never came. The terraces are rimmed, curved, pool-basinied, beautifully and meaninglessly ornate, the way natural things often are. Heat and water and mineral, left alone long enough, become sculptors.

And there are the mats. Down in the overflow channels where the water runs hottest and most sulfurous, dark green-black skins spread across the stone like oil slicks with opinions. Thin as paint. Alive, technically, in the way a rumor is technically speech. They eat the sulfur, the iron, the heat, the light; they are the only things in this landscape that photosynthesize or chemosynthesize or whatever it is they do — the scholars in the lower town argue about it in a desultory way, and the mats do not care. The channels where they thickest grow are marked with small soapstone posts, because the mats' presence means the water is good for the leaching-tanks, and the mats are the town's cheapest assay.

## The Town Itself

Vhen-Aldt is not large, but it is *organized* — a low, layered sprawl of stone and brick and rust-brown iron across perhaps a kilometer of plateau, densest at the cliff edge where the water emerges, thinning to scattered out-buildings and slag heaps toward the interior. It has been built, rebuilt, and built upon for long enough that the ground floors of half the buildings are now the basements of the others. Streets enter buildings at what is obviously a second story, because the first story is two centuries of rubble and renovation beneath you. The town does not grow outward; it grows *upward through accretion*, like the travertine.

It is an industrial town, and the industry is the water and the heat together. The plateau's warmth is not decorative — drill a shaft anywhere here and at forty meters the rock is hot enough to boil the cistern water. So the town is a town of steamworks: engine-houses whose boilers are simply pipes run down into the earth; foundries whose furnaces are fed by geothermal steam raised through iron stacks stained white and yellow with mineral bloom; evaporatories where the dripping cliff-water is cooked down for its salts — the white industrial-grade salts, the blue copper-flavored ones, the rare violet ones that pay for everything else. The great condensing-hall at the cliff edge is the town's cathedral: a hundred meters of iron piping and slate fins, thrumming faintly, endlessly, pulling pure water out of the mist itself, and the condensate runs down the fins in such a continuous sheet that the building sounds — quietly, always — like a slow rain falling on a tin roof that is also a machine.

Nothing here is loud. That is the strangest thing about the industry of Vhen-Aldt, and visitors comment on it within the hour. There is no hammering, no roaring furnace, no shouted orders. The steam is throttled rather than vented; the engines are the low-pressure kind, slow-turning, built for endurance rather than power, and they lope along with a soft, breathy, chuffing rhythm like very large animals dozing. The loudest recurring sound in town is the noon whistle, which is a polite, bassoon-like moan rather than a shriek, and after it fades you can hear — for a moment — every drip in the town at once.

## The Animals

The town is quiet, and the town is *empty of plants*, and yet the town is full of life, because life, given heat and water and minerals and no competition, turns out to need very little encouragement.

The cliffs and terraces belong to the condors — enormous, bald-headed, ash-grey birds that ride the thermals rising off the escarpment in slow, patient spirals all day. There must be a thousand of them. They nest on the travertine shelves in the roar of the warm updraft, never needing to flap, and they have a working relationship with the town: the evaporatories attract insects, the insects attract small things, and the small things that die in the hot channels are collected and paid for by the fat-renderers at the cliff-gate. The condors have learned the schedule. They assemble above the foundry at shift-change like a congregation.

Below the birds, in the bright air between buildings, come the insects — a soft, murmuring haze of them, drawn to the moisture and the sulfur-sweetness, small dark hovering things with wings like smoked glass. They do not bite. They simply *attend*, everywhere, in loose low-density clouds, and the light through them at dawn and dusk — the sun here is ferocious, an enormous white lamp in a washed-pale sky, and the light off the wet stone is blinding enough that the town's people wear waxed leather eye-flaps — the light through them turns golden, and the streets full of warm mist and golden insects and bright wet stone look, briefly, like somewhere else entirely.

And under everything, in the mats and the channels and the leaching-tanks, the small ones: pale, blind, segmented things — a local word for them that translates roughly as *the pale tenants* — that graze the sulfur-mats and are in turn netted by the thousand for the condor-renderers and the fish-ponds, and are themselves food for the fat, slow, warm-water fish that thrive in the condensation ponds. The food web here is short, simple, and utterly dependent on heat and water and rock. It is a whole ecosystem that never received the memo about plants. Everything in it is either blind, or a bird, or a fish, or a person.

## The Light and the Quiet

Because the air is high and the atmosphere thin and the mist above it reflective, the light on the plateau is *enormous* — flat, white, and shadowless at noon, so bright the wet streets mirror the sky and the town seems to float on its own reflection. Shadow only exists early and late, and then it is sharp and long and dark blue, and the whole town arranges its day around avoiding the one and using the other.

The silence is the last thing, and it is not quite silence — it is the sound of a place where every process is either slow or quiet or both: the engine-house chuffing, the drips, the faint hiss of the condensing-hall, the wind, a condor's wing doing nothing at all somewhere above the town. Conversation here is carried on at half-volume, by habit and because there is nothing to shout over. It gives the place its character, in the end: a bright, wet, hot, industrious, empty-looking town perched on the roof of its own warm world, quietly steaming, quietly working, quietly dripping — and if you sit still long enough on any wall-crown at dusk, with the mist coming up the channels and the insects turning gold and the condors settling onto the white terraces like ash, you will find the quiet is not emptiness at all. It is the sound of everything here taking its time.
