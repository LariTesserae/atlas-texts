**The Works**

You hear it an hour before you find it.

The approach is emptiness on emptiness: flat bog gone white and stiff, wind coming off nothing from nowhere, one old line of fox prints filled with rime and going nowhere either. For miles there is not a standing thing — no fence, no wire, no roof, no smoke — and into this silence comes a sound that has no business existing: the deep-room murmur of machinery. Steam valves easing open. A slow piston knock. The wet, patient gulp of a boiler being fed. It sits at the edge of hearing, rhythmic, tireless, the sound of work being done somewhere ahead. There is no work for a hundred miles in any direction. The sound is coming from under the ground.

What you see first is weather that isn't weather: a smudge of fog sitting low in a fold of the land, stubborn while the sky above it moves. Only when you're nearly on it does the fold become a rim — a broken rampart of black stone so fresh its fracture faces still shine, glass-edged, unweathered, rock that was under ice within living memory and has been bare for perhaps three winters. The ground here is younger than the animals that cross it. Nothing has had time to soften. The stones have never even been rained on enough to dull.

You climb the rim through hoarfrost, and then you descend out of winter. Frost gives way to dew. Dew gives way to steam. Ten steps down, the air touches your face and it is warm — thick, mineral-wet, smelling of struck matches and hot iron, with a faint sweetness underneath like rain falling on a warm engine block. The air at the rim is minus twelve; at the bottom it is plus six. You pass through several climates in eighty steps.
