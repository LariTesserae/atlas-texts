You come up out of the grey sea on a rack-and-pinion stair, through a skin of cold wet, and break into light so bright it has a texture. The sea below is cloud — the keepers call it a sea anyway, because it has tides and moods and because things live on it — and standing out of it, ten days' walk around and a week's walk down, is the Headworks. A mountain that was never once a mountain. Everything above the cloudline is made.

The horizon is a balustrade. There is no skyline that was not drawn first. What looks like summit rock is poured conglomerate, toothed for grip, dished so that each morning's dew runs into a groove and the grooves run into the cisterns. What look like crags are the stumps of ancient crane-bases, matured into their weather. Dig anywhere — and the keepers have, everywhere a spade can fit — and you do not find soil, you find story: plate over void over vault over deeper plate, strata of intent going down past the reach of their longest tools. There is no ground here. There are only roofs. A person walking the Head is walking on the ceilings of other people's work.

Even the soil is a recipe: crushed brick, rotted orchard waste, silt scraped from cistern bottoms once a generation, laid to a specification nobody alive has read but everyone keeps. Even the boulders are cast — textured on purpose so lichen will take to them — some of them the stumps of machinery, some of them simply seats. The pale terraces are grit-iron plates whitewashed in lime, and the whitewash is not decoration but instrument: lime darkens where damp lingers, so the whole white country doubles as a map of its own wetness, read every morning like a tide table.

And the Head is damp the way a sleeping animal is damp — from condensation, from schedule, not from weather. High dry weeks, there is only dew, and dew is a whole economy. The great cold risers sweat at dawn; keepers come out in the blue light with long squeegees and do the wiping, drawing the night's catch down into the channels. It is the quietest shift of the day and the most competitive. Then for a month each year the grey sea rises — the keepers call it the Dripping — and the gardens stand in fog and every railing plays a thread of water and the cisterns fill with a sound like applause held under a blanket. Rain itself is rare and ceremonial: when a storm actually climbs the rim, the whole upper country becomes waterfall, storm-bells go off in the flue forest, and children are sent out with every basin in the house, because rain belongs to whoever's surface catches it.

That is the first law anyone teaches you: rain belongs to the surface. So every surface is tended. Ownership here runs vertical — you may own a roof, a groove, a pipe's elbow, and you tend it like a small farm.
