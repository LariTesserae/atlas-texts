# The Sounding Plateau

It begins before you arrive. Hours before, the horizon starts to hum — though "hum" is too gentle a word for what it becomes. By the time you crest the final escarpment and see the plateau spread out beneath you at three and a half kilometers' elevation, the sound is no longer something you hear. It is something the air *is*.

## The Field

The plateau is a forest of stone, and the stone is packed so tightly there is barely room to walk between the columns. Thousands upon thousands of pillars — some waist-high, some towering past thirty meters — crowd the surface like a crowd frozen mid-gather, shoulder to shoulder, no gaps, no clearings, no paths. They are not spires in the desert sense, smooth and solitary. These are *dense*. Their flanks nearly touch. To move through the field you turn sideways and slide, and the rock is cold and damp against your shoulder blades on one side, then the other.

The columns are made of a pale, banded stone — ash-grey and cream in alternating layers, like the growth rings of some mineral tree. Water has cut them, and water still owns them. Every pillar weeps. Thin films of moisture crawl down their faces in unbroken sheets, gathering into threads at the corners, dropping from the lowest overhangs into the crowded dark at the base. The ground between the columns is not soil. It is gravel made entirely of the pillars' own collapsed ancestors — rounded, water-worn chips and slabs that your boots sink into with a sound you cannot hear, because nothing under a shout can be heard here.

## Why It Roars

The deafness has an architecture. The plateau sits at the lip of a great bowl in the range, and the prevailing wind — which has blown from the same quarter for longer than anything alive remembers — pours over the escarpment, drops, and funnels into the stone forest. The columns are hollow in their upper reaches. Erosion opened their crowns ages ago and the rain enlarged the hollows into mouths, throats, chambers. Each column is an organ pipe. Each pipe is tuned by accident of its height and hollow, and the wind plays all of them at once, forever.

The chord is not uniform. Deeper in the field the pillars are taller and the note drops to something felt in the sternum rather than heard in the ear. Near the margins the pipes are short and the sound climbs toward a hiss, a whistle, a shriek of steam-kettle intensity. Between the columns the wind shears and eddies, and these small turbulences make a granular wash of noise on top of the fundamental drone — a hissing static, the sound of ten thousand small waters.

And threaded through all of it: actual water. Somewhere beneath the plateau, at no great depth, an aquifer presses upward. Springs rise in the hollow centers of the tallest columns and spill from their mouths, so that the biggest pipes gurgle and burble while they drone, water and wind sounding together. The runoff threads down the packed lanes, joins, braids, and finds its way in a thousand small cascades over the plateau's western lip — where it falls as a single wide curtain into the bowl, adding a roar of pure liquid to the roar of stone and wind. The falls do not freeze. They do not surge. They fall at the same rate, hour after hour, in the same bright cold light.

## Light and Cold

The light is severe. At this elevation the air is thin and the sky an unbroken, hard enamel blue, so the sun pours down unfiltered and the brightness ricochets off the pale banded stone until the whole field seems lit from below as much as above. The wet pillar-faces gleam. Every film of weeping water is a mirror. Walk the lanes and you pass through corridors of moving light — reflected sun wobbling across the walls in slow water-shapes.

The light is bright but the cold is honest. Cool, not bitter — a few degrees above freezing at midday. The stone drinks warmth out of your hands in seconds. The moisture on the pillars never freezes and never dries; it simply persists, a permanent sheen, the temperature of the whole system balanced exactly between its two possible fates. This is the strangeness of the place, and after a day here it stops feeling strange and starts feeling like a law: nothing here is becoming anything. Everything already is.

## Stillness Inside the Noise

This is the paradox that takes longest to absorb. The plateau is the loudest place you may ever stand, and it is also the most *still*. The chord does not change. Not with the hours, not with weather fronts, not seasonally — the wind is an old, fixed circulation that the mountains themselves sustain, and it does not vary. The springs discharge at their constant rate. The falls pour at their constant rate. The erosion that shaped the field happened so long ago that it has effectively finished: the columns have settled into their spacing, the gravel between them has compacted to its own equilibrium, and the rate of stone falling to ruin is matched exactly by nothing at all — it has simply slowed to imperceptibility. A place that has finished eroding and kept its erosional shape. A noise with no beginning, no end, and no middle.

Stand still in one of the narrow lanes for an hour and you will notice that the water-threads beside you have not shifted by a millimeter. That a drop forming at an overhang forms at the same size and falls at the same interval, over and over, with the patience of a metronome. That the wobble of reflected light on the wall repeats itself. The sound is so total it functions as silence — a wall of it that nothing new can cross. You stop listening outward. You start hearing the inside of your own head.

## Life

There is almost none. The wet stone supports nothing green — no lichen even, which is the detail that eventually unsettles naturalists, because lichen goes anywhere. Here it does not. Whether the constant sonic battering scours spores away, or the mineral film on the water is wrong in some subtle way, the field stays bare. The columns are stone, wet stone, and nothing but.

Fauna arrives only as rare visitors. Once or twice in a long stay you might see a single bird — a large dark one, a chough or something like a chough — ride the updraft along the western falls, tilt through the spray-curtain, and vanish over the bowl. It does not land. Nothing lands. And in the still hours of dusk, small pale moths come up from somewhere below the plateau — from the warmer air of the bowl, presumably — and drift among the lanes in ones and twos, landing on the weeping pillars with their wings folded, drinking from the film. They are the only creatures that use this place, and they use it barely, briefly, and without altering anything.

## The Rules of It

If there are rules, they are these:

**Nothing changes, and nothing is dead.** The plateau is not a ruin. It is not aftermath. It is a system that reached its equilibrium so long ago that time here has nothing left to do. The water cycles. The wind sounds. The stone stands. All gradients have been spent; all falls have been found. It is a landscape at its own final temperature.

**The sound is the weather.** There are no other conditions here. No calm, no storm, no quiet after rain — only the chord, eternal, in its infinite slight internal variations, like a piece of music that has been playing so long the music and the silence have merged.

**You cannot leave a trace.** The roar erases your footsteps almost before you take them — the gravel resettles, the moisture films re-close over every surface your hand touches, and the field absorbs you, briefly, the way it would absorb a passing shower. Not hostile. Not welcoming. Indifferent at the deepest possible level — the indifference of a thing that finished becoming and no longer notices arrivals.

Sit down at the foot of the tallest column, in the packed lane where the note drops through your chest like a slow heartbeat, and put your palm against the wet banded stone. You will feel it thrum. You will feel the water rise cold between your fingers. You will look up through the crowded crowns of the pillars at that hard bright blue, with the roar pressing in from every side like an ocean, and you will understand that you are inside an instrument that no one built, playing a piece that no one wrote, to an audience of pale moths and one passing bird — and that it will go on playing, unchanged, whether you are here or not.
