**The Pocket**

You reach it at the top of the salt road, where the switchbacks give out and the world flattens into raw white stone. The air is thin enough that you notice each breath as a small labor; the sky above is blue-black at noon, the way deep water is blue. And below the trail, cupped in the newest part of these young mountains, is a bowl a few hundred paces across — a place the herder families call the Pocket. Small enough to walk its floor in a quarter hour. Bright enough that the first thing you do is squint, and the second is pull your collar up against the light itself.

The whole bowl is loud with brightness and nearly silent. That is the first paradox the Pocket sets in front of you. The second is green: a deep, felted green in a place this high and this cold, where nothing by rights should grow.

---

Start with the bones. This range rose recently — a hundred lifetimes ago, or a thousand, which for stone is this morning — and it has not yet been worn smooth by anything. The rock is pale limestone veined with dark green dolerite, and its edges are still knife-sharp; every scree slope shrieks if you cross it, the shards so fresh they haven't learned to sit quietly together. Frost has begun the slow argument with the stone, sorting the ground into stone stripes and low lobes of soil that creep downhill, visible year to year if you know to look. In the shadow of the north wall survives a remnant of the ice that carved the bowl — a dirty little glacieret, barely a city block of ice, leaking a bright thread of meltwater.

And everywhere, in the trail, in the walls, in the slabs the frost heaves up each spring: shells. Ammonite spirals the size of a fist, oyster banks like roof tiles, belemnites like brown fingers. Salt crusts effloresce on the freshly split shale, white blooms that the wind can't quite keep up with. The Pocket was seabed when it was lifted; the sea is still leaving. Every year the frost pushes more of it up through the ground, as if the old floor were insisting on surfacing.

The cold here is a dry cold, sharpened by altitude — frost every night, even high summer — but the basin's center holds a tarn the color of diluted milk, rock-flour blue, no larger than a threshing floor. It freezes each night into a pane of ice and thaws by mid-morning, the panes rafting to shore and ticking against one another.

---

The water arrives as weather, not as rivers. This is the engine of the place, and it runs on a schedule.

Each mid-morning the clouds come up the eastern valleys, pour over the low col in the rim like milk over the lip of a jug, and fill the bowl to its brim. Fog, hours of it, luminous gray, cold on the face. The Pocket is a dish set out to catch sky. Everything green in it is built to harvest fog — the great silver rosettes stand on woolly trunks like heads of soft spears, and each leaf is a felted channel that walks a bead of condensed water, drop by drop, inward and down, delivering rain from a cloudless sky directly to its own roots. You can stand in the fog and hear the whole basin dripping: a fine, dense, ticking rain with no rain falling. The moss country between the rosettes drinks the rest. By dusk the fog pours back over the rim the way it came — the herders say the mountain is learning to breathe, inhale at mid-morning, exhale at dusk — and the damp it leaves behind comes out of the ground all night as a smell of wet stone and, faintly, of salt.

One seam along the south wall is warm. The young uplift still vents a little heat through a crack — not a hot spring, just a seam where snow never lingers and the mud never fully freezes, a green tongue of ferns and fine grass in the freezing bowl. Everything rare and tender in the Pocket lives on or near that seam, including, on the worst nights, the herders' flocks.

---

The vegetation concentrates the way wealth does: in pockets. Rosette colonies stand well spaced, each crown with its own province of bare ground around it, all of them leaning the same direction — crowns tilted against the morning fog-push, so their funnels face the wind that feeds them. Cushion plants lie around them like green saddle blankets, dense enough to sit on. In the lee of the old corral the moss runs knee-deep and springy, audibly wet underfoot, and inside the moss forest — which is an actual forest if you are two millimeters tall — there are snails, transparent-shelled, grazing the fog-fed leaves. Low suns of composite flowers open across the cushions in the afternoon, tracking the sun's face so faithfully that the flower heads run measurably warm, little hearths of yellow in the cold.

And the animals, which are the real abundance of the Pocket: distributed, spaced, each colony its own small nation, but everywhere once you look. Vizcachas sunbathe on the warm stones in rows, like loaves, and their pikas work the moss margins with a single thin whistle-note that carries the whole silence in it. A culpeo fox hunts the fog at dawn, using it as cover, and you may see only the tip of its enormous tail moving through the gray like a brush being drawn. Sunangels — hummingbirds with throats like embers — work the rosette spikes at midday, and their wing-whirr is the loudest sound the basin produces on a still day; at night they hang on their perches like ornaments, cooled to a breath, waiting for the sun to wind them again. Bumblebees shiver themselves warm before takeoff. A caracara patrols on foot, walking the rim like an official. Woolly camelids come up from the lower valleys at dusk to lick the salt scrapes where the sea still pays its last wages. And along the warm seam live the pebble frogs, which do the thing everyone here half-disbelieves: freeze solid each night, glass-hard, and thaw at first light and hop off as if nothing had been decided.

---

The human marks are old, faint, and few, in the way of tools that belong to a place rather than impose on it. A drystone corral, half-tumbled, lobe-shaped, chest-high. An overhang near the tarn littered with obsidian flakes and the split bones of snared rodents. A line of white cairns threading the basin floor at thirty-pace intervals — waist-high, and each capped with a fossil shell, set with the spiral facing the sky.
