The trail runs out a hundred meters below the top. After that the mountain is climbed on hands as much as feet, and the rim, when you reach it, is less a summit than an edge — and past the edge, folded down out of sight of the world, is a bowl.

Three hundred paces would cross it. Four hundred would overcross it. It is a room: a shallow stone basin ringed with low ramparts of frost-shattered slabs, holding stillness the way a cupped hand holds water. The wind runs along the rim like water along the lip of a glass and does not come in. Somewhere on the last pitch of the climb you crossed the ceiling of the living world without ceremony — the last lichen, the last midge, the last of everything that breathes — and the bowl sits above that line. It has sat there a long time.

What strikes you first is the light. The basin rides at the surface of the cloud deck, half in and half out, and so the light has tides. When the deck swallows the rim, everything goes pearl and sourceless — no sun, no shadows, light arriving equally from every direction so that stones seem lit from inside and you cannot tell raised ground from flat. When the deck sags a few meters, the bowl surfaces like a raft: an ocean of white cloud below, a sky overhead so thin and black-blue it looks like a hole in noon, and a sunlight with real savagery in it, doubled off the pale ground until your eyes water. The stones themselves are white and near-white — old granite scoured to clean facets, quartz seams, a feldspar glare, mica scattered through everything like spilled glitter. No dust of decay darkens them, no lichen, no soil. They look scoured, set out, arranged.

And arranged is not far wrong, because the floor is crowded. Stones stand shoulder to shoulder here the way people stand in a marketplace — more stones per glance than the eye wants to accept, layered floor to sky: cloudlight, then the mist at knee height, then the white fur of rime, then the standing stones, then the netted ground, then the ground beneath that. The frost has sorted the floor into polygons, a meter across, ringed with raised borders of pebbles laid like beading, their centers fine pale silt. And the centers are not done. Every year, a few new stones surface in them — lifted on needles of ice grown overnight, set down, lifted again — as if the basin were still unpacking itself from below, still delivering up some buried gravel hoard a fistful at a time. Nobody is coming to collect it.

The water here never properly falls. It arrives sideways, as fog, and it leaves upward, as light. That is the whole weather: the deck breathes against the mountain, droplets bead on every edge and every facet, and at dusk the beading freezes into hoarfrost — every stone in the bowl grows white feathers overnight, and by ten in the morning the sun has burned them off again. Day is a melt. Night is a crystal grown. Between them, the fog sometimes pours over the rim in the strangest way the place has — pushed by winds below, it rises over the edge and falls upward, sliding into the bowl at knee height like slow smoke in reverse, a waterfall with no sound, dissolving as it goes.

The old builders came for the water, maybe. They knew this fog, whoever they were, and they left their tools for it: boulders chosen and hollowed with fist-deep cups, dew-catchers, each one gathering a finger of fresh water on a foggy night. Nothing drinks it. By dawn it is a lens of ice in the stone, an eye with an ice iris, and by afternoon it has gone back into the air. The same hands stacked cairns — dozens of them, knee-high, balanced with a patience you can feel, each capped with a single dark pebble like a full stop. They built wind-cells too, chest-high horseshoes of slabs, open away from the usual storm, big enough for one person to sit in. Their leavings lie where they dropped: a coil of rope gone silver, a broken staff, a copper chisel gone green at the edge, obsidian flakes where someone knapped a tool and walked off. Nothing rots here, because rot is alive and nothing here is. A dropped thing outlasts its owner by geology.

The one pool of open water is the place's quiet joke. In the lowest floor of the bowl lies a tarn two paces across, the color of old pewter, bitter with salts, ringed with a white bloom like frost that doesn't melt. It has forgotten how to freeze. On nights when the stones crack with cold, it lies liquid, flat and smug. At dawn it exhales ice flowers — fist-sized blossoms of hoarfrost built up out of its own breath, growing in clusters on its still surface — and by mid-morning they are gone and the water is again the only thing in the bowl that refuses the season.

The sound of all this is the sound of almost nothing. Fog has a fizz — a fine, steady beading-static that you stop hearing and then hear again. At dawn the needle ice lifts the silt in a whisper like silk tearing very slowly. At dusk the cold takes hold and a wave of tiny clicks crosses the floor as the stones contract, scattered, unhurried, like applause from very far away. Once a day or less, a pebble finishes a century of frost-creep, rolls off a polygon border, and clacks to rest — one clean note, then silence with a long tail. There is no scratching, no breathing, no wing. It is the specific silence of a world with nothing alive in it, and you feel the vacancy under the sound rather than in it.

Breathe, and the breath is short and costly; two steps up the rampart and you rest. The air is too thin to carry much of anything to the nose — cold stone, wet dust, and near the tarn a faint salt, like the whole mountain ground fine and tasted.

The rules of the basin, if it were asked:

Nothing lives here, so nothing rots; every loss is permanent, and so is everything left behind.
Water arrives only as fog and departs only as light, and the ledger balances.
The day melts; the night crystallizes; the fog falls upward over the rim.
Gravity wins, but slowly — a finger-width a century — and the ground keeps surfacing stones toward no one.
The light comes from everywhere or from nowhere, and casts no shadows to hold your place.
And if the bowl has a name, no one is left who uses it — only the towers, standing in the crowd of stones like people who stayed when the people went.
