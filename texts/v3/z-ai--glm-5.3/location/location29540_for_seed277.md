The first thing the dark tells you is that it is warm. Not sun-warm — there is no sun here to remember — but warm the way a shut oven door is warm, warm from within. The darkness touches you before the ground does, and the ground, when it comes, is warm too: a broad, level stone that has been smoothed by something other than water. Underfoot it feels almost greasy with damp. If you put a bare hand against it, the rock breathes back a faint heat and a smell that does not belong to any wilderness you know — wet chalk, hot iron, a thread of sulfur, and under all of it something like ozone. It is the smell of a machine shop. There is no machine.

There is no anything. Nothing built, nothing made, not a wall, not a shard, not the ghost of a tool. This place has never been developed; it has never even been *touched* in that sense. And yet the smell is not wrong, and neither is the sound — or rather, the sound's absence, because the loudest regular thing here is a drip. Condensation letting go. It comes from every direction at unhurried intervals, a fine percussion of water finding the lowest point of the world, one drop at a time.

And beneath the drips, beneath everything, is the hum. You do not hear it so much as notice you have been standing in it: a tremor under the ball of the foot, a note at the bottom of silence, like a train passing far underground that never arrives. The plateau idles. That is the only word for it. Something enormous is running here at low throttle, and it has been running so long that the running has become a property of the ground, the way warmth is.

The heat arrives before the touch does. That is how you meet the animals — a wall of warmth coming toward you out of the dark, wide as weather. Then the smell, hot dust and digestion. Then the sound: a deep, slow, continuous grinding, unhurried as a mill turned by a patient river. They are browsing the stone. These are the great grinders, the draft animals of an industry no one organized, and they eat rock — the mineral crusts on the old terraces, the ore seams that seam the plateau like marbling in fat. Their bellies are furnaces. Somewhere back in deep time, a lineage of grazing things found that the ground was already faintly warm and already faintly edible — that certain stones, in a long hot gut, will smolder — and the gut microbes scaled up from there, generation by generation, until the digestion of stone became the dominant chemistry of a continent.

What they excrete, they excrete as something almost geological: a pale, dense slag, laid down in poured-looking terraces that tick and crack as they cool, ridged like metal run out of a crucible and left in place. Over epochs the herds have paved their world in their own product. The floor you are standing on is slag — old slag, weathered slag, slag gone smooth and gray and indistinguishable from any plateau stone, but slag all the same, meters of it, kilometers of it, the accumulated output of the oldest production line running. The routes they walk have been walked so long they have worn into shallow corridors, polished bright-feeling underfoot, branching and merging across the dark like something drawn by a logistics system. There is no conveyor here, but there are conveyor lines, a few of them so old the mist flows along them like a river finding its bed.

Because there is mist, always mist. That is the water here — not rivers, not rain exactly, but damp, working in the small. The animals exhale enormous volumes of vapor into the cool of their own high altitude; it condenses on everything: on stone, on hide, on the black mats of vegetation, on you. Dew is the country's drinking water and its drinking fountain. The grinders' own hides are channeled for it — grooved so the condensate runs from their broad backs down to the mouth by simple geometry. Their abandoned wallows seal themselves with a slick secretion and become dew-ponds, pockets of still water scattered along the corridors between one presence and the next, warm as bathwater, each with its own small cloud hovering an inch above its surface. The mist itself moves like a circulatory system: it drains down the worn corridors, gathers in the low places, and at the plateau's rim it pours — silently, continuously — off the edge in slow waterfalls of fog, back down toward the dark lowlands nobody here has ever seen. The whole plateau is a still. A continental distillery, running on animal heat.

The weather, such as it is, is manufactured the same way. A herd of any size holds up a dome of warm air over itself, and when the moisture of a thousand breaths rises and meets the cool of altitude, it comes back down over the same herd as a fine warm drizzle, hyper-local, moving with the herd. It can be raining over one kilometer-long slow mass of animals and dry two hundred paces away. If you want to know where the herds are tonight, you smell the rain in the dark.

Vegetation exists downstream of all this, and it knows its place. It grows where the industry has already been: dark green-black mats rooting in the nutrient of old wallow-muck and weathered slag; lichens that live on the metal skim of the fresh terraces, patient as plating; lines of low growth tracing the corridors like current along wires. Where no herd has passed in a thousand years, there is bare rock, as blank as a factory floor on a holiday. One plant species carries the only steady light in the landscape — pinpricks of cold pale-blue along its leaves, just enough to mark the corridors, faint as instruments left on. It is not decoration; it is advertising, for the small insects that navigate not by light but by the electric texture of the dark, and find their flowers by the glow the way you find a switch panel.

Electricity, in fact, is everywhere, and there are no wires. Every grinder carries a standing hum of charge that the next grinder can feel; the herd holds together in the dark by that hum, a fence of static keeping each vast body from wandering out of company. When two herds pass near each other, the interference rises and the fine hairs on your own arms rise with it. Sleek low-slung things patrol the mist corridors reading the world in fields — you'll never see them, only feel a soft ripple of attention pass over you like a beam. And there are hunters, and the hunters hunt blind, following the electric breath of prey through the wet dark. The kill, when it comes, is almost silent: a muffled clap of static, a pocket of night snapping shut, then nothing but the drips again.

The rest of the abundance works small and close to the ground. Insects that tick like cooling metal as the terraces contract at night-equivalent. Scaled things that climb the grinders' flanks and clean the hide, tolerated in the way industry tolerates its maintenance crews. Dew-pond dwellers that never leave their pocket of water. Scavengers so thorough that there are no bones here either — nothing the grinders produce is wasted, not even the grinders. Everything is secreted, exhaled, worn, or eaten. Nothing is made, and nothing is left over.

The balance is old and exact. The rate at which the herds eat stone equals the rate at which mist and time dissolve the terraces back down. The corridors wear deeper and the vegetation stitches them slower, and the two cancel out. This is a machine that has been at equilibrium so long that "running" is the wrong word — it isn't running anymore, it just *is*, the way a river isn't running to anywhere. A herd takes a season to pass a given point. The tremor changes its note over weeks of walking, region by region, like crossing a factory floor the size of a continent, past department after department of the same patient production.

Stand still long enough and the idle hum of the ground begins to seem like something breathing in its sleep. That is the whole character of the place: a factory with no maker, a production line with no shift change, running so far into deep time that the product has become indistinguishable from the world — because the product is the world, the terraces, the corridors, the damp, the warmth, the dark. It has no lights, no noise, no workers, no waste. It only has output. And the output is a warm, wet, quiet place to stand, in the black, listening to the condensation let go.
