# The Court Beneath the Unfinished Rain

You come in through the wound.

Three seasons ago — four, maybe; time down here pours like honey over the back of a spoon — a wall of water found a weak seam in a low ridge and went through it. The rock tore. The cleft you swim through now is still sharp: fresh fracture faces, faintly glassy, edges keen enough to open cloth and then skin. The gravel under your palms hasn't had time to round; every pebble is still a small blade. The young valley bleeds where the surge cut it — a fine rock-dust hangs near the scars and drifts like smoke wherever the water is disturbed. Nothing here has eroded. Everything here is in the first chapter of its geology.

Then the passage opens, and there is the room.

It's a court. That's the only word that fits. An oval of drowned ground perhaps forty paces across — except when it isn't; be patient, I'll get there — lying at the bottom of a basin so low that everything in the region, given time, drains toward it. Three walls are pale broken rock, raw as a quarry's face, un-mossed, un-softened. The fourth wall is old.

That's the first thing that doesn't add up, and you should sit with it before you look at anything else. The fourth wall is dark stone rounded like riverstone, shaggy with moss that has taken generations to grow, and cut into it is a stair — nine steps descending into the grass, the lowest buried in sward. The stair is worn. Hollowed at its center by feet. Softened like sea glass. Everything about that wall and that stair says *centuries of rain*, and the valley around them says *three seasons*.

So here is the first law of the court, learned by whoever has sat here long enough to learn anything: **the flood did not only bring water. It brought a past.** Pre-worn, pre-mossed, delivered wet. The garden pressed against that old wall came with it, grown before it arrived, and the court simply holds what the water set down.

And it holds a great deal. The green is the population here. A sward of hair-grass covers the whole floor, every blade drowned upright and still green, bending to a tidal breath that takes minutes to complete its stroke. Ferns stand in ranked drifts, dense enough that their fronds rest on each other like hands. A stand of camphor-laurels rises at the far edge — leaves on, none fallen, none dying — swaying with that same slow breath as if the whole room inhales once, holds it, and lets it go. Irises crowd the low places. Moss shags every stone, every root, every horizontal surface. A pale vine climbs the laurels, using its own older stems as trellis, so that in places the growth is stacked three and four layers deep, like sediment with leaves. There is no bare ground. There is no bare anything. Put your hand anywhere in this room and you will be resting it on something living, and the living thing will not mind, and after a while it will begin to grow over your fingers.

The temperature is snowmelt — cool enough that your fingertips ache after a while, cool enough that the laurels' leaves feel like cool leather. The water tastes of high places and green. It is clear in a way that clarity usually isn't: the darkness of the court doesn't come from murk. It comes from the water's sheer patience. It darkens with depth of attention, not distance.

Because it is dark. You see the room by three thin allowances. First, the green itself glows at its growing tips — fern fiddleheads luminous with a cold green light, brighter on the moss of the old wall, as if growth here produces light as a byproduct the way fire produces smoke. Second, light *settles*. In the hollows of the stone lie small pools of old brightness, weeks out of date, dimming — light that came in with the flood and has been sinking ever since, lying in the bowls like sediment. You can stir one with your hand and watch it cloud and slowly resettle. Third, far above — forty meters up, and this is the thing you must understand before you swim anywhere — hangs the underside of the rain.

It is still falling. It has been falling for generations. The flood was never a single event; it is a curtain of rain, descending, and this court lies at the bottom of it, and the wall of water's lower edge glints faintly overhead like a slow grey aurora, moving down a finger's width in a year. That is the second law: **water only rises here.** Nothing recedes. Swim up and you will find no surface, only the rain's soft ceiling, and you should not. The rain is the surface.

Expect animals, and find almost none. It is the strangest gap in the place — water this fertile should swarm, should glitter with shrimp and fry and the small teeming machinery of a drowned world. All the fecundity went to the green instead. What fauna exists appears rarely, and singly. There is a heron. Grey, plumed dry beneath all this water, breathing it without complaint, standing among the irises for a day at a time without moving, and when it does move it is to fish — not for animals, but for the pools of settled light, which it takes one glimmer at a time, swallowing them. There are pale frogs, blind, that sit on the half-buried boundary stones near the old wall, and they are warm, which is wrong, and they may not be alive in the ordinary sense, though they are certainly present. There is one fish — a large pale elder of a tench, older than the flood's arrival, that patrols the walls in a circuit lasting hours, keeping some office no one has named. That's the census.

The sound is nearly nothing, and the nothing has structure. Your own blood, first. The creak of laurel stems against one another. A tick, now and then, of stone contracting in the cool. Once an hour, a soft *whump* from far above — the rain's lower edge letting go of a burst that will take years to arrive. And at long, irregular intervals, the bell.

There is a bell. A small bronze bell in a frame of dark waterlogged wood, standing near the hut's remains — one post and a footprint of stones with the sward growing through, all that's left of whoever cut the stair. The bell hangs mouth-down, full of water, and when it rings — it rings in a register below hearing, a hum you feel arrive through your sternum rather than your ears. It rings when the water gains another centimeter, as far as anyone can tell. It is the only thing here that counts.

The carvings on the boundary stones have been worn to suggestion. The garden is older than its valley. Sit with that too.

The other laws you learn slowly, the way the room teaches everything. **Distance here is attention.** Look away from the far wall and back, and it has drifted a few paces — closer, usually, if you have been kind; the room is about forty paces across except when it isn't, and it is best not to measure it, because measuring makes it self-conscious. **The ground has no floor you can find.** Dive down through the sward, down through the root-felt, and the world keeps going — further down than the court is wide. Roots and dropped stones go and do not stop. This room is a cell in something enormous, one pocket in a drowned body with no known outline, and the court is merely where the body keeps its garden. **The water remembers.** Some hours the water smells of low tide, though there is no tide. Some hours you almost hear gulls. It was snow once, and cloud before that, and sea before that, and it carries the residue of every shore it has ever lain on, which is how the past arrived here pre-worn and the stair came pre-hollowed. The flood is a courier that has been everywhere.

And the last law, which the heron knows and the frogs sit on like a secret: **drowning here is not death. It is a change of citizenship.** The leaves never fell. The frogs are warm. The heron breathes water. Everything green in this room is made of things that should have died and were instead, patiently, transferred.

One more thing, and then you should go. If you stay long — and hours pass like minutes here; check your pockets, they hold silt; check the light, it has aged — the green begins to grow toward you. Not fast. Millimeters an hour. Not hostile. Curious. The moss wants to learn your shape, the ferns want to know what your shadow is made of, and they have nothing but time, and the water only rises.

The exit is downstream, through the same keen-edged cleft, out along the wound the flood made and the flood is still, in its way, making. As you go, the room lets you feel its whole patience at once: the court as one held breath in a transformation measured in centuries, the garden waiting at the bottom of unfinished weather. Someday the rain will descend its last forty meters and touch the laurel leaves, and the flood will be *finished*, and nothing here — not the frogs, not the elder fish, not the counting bell — knows what a finished flood means.

Behind you, faint in your sternum, the bell hums once. Above you, the rain comes down by the width of a thread, patient as everything else in this small, packed, drowningly alive room at the bottom of everything.
