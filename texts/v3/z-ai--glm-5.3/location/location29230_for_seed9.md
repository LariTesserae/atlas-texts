The gate is a sheet of waxed plywood stenciled with numbers — POUR 411, TERRACE SET NINE — and the moment you pass through it, the climate changes. Outside, at this altitude, the air is thin and cool and moves like it has somewhere to be. Inside the hoarding, the air is thick, warm, and still, and it smells of wet chalk, pine tar, and cut grass. The crews call this basin the Cradle. It is nine years old, two hundred paces across, and still being born.

The ground descends from the rim in terraces, an amphitheater of different ages. The oldest ring, at the top, has darkened into something you would swear was soil — head-high copses of nurse-alder, ropey vines, moss gone deep and furred. Below that, three-year stone: fern country, waist-high green, channels of water braiding through it. Below that, this year's terraces, where the moss grows in bright bands and the surface still shows the grain of its formwork, the way a young face shows bone. And at the center, alone, lies the newest pour: jet-black, gleaming, soft at its edges, breathing threads of steam, draped in wet hessian blankets like a patient or a sleeping animal. It was poured on Tuesday. It is warm as a flank. Nobody walks on it yet.

Understand what the stone is. It is made. All of it is made — the terraces, the rim, the walls, and the ground beneath the walls, and the ground beneath that. The slurry arrives hot from the mix-house down the rail line: crushed ancestral stone hauled up by the bucket-line from terraces being demolished below, shell-meal, bone-meal, loam, and spores mixed in at the batch, so that the rock is born pregnant with moss and fern. What other places call concrete, the Charters here call green-stone, and it sets for years, and the setting gives off heat. That is the secret of the warmth: the Cradle heats itself. The whole basin is a slow exotherm, a compost of minerals. On cold mornings, when the plateau's cloud lies below the rim like a second sea, the Cradle floats above it trailing its own small steam, and sometimes a cloud climbs the outside wall, comes over the top, and dissolves into the general mist as if the site had absorbed it.

Water is the site's voice and its clock. A header tank glints at the top of a scaffold mast, and from it gravity does the plumbing — copper and rubber lines running down the terraces to misters that breathe on for an hour and off for two. Under the great sailcloth canopies strung from the masts, the mist condenses and rains, gently, indoors, while open sky shows beyond. The retaining walls weep continuously. Set into them at intervals are carved heads, faces of the old finders, mouths open, and the mouths are the drain-tubes — water runs from them day and night, feeding curtains of moss that hang like green beards. The keepers say the faces weep for the terraces buried below, the ones mined for aggregate, and there is a law about that too: the mountain eats its dead. Nothing here is wasted and nothing here is wasted *away*. The only erosion in the Cradle is deliberate, planned, invoiced. Everything else is addition.

Stand still and you can watch the place grow. The moss species are keyed to curing temperature — each kind colonizes the stone only within a narrow band of warmth — so the terraces wear their age as color: grey-green on the nine-year ring, yellow-green on the three-year, a fierce almost-fluorescent spring green where last winter's pours are just now cooling into habitability. The age-map paints itself. Ferns in this heat and wet open their fiddleheads in a single afternoon; you could, patient enough, sit and see it happen, the coil unwinding like a watch spring running down. There are orchids at fixed stations with little brass tags. There is a pearly fungus that eats nothing but the pine-tar grease on the formwork, so it blooms in straight lines wherever shuttering once stood — the site's memory of its own scaffolding, written in fungus. And the Charter law is absolute: no naked stone past its first year. Bare rock is considered a wound.

The industry is everywhere and almost silent. The loudest machine in the Cradle is the Matron, a rail-mounted gantry tender that walks the rim all day at the pace of a grazing animal — tucking blankets, testing cure with taps of a small silver hammer, one soft note per terrace, a sound like a spoon on porcelain. Through the rails you can feel the mix-house as a far heartbeat, two seconds between pulses. Gauges with ivory needles. Carbide lamps. A bothy of whitewashed corrugated iron where the keepers keep a drip-clock — time here is told in water, and why not, since water is what everything else is told in too.

So the sound inventory is short: drips, in a range of pitches depending on droplet size and what they land on; the slow breath of canvas; the tick of cooling stone at dusk, hundreds of small contractions, like the whole basin settling its accounts; the click of frogs; the Matron's trolley wheel, one squeal a minute, regular as a heartbeat's awkward cousin. Once every seven days, the horn from the mix-house — a long low note across the plateau — and then a new terrace is born and the entire site reorganizes around it. Rail lines to nowhere become rail lines to somewhere. A staircase that ended in air all summer finally lands.

The fauna is a handful of colonists, presences rather than populations. Midges surfacing over the header tank at noon. Pale flatworms living in the permanent damp of the felt blankets. A few small geckos on the warm south wall, motionless as rivets. The frogs came in as eggs stuck to moss bales from the lowland nurseries and now hold the weep-channels in a loose hereditary line, each head under its own carved weeping face, and at dusk they click back and forth up the terraces like the basin counting itself. And there is a heron. It comes most evenings, stands on the edge of the newest blanket — on the warm steam, on the sleeping stone — for an hour, and leaves without hunting. The keepers have stopped trying to explain it. They call it the inspector.

Here are the rules of this world, as far as anyone states them. Land is not found here; land is grown. Beneath the oldest pour is another pour, and beneath that another, stratified intention going down farther than the cores have ever reached, so that bedrock is not a fact but a historical claim, and not a very credible one. Nothing in the Cradle is original ground. Even the soil is poured. Stone must be seeded green at the batch or it is considered stillborn, and the setting years are understood as a kind of gestation — the keepers put their palms flat on the young terraces at dawn to read their heat, speak to them in low voices while they work, and blanket them at night, they say, for the stone's sleep, not the heat's retention. Whether the stone dreams is not a question the Charters answer. The keepers act as though it does.

And the Cradle is only one basin. Beyond the hoarding, other cradles steam on the plateau's shoulder, dozens of them, each with its rim scaffold, its cloud of interior mist, its Matron walking its rail. The whole high shoulder of the made mountain is a nursery, still accreting, a reef of intention building itself upward and outward over generations. But inside the boundary it is quiet, warm, wet, green — dense the way a jungle is dense, every cubic meter occupied by stone, felt, moss, fern, vine, scaffold, conduit, mist, drip, near-touching, layered like pages.

The terraces are slowly filling. Each pour rises a little; each decade the amphitheater is shallower than the last; some year, no one living will see it, the basin will reach level ground, the hoarding will come down, and the forest will simply walk in. Until then the newest stone lies at the center under its wet cloth, ticking softly as it cools, growing toward the day it can hold a keeper's weight, then a nurse-alder's roots, then a canopy, then weather of its own. The weeping faces run. The frogs count. The heron inspects. The horn will sound on the seventh day, and the mountain will add another ring to itself, quietly, in the warm rain it makes for the purpose.
