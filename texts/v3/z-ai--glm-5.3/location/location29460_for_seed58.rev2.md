The lake is gone from the surface of this country. Above the settlement there is only hardpan — a cracked flat of silt the color of old bone, mussel shells rasping underfoot for a kilometer in every direction. But the lake is not finished. It went down. Somewhere between thirty and sixty meters beneath the flat, in galleries the water carved for itself and then abandoned, the lake is still leaving the rock. One bead at a time. The town exists to meet each bead on its way out.

You descend by a winze — a ladder-shaft with a winch for freight — and you cross the dew line about ten rungs down. Above it the air is dry and hot and smells of dust. Below it the air is hot and wet and smells of chalk and iron, and faintly, unmistakably, of a lake, though most of the people born down here could not tell you what a lake smells like. They just call that smell *the old water*, and they live inside it.

The town occupies four chambers and the galleries that braid them together, and you can walk the whole of it in six minutes if you don't stop. But the Long Chamber's ceiling is high enough to be called a sky, and the walls hold the marks of their birth: scallops the size of thumbs and fists, flow-ripples cut by the last water to move through here, still sharp, still bright. There is no dust anywhere. The place is too new for dust — drained within living memory, settled within a generation — and so every surface looks freshly washed, because it was. Stalactites snapped off to clear headroom still weep from their stumps; the stone keeps trying to be a wet cave. Underfoot, the lake's mud is drying into adobe, curling up into polygons that children collect like leaves.

The heat arrives before the lamplight does. It comes up out of the deep rock below and pools, and the air sits at blood warmth near the floor and well above it near the ceiling, where a haze of near-steam gathers in a soft dark layer. The top three meters of every chamber belong to the heat. Nobody builds up there. People move the way you move in a fever — slow, deliberate, in felt-soled slippers that make no sound on the mud, wearing loose weave that never quite dries, skin carrying a permanent sheen that has nothing to do with bathing. The town's oldest saying is that it drinks its own walls.

Because that is the industry here. The settlement is called the Still, and it is one — a distillery with bedrooms. Blowers push the chambers' damp air up the cold stacks, tall pipe-runs into the old upper galleries where the night-cooled rock waits, and there the moisture films, thickens, gathers, and runs back down the standpipes to the cisterns. When the weather turns on the surface — when the hardpan cools at nightfall and the pressure shifts — the whole system feels it an hour before the sky does. The chambers exhale. The condensation peaks. For ten or fifteen minutes it *rains* in the pipe canopy overhead, hissing and ticking, and people pull oilcloth drip-hoods off the pegs at every doorway and go about their business under a plumbing sky. This is the Rains, and it is a night thing; the town's dark hours are its wet season, every day.

Water here is not gathered so much as *caught*, and catching it is property. Every ceiling, every pipe joint, every wall-seam is deeded — if your room's roof yields condensate, the condensate is yours, runneled away through tiled channels that all slope toward the cistern vault. Disputes over ceiling rights go to the Runnel Court, which convenes on the Market Flat on the day after each big Rain. Even cold is property: the chilled pipe-racks are leased, and resting your shoulder against a stranger's cold line is a small theft, recognized as such by everyone. And the water itself passes over trays of crushed mussel shell before it is drunk, because everyone learns young that *naked water* — soft, distilled, having never touched soil — hurts you if you live on it. The town seasons its water with the lake's own bones.

The density of the place is vertical and total. Eight stories of balconies are cut into the wall of the Long Chamber, homes stacked over homes over workshops, laundry strung in the stack drafts where the moving air actually dries things. Overhead runs the second town — the canopy of plumbing, gutters under gutters, cables and standpipes layered like roots. Every lamp is bracketed to stone. The amber circuit runs off a small dynamo on the blower line, one lamp every ten paces, each wearing a halo of humidity, and beyond the lamplight the dark is immediate and absolute, a wall you could lean against. The one bright thing is the sunwell: a bore straight up to a mirror-head on the flat, throwing a single cone of true daylight down into the Green for a few hours each day. When the circle of light touches the fern benches, it is noon, and that is the town's whole clock.

The Green is nearly the whole of the vegetation in the world, which is why it has an article and a name. Moss lawns, fern benches, long troughs of a propagated taro-line fed by drip-runs, and pipe-vines trained up the cold lines to drink the condensate straight off the metal. Nothing here is grown from seed. There is nothing to carry pollen, so everything comes by cutting and spore, cloned down the generations — the ferns are all one fern, met a hundred times. The moss spreads on its own now, in visible rings: a fringe of new growth after each Rain, so you can count the town's age in the moss the way you'd count it in a stump. Green follows the pipes. Where the plumbing leaks, the wall slowly furs. Beyond that, the stone stays stone.

And there are no animals. Not few — none. No rats in the granaries, no mites in the flour, no flies at the taps, no birds lost down the winze, no cats, no dogs. The grain keeps forever and the granaries are run like banks without guards. The sound this absence makes is hard to notice until you do: bare ceilings, silent store-rooms, a stillness in the rafters that no settled place has a right to. Newcomers' skin itches for weeks with insects that never come. The old-timers say you can finally hear the town itself — the ticking of the condensate, the breathing of the stacks. The stacks' note, when the chambers exhale, is the low blown-across-a-bottle tone that would set dogs howing in any town above ground. Here it just happens, and nothing answers it, and the sound goes out over the hardpan through the winze and is gone.

What sound there is stays small. The base layer is ticking — thousands of drip-tips releasing their beads — and when the blowers run, the vibration sets every tip dripping in time, a synchronized rain ticking on the gutters, the town's own metronome. Wet stone carries low voices along the galleries, so people speak softly, not by law but by the room's acoustics. The shift bell is bronze struck with a felt mallet; at full voice, brass would be a kind of violence here. Work happens in the cool hour after the draw, when surface air sinks down the stacks and takes a few degrees off everything. During the heat's hours the town folds into its shaded terraces and waits. Open flame is forbidden above the third terrace, where the haze lives. Gloves in the low galleries — the young rock cuts like glass.

The strangest thing the Still has found so far was a sealed pocket, high in the fourth chamber, that the draining lake had left full of its own air. When the drillers broke it open, decades of held lake-smell came out in one breath, and several of the elders wept without being able to say why. That air is in the town's lungs now.

It is a young place, and it will not age slowly. The same deep heat that makes the rock sweat is the heat that dries it, and year on year the gutters run a little thinner; the Still keeps its own life measured in condensate, chalked on the cistern wall like a tide record. Every dry year softens the scallops' edges a little, curls another square of mud, adds one ring of moss. What the town is drinking, bead by bead, is a memory going up. At night, when the pressure turns, ten thousand drip-tips tick as one, the canopy hisses, and for a few minutes it rains indoors — the last of the lake, exhaling through the rooms where the light is amber and the water is the reason for everything.
