# The Loomworks Hollow

A place best understood as a machine that plants have been allowed to finish building.

## Terrain and Boundaries

The hollow sits low — a shallow basin in otherwise unremarkable moorland, cool enough that the air arrives damp and leaves the ground reluctant to give it back. Water is never gathered here, only visiting. Mist at dawn. Condensation all day on anything metal. A thin seasonal rill threads through the basin's lowest point, appearing and vanishing beneath mats of root like a sentence you keep losing. Rain comes in brief, businesslike spells — just enough to slick the moss, bead the copper, fill the shallow cisterns that everything else drinks from slowly for days afterward.

The whole settlement occupies perhaps three hectares. You can stand at its edge and see all of it at once, which gives the place a strange completeness — it has a boundary, and everything inside it knows it. Beyond the rim: open heath, wind, distance. Inside: stillness and a canopy so thick that from the air the hollow reads as one enormous tree.

## The Plant as Architecture

The dominant species is something between a fig and a willow — nobody planted it, or somebody planted it a very long time ago, and the distinction has stopped mattering. It does not grow *in* the settlement. It grows *as* the settlement. Its trunks have swallowed the load-bearing posts of the original industrial works; its aerial roots have accepted the pipes, running along them, thickening where a fitting needs reinforcement, thinning where pressure must pass through. The buildings are not ruined structures that plants have colonized. They are structures the plants have *assumed responsibility for* — and the responsibility has been held so long that it's now impossible to say where architecture ends and growth begins. A wall is lattice-root and brick in roughly equal proportion, mortar replaced over the centuries by a cambium layer that heals its own cracks.

Gaps between the structures are wide, deliberate, and green with low cover — sedge, moss, liverwort furring the walkway stones. The sparseness isn't neglect. It's sightlines. Every building can see every other, and the plant network beneath them apparently prefers it that way; where two structures stood too close, the roots bulged the ground between them until one was slowly, gently, lifted apart. Nothing here crowds. There is room for the rain to fall through the canopy in visible threads, room for a person to walk between works without touching anything except by choice.

## The Works

The machinery is old industrial stock — a foundry engine, belt-driven looms, a small hydro-wheel in the seasonal rill — but it is not abandoned, and this is the place's quiet strangeness. It runs, at a pace no human operator would recognize as production. The wheel turns when the rill runs. The looms work a slow cloth of bast fiber and root-hair that nobody seems to come collect, though bundles of it, cured and stacked, fill one shed in neat, patient rows. The engine is cold, but the plant has threaded coolant lines through its own tissues, and on cold mornings the trunk nearest the exhaust manifold is always warm, faintly, like a large animal sleeping.

Whatever this place was built to make, the making continues. Only the schedule has changed. The rhythm is botanical now: a loom advances one shed when sap pressure rises in the morning, rests at the heat of the day, works slowly through the afternoon. The whole works keep something like hours.

## Atmosphere and Sound

Cool, green-tinged light through the canopy — ordinary daylight, filtered to the color of pondwater. The air smells of wet iron, cut bark, and the cold mineral breath of the cisterns.

Sound is the place's most specific feature: there is almost none, and what there is, is *process*. Dripping at intervals that vary with the temperature. The loom-sheds' dry click, maybe forty times an hour. The cistern filling during rain with a soft, multiplied patter that resolves into something like a chord as different pools reach different levels. Wind above the canopy that the hollow's depth reduces to a general hush. If you are quiet long enough, you can hear the works breathing — which is to say, you can hear the trunk-swell of the day's warmth, a sound below sound.

Fauna is nearly absent and almost ceremonial in its rarity: a single wren that lives in the works and inspects the looms each morning; a heron that visits the cisterns perhaps twice a year and stands there for hours, apparently just to have stood there. Insects are few and slow. The plants do not seem to mind company; they simply do not require it.

## Rules of the World

- **Maintenance is mutual.** The plant repairs the machine by growing; the machine supports the plant by standing. Neither could have achieved the current arrangement alone, and neither remembers the original contract.
- **Water is transient and must be caught, not stored.** Everything here is designed to hold moisture briefly — moss as reservoir, cistern as momentary pool, condensation as daily ration. Permanence belongs to the plant alone.
- **Nothing is in a hurry, but nothing is still.** Every surface is mid-process: healing, thickening, dripping, wearing, weaving. The hollow is in its own state, weathered into balance, and its balance is a *moving* one.
- **Boundaries are real.** What lives inside the canopy lives by the hollow's slow clock. What stands outside waits in the wind. The rim is a threshold of time as much as of space.

If you visit, you will find no one to greet you, no sign prohibiting you, and no machine that needs you. You will only notice — gradually, over hours — that the works run a little better with someone watching, the looms clicking a fraction more evenly, as if the place is the kind of engine that performs for witnesses, and has been performing, for a very long time, for almost no one.
