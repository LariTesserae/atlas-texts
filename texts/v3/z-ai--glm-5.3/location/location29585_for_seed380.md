**Station Nine**

There is a grate in a street that no one looks down anymore. The traffic passes over it; the rain runs past it; once in a long while a key or a coin falls through, which is how the room below receives its mail. Beneath the grate, a stone stair descends twelve steps, doubling back on itself, and at the bottom is an iron door that has rusted to the exact size of its frame. Set into the wall beside it, a bronze plate: *LIFTING STATION No. 6* — no, look again, the second numeral has been softened by a century of damp into something between a 6 and a 9, which suits the place, because everything in it is a loop with a tail.

Inside is one room. That is important: you can stand at the foot of the stair and see the whole of it — vaulted, brick-ribbed, perhaps sixty paces long and twenty wide, with two short flooded galleries at the far end. It is a kingdom whose edges are visible from its doorstep. The lower courses of the wall are glazed brick, black and sweating, laid in 1911 by engineers who expected water and were right. Above the vault sits a saturated stratum of clay and gravel; above that, the city. The room is, in the most literal sense, a bubble of air held beneath the water table by architecture. A breath a city has been holding for a hundred years.

The air is cellar-cool and utterly still — eleven degrees, the temperature of October, always. It smells of wet iron and chalk and a thin thread of ozone, and breathing it is like drinking very cold water. Every pipe wears a fringe of droplets. The vault weeps from forty-one fixed points, patient as anything, and on the floor beneath each one, a century of percussion has drilled its own small cup into the concrete — forty-one cup-holes of different depths, forty-one different notes. The floor is an instrument nobody meant to build.

The room is nearly empty of things and crowded with animals, and this is not a contradiction. What the machines left behind — two great horizontal pumps on a raised plinth, a gantry crane rusted into its rails, a control desk with bakelite switches — stands at wide intervals like standing stones, with unowned space between them. The emptiness *is* the habitat.

One pump is dead. Its inspection plate was removed long ago, and inside its casings, white fungal threads run like wiring along the impeller vanes; pale shrimp colonize its bearings; the iron interior has become reef. The other pump runs. It has run, unowned, since before anyone currently alive was born — the department that operated it dissolved, the records went elsewhere, and no one ever opened the breaker. The breaker outlived everyone who could have opened it. The machine turns over once every fourteen seconds with a knock at the bottom of the stroke — a heart at four beats a minute — and the sound carries down the vault and comes back off the far wall several seconds later, so that each beat is heard twice.
