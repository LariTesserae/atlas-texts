# The Throat

Begin with the mountain, because the pocket cannot be understood without it: a volcano four days old, still ringing faintly from its own birth, a black cone shoved up through old seabed at a height where the air outside is too thin to hold heat — yet the cone holds heat the way a cupped hand holds water. It has not yet learned to cool. Its flanks are glassy basalt that has never felt rain erode it, ridges still sharp as split flint, ash slopes that crunch underfoot like fresh bread crust because nothing has settled them.

The pocket is near the top. Not the crater itself — the crater is a fuming wound, unvisitable — but a subsidiary fissure a few hundred meters down the north face, a slot in the rock you could pass without noticing if the steam didn't give it away. A slow exhalation of warm vapor rises from the slot and condenses immediately in the thin cold air, so the fissure wears a small permanent flag of cloud, a plume the size of a held breath.

Inside, the world changes scale and stays changed. This is the first wrongness, and you feel it in your inner ear before you understand it with your eyes: the fissure is a slot on the outside and a cavern inside, a chamber perhaps forty meters across, and the walls curve in a way that makes the far side difficult to hold in a single glance. Your depth perception files a quiet complaint and then gives up. Distances in here are approximate. Walk toward the far wall and you may arrive at it, or you may find yourself approaching it still, from the same angle, after walking longer than the room allows. The geometry doesn't loop so much as it *hesitates* — space in the pocket behaves like syrup, reluctant to commit to a shape, and your presence is heavy enough to dent it.

The chamber's floor, walls, and ceiling all weep. This is the water. Steam rising from the volcano's young heat meets the cold stone of the outer shell, condenses everywhere at once, and the whole interior is in a state of continuous gentle rain that makes no sound — not falling drops but a film, a migration of water down every surface, beading on the glassy rock, gathering into threads, running in branching seams that find each other and become rivulets that find each other and become shallow braided streams crossing the floor. The air is saturated and warm — not scalding, the heat here is diffuse, thermal like a body's interior — and every few minutes a place on the ceiling releases one full drop into a pool with a soft *toop*, the loudest sound in the world for as long as you listen for it.

There are no plants. No green, no root, no seed, nothing that photosynthesizes — light enters only as a gray daylight shafting down the slot and it lands on bare rock and is wasted. The base of everything here is a film, a living scum almost too thin to see, that grows where the hottest water seeps out of the floor's cracks — dull amber and pale gray, matting the stone like breath on a mirror, feeding on the chemistry of the young mountain itself, on sulfur and iron and the planet's raw interior juices. It grows slowly and it grows everywhere the water is warm, and it is the only soft thing in the room.

And on that film, everything else.

The fauna is overwhelming — not in size, none of it larger than your hand, but in *number*, in saturation, in the sheer density of bodies per cubic meter of air. Most of it is a single kind of animal, or a single animal in many costumes: a pale many-legged creature, blind, chitinous, wet-looking always because it is, which swarms the film in slow-moving carpets. They graze the amber scum with small scraping mouthparts, and the sound of the swarm is a whisper, a dry sifting, like sand poured over sand, and where the swarm is thickest the sound layers until it becomes almost a texture in the air rather than a noise. They molt constantly — growth is fast in the warm wet — and the floor is drifted with shed exoskeletons in all sizes, translucent husks that the water carries and piles in the low corners like gray foam.

But the grazers are not the strangest thing. That distinction belongs to the crabs.

There are crabs here — squat, wet-shelled, the color of the rock, with black eyes that do work, that watch. There are perhaps a dozen, which in a chamber this size makes them rare, aristocratic, solitary in a way the swarming grazers never are. And the crabs have hands, or near enough. Their inner limbs are dexterous, and they use them: they pick up shed husks and use them as scoops to carry water to dry film-patches they want to cultivate. They select specific stones and wedge them into the streams to divert the rivulets, small dam-works, each one adjusted over days, and the pools behind their stones grow warmer film faster than the open floor. They tap stones against stones — deliberately, repeatedly — to crack open the hollow nodules that form where mineral water drips, and inside the nodules is a dense amber paste the swarming grazers can't reach. When they fight over a good stone, they fight over the *stone*, not the food. The stones are heirlooms. The pocket has no artifacts because it has no makers — it has owners instead, and the distinction matters.

There are also things that eat the grazers — low flat hunters that lie in the molt-drifts indistinguishable from the husks themselves until a grazing carpet flows over them, and then the carpet develops a hole that widens and closes, and the hunter is fat and the whisper of the swarm continues without change. Predator and prey at this scale are both silent. The whole food web makes less noise than a single raindrop.

The second wrongness, after the geometry: time in the pocket is not entirely reliable either. It runs, mostly, but it stumbles. You can watch a crab work its stone-dam for an hour and feel the hour pass honestly, and then find that the daylight shaft has moved across the floor much too far, or barely at all. The swarming grazers seem not to notice. Whether they don't experience the stumbles or whether the stumbles are actually theirs — whether the pocket's slack geometry is a property of the mountain's rawness, the world near a fresh wound simply not yet set in its shape, or whether it is something the density of life in here has slowly done to the room, a hundred thousand warm bodies denting the syrup of space the way hands dent dough — cannot be determined from inside. The crabs have an opinion, possibly. They rebuild their dams after every stumble, without hurry, as if the stumbles were weather.

Atmosphere, then, in sum: the mineral equivalent of a gut — wet, hot, dark-walled, acoustically soft, chemically fed, absolutely packed with quiet life, freshly minted by the planet and not yet fully agreed upon by reality, hidden behind a flag of steam at the top of a mountain that didn't exist last week. Outside, the thin cold and the enormous view. Inside, forty meters of warm dripping room that is longer than forty meters, where a blind whispering carpet eats the mountain's blood and a dozen patient tool-users farm it.

If you crouch by one of the dammed pools, a crab may approach your boot, feel it with a careful limb, judge the material, and go back to work. Your boots would make excellent water-carriers. Give it time. The mountain is young. Everything in here is still learning what a thing is for.
