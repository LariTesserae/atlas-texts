The hill is a pump house.

You understand this only after a while, because from the marsh it looks like ground: a low wooded rise among the reeds, ivy-strangled, its slate roof furred green, its shoulders wearing birch and alder like a coat. The land here leans toward the sea, and has always leaned; the water table sits just under the turf, close enough that every footstep leaves a shining print. The machine inside the hill exists to disagree with that lean. It has been disagreeing for so long that the disagreement is now a fact of geography — the way a river's disagreement with a valley is.

You feel it before you hear it. Coming up the drover's track, the ground-tone arrives through your boots: a slow, enormous pulse, once every few seconds, deep enough that the sedge shivers on the beat. The hill is humming. It has been humming longer than the trees on it have been alive.

There is a door in the hill's flank. It is iron, painted so many times that the paint has become a geology — ochres and oxide-reds in layers like sediment — and the handle is polished to the smoothness of a riverstone by nothing but water and air. It is not locked. It was never locked; it was only heavy, and the heaviness was the lock. Inside, a cast-iron stair goes down eleven steps into warmth, and the air changes on the fourth step: dense, wet, warm as a laundry, smelling of rain that finished a moment ago. The whole world in there has the smell of the minute after rain, held permanently, like a specimen.

And then the sound closes over you like water.

The room at the bottom is small — five paces by nine, ceiling low enough to touch — and it is a room inside the machine the way a seed is inside a fruit. One wall is not a wall but the casing of the great pump, warm as a flank, trembling faintly with each stroke. The floor is an iron grating over a channel of black, glassy water that moves at one fixed speed, has always moved at that speed, the way a river moves rather than the way a tap runs. Above, pipe-runs cross the ceiling like rafters, sweating. A stand of handwheels stands at rest, spokes gone brown as chestnuts. A gauge glass with a red needle. A cast-iron bench. A manifold of amputated pipework, stenciled in flaking white: GALLERY C — CONDENSATE. KEEP DOOR SHUT.

That is the entire inventory. The room is almost empty, and the emptiness is the first thing you notice after the sound — the space between the few objects feels deliberate, held open, like the space in a shrine. And the room is only one lit pocket in a much larger vacancy: all around it, above and below, the works stretch away hollow — tank volumes, duct voids, shafts standing dark and unentered, kilometers of interior air. This gallery is a single comma of light in all that hollow iron.

Everything in the room is wet, and the wetness is the machine's own breath. The pump's waste heat warms the air past what the walls can hold; moisture condenses on every surface and the iron weeps. It weeps in fixed places. There are perhaps thirty drip points on the ceiling, each with its own rate, its own tone, its own assigned landing spot — runnels that have cut nothing, because they run in machined channels cast for them, or for something like them.
