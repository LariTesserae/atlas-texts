There has never been rain here. That is the first thing to understand, because everything else follows from it: the dryness, the quiet, the tall thing standing out of the grey sea of dust like a mast out of water.

The sun is an ember in a haze — an old sun, dim and orange, the kind that gives light generously and warmth grudgingly. It lights the world the way late afternoon lights a workshop: everything visible, nothing warm. Below the ember lies the Loose, the Loess Sea, thousands of miles of dead basins where the land used to be. It isn't there anymore, mostly. It blew away. It is still blowing away, and this is the crucial fact of the place: the world below is leaving, and it is arriving here.

The Mast stands two miles of steel above the basins, on the high shoulder of what remains, and its work is catching. The wind at this altitude is a permanent river — smooth, enormous, tirelessly carrying the ground away grain by grain — and the Mast combs it. Its outer skin is a coat of charged plates and sieve-terraces, hung floor over floor for a third of a mile, harvesting the loess that would otherwise be lost to the sky. Everything the city has, it caught. Everything it is, it winnowed out of the air.

Masthead is nine years old. That matters too. The burrs are still on the cut steel. The paint is only now beginning its first slow surrender, and only on the windward faces — no rain has ever fallen on it, no frost has ever split it; the only weathering here is the wind's long opinion. Riveters are still working on the upper decks while, below, scrappers are already salvaging the first scaffold sections. The city is built faster than it is finished, and parts of it are unfinished faster than they are built.
