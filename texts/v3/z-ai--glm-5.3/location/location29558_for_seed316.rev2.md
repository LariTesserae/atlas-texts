# The Receiving Floor

There is no silence to arrive out of. You enter this place the way you enter water — the noise meets you kilometers before the land does, as a pressure against the eardrums, a fullness in the sinuses, a texture the air has worn for so long it thinks the texture is the air. Then the warmth: everything is blood-warm here, mud and rain and wind alike, the whole plain holding one temperature like a held hand. Then the wet, which is not falling anywhere. It has already fallen. This is a landscape that exists permanently in the minute after rain — the rain happened, is happening, is about to happen; all three tenses share the same warm amber air, and every surface glistens as if it has just been told a secret it will repeat forever.

## The bottom of everything

Understand what this is before anything else: this is where the world put itself when it was finished.

Erosion is a transport economy. Mountains are made, weathered, and shipped downhill, load after load, age after age, until every grain arrives at the lowest place there is — and this is the lowest place there is, the last downhill, the floor of the finished world. Every mountain the planet ever had is here. That is not a figure of speech. Each of the four thousand kilometers of plain in any direction is a warehouse aisle of dissolved ranges, digested continents, the powdered memory of everything that ever stood up. Nothing here can erode further. Erosion did not stop; it *completed* — and a completed thing, unlike a merely ended one, can afford to keep moving.

So the relief is essentially nothing. The entire region is flat the way water is flat, which is to say *actively* flat, flat as an achievement. The horizon is a straight line in every direction, and the mud beneath you is the same four materials in endlessly varying moods: clay, silt, brine, and a thin living skin called the bloom. Depth without structure. History without order. A handful of mud lifted from anywhere is a census of the former world — quartz from one dead range, feldspar from another, a fleck of shell, iron that was once somebody's mountain — all of it milled to its final size, all of it at rest, none of it distinguishable.

## Water, everywhere nearly

The water table is not a table here. It is the surface, lying down politely on top of the mud, or standing in shallow pans that open and seal themselves like breathing mouths. The pans of open brine are called shine-pans, and they are the plain's mirrors: each one holds the low amber sky so faithfully that the world seems to be doubled downward.

It sweats. On the still evenings, the ground exhales its own breath upward in warm ghosts, and if you watch the mud closely you will see the fine threads of rain rising — drizzle falling upward, returning to the low clouds the plain made from itself, closing the loop. The air is at one hundred percent of its water. You can drink the air. There are hours when you cannot tell whether you are standing in a fog or the fog is standing in you.

And there are stones — the only stones on a continent of mud — the *last stones*: pebbles too hard for the world to finish, the resistant surviving cores of dead ranges, scattered one per mile, each one the entire remaining remnant of a mountain. On cool nights each of them chills by a single degree against the sky, and condensation finds them, and every last stone sits in its own small wet halo, weeping. The weeping rocks of this place are real. They are just small. That is what happened to the world's hardness: it shrunk until it fit in your palm and cried.

## The busiest flat thing there is

Do not mistake the flatness for rest.

The whole continental surface is in motion, centimeters an hour, a conveyor of mud rearranging itself forever. There are swells — mudwaves — a hundred kilometers from crest to crest, a meter and a half high, migrating at the speed of a walking animal. Stand on one for an hour and you will feel the plain breathe underfoot. There are silt gales, walls of hissing airborne mud that move like slow dark weather, scraping a swath sterile and moving on. There are dry tides, bloom-flushes, pans opening, pans sealing, crust doming and cracking and weeping from its wounds.

Nothing here is lost; everything is diluted. And because nothing can be destroyed — everything being already in its final form — all this turbulence is *pure* rearrangement, churn without consequence, an anthill with no ants, a bazaar where all the goods are the same and none of them are for sale. The plain maintains its perfect level the way a juggler maintains height: through total, ceaseless, meaningless effort. Flatness here is not a state. It is a performance, and the performance has been running for ten million years.

## The sound, which is a substance

The loudness deserves its own accounting, because you will come to treat it as a material.

None of it comes from anything alive. It comes from the plain's business. The condensation alone is deafening: a continent of dew-point surfaces ticking in unison, an applause that never ends and never had an audience. Under it runs the groan of the migrating swells — a ship-hull note from the body of the world, felt in the sternum more than heard. Above it the silt gales hiss, the pans slosh, the last stones drip. Over everything stands the *note*: a fundamental hum the whole saturated surface produces simply by being so large and so wet and so busy, a note with tides of its own, swelling and easing on cycles of days, so vast it has weather.

And with all the physical weather of the world finished, sound is the last eroding agent left. It works slowly, but it works: the pans are fluted and polished in patterns that follow the note, and the mud behind each last stone lies in an acoustic lee — quieter, measurably, and the bloom grows paler there, a pale streak pointing away from the loudness like a compass needle. The light here is too diffuse to cast shadows, so the plain's only shadows are cast by noise. That pale streak is the world's remaining idea of shade.

You will stop hearing the sound as sound. It becomes the floor. But learn its one exception before you learn anything else: when something large is about to collapse, the plain inhales — half a second of total, obscene quiet, the only silence on the continent, a delicacy nothing gets to keep. Then the thing lets go, and the hum takes the floor back.

## A light with no source

The sun is not a presence here. It is a rumor — a pale coin swimming behind ten kilometers of permanent cloud, giving light that arrives already spent: amber, shadowless, sourceless, the light of a late afternoon that has lasted ten thousand years. You cannot tell where it comes from; it seems to be remembered rather than transmitted.

Warmth, meanwhile, is simply the local condition of matter. Nothing is colder than anything. Steam never shows, because nothing has anything to rise away from. At the dark hours — there is a night, and the night is only a deepening of the same held breath — the plain generates its own faint light in patches, a cold green-gold luminescence wherever the bloom is thinnest or something below the surface is beginning to happen. Light here is not a state. It is an event. It happens where the plain is busy.

## What grows, what moves

Vegetation is almost an apology. The bloom is the whole of it in practice: a film of algae and iron-eaters a few microns thick and continental in extent — technically the largest green thing that has ever existed, though it would weigh nothing, though it is thin enough to be a rumor of a plant. It colors the mud umber, rust, bottle-glass, purple-black, and it is the only thing holding the surface together at all. When a silt gale scrapes a swath raw, the bloom re-skins it within a day — green snow falling upward out of the mud's own spores.

In the deeper pans stand occasional sedge, leaning all one direction, away from the next storm, growing in water that will not survive the season. Their dead stalks get incorporated into the crust, and so they become the only sticks on a continent without trees.

The bird census is three. Some days you see all of them. After each bloom-flush there is a hatch of midges for one hour, and everything with wings arrives from nowhere, feeds, and leaves, and that hour is the plain's entire season. The worms live down in the pans, and their burrows are the only quiet rooms in the world — put your ear to the crust and a hand-span down there is *nothing*, which is so expensive here it feels like theft. There are fish in the deeper shine-pans. Some of them are fish. The plain can condense fish too, when it has fish on its mind, and it does not keep records.

It is crowded and it is empty. Nothing lives here, and the place is full. Both of these are true, and the reason is the strangest thing about the plain, which is the next thing to tell.

## The crowding

The plain is supersaturated with shape.

It has eaten too many mountains to remember them as ingredients. It remembers them as *forms*. Every cubic meter of the mud is at capacity — matter at maximum packing, water at maximum saturation, air at maximum humidity, sound at maximum occupancy, and under all of that, a seed bank of shapes crowded shoulder to shoulder in the dark, nascent figures budded edge to edge with no room between them, waiting. The surface you walk on is only the visible fraction of the standing-room crowd below.

And when the medium is disturbed — by a storm, a footfall, a shout, by the constant nothing that is always happening — a form precipitates out, the way sugar strings out of a supersaturated syrup when you tap the glass. A face the size of a barrow rises and is gone. A hand. A doorway standing open, with no wall to prefer. The arrival is loud — a tearing-calico sound, a cough from underground — and here is the first crack in the world's behavior: the sound arrives half a second *before* the form does. Sound outruns cause on this plain. You hear the slap of a bird's landing while the bird is still deciding to descend. You hear a collapse in the future's tense.

Some nights, a whole mountain condenses — one of the *actual* former ones, profile correct to the ridgeline, the plain's memory of a peak it digested ten million years ago — three hundred meters of mud standing up out of nothing, weeping from its own flanks, holding for the length of a storm before it lets itself back down. On those nights the shine-pans show it before it rises: reflections in this place lag, or lead — your own reflection in the brine looks up slightly before you do, at the place the mountain will stand. Footprints fill from beneath. And on the far horizon, sometimes, land bulges upward in the wrong direction, the world's curve reversed, which is only the largest of the dreams queuing to be rained.

The strain in reality is tidal. It shows under load, like an old rope: mostly the world behaves, and then, for half a second, a mountain stands up in it.

## What was built, or nearly

There are structures here, and you should be told plainly what kind they are, because the answer is the place's best trick: *building, on this plain, is a weather.*

A causeway of white bloom-crust crosses a pan for nine hundred meters and ends in mid-air, its far terminus worn smooth by nothing. Twelve grey steps descend into the mud at the edge of a shine-pan — the top step above the surface, the rest buried, and the stair holds its alignment through every storm, which is either construction or memory. There are hearth-rings, plough-lines across a square kilometer dissolving at the edges, ring-mounds like the ghosts of walls, middens of small white shells belonging to no mollusk anyone has catalogued.

And in the northeast quarter there is an arch that precipitates after every storm in the same spot, the same proportions — a habit of the medium, the way a crystal has habits. No one built the arch. The plain forms it the way cold forms frost on glass. Some structures recur so reliably that they are, for all practical purposes, buildings — buildings whose builder is the weather.

Somewhere in the plain's seed bank are the shapes of doorways, thresholds, lintels, and someone, sometime — or no one, ever. The place does not distinguish between ruins and forecasts. After a while, neither do you.

## The rules, so far as they hold

1. Nothing here is destroyed. Everything is already finished. Matter has reached its final size; change is rearrangement, forever.
2. Nothing stands unless held. Falling is the ground state. Standing is a temporary opinion, load-bearing for about a minute.
3. The medium is supersaturated with shape, and disturbance precipitates form. Since the plain cannot be quiet, the precipitation never stops.
4. Sound outruns cause. The future is audible, faintly, half a second early.
5. Water moves in loops. The water table is the world's skin, and some rain falls upward.
6. Height is a memory. It exists in the past tense and in dreams, and the dreams sometimes stand up.
7. Everything you find was either made by someone or condensed by no one, and there is no test.
8. Nothing is lost; everything is diluted. A handful of mud is a census, and the plain is the whole country counted.

## The dark hour

Stand out at the dark hour, if you want the place entire. The hum is at full tide. The mud is blood-warm to your boots; the air is drinkable; the light is remembered. Somewhere out past the shine-pans you hear tearing calico — and wait, nothing yet — and then the profile begins, luminous faintly along its ridgeline where the bloom is thinnest, a mountain raising itself out of the flat with the rain of its own flanks already running. It stands while a silt gale hisses underneath it. Its reflection in the pans was standing before it was.

Then the plain inhales. That obscene half-second — the only silence on the continent, and you can hear the whole plain listening with you.

And it lets its mountain down, and the hum takes the floor back, and the surface resumes its argument with itself at centimeters an hour — the finished world, rehearsing with everything it ate all the shapes it will never need again, in the only direction it has ever understood, which is down, and the only direction it has left, which is around.
