# The Still Ocean at the Top of the World

There is water here, and there is height, and the two have made peace. The place is a sea that lives three miles above where seas should be — a drowned plateau, the summit of a mountain so old its name has been eaten by the roots that hold it up. Long ago the water came and never left. It pooled in the mountain's crown, went still, and has been still since.

What holds all of it — the water, the weight, the whole suspended ocean — is plant. Not plants scattered *in* an environment, but plant *as* the environment: every surface, every boundary, every load-bearing structure is living tissue. There is no rock beneath the water worth mentioning. The floor of this sea is a rhizome mat ten thousand years thick, layered on itself like compressed cloud, dense enough to walk on if anything here walked. The shores are not stone but the flanks of colossal trunks, grown into buttress and terrace, bark rising out of the water in grey-green ridges the size of hills. Where a cliff would be, there is instead a woven wall — roots crossed and re-crossed and fused, a weaving that no hand did and no hand could undo. Everything is made. Everything grew.

The light is nearly nothing. The plateau sits under a permanent ceiling of canopy — not a forest canopy, thin and broken, but a closed vault of leaves and living architecture through which the sun arrives only as a rumor, a cold green suggestion that fades entirely below the first fathoms. Under the water there is no gradient of light, no bright zone dimming into dark. There is simply dark, complete and soft, a darkness with texture to it because the water is never empty. It carries spore-dust, drifting root-hairs, the slow silt of a place that has been quietly metabolizing for longer than most geologies.

The cold is a working cold, not a hostile one. The water sits near the temperature of deep cellars, and everything here has arranged itself around it. The trees do not drop their leaves. Nothing here drops anything. There is no autumn, no season, no year with a different weather in it. The great trunks circulate warmth from somewhere below — you can feel it with a hand pressed to the bark: a faint, steady heat, the pulse of a body that never sleeps and never hurries. The system closed itself an unimaginable time ago and has been in balance ever since. Water rises through the roots, condenses on the canopy vault, rains back down in a fine perpetual drizzle that is not weather but circulation — the place raining on itself, internally, forever.

It is loud. This is the surprise of it. Nothing moves and nothing changes, but the sound is enormous and continuous: a deep subsonic groaning of the great trunks under load, a hiss like distant surf from the internal rain, and beneath the water a constant crackle and tick — the sound of ten million small bodies feeding, rasping, calling. Because the fauna are abundant beyond counting. In the dark water the animals have become their own constellation system — most of them carry their own light in cold blues and greens, so that what you would see looking down into the drowned plateau is not blackness but a slow, drifting starfield of creatures: whole communities of them living in the root-mat like tenants in a coral city, grazing the algal film that grows on every submerged surface, singing to each other in chirps and long whale-like sighs that carry for miles through the still water. The birds — or the things that fill the bird niche — nest in the canopy vault in colonies of thousands, and their calls echo strangely in the enclosed air, doubling and trebling off the leaf-ceiling until the sound is architectural.

The scale is regional and the density is not. Between the great pillar-trunks there are open reaches of water a half-mile wide where nothing breaks the surface for its entire length — just cold dark water and the faint glow of the fauna below, and silence of the specific kind that comes from great space rather than absence of life. The presence here is scattered the way stars are scattered: enormous presences, far apart, each one a settlement. A single trunk rising from the mat might host its own complete world on its flanks — gardens of moss-forest, hanging root-chambers flooded and drained and flooded by the circulation rain, whole faunal nations — and then a mile of empty water before the next one. You could spend a life in the territory of one trunk.

There are no rules here that anyone wrote. The place is pre-tool, pre-language, pre-everything — its only law is equilibrium, and it enforces that law without malice. Whatever enters the water and cannot hold its place in the balance is absorbed, quietly, by the mat. The world has reached its final form and stopped. Erosion happened once, ages back, wore the mountain down to almost nothing, and then the roots caught what was left and built it back up as something better than stone — something that could carry an ocean on its shoulders and not think about it. This is the end of the story of that mountain, and the end has lasted so long it has become a beginning no one will ever need. Nothing here is going anywhere. Nothing here has to. The rain falls inward, the dark water holds its glow, the trunks groan their one long note under the weight of the sky-sea, and the whole vast arrangement simply continues — complete, ancient, made entirely of life, and perfectly, deafeningly at rest.
