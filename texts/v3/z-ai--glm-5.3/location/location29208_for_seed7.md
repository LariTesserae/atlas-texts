**Kiln Hollow — Borehole GH-7**

The shaft ladder goes down forty meters and the world changes twice on the way. First the light goes: it narrows to a grey thread from the collar far above, then to a rumor, then to nothing. Then the air turns — at about the thirtieth rung the cold of the surface lets go all at once, and the atmosphere becomes warm, wet, and thick, like opening a greenhouse door in January. That transition is the only boundary Kiln Hollow has. Below it, everything else is interior.

The chamber is cathedral-sized and shaped like the inside of a shell. The walls are scalloped — fluted, sharp-edged scoops left by ancient hot water that pressure-carved this void out of the rock in fast geological time. Nothing here has been worn. The stone still holds its edges like broken crockery; drill scars from the breakthrough remain crisp, half-moon scoop marks on the far wall around a perfect circle where the bore punched through into the void twenty-odd years ago and let the heat out. In a sense this is the newest landscape in the region, and one of the oldest rooms.

At the chamber's head stands the wellhead: a valve stack like a rusted Christmas tree, capping a hole that breathes. The concession that drilled it folded before the pipeline was finished, and the crew capped the bore with a valve left deliberately cracked — closing it fully would have pressurized a fracture and blown out the hillside. So the earth has bled steam through that controlled leak for decades, and nobody has paid for it, and nobody ever will. The machine outlived its company and changed professions.

What remains of the station is scattered, not sprawled. A pipe-run follows the wall, its lagging split open like seed pods, flowering orange rust. In an alcove sits a turbine that was installed but never coupled — a rotor that has never once turned, built to make electricity for people who stopped existing as a company before it could start. A control kiosk with a stenciled **GH-7** on the door. Inside, a chart recorder still runs on the trickle of power the site draws from its own well: the pen arm swings faithfully with each pressure cycle, writing a record on a brass drum that ran out of paper years ago. It has been documenting the weather of this room for an audience of nothing. Orange surveyor's dots lead from the shaft foot to the wellhead — the only road. A hardhat. A mug furred with mineral deposit. One bootprint pressed into dried mud, directly under a ceiling drip, being slowly deleted one drop at a time.

The steam feeds the ceiling. All night and all day — and here those words mean nothing — moisture condenses on the high stone and falls back as warm rain, intermittent, localized, wandering. The drips are tuned: each pool in the uneven floor gives a different pitch, so the dark has a chord of tiny percussive notes. The runnels they feed are young watercourses, millimeters deep, cutting the first groove of their lives. Where they run over ledges, the flowstone is nascent — beads, nubs, the stone's first teeth. In a thousand years these will be curtains. Tonight they are barely a suggestion of stone, laid down over the drill marks in a film so thin it only changes the way the light doesn't reflect.

Because there is so little light. The shaft thread far above is the entire sky, a point-sized, weather-driven star. When clouds cross the collar, it brightens and dims. During storms above, a flash can flood the whole chamber for a quarter second and show everything at once — the shell walls, the grove, the rust — a vision you might wait years to repeat. Otherwise the dark is a material, and the place is known the way a face is known: by warmth. The floor heats through boot soles. The air closest to the leaves holds a shimmer too faint to see but present to a cheek held near it. Palms become eyes.

And there is a smell that should not be down here: green. Crushed leaf, humus, wet growth
