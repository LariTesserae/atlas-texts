The sound arrives long before the place does.

You come up onto the plateau through mineral haze — a high, thin, sourceless gray light with no sun in it — and the first sign is a pressure against your eardrums, felt before heard, like standing near a great river you cannot see. The land underfoot is paved. Not path-worn paving, not old road: fitted slabs running to the haze in every direction, joints sealed with a pale crystalline mortar that has the dull gleam of salt. There is no soil between the slabs. No dust in the cracks worth mentioning, no lichen, no stem, no seed husk. The paving is the ground. If there was ever anything natural underneath it, that fact retired from the world a very long time ago.

Warmth comes up through the slabs. Not sunlight warmth — under-warmth, kiln-room warmth, the temperature of a wall just past firing. And through the seams of the paving, in the joints of the salt-mortar, thin lines of ember-orange light leak upward from somewhere far below. All the shadows here point the wrong way. Anything standing on the plain throws its shadow upward onto nothing; the undersides of overhangs are lit like faces around a fire. It is dim everywhere, a dusk made of haze above and stove-glow below, and it has been this exact brightness, you understand very quickly, for longer than brightness has been measured here.

The place itself, when you reach it, is small. A low hill the size of a chapel or a small grove, with a clear boundary — a ring of set stones where the slab-paving changes pattern — and beyond the ring, more made terraces step down into haze. The hill is made entirely of fired clay. Vessels stacked and nested and wedged into vessels, throat-to-belly, shoulder-to-shoulder, thousands of them, from pot-shapes the size of a torso up to room-sized bells lying on their sides half-swallowed by the mass. The density is total. There is no air-space that is not a designed passage; there is no gap that is not a pipe. Every square span of the hill's surface is a rim, a lip, a handle, a glaze.

The clay is coil-built. You can read the whole grammar of hands in it — even where ten thousand years of gas-flow have polished every coil to a streamlined roundness, even where the glazes (ash-gray, oxblood, salt-white, a black glass where the heat vitrified everything) have blurred the record, the making shows. Some of these vessels were fired in the vent itself; the builders used the world's own heat as their kiln. Sheets of hammered native copper are pinned over certain mouths with crude clay nails, beaten thin, uneven at the edges, dark with a tarnish the color of dusk. Lay a palm flat on one of these sheets and you feel the trembling — a shiver far too fine to see, the metal ringing in sympathy with something, and it has been trembling at exactly this amplitude, at exactly this frequency, since the hand that pinned it let go.

Because the sound — you cannot describe this place without describing the sound, and the sound cannot be described, only undergone. It is deafening. It is a waterfall with the water removed. It is a dry sea falling forever through a narrow place. Layer it: underneath everything a bass thrum that you feel in your sternum and the floor and your teeth, the drum-skin of the whole plateau; above that, hoots and horn-tones from the big bell-mouths, low and round; through everything a hiss of gas in the packed finger-width channels; and at the very top of hearing a whine so thin it is almost a flavor. Together they do not blur into noise. They hold a chord. The builders tuned this hill — every neck, every chamber, every copper membrane — until the roar resolved into a single sustained harmony with no beat in it, no drift, no waver, no decay. It is not weather. It does not gust. It is a fact of the place, like gravity with a pitch. If you shout inside it, your shout makes no dent; you feel your own voice in your jaw and hear none of it.

The dust behaves strangely, or rather it behaves perfectly, which is stranger. Mineral dust rides fixed streamlines through the crawlways, bright lines of moving air that never alter course. It settles into drifts that look freshly poured and are ten thousand years old — every drift a shape at equilibrium, fed and lifted at exactly equal rates, its outline permanent. On the horizontal plates the standing waves of the chord have drawn the dust into ridged figures, radiating patterns like the figures sand makes on a struck plate — except these were drawn once and then held. The roar drew its own portrait and framed it. And in certain spots, outlined cleanly in the dust, are circles of bare clean glaze: the nulls, where the sound cancels itself, marked out in absence.

Inside the hill the passages are shoulder-wide, warm as a held hand, and the packing is absolute — you move through a solid whose gaps are tubes. Then a crawlway opens without warning into a null: a small room where the roar drops away like surf behind a dune, and you surface into silence so sudden you hear your own blood. The nulls are finished rooms. Seats worn smooth. Shallow cups worn into bench-tops. And on one wall, scratched and then dotted with hammered copper dust so the marks glint, a tally: rows of strokes in fives, fives, fives, and then a half-finished group, and then nothing. The counting stopped mid-stride. Nothing in this place will ever complete it. It is the only event left here, and it is an absence shaped like one.

At the hill's heart is the throat: a vertical shaft, wide as a doorway, exhaling a warm constant updraft that has the smell of hot stone and nothing else. It is lined with courses of ancient brick — layer under layer, older the deeper the eye follows them, until the brick is the world. Far down, at the limit of sight, something glows a dull permanent ember-orange, fire-colored without flame, steady as a lamp that cannot gutter. The hill, you realize, is not the source of the note. The hill is the embouchure. Somewhere below this lining, something enormous is exhaling, steadily, at a rate that never varies, and has been since before the first coil of clay was pinched — and the builders did not make the sound. They built the instrument that holds it. A reliquary for a note.

There is no life. Nothing nests, nothing burrows, nothing browses; no rot, no decay, no spore — the dryness is beyond even the patience of decomposition, drier than a tomb, and a tomb at least has decay. In the shaded seams of the interior glazes there is a rust-colored stain, a film so thin it might be mineral weathering, except that it sits only where the gas is richest, and it is always exactly the same thickness. It may be alive. It may be the last, simplest form of life this world produced, living on the warmth and the chemistry of the breath. If it is alive, it is alive the way this place is stable: fixed, complete, growing no larger, dying not at all, doing nothing about it.

The rules, then, as well as they can be stated: the gas rises forever, at one rate, from one depth. The warmth is its warmth. Water cannot stay here — any moisture rides the updraft out; there has been no rain since before the paving. Sound in this hill is conserved like matter — shaped, tuned, and held, unable to decay because nothing lets it. Light comes from below and never varies; there is no day here, no season, no clock but the unfinished tally, which keeps time by refusing to. And erosion has finished — the wind polished every surface to its final streamline and then retired; fingerprints survive only in the little shadow-pockets the flow could not reach, the last sheltered records of the hands.

The place is not dying. It is not even old in the way old things are old, still slackening, still settling. It is concluded. It stands on the far side of history, a struck bell whose ring was so long it became permanent, a chord that has no beginning and therefore cannot have an end. Stay long enough and your heartbeat leans toward the bass. The roar has room in it for everything you are.
