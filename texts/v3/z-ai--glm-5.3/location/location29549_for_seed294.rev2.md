# The Drowned Cathedral of the Upper Dark

There is a place where the water goes up forever, and someone built a world inside it.

You arrive—if arrival is even the word—already submerged, always submerged. There is no surface here. The ocean is not an ocean in the way you knew oceans; it is a total medium, a cold black amniotic everything, and it presses not down but *through*, from all directions equally, as if the water were not falling anywhere but simply *persisting*. The temperature hovers just above the point where motion becomes reluctance. Not freezing—but thinking about it. The cold is patient. It has had a very long time to learn patience.

Because everything here is old. Erosion 2.8: this is a landscape in its final form, or what passes for final. Nothing is being carved anymore; everything has *been* carved, worn, resolved. The shapes you drift past are the answers to questions the water asked a hundred thousand years ago, and the water has long since accepted the replies.

And every one of those shapes is made. That is the strangeness that is not strange here, because there is nothing natural to compare it to. There is no rock that was not placed. No seabed that was not laid, tile by tile, by something—no ridge, no channel, no wall that did not come from intention. The world is a construction with no constructor visible, a city without a species, a machine with all the moving parts removed. Whoever built it wanted no tools left behind, wanted no devices, only the *result* of devices: slopes engineered for currents that still run, apertures angled for light that no longer comes, vast fluted columns rising toward a ceiling of water that might as well be sky.

High, this place. You feel it in the pressure's peculiar texture—thin somehow, spacious, as if altitude could exist underwater, and it can, here. The construction ascends in terraces and broken stairways, thousands of meters of made-stone receding into dimness, and you drift among the upper galleries where the light is a permanent blue-grey dusk, a light with no source, light like the memory of light diffused through a hundred meters of made-water.

There is no vegetation. Nothing green, nothing growing, nothing that photosynthesizes or wants to. The surfaces are mineral and metal and something else—materials with no name, smooth as bone, cold as intention. Faint films of something simpler-than-life coat the lower walls: pale sheets of chemotrophic mat, not plants, barely even alive, just chemistry that learned to hold its shape. They ripple in the currents like breath on a mirror.

The fauna, though. The fauna is the anomaly, the abundant thing in a world of sparseness. Because the water is *full* of them—pale, eyeless, built-of-nothing-you-recognize swimmers, thousands upon thousands of translucent bodies moving through the drowned architecture in slow purposeless abundance. They are the wrong scale—too large, many of them, for a world this cold and dim, great soft luminous masses drifting between columns like clouds with opinions. They do not eat the mats. You never see them eat. They school around the constructed apertures, funnel through engineered channels as if obeying blueprints written before their species existed, using the dead city as a body uses a skeleton. Perhaps that is what they are: the builders gone soft, gone animal, gone mindless, still running the old routines.

And the world is *turbulent*. That is the cruelty of it—or the vitality, depending on your temperament. Nothing settles. Currents scream through the drowned corridors on no schedule you can learn; the water convulses, spins, drops through made-shafts in sudden sinkhole plumes that rearrange everything loose. Turbid clouds bloom off the terraces and collapse. The mats tear loose and reattach. The great swimmers are flung about like snow in a headlight. The dynamic is total: an ancient, finished, immutable form filled with violent, ceaseless motion. The architecture has said everything it will ever say, and the water will not stop interrupting.

For all that—sound is scarce. Most of what you hear is felt rather than heard: a bass wash of distant turbulence, the low moan of water forcing itself through an aperture built ten thousand years ago for exactly this, the faint click and tick of thermal contraction, the soft granular rasp of mats detaching. Silence here is not absence. It is a structure, like everything else.

Spend long enough drifting in the upper galleries and you begin to understand the rule of this place, which is the rule of all ruins that succeeded too well: *the makers finished*. Not died—finished. Every surface resolved, every current accounted for, every aperture cut to its final dimension. What remains is the completed thought, enduring in cold dim water, animate with borrowed life, storm-driven, inert, patient, done.

The world has settled into its answer. The water keeps asking anyway.
