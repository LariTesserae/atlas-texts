# The Verdigris Terraces

The sound reaches you before the place does. From kilometers out — a wall of layered sound, like ten thousand bells being struck underwater, like a cathedral organ played by rainfall — the Terraces announce themselves. The noise is not chaotic. It has structure, and that is somehow worse. It pulses on a slow four-beat cycle, and locals (there are locals, few as they are) say you begin to dream in that rhythm within a week.

## What's There

The land is low, barely above the waterline of a river system that no longer quite behaves like rivers. Water moves through this region as intermittent abundance: dark threads threading between terraces, sudden vertical curtains of condensation falling from the underside of the canopy structures, dew-harvesting fields that gleam at the boundaries where warm air meets cold stone. Rain comes at intervals the place seems to schedule itself, arriving with the fourth beat of the great sound.

It is *young* land. That's the strangest thing about it. The rock underfoot is fresh-lipped, sharp-edged, unweathered — basalt and something else, a material with a faint blue-black sheen that takes fingerprints and holds them for hours. The cliffs have not yet learned to crumble. The terraces step down toward the water in clean machine-cut increments, as if the landscape was extruded last Tuesday and the calendar is lying about everything before that. Mosses and creepers have already claimed every horizontal surface with impatient, almost rude speed — the vegetation did not wait for the geology to finish. Trees that should take centuries stand full-grown on ledges cut the month before. The whole biome is a race between something that builds and something that grows, and the growing is winning.

The lushness is total and slightly wrong. Leaves too large. Green too deep, almost black at the dim edges of vision. Flowers that open facing *downward*, toward the stone, as if listening to it.

## The City of Few

At the center of the terraces stands a settlement — layered, vertical, dense with construction in the way a coral head is dense — and nearly empty. That emptiness is the place's second defining feature. The city was built, magnificently built, for hundreds of thousands. Perhaps two thousand remain. You can walk its sounding-bridges for an hour and meet no one, your footsteps swallowed by the noise, and the sensation is of being a lone corpuscle in a vast circulatory system that has decided, for its own reasons, to keep running anyway.

The architecture is organic-inflected: grown struts and poured stone and tension-cables braided from a living fiber that repairs itself. Rainwater is gathered in cistern-scale throat-pipes and sweated out again as mist. The buildings *breathe* on a slow cycle, and this contributes to the sound.

## The Technology

Every surface of the city, every terrace lip, every column of the great river-gates is inscribed with what the inhabitants politely call "maintenance." It is nanoscale infrastructure made visible only when it wants to be: sometimes at dusk (there is a dusk, a long one, the light never getting bright, the whole region living in a permanent late-afternoon amber-grey) you can see the air over the terraces *crawl* with organization, motes moving in purposeful streams, repairing the young rock before erosion can teach it anything. The land stays sharp-edged on purpose. The machines are fighting time to a standstill, and the vegetation is fighting the machines, and the people are somewhere in between, tending both.

## Life and Noise

Fauna is abundant and loud — must be. Voice is currency here; the sound-floor is so high that everything evolved to scream, thrum, or sing structurally. There are herons the size of horses that communicate by resonating their hollow crest-bones, adding a deep brass note to the chords of the place. There are swarms of small bright amphibians in the wet pockets that chorus on the offbeat. Something in the deep river-channels answers the machines' rhythm with its own, and no one has determined which side is copying which.

The noise never stops. Sleep happens in the city's sound-sheltered cores, in rooms of engineered silence so absolute that newcomers weep in them, from relief or from the sudden awareness of their own pulse.

## The Rule of This World

The one rule everyone learns: **the landscape is still being decided.** It emerged recently, it is being maintained and grown and eroded and repaired simultaneously, and nothing here — not the stone, not the silence of the empty streets, not the two thousand people — is finished. The river moves because it hasn't agreed on a bed yet. The trees grow fast because they don't trust the ground to stay. The city is loud because it is calling, constantly calling, for everyone who has not yet arrived.
