You hear it before anything else — a long way before, because the sound comes up out of the ground like a pulse through a wrist. *Kuh. Kuh. Kuh.* One knock every ten seconds, deep and unhurried, the footsteps of something enormous walking in place. The adit slopes down through rock that gets warmer as it goes. By the hundredth step the air has turned to breathing soup: wet, mineral, faintly sweet with ferment. By the two hundredth, sweat stands on your arms without running. Then the tunnel opens, and you are inside the room.

It is a chamber the size of a small church — nave, apse, dome, all of it — hollowed half by water and half by hands, and you can see the whole of it at once, which is somehow its strangest feature. Nothing here is hidden. Everything is only dim.

The light comes from four sources, none of them the sun. From the far end, where a riveted iron wellhead rises out of the floor like a buried helmet, its seams glow dull cherry — the hot rock below shows through the casing like coal through a grate. The workers must have called it something; the moss has taken the sign down. Call it the Ember Mouth. Around the walls, shelves of fungus give off a pale green foxfire, enough to read by if you are patient. There is a gauge glass or two still lit faintly from behind by heat. And on the catwalk, hanging from a rubber-sheathed wire gone stiff as root, one carbon-filament bulb burns a soft orange that has outlived its makers, its patent, and possibly the language it was ordered in.

Spanning the ceiling like the skeleton of a grasshopper is the engine. A beam engine, columned in cast iron, brass rods gone velvet with oxide, a flywheel the size of a dinner table turning exactly once per stroke. It was built to lift water — seepage from the flooded levels below, up and out through a discharge main — and it still lifts water, though the discharge pipe rusted through generations ago and now spills its cargo into stone launders that terrace down through the chamber, feeding everything. It is a pump that became a gardener by accident, and the garden organized itself around the accident without ever knowing it was one.

On the cylinder housing, stenciled in a hand that expected to be obeyed: **LOWER ADIT — PUMP No. 3 — DO NOT STOP ENGINE.** No one ever did. There was no one left to.

The tempo is the first thing the body learns, because the tempo is everything. The engine would run faster than this. There is a fern grown into the governor's linkage — a single wedge of green in the brass balls' orbit — and the engine has accepted the fern, and the fern has accepted the engine, and over what must be decades they negotiated a speed slower than either would choose alone. Six strokes a minute. The flywheel's shadow rolls across the garden with each turn, a slow dark sun making its rounds. And in the fern banks, in the fig's leaves, in the mosses on the launders, you can see the whole green population lift slightly at every stroke, as if the garden breathes on the engine's count. Perhaps it does. It has had a century to learn the rhythm, and nothing else to learn instead.

Because this is the other thing: it rains indoors. Steam comes off the wellhead and the exhaust, climbs to the domed ceiling, and finds the cold rock above. It beads there — the whole dome hung with condensation like a sky of slow stars — swells, and lets go. Different zones of ceiling shed at different intervals, so the chamber has weather, and the weather has names, or had them. There is a broad sheet of rain that falls over the fern bank every hour or so — the tally on the old chalkboard calls it *the Ten o'Clock*. There is a thin muttering drizzle that haunts the doorway. There is a corner above the fig that never rains at all, where the moss has gone gold and papery like a held breath. It is always about to rain somewhere in this room. The water moves in a ring the size of a church: up as steam, across as mist, down as rain, home to the condenser pool, and lifted again by iron. Weather here is not a vast thing happening to everyone. It is local, punctual, and personal.

And everything grows toward the warmth. The hearthferns cant their fronds toward the Ember Mouth the way field plants track the sun, because in this world the wellhead *is* the sun, and the flowers that opened under it made themselves ember-colored, deep rusts and smoky ambers, tuned to the only light there was. The steam orchids sit in clusters around the exhaust vents, buds clenched for days, and when the valve pops and scalds the air, they open all at once with a sound like soft applause, and scatter seed into the mist. The steam sows them. The rain does the rest of the gardening: the funnel-flowers are built like gutters, shaped to catch a falling drop and wash their own pollen down to their neighbors' stamens. The rain is the only pollinator. No bee ever came down here; the flowers never evolved for wings or eyes. What beauty they hold, they hold for no one — or, stranger and gentler, for a glow in a pipe.

The lushness is total and unbroken, because nothing eats it. Moss stands knee-deep in banks, unpruned for generations. Fungal shelves stack the catwalk rails like wet parchment. Watercress ropes the pool in green lianas. A single fig — the only tree, old as the engine's freedom — grips the beam frame in aerial roots and holds the iron the way the iron holds the rock. Every surface has been claimed several times over, each layer shading the last, a slow war of posture fought over centuries with no casualties and no victors, since the only apex predator in this room is silence.

That silence is the thing you keep reaching for. Between strokes, under the hiss, the ear hunts for insect sound — a click, a tick of legs, wings — and closes on nothing. No flies. No spiders' lines. No bird, ever, no scratch of claw in the dark above. The workers noticed it too; the chalkboard says so.

*Day 15,892. Knocking answered again from Level 6. Water sweet. No flies since the third year. — E.V.*

Later hands added tallies beside it, five-barred gates by the hundred. Then nothing. The mug on the shelf has been cemented there by moss. There is a hollow in the bank, boot-shaped, that nobody has stood in for a long time. The steps are dished smooth as river stone.

And the knocking: on the exhaust stroke — sometimes, not always — something knocks back from the flooded levels below. Water hammer, surely. A trapped bubble in a dead shaft. The dark under this floor is all water, and water in iron has a hundred voices. But the tally kept count, and the count says it has answered for as long as anyone was listening.

The rules of the place are not posted anywhere; they are simply true. Heat is the currency, and everything here spends it or banks it. The chamber keeps two clocks, one iron and one water, which agree to within a minute a decade. The bearing under the flywheel wears down about a hair a year, which is the fastest thing that happens. Nothing here is in a hurry — not because haste is forbidden, but because it has been tried, by the makers of the stencils, and what they left behind ran out of hurry before it ran out of steam. The garden built its year around the engine, and the engine built its rest into the garden, and neither is in charge. The room has settled into itself like a stone settles into a riverbed: not at peace exactly, but in balance, which is a different and more durable thing.

Stand in the fern bank when the hour comes. The stroke falls; the leaves lift; somewhere overhead a thousand heavy beads let go at once, and the Ten o'Clock comes down through the foxfire in silver ropes, and the orchids open for it, and the fig drinks with its roots in the air. *Kuh.* Steam rises. The room exhales.
