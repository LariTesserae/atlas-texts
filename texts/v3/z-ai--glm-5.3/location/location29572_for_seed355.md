# The Weeping Basin

There is a city at the bottom of a low country, and the city is always in the act of becoming water.

Let me place you first on the ridge above it — the only high ground for a long walk in any direction, a spine of red-gray stone no taller than a house, warm underfoot even at what passes for night here. Below you the land opens: a basin, broad as a slow morning's walk across, floored with the city the way a puddle is floored with sky. The darkness is not absolute — that's the first thing your eyes negotiate with. There is a sourceless dimness, a gray-gold ambient light with no identifiable origin, as if the air itself is barely luminous, or as if the light is something the wet surfaces remember and are giving back late. It is enough to see by. It is never enough to see *well*.

The heat comes up from below. Not from any sun — the sky is a low, featureless darkness, sometimes moving, never clearing. The heat is the ground's own. The stone of the basin breathes warm air upward, and the water that saturates everything drinks that heat and gives it back as steam, so the whole city sits under a slow, shallow dome of haze. In the open stretches between districts you can see the steam organize itself into soft vertical columns and then, over minutes or hours, watch those columns lean, drift, and condense against the flanks of buildings, running down the walls in long vertical streaks. Every wall weeps. Every wall has always wept.

## The Form of the Place

The city is built — unmistakably built, and built by hands, and built in layers. The lowest courses are massive: fitted blocks of a stone nobody local could name, dark and slick, laid in the manner of people who expected the water and made their peace with it. Canals run between districts, straight where they can be, patient where they can't. There are cisterns, aqueduct-stubs, stepped ghats descending into the standing water, arcades and terraces climbing the low hummocks. Above the ancient courses, later construction — smaller, more hurried, in brick and a pale plastery stone that has gone soft at the edges. Above that, later still, structures of rammed earth and timber that the wetness has half-swallowed. Three or four cities stacked, each one a retreat upward from the water that was always rising or always falling; the record is ambiguous, and the ambiguity is structural.

But here is the thing you notice by the second hour, and never stop noticing: the layers do not agree with themselves. A stairwell of the second city ends in a doorway of the first, but the doorway is on the *ceiling*. A canal passes under an arch and comes out running three feet higher than it went in. A colonnade has nine columns from one direction and eleven from the other, and both counts are correct; the discrepancy is not an illusion, it is a property. Somewhere in the deep past of this place the rules of geometry loosened — the way soil loosens, the way a cliff-face loosens — and then, crucially, they *settled*. This is not a place where reality is breaking. It is a place where reality broke a very long time ago, and the break has weathered. The impossible angles have been worn smooth by attention and by rain. The inconsistent rooms have furnished themselves with the habit of being inconsistent, reliably, every time. The city's physics are established, like its erosion. Everything wrong here has been wrong long enough to develop rules.

You learn the rules quickly, because the rules are patient. Do not rely on the count of anything. Trust direction-of-slope over direction-of-flow when the water disagrees with the ground. If a corridor is longer walking out than walking in, that is the corridor's nature; do not test it twice with an eye on your footsteps, because watching makes it self-conscious, and self-conscious corridors change. There is a class of rooms that are larger inside than outside, and the locals-of-nobody — there are no locals, we'll come to that — would have called them generous, and the generous rooms are the safest places in the basin, because whatever else happens here, it seems to prefer to happen in the honest rooms.

## The Water, Which Is the Main Inhabitant

Water is near the surface everywhere. In the open ground between the districts — and the city is a loose city, a sparse city, its clusters separated by broad flats of dark mud and standing pools — you can see the water table as a bright seam an inch or two under the mud's skin, trembling when you step. Rain falls sometimes, or what is nearly rain: the dome of steam condenses at its apex and falls back down as warm, heavy, sweet-smelling droplets, so the rain never comes from far away. It comes from the city's own breath. The whole basin is a slow fountain: water rises out of the warm ground, becomes haze, becomes rain, returns. It is a closed and gentle cycle and it never stops, and this is most of what "dynamic" means here — not the chase of predator and prey but the patient circulation of one substance through every state it owns.

The canals flow. Some flow downhill. A few flow in loops with no source and no mouth, turning like mill-races with nothing to grind. The loop-canals murmur — this is one of the few sounds the basin makes. The other sounds are the dripping, which is a percussion section with more players than you can count; the deep soft groan of stone expanding under heat; and, rarely, a long exhalation of air from somewhere beneath the streets, the basin clearing its throat. That is all. No birds — there are no birds, no insects, no fish, no animals at all. In the canal water, small lights drift: not creatures, or at least, not creatures the way your word "creature" works. Warm motes of glow, soft-gold, moving against the current with the unhurried certainty of things on errands. They do not interact with anything. They seem to be a property of the water, the way the luminosity of the air is a property of the air. Some of them are beautiful. One or two of them are, if you look too long, faces.

## The Sparse Green

Vegetation survives in the seams, and only in the seams. Between the flagstones of the old processional way, a pale rush grows in straight lines, as if planted, and it seeds a fine white fluff that the steam lifts and carries for a few feet and then sets down again — you watch the same seed travel ten feet in an hour, in patient stages, and the patience of it is somehow the most moving thing in the basin. A single species of broad-leaved stalk grows in the shallow pools with leaves like wet leather, hot to the touch. Nothing grazes them. Nothing blooms. There are no flowers here — flowers are a technology for talking to animals, and there are no animals to talk to. What grows here grows for the water alone, in a silence that is not empty but *unaddressed*.

## Living in It, If You Did

Suppose you had to stay. You'd take one of the generous rooms above the canal-loop in the third district, where the murmur of the water comes through the wall like a sleeper's breathing. You'd learn the drips by name — the fast one, the two slow ones, the irregular one that means the steam is shifting overhead and warm rain by morning. You'd notice that the city, for all its broken geometry, keeps itself: the water never floods the upper floors, the stairs that go nowhere are stable in their going-nowhere, the weeping walls never weep so hard as to be unkind. This is the deepest fact of the basin, the one that takes longest to see: the catastrophe here is *old enough to have become a climate*. Whatever happened — whatever bent the angles and dissolved the animals and taught the light to come from nowhere — happened beyond the reach of memory, and since then everything has simply been weathering into a livable shape, the way a fallen log weathers into a home for moss. The basin is not hostile. It is a place where reality sustained an injury in deep time, and the injury healed wrong, and the healed-wrong version works, and has worked for so long that "wrong" has quietly lost its meaning.

At the far edge of the basin, the ground rises a little and the steam thins and the city stops mid-wall — a wall that simply ends, its courses trailing off into mud, as if the builders ran out of city, or out of world. Beyond it, dark flats stretch away into that sourceless half-light until seeing gives out. You stand there a while on your last evening, warm rain beginning, each drop loud on your shoulders, and you understand that the basin does not end so much as fade, and that the fading is also a kind of structure — the outermost layer of all those layers, the one made of distance itself.
