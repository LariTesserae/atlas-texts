The plateau has finished.

Other landscapes are drafts — coasts mid-argument, ranges still shouting themselves upward — but this upland settled its accounts an epoch ago and has lain still ever since, two kilometres above the sea, flat to the limit of what flat means. There are no horizons here in the plural. There is one horizon, a perfect circle visible entire from any side, so exact it looks ruled. Wind crosses the plain without being slowed, and without being interested. The rock underfoot is the same warm, fine-grained gray for a hundred kilometers, worn past even the memory of features — not bare, exactly; *finished*. Erosion here is not a process. It is a completed work.

The days are warm and clean and long. The air, at this height, is thin enough that distance stops meaning anything: things are either near or far, with nothing in between to measure by. Rain remembers this place once or twice a year, punishes no one, and forgets it again. What grows up top — whiskers of grass, low silver shrubs with leaves like filed metal — grows in a wide, courteous scatter, each plant keeping distance from its neighbors as if at a formal gathering that has gone on too long.

You can walk a day on this plain and see nothing change but your own shadow. Then you hear it.

Sound first. That is the rule of approach, and no one believes it the first time: you hear the place four kilometers before you see anything at all. A low, wide, steady noise standing upright in the empty — surf, unmistakably surf, in a world with no sea in any direction. But it doesn't swell and die the way surf does. It holds. As you close the sound thickens, stratifies, and from far out you can begin to pick out the layers: a broadband shimmer, a ticking like rain that never lands, scattered whistles and clicks, and underneath everything a hum you feel in the sternum before you hear it with your ears. And still you see nothing. The plain lies flat and gray and total, and the noise pours up out of it like heat out of a hatch.

Then the land dimples. A collar of slabs rises to meet you — ankle height, then knee height — big fitted paving stones, shaped, furred with lichen, pushed up in a low ring around a sink in the earth. The sink is perhaps a hundred and fifty meters across and forty deep, a green pupil in the flat gray face of the world. From the rim, looking down, you understand what all the noise is for, and for a while the plain you crossed stops existing.

The pit is full of life the way a bell is full of sound: entirely, and on purpose.

Going down is going through weather. The rim's warmth becomes greenhouse warmth; the plain's dryness becomes a damp you can taste; silence becomes pressure. The walls of the sink are the same gray stone as everything else, but here the stone sweats — a dark sheen of condensation forming high on the north face, tracking the shade like a tide mark. The air smells of warm rock, wet dust, green rot, and something mineral-sweet underneath, like water that has been through metal.
