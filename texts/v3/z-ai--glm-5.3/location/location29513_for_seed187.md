You hear it ten minutes before you see it. From the ridgeline, walking down into the low country, the sound arrives ahead of everything else — a sustained industrial chord, deep and layered, the idling of some enormous engine. Except nothing here was ever built. The sound comes out of the ground, out of the basin that opens below you like a cupped hand, and it does not fluctuate as you approach. It is not machinery warming up or winding down. It is machinery that has arrived.

The basin is the lowest land for miles, and the air knows it. Cool air slides down the slopes every evening and pools here the way water would, if there were water. There mostly isn't — the uplands around are dry, tan and crackling, and the dampness begins only where the sound begins, at a boundary you can walk across like a shoreline. Inside that line, everything is green. Everything is one green thing.

What fills the basin, from rim to rim, is a single plant. That word does almost no work here, but no other word does better. It has the mass of a hill and the organization of a mill: a vast trunk-body sunk into the center of the basin, buttress roots splayed out around it like the feet of cooling towers, the whole crown storm-flattened centuries ago into a plateau with the evenness of something machined. Its runners pave the basin floor entirely — there is no bare soil, no stone, nothing mineral you can put a hand on. The ground is alive and taut, and when you step, it springs back. Somewhere far back in time this was a fig, or something the figs will one day descend from. It has been settling into its final form since before the oaks uphill were acorns. The last change it permitted itself was to stop changing.
