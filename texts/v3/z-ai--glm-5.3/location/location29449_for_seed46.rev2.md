There is a seam in the high backlands where the world was poured and cooled too fast. The host country around it is old — a plateau of sun-cracked argillite, hot enough at noon to make the horizon swim, so dry that even the lichen has given up and become mineral. Two kilometers of that ancient flat, and then a shadow in the rock, two hands wide, breathing out. The draft from it is warm. It smells of hot iron, and — impossibly, in a place that has forgotten rain — faintly of petrichor.

You can pass the seam a thousand times without seeing it. If you enter it, you will not remember the squeeze. Memory declines to record the dimensions of the passage, and that refusal is the first fact about where you are going.

Inside is a casting void. The world was cast, once, like a bell or an ingot, and this pocket is a bubble that failed to collapse — an inclusion sealed in the pour, still warm from it, and still working. The light comes on when you arrive, or perhaps it was already on: an even, mild, overcast-afternoon light, shadowless in the way of a well-lit workshop, with no sun anywhere to point at. Your eyes will report a pleasant gray day. Your skin will report a kiln with the door left open. The heat rises from below, patient and radiant, and it never stops.

The pocket is small and it is not. You could walk its length before your tea cooled — the valley floor is maybe six hundred strides, the far wall visible the whole time — and yet descending from the entry rim to the basin below takes the toll of a real mountain. Your knees and lungs insist on the altitude. Pace the floor off and you will get a different number each day; across finished ground your count holds, across raw ground it drifts, as if the numbers get tired. The pocket deals in felt elevation, not measured. Measured, it contradicts itself, and it does not seem embarrassed about this.

The terrain is in two conditions, and the boundary between them is the most important line in the place. The finished ground is annealed: dark, ringing faintly underfoot like struck porcelain, real all the way down, hard enough to trust. The unfinished ground is something else — a pale, matte, dry floor with the look of damp foundry sand that contains no dampness at all. It takes a crisp impression and holds it. Thumbnail, boot heel, the pad of a palm: the raw floor remembers you for hours. It is warm and very slightly yielding, the way clay is yielding an hour before it isn't. This is the green stock. This is what the place is for.

Because there is industry here, though nothing was ever built. Out on the floor, widely spaced — long bare stretches between presences, so that each machine stands alone in its own weather of heat-shimmer — are the working bodies of the pocket. There is a Press: a ram of banded iron taller than the hills it makes, freestanding, unmarked, warm to lean against, motionless for years at a stretch. There is a Feeder: a lattice of dark trusses grown jointed from the rock like the hinge of some enormous bivalve, advancing fresh slabs of unbanded basalt toward the Press at about a coin's width per day. There are trimmers, cutters, a slow winch winding a cable of braided mineral fiber, survey obelisks with facets too precise for wind to have made. None of it was assembled. There are no maker's marks, no fasteners, no welds — the machines are differentiated rock, the way a crystal is rock that decided to be about something. Geology here has taken up manufacture. Tectonics, outsourced. The Press does in one stroke what a mountain range does in an epoch, and it takes nearly as long to do it: the ram resolves downward over decades, without impact, with a note felt in the sternum rather than heard, and a fold of new hill stands up out of the floor with parting lines along its flanks, steaming heat into the mild gray light.

The light is not used by anything here. That is its strangest feature and the easiest to miss. It is amenity lighting. It is the light of a workroom in which nothing is currently working — even, forgettable, sourceless, never changing. There is no night. There is no day. There are shifts instead, and you feel them as pressure: a soft subsidence in the ears, the scheduled wind arriving (a slow, ventilating breeze that crosses the floor like a trolley, carrying the resin-sweetness of the plants), and then the wind withdrawing and the silence re-texturing itself. The seam you came through exhales during shift change, a long warm sigh with that ghost of rain in it — the smell of a process that is rehearsing weather before it gets around to inventing wet.

Wet has not been invented here yet. Bring a canteen and you will watch your water lose its nerve at the threshold: it pours like fine sand, beads into warm granules that roll like dry quinoa, forgets how to be a liquid. The pocket was never issued water, so it runs on heat instead. Heat is the water table here, rising from the deep pour beneath the floor, and the vegetation taps it.

And the vegetation is lush — genuinely, darkly lush, in three widely separated stands across the floor, with long naked reaches of stock between them, so that each stand reads like a held chord between silences. The plants are setters: nine-foot spears of waxy deep green, blackening toward the base where the ground is hottest, faintly oiled, warm in the hand as a tool left in the sun. They do not drink. They fix heat directly, slowly, the way other plants fix light, and they lean toward the kiln-floor rather than the sky, ignoring the ceiling light entirely. Their roots are the finishing department. A setter drills into unfired ground with roots like rebar, and the ground cures behind it — you can date a stand by the firmness underfoot, walk from a patch that has merely set into a patch that has annealed, and feel the floor close behind the green like a wound deciding to heal. Rootlines are property lines here. Every stand owns its finished ground outright.

The understory is a matte suede-green film that grows only on cooled slag and holds any impression you give it for half a day — a moss that is mostly promise. And once a decade, each setter fruits: a single bead of clear resin, warm as a banked coal, in which one small rule of the world sits finished and sealed. Where a bead falls and is not gathered, the ground cures in a meter-wide circle and a seedling starts. That is reproduction. That is also how the pocket grows. The lushness is not decoration; it is the production line's last station.

There is no fauna. Nothing moves that you could startle, nothing watches you except the machines, and they do not watch — you are not on the bill of materials. The Press will never press you. That is not kindness; it is spec.

Sound is kept in inventory here. The quiet has a texture you learn to read: mostly a deep sub-audible hum felt in the molars, the Press's century-long note; then the small scheduled events — the Feeder's weekly clack, a bright *tik* when a resin bead drops and the silence afterward is subtly different around where it landed, like a set gem. On finished ground your footsteps ring faintly. On raw ground they make no report at all — the unfired floor hasn't learned to answer. If you speak, your words come back a half-beat late and slightly reduced, as though sampled, and the pocket files the sample somewhere.

The breakdown of reality is gentle here, and everywhere. Effects arrive on schedule rather than immediately; dropped stones fall a beat late, politely. The walls of the pocket are a horizon with a wall's heart: approach them and they are two feet away and warm; look along them and they recede into what could be a distant range, though the pocket has no room for distance. Unfired ground keeps, poorly, the shape of whatever last regarded it — stare too long and it stirs, considering your outline. Do not stand still on it longer than it takes to consider. On the north wall there is a stratum with a person-shaped void in it: a casting flaw with excellent posture, arms at the sides, patient, finished into the rock mid-era. The place offered to complete someone once, and someone accepted, or failed to decline.

The rules, as far as they can be told:

— Nothing here was built. Everything here is being made.
— Decay has not been installed. Nothing rusts, nothing rots, and your own time may or may not be charged against you; the place runs shifts, not days, and the ledger is not shown.
— Heat is the water. Drink shade. The setters spend their heat, and their stands are the coolest rooms in the pocket.
— Do not stand still on unfired ground. It will offer to finish you. Decline.
— Take nothing finished. Everything finished is a part.
— When the scheduled wind comes, put your back to the Feeder; it carries seed.

Leaving is the same seam, though from inside it is only a warm draft in the wall, negotiable in width. You go out with the sense of having toured a factory between strokes, everything mid-gesture, the ram two fingers lower than when you came. Behind you the work continues without witnesses, as it has since the pour: the Press folding hills, the Feeder feeding, the setters annealing a country one root at a time, three green stands slowly signing the floor into reality. In some distant epoch this pocket will be a mountain range with weather and wet and a sky, all of it already scheduled.

Outside, the old plateau feels different once you've seen it — finished, somehow. Done, cooled, abandoned on the shelf. You find yourself checking the horizon for parting lines, and listening for the clack. And on hot afternoons, when the wind comes off the flats carrying the smell of rain that the dry land can't account for, you know exactly which direction it's from.
