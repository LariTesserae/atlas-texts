# The Gallery in Monolith 14

You hear it from kilometers out, before the ridge even clears — a flat white roar that doesn't pause and doesn't vary, the way a river doesn't pause. It is the sound of the reservoir's first snowmelt going over the new spillway, and it has been going on for weeks without stopping. By the time you climb the works to the crest road, the sound is no longer something you hear. It is the texture of the air.

The crest road runs along the top of a wall a hundred and ten meters high and three hundred wide, poured last year in numbered blocks — monoliths — and the parapet still carries its stencils in fresh paint, MON 12, MON 13, MON 14, bright and unweathered as the day they were sprayed. To your left the lake lies in the high basin, so new it hasn't learned a color yet: a gray-green sheet at 2,190 meters, cold with snowmelt off peaks you can see. Scattered across it stand the gray crowns of drowned trees — the valley's entire forest, felled by filling, a few dozen snags that were not cleared in time and will not rot for years. That is the vegetation of this landscape. Below you, downstream, the valley floor is a kilometer of haze with a river re-forming in it, and a line of transmission towers walking away down-valley, and roads cut into the slopes in switchbacks. There is no unmade thing in the view except the sky and the far peaks, and even the peaks have their brass survey disks set in the summits, so that nothing you look at is entirely wild.

The door is on the downstream face, in Monolith 14. On the plans it is a pencil line a centimeter long. On the dam it is a steel door a meter wide, set in a sheer concrete cliff, reached down a stair tower in the abutment and then along a catwalk that hugs the face. The stencil reads G-4 · CREST DRAINAGE & PIEZOMETRY · MON 14 · AUTH. ONLY. Earplugs are required past this point, in a dispenser bolted to the frame. On the concrete sill, being rained on by the door, lies a single dry seed blown up from somewhere below. It is the complete flora of this world. It will not sprout. There is no soil here, only concrete's own pale dust, and it is too cold.

You go in.

The temperature drops four degrees in the first stride and stays there — nine degrees, forever, the temperature of the mass. The light drops further. Caged bulkhead lamps, warm-white, one every nine meters, each one a small orange pond on wet concrete with black water between lamps, and the corridor runs sixty-four meters into the body of the wall, two and a quarter meters wide, horseshoe profile, falling in a gentle 1-in-200 grade toward a sump at its blind end. Your eyes are useless between the ponds, so your hand takes the wall, and the wall is slick and cold and trembling. The whole structure is trembling. The roar does not come into this room. The room is *inside* the roar — on the right flank, three meters of new concrete away, a river's worth of melt is falling down the spillway chute, and the sound arrives through the bones of the building, through floor, wall, crown, teeth.

Everything you touch is made. There is no "behind" the wall. The floor is concrete on concrete; the world ends at the pour. And the concrete is young in ways you can read with a palm. The form-tie holes are crisp grids, edges unworn, and between them runs the printed wood grain of the formwork boards — the only trace of anything that ever grew, a texture pressed into the pour. The expansion joints still show their neoprene seals glossy as the day they were laid. The handrail's safety yellow is unchipped except for one bright freckle of first rust where the sealant missed. In an old dam the galleries grow stalactites — lime straws, centimeter by century, the dam's own indoor geology. Here there are none. Only at one joint, at eighteen meters, the very first bead of white has begun. The youngest geology on the continent is starting here, indoors, in the dark, and you can put your finger on the exact point where it started.

Count the presences in sixty-four meters. Seven lamps. Nine piezometer stands, each a chrome-rimmed gauge on a stub of pipe, three meters apart. One steel instrument cabinet, one chalkboard hung beside it, one handrail, two process pipes rising out of the floor and turning into the crown, one cast-iron grate at the blind end where the floor drain disappears. That's all. The rest is distance — long clean reaches of wet dark between one small lit object and the next, the emptiness not a failure but the point, the way a long rest is part of the music, if the music here were not entirely drowned.

Because nothing here is rock, but everything weeps as if it were. The mass is chilled through by the lake — snow, a season ago, pressed against the upstream face — and the air that drifts in the adit arrives warmer and wet with spray, and so every surface sits below its dew point and gives water back all day. The ceiling weeps in ranks. Threads run down the wall from the tie holes and gather into the cast-in channel of the floor drain, where they join a black, mirror-smooth run of water that carries each lamp back as a bent thread of orange. Drops fall from the crown through the lamplight, silver for their fifteen centimeters, and land without any sound of their own; the roar confiscates small sounds and gives back nothing. Even the instruments weep on the inside — the gauge glasses are fogged from within, the needles read through mist, and in one face a bead of condensation crawls like a slow snail toward the six o'clock position. You are the driest object in the room, and you are not dry for long. Every twenty minutes or so the floor gains a new faint tick, a tightening you feel through your soles before you understand it: the sump pump, cycling, lifting the gallery's gathered seep back down toward the river. A hidden clock, running on indoor rain.

The sound has layers if you stand still enough to feel them. The broadband white of the spill, felt in the sternum like a hand pressing. Under it, whenever Penstock 1 is drawing load, a slow deep throb in the molars and the skull hinge, the turbines down at the power house, a note you hear with your jaw. A fine buzz in the handrail, the rail's own private resonance, faithful to itself. The roar changes twice a day — dawn and dusk, when the downstream cities take their power and the gate schedule shifts, the whole monolith's pitch drops a quarter-tone, and the chart recorder's pens in the cabinet flex to write it down. No one speaks in here. You could scream and not hear yourself; the two workers who service this gallery leave their conversation in chalk on the painted board — readings, dates, two handwritings, one square and patient, one hurried, slanting up to the right. The only talk in the loudest room in the mountain is written.

The gauges and the cabinet are the room's reason. Each standpipe reads what the lake is doing inside the wall — pressure crawling along joints, water trying to get under the structure and lift it, the ancient wish of every reservoir to float its dam. The cabinet hums, three green LEDs and one amber, and a chart drum turns with slow confidence, drawing the lake's push as a line that is almost straight. This little corridor is the inner ear of a hundred-and-ten-meter body, the organ with which the thing senses the water it was made to hold, and the rules here are few and absolute: water finds every path, and this room exists to watch it finding them; cold surfaces make weather indoors; sound travels solids better than air, so the roar never enters, it simply *is*, arriving through your feet; and nothing lives where the sound is this total. No birds work the face of the dam. Nothing nests in a roar. Even the midges that will eventually find every damp corner of this planet have not found this one yet.

At dusk the gallery works unvisited. The lamps burn on wet concrete, the drops fall, the drain runs, the chalk readings age by an hour, the pens draw. A door downvalley opens and a worker checks the cabinet and leaves, hand-signing to his partner at the adit — *good, all good* — and then the room is alone again with its job, which is to notice, gram by gram, whether the made mountain is holding. The seed lies on the sill in the dark. The first bead of lime at the eighteen-meter joint thickens by a width you could not measure. This is a landscape in its first year of existing, still curing, still chemically alive, learning to hold a lake that was snow last winter.

You come back out the door at full dusk and step onto the catwalk, and the roar is out here too, unbroken, as if the wall were somewhere else entirely. Fog has risen to fill the valley floor a hundred meters down, so that the downstream world is a gray sea with the transmission towers wading in it. For the first minute you still hear everything with the roar laid over it, a phantom monolith carried in the skull, and for an hour afterward there will be a fine ringing where the roaring was. Behind you the door closes and the sound continues without a seam, as it has for weeks, as it will until the melt ends and the gates throttle down and this room begins its long quiet life of drips and, someday, straws of stone.

The room does not know you were there. It is busy being the ear of a thing that is learning, drop by drop, what it costs to hold a lake.
