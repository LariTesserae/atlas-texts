You come down to it by the Return Level, two miles of gentle grade bored through wet limestone, and the place announces itself in the wrong order: warmth first, then wet, then sound, with light arriving last of all. By the last half-mile the air has gone blood-warm and soft as a mouthful of steam, and your hand-lamp has gathered a private weather of moths around its flame. The smell arrives with them — warm chalk, wet iron, a faint sweet-sour of gutters, and under it all a bitter dryness you will later learn to recognize as wing-scale.

Then the level opens like a throat, and you are inside the Ballroom.

It is a single room, if a room can be ninety yards long and forty high, and for the fifty-three people who live in it, it is the entire world. There is no horizon here. The world ends at the ribs and the back — the walls and ceiling, in the miners' old speech — and the back is so far up that it stays lost in its own dark except where a lamp's beam crosses it and comes back full of wings. At the far end glints the Pan: a shallow lake, warm as a bath and black as tea, a subterranean marsh-edge with a shore of ochre crust where the mineral water has been drying into terraces of its own stone.

The rock is young. That is the first thing the walls tell you. Every face in the Ballroom has been cut within living memory — drill crowns still sharp-edged, no patina, no weathering — and the chamber is too new, too wet, for dust. Instead everything gleams as if it has just rained indoors. Every cut face weeps. The older walls weep less; you can date a wall by its sweat, and the driest face here is a decade old. A fresh cut starts the clock over: for weeks it runs like a cut thumb, rills threading down the raw stone, gathering in the gutters, dropping into the sumps. The Ballroom is being born from both directions at once — a stripping crew takes a handspan a month off the north rib, widening the room the slow human way, while the outfall builds its own travertine, rim by rim, and over the Pan hangs the chamber's first stalactite, a wet finger of rock twelve years in the making and not yet a handspan long.

Below this room lie the Drowned Levels: three hundred fathoms of galleries given over to hot water the year a rise cut the Hot Vein. The mine's answer is this chamber. Three great pumps stand at the low end — the crews call them the mares, Dun, Roan, and Gray — lifting the flood in slow, patient strokes and sending it out along the adit to the surface river. Part of the scalding water is flashed to steam in the Kettle before it goes, and the steam drives the engines that pump the rest. The enemy powers the machines that fight it. The hotter the flood, the harder the mares can be made to work; the mine drinks its own war.

The rule that organizes everything is written in brass: a row of bolts set into the sump plinth, and the water must stay below the second bolt. It is measured in inches a day. Every eighth hour the mains blow down and it rains indoors for six minutes — warm rain, mineral-sweet — and the children treat it as weather, which is the only weather they have.

Because of the vents and the open flood, the air never dries. You stop drying; that is the first thing the Ballroom takes from you. Sweat stands on you permanently, like the condensation standing on the walls. Laundry steams on racks beside the vent mouths — the only dry clothes in town are clothes in the act of becoming dry. The bathhouse, the Tubs, is heated for nothing by the flood it drains. Stews are lowered in a wire basket down the Mother Borehole and fetched back cooked; eggs in seven minutes. Near the Kettle the riveted iron is too hot to rest a palm on. High above, the cool young back condenses the room's whole breath, and it spits — a tick, then a fat drop on the neck. You learn not to look up when you hear the tick.

The town occupies terraces cut into the floor. Nothing here sits on bare ground: the water table lies a foot below, so every building stands on a plinth like a wagon fording a stream, streets run on laid rock, and digging anywhere deeper than the clay skin brings seepage within the hour. The houses are brick and riveted iron sheeting, tarred canvas, oak lintels, and they stand far apart — chessmen in an early game. You can stand in the middle of Ballroom and see all of it with room to spare. Streets that aren't there yet are staked out with surveyor's twine at ankle height; a lamppost wears no lamp; a gable stops mid-course, its mortar still pale. The census is kept in chalk on the bathhouse wall — fifty-three souls — and changed, when it changes, without comment.

No one is buried here. The water is too close; graves would fill like cups. The dead are shelved in oak-doored niches cut into the ribs, dry and high, which is the only way Ballroom keeps anything: not in the ground, but above it.

Dark is the default. Light is a civic service, like drainage, and it is a dim one — amber globes on iron posts, gauge-lamps in the pump hall, a red bulb burning somewhere always. The lamps are dim partly by charter, because bright light draws the swarm, and partly because the swarm is forever laying darkness down: the glass frosts over with wing-scale faster than the lampwright can clean it, so every light in town is dimmed a little by insects alone.

And every lamp, since it is the only sun this world has, grows its own weather of green. Collars of moss and alga rim each globe — the lampwrights call it lampenflora and weed it like a civic garden, trimming the halos so the light isn't eaten. The canteen keeps a pot of mint in the spill of the nearest lamp. That pot is the whole of Ballroom's agriculture.

Now the truth of the place, which takes a newcomer a day to stop flinching at: the Ballroom is almost empty and completely full. The emptiness is of things — fifty-three people, three mares, a Kettle, an Ox of a ventilation fan, a fan's worth of buildings strung wide across a wet floor. The fullness is everything else.

Over each lamp stands a column of midges like smoke that has decided to stay. Moths come down the air shafts — the Nostrils — in numbers that have to be seen as weather, and tick against the lamp glass like sleet; beneath every globe the floor is a drift of shed wings a finger deep. What you first take for dust moving across the ground moves against the draft, and is springtails, living ash in drifts. The ribs are furred with cave crickets that flick aside as you pass. The gutters breathe with drain-flies, small and dark as punctuation. The sumps look rusted until you look closely and see that the red is larvae — bloodworms in films dense enough to color standing water. The vent mouths drip pale, warm, microbial strings that the crickets graze like cattle. And at dim-time, when the lamps come slowly down, the roof exhales bats.

There are rules for all of it, and everyone keeps them:

- Keep the water below the second bolt.
- Never dig past the clay skin. The ground underfoot is a lid, not a floor.
- Dim slowly. Sudden dark throws the swarm against faces and food.
- Brush the screens twice a shift; the Ox breathes through them, and the Ox chokes on wings.
- Eat covered. Cover what's yours.
- Mind the twine; the future streets are strung at ankle height.
- The dead go to the niches. Nothing is put in the ground here.

So the people eat with lids on, drink with their eyes on the rim, wear veils indoors like beekeepers at a wedding, and breathe shallow. Every machine intake wears a veil too, like the women, like the bread. The screen-women sweep the fan-housings twice a shift and fill a forty-pound pan a day, and the mine, of all things, weighs the wings and keeps the record. The flies make a generation in a week; the town raises a wall in a year. Everything in Ballroom is slow except the flies, and the flies are the town's weather.

It is loud here, but as a fullness, not a roar — a floor of sound that everything else happens on top of. The mares beat once every two seconds, and the echo comes back off the far rib half a breath later, and that echo is how you know the size of the room with your eyes shut. Drips tick and patter from the back. Moth-wings tick on glass. There is a hum you would swear is the transformers, except that it survives the shutdown of every fan in the place: it is wings. The locals speak in the gaps between strokes, unconsciously, sentence-timed to the pumps; an outsider talks over the beat and sounds like it.

Time moves in only one register here, and it is slow. In a day the water rises or falls an inch, and the lights ease down an hour at a time through dim-time like a child lowered into bed. In a month the stripping crew takes its handspan off the north rib, the fly-count peaks and halves, and the Nostrils stream with fresh moths. In a year the town raises a house, the stalactite grows a thumbnail, and the second bolt is re-set a finger's width higher on the plinth, which is what passes for a triumph. In a generation the driest wall dries a little more, and children born in Ballroom hear the sky described and file it with other folklore.

Give the Ballroom a hundred years and it will be a proper underground city — streets finished, lampposts lit, walls gone dry as old chalk. Or give it a hundred years and the Deep Wet will have climbed a bolt, then two. Both futures are kept in the same ledger, in the same careful inches-per-day hand.

At dim-time the lamps come down and the swarm settles out of the air like weather clearing. Mist pools at the Nostril mouths where the cool shaft-air leans into the warm room. The bats funnel out to hunt the Nostril-dark, the roof empties of everything but dark, the mares beat, and the water stands a hair below the second bolt. The youngest room in the mine goes on becoming itself — wet, warm, loud, almost empty, and completely full.
