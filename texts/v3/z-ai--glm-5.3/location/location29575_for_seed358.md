# The Nursery Works

You descend through a shaft-house of corrugated tin and riveted iron, down a ladder cage that drops through forty meters of dripping clay and limestone, and the sound reaches you before the place does. Even at the top of the shaft it is a pressure on the ears — a sustained, layered roar, like standing inside a waterfall and a factory and a train tunnel all at once. By the time you reach the bottom, conversation is impossible. Workers here communicate by lamp signals and by hands shaped slowly in front of each other's faces. Some of the older crews have gone half-deaf and prefer it that way; they say the silence above ground frightens them now.

You step out onto a wooden catwalk and the place opens — and it keeps opening, farther than the light can follow. This is the strange first fact of the Nursery: it is *below* everything, sunk under a marsh that pockmarks the surface with reed-choked ponds, and yet it is *open*, vast, a regional darkness the size of a small valley lying on its side. The cavern is young. That is the second strange fact, and the reason anyone is here at all.

## The Geometry of Something Unfinished

The cave is not yet a cave. It is the wet idea of one — a half-carved vault that the water is still writing. The marsh above is warm, and its water sinks through the limestone in a thousand slow places, and here below, the stone sweats in return. Every surface films with condensation. The walls are tender, geologically speaking: soft chalk and young breccia, crumbling under a thumbnail, shot through with unhealed fissures that weep threads of water so continuous they look like glass rods. In some chambers the ceiling has not decided what it is yet — it hangs in curtains of wet clay and gravel, sagging like cloth, held up by the constant grip of the water itself and by iron rods the crews have driven upward like pins in a dress.

Everything is packed. Where the stone is not actively falling, it is crowded with what is growing or what has been installed. Stalactite embryos cluster like candle wax, thousands of them, many no thicker than a finger, the whole ceiling furred with beginnings. Between them run the pipes.

## The Works

The Works — that's the local name for the operation — exist to *hold the cave open faster than the cave can fill itself in.* Pumps the size of locomotive boilers sit on gravel islands, in ranks, their intake hoses sunk into sump-pools of gray water. The noise is mostly theirs: a bass thrum that lives in your sternum, punctuated every few seconds by the slam of a check valve, the shriek of a piston, the deep gong-note of water hammer traveling through a kilometer of iron. Add to that the falls — the cave's own voice, the water dropping from the ceiling fissures into black pools — and the echo. Everything echoes. The cavern is shaped wrong for quiet. Sound folds back on itself and stacks.

The crews run the pumps, shore the sagging ceilings, drill weep-holes to relieve pressure before a wall bursts, and mark the cave's growth with chalk lines and survey stakes. Their stations are scattered throughout the vault — a dewatering platform here, a compressor shed there, a rack of standing lamps in a niche, an old sinkhole-shaft with a rusted headframe where materials come down. Each station is an island of lamplight in the dark, and between the stations the cave is left to itself, which is to say: left to the animals.

## The Tenants

Because the third strange fact of the Nursery is that life got here first. Fauna is abundant — absurdly so for a cave, and the surveyors have stopped trying to explain it. Blind white isopods cloud the film of water on the walls, so dense in places that the rock looks furred and pale, and they crunch faintly underfoot where the catwalk ends. The sump-pools boil with pale amphipods drawn to the warmth of the pump outflows. Fish — eyeless, translucent, their organs visible like a diagram of themselves — hang in the quiet water below the falls, in schools that part around a dropped bolt and close again without hurry.

And the bats. Colonies of them pour out through the sinkholes at dusk and pour back before dawn, and their sound, folded into the machinery's roar, is a high grain of shrieking that you feel behind the eyes. The guano accumulates in cones on the gravel banks, and on the guano grows a fungal mat pale as candle-tallow, and in the mat live mites, and in the mites live the smallest of the cave's uncounted tenants. The place is a food web stacked vertically in the dark, every trophic layer compressed into arm's reach of every other. Nothing here has room. Nothing here seems to mind.

## The Light

There is almost none, and what there is, is industrial. Carbide lamps and electric bulbs strung on rubber-sheathed wire, yellow and low, each one wearing a halo of mist. The condensation is so constant that the air itself gleams faintly wherever light touches it — you see the beams as much as you see what they land on. Deep in the cave, where the Works haven't reached, there are chambers no light has ever entered except by lantern, and the crews report that in one of them the ceiling drips have built a single white column, thin as a wrist, growing at a pace you could measure in a human lifetime with a ruler. They have not measured it. It feels wrong to.

## The Time

Everything here is slow, and the slowness is the point. The pumps run day and night but they are fighting a process with a million-year head start. The chalk survey lines creep down the walls — a centimeter a month of new dissolution, new room, new ceiling weight redistributed. The cave is being born and excavated simultaneously, built and un-built, and the Works are less a factory than a midwifery: hold the walls, drain the birth-waters, keep the opening open.

Stand on the catwalk at the end of a shift, lamp turned low, and you feel the whole place breathing at its own pace. Warm mist condenses on your face. The roar is so total it becomes a kind of silence, a floor beneath which nothing quieter can be heard. Water shapes the stone. Rods hold the clay. The isopods graze the wet. And somewhere out in the dark, past the farthest lamplight, a stalactite adds an invisible mineral thread to its own length — the cave, patiently, becoming a cave.
