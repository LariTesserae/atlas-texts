You come down into the country by ramps. Not ladders, not shafts — ramps, wide and shallow, cut into the rock in some century nobody remembers, worn in their middle into a smooth trough by an unimaginable tonnage of feet. The air comes up to meet you and it is cool, cellar-cool, and it smells of chalk and of rain that has just fallen on stone, though it has not rained yet and may not for days. That smell is permanent here, the way salt is permanent at a coast.

And the light comes up at you. This is the first wrongness, the one you have to make peace with before anything else will make sense. You descend, and it gets brighter. Not the faint foxfire people imagine when they imagine underground — a real light, diffuse, sourceless at first, then organized. When the ramp finally opens out, you are standing under a sky.

It is not a sky. It is the vault: a ceiling of rock a thousand meters up — cloud-height, exactly cloud-height, which is why the eye accepts the lie — and the rock is farmed, edge to edge, by a lichen that has never needed a sun. It eats the chemistry of the stone and pays the debt back as light, a pale white light, brighter than a north-country noon. There
