Call it the Highwater — a sea that lives at altitude, held in the cup of a mountain range the way water is held in a bowl, three thousand meters above any coast. There is no shore here the way a lowland sea has a shore. The water simply ends against stone, all the way around, a cold sweetwater ocean lapping the roots of peaks that make a grey wall of the horizon.

And above it there is no sky. There is rain.

The rain is permanent — not an event but a medium, a second ocean turned upside down. From below, the surface is a ceiling of falling water, a drum-skin several fathoms overhead that never once goes quiet. The whole sea listens to it constantly: a huge soft static, the sound of a million million drops striking a skin you could almost touch. If you are here, you learn the place by ear before you ever learn it by eye, because the eye is given almost nothing.

The water is cold — a breath above freezing, the kind of cold that feels like stillness, like the patience of things. It is layered like a mood. Down to thirty meters it is pewter, faintly green, lit by nothing but the grey rumor of the storm above. Below that it is slate. Below that, ink, with sparks. Those sparks are the only steady light in the world: lantern-kelp, whose every blade ends in a small cold lamp, a blue-green coal that neither flickers nor warms. A grove of it stands lit like a harbor with no ships.

When lightning walks the storm — and it walks often — the whole column fills with white for a heartbeat, and in that flash you see how far the country goes: walls, cairn-lines, groves, shoals, leagues of drowned plain all at once, a photographic negative of a grassland. Then dark again. Then you doubt what you saw.

The floor of the sea is a steppe. It was one before the water came, generations ago, when the rain began and did not stop, and the water climbed the passes year by year while the people of the plateau walked their herds up and out and did not come back. Now their land lies under fathoms of sweet cold water, and the water has been here so long that everything has finished falling apart. That is important. Nothing here is ruined; ruin is recent. The field walls have been rounded into the water's own idea of walls, shoulder-high, patient, furred with algae at their joints. The cairn-lines that once walked the herding routes from village to village still walk them, stone by grey stone, across distances too big for the eye. The silt lies on the old grassland like flour on a table, and the shoal-hills — the old gravel rises, the old riverbeds — show through it as lanes and ridges. It is a settled place. Established. Whatever erosion still happens here happens at the pace of the rocks' own choosing.

And it is green. It is astonishingly green, in a world with no sun to speak of.

The meadows came back after the flood — cold-water grasses, glassy and dark, growing in beds that cover whole townships of the drowned plain, bending together when the current leans on them with a long soughing sound, like surf heard through a wall. The groves are the great ones: false-kelp with hollow stems, bladder-bell plants drifting at the surface, and the lantern-kelp, standing in its dim harbors. The lushness of this place is not spread evenly. It gathers. Nowhere does it gather more than at the Hot Groves, where warm water sieves up through the old river gravel from some furnace under the plateau — small country, loud with bubbles, lit like a feast, where the kelp forgets it is winter and grows a hand's width a day, and the leaves come wide as doors. These groves are few and far between. Between them: the vast cold openness, the silt plains, the long patience of grey water. Lushness and emptiness in balance, groves like islands, islands like punctuation.

Now the strangeness, which is the first law of the place, though nothing wrote it down:

There are no animals here. None. Not one. No fish in the meadows, no insects in the kelp, no birds — there is nowhere for a bird to land; even the tower-islet stands bare. Nothing with blood can stay in this water; that is simply true, the way gravity is true, and no one is left to ask why. In all this wet green, not one mouth. And so the plants have taken the vacancies. The meadows eat their own dead through the roots — the floor is a single slow stomach of grass, and nothing rots loose, and there are no scavengers because there is nothing to scavenge. The seeds have taken the fish's job: small buoyant pods with thread-fins that rise from the meadows in their thousands and school upward, wheeling, turning as one body, like fish that decided to be plants just a little longer. They drift to the rain-ceiling, touch it, and sink back down. No eyes anywhere look at them do it. If you are here, yours are the only eyes in the country, and the country does not know what to do about that.

Because sound carries so far in cold water, and because nothing alive makes any, the place is loud with its own geology and architecture.

The rain-ceiling never stops its drumming. The earth exhales in columns through the old riverbeds, and each bubble grows as it climbs so that its note falls as it rises — the gas fields sound like long slow sighs played backward, fluting for hours without pause. The bladder-bells knock together at the surface, chiming through the rain-noise, muffled and far. The small bergs come down from the glacial inlets on the mountain wall, clear as windows, and ground themselves on the shoal-hills and grind, and the grinding is this world's idea of thunder.

And then there is the village.

The people of the plateau built with granite and slate and clay, and one of their villages sits drowned in forty feet of water with its walls still hip-high, its doorways still doorways. The current leans into it, and the village plays. Doorways become flue-pipes. Chimneys become reeds. Window-holes are tuned by depth. The drowned houses have been an organ for generations now, and the storm above is the organist: when the rain deepens, the chord deepens; when the wind turns, the piece modulates, one sustained tone sliding into another. You can hear it a league away across the silt plain. At storm-spate it is the loudest thing in the Highwater, a vast held chord with the rain-drum under it, and the seed-schools rise into it and hang there, listening, if a seed can be said to listen.

Close by, the remains give themselves up slowly. A ground-slate knife in the silt, its edge still keen, because nothing here has ever needed to cut and nothing dulls it. Clay whorls. Stakes of alder from the old fishing weirs, black and sound, wood kept honest by the cold and the dark. A shrine knoll with a circle of horn piles — the herds' own horns, piled the way herders pile them — leached to chalk now, and even the chalk is going; the meadows have been drinking them for years, root by root, closing every loop.

Only one tower was too tall to drown. It stands out of the sea on a knoll near the drowned village's edge — the Horn, black stone, slick with rain, the only dry thing in the world. Wind moving across its broken mouth makes a single low horn-note that carries all the way to the deep floor, and when a squall crosses the tower the note bends. It is the closest thing the place has to a voice.

The dynamics here run on storm-moods rather than seasons. Spate, when the rain hammers and the organ plays full-chorded and the bergs calve fresh from the inlets. Sough, the long grey middles, current-driven, growth-paced, the meadows breathing out oxygen until the shallows fizz faintly with it, a static under the static. And rarely — a few times a year — the stillness: the rain fails for an hour, the whole sea-surface goes mirror-flat, the drumming stops for the only silence the country knows, and every seed in every meadow rises together to touch the calm ceiling before the weather closes again.

The laws of the Highwater, then, which you would know in your bones within a day:

Water is the air here. Green is the animal. Sound never stops, and it comes only from stone, ice, rain, and ruins. Nothing with blood may stay. Light is currency, spent mostly by plants, in small denominations. Everything here finished falling apart long ago, and what remains, remains.

On a full-spate night the country plays itself to the dark: the drum-ceiling, the sighing vents, the chord from the drowned village bending under the storm, the Horn's low note under everything like a pedal-point, the bells far off, a berg grinding somewhere on the shoals with enormous unhurried patience. In the ink below the slate below the pewter, the lantern-kelp stands in its cold harbors with its lamps lit for no one, and has for generations, and the rain goes on falling on the roof of the world.
