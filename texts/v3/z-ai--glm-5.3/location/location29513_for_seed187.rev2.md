You come to this place across the end of a long argument. The mountains here finished eroding ages ago — that is the first thing to understand. What remains is a peneplain, the landform that means an erosion story has been told all the way to the last page: relief measured in centimeters, the horizon a clean pencil line, every slope in the world already taken downhill. The air is cool and faintly mineral, and the land is damp the way a wrung cloth is damp — no streams, no lakes, just a darkening in the ground wherever you press it. You hear the works long before you see them. A broad steady roar, industrial in texture, factory-loud from fields away — except there are no fields, no roads, no factory, and the sound never swells, never fades, never rests. It has been at this volume, to the best of anyone's knowledge, since before there was anyone to know.

The works sit in a shallow dish in the plain — four hundred paces across, walled at the rim by pooled fog where the cool air settles, so the place has a boundary you can stand on. From the rim you count eleven pillars. They stand like columns in a nave, spaced so far apart that you never see more than two whole at any one time; the rest are present only as sound. Each pillar is a giant reed: hollow, segmented like stacked pipe, grey-green, the girth of a grain silo, forty or fifty meters tall, crowned with a broad umbrella of blades. And under every pillar, it is raining. A silver cylinder of falling water, ten paces wide, in air that is otherwise merely damp. Step into the ring and you stand inside a private weather. Step out and your shoulders are only misted. There are eleven weathers here, and no others.

The pillars are engines. This needs to be said plainly, because nothing else here will say it: there are no machines in this place, no artifacts of any kind — no plank, no potsherd, no tool, no fence, not a single surface that was shaped by a hand. Everything grew. And yet each pillar is an engine in every sense except the legal one. The blades overhead sweat constantly; their undersides sit below the dew point of the cool air forever, and the condensate runs to the blade-tips and drips, metered through drip-valves of hardened tissue at the base of each blade — calibrated orifices, no more designed than a pitcher plant's lip. The water descends in spiral gutters formed by the stalk's own leaf-bases, and at intervals it is simply dropped — allowed to fall free through internal shafts, forty feet, onto drum-diaphragms of membrane. The impact knocks. The knock is a pressure stroke: it shoves sap upward in pulses, the way a heart shoves blood, and between strokes the lift is done by capillarity alone, water climbing tubes so narrow that cohesion does the work of any piston. No fuel. No fire. At the crown, spent vapor exhales from vents in a soft continuous hiss — steam without heat, a cold stack breathing. The stalk walls are slick and chill to the touch, sweating like the housing of a machine that has run cool for ten thousand years. If this is a metaphor, it is one the place performs involuntarily. Industry, stripped of its artifacts, turns out to be ecology running at a fixed rate.

And the floor is where the metaphor goes to be metabolized. There is no ground here, not really — there is a mat, a meter or more of living substrate: root-felt, moss, liverwort, fungal cordage, woven tight and laid directly over the planed skull of the vanished range. It gives underfoot like cork and wrings out a few degrees of cold water at every step. It is the floor, and it is also the refinery. Everything that falls in the works — every shed blade, every insect wing, every spent beetle — is taken in and processed by the mat's resident economy of millipedes, springtails, worms, and fungal thread at exactly the rate it falls. Nothing accumulates. The composting never gets ahead and never falls behind. Underneath, the mycorrhizal mesh runs like basement conduit, trading sugar for mineral across the whole basin, a commodity exchange older than any market that ever had a building.

In the center of the dish, the mountain's last knuckle shows through. A knee-high boss of grey rock, polished first by the ages of water that ate everything else and now by snail-graze and beetle feet — the topmost atom of the range that once stood here, reduced to a threshold. It is the only mineral surface in the works. The high point of a mountain chain now used chiefly as a wren's podium. Around it, sunk into the mat, are the sumps: cold black pools the size of tables, water that tastes of iron, held in pockets of the substrate. Newts patrol them. The levels never move, because the pools are the reservoirs of a closed loop — up the pillars as vapor and capillary flow, onto the cold blades as sweat, down the gutters as metered drips, back to the sumps along shallow channels the mat has grown for the purpose. It is the only river here, and it is vertical, and it never reaches the sea. This basin sits near the bottom of the world's drainage, and the world's water mostly doesn't bother to arrive; the works run on their own sweat.

Between the pillars, the industry is mostly logistics, and the logistics are carried by animals, because animals are the only moving parts available. Ant lanes are worn into the mat like sled-ruts, traffic running conveyor-steady in both directions, hauling resin beads to swollen thorn-silos and queuing them there in little strings. Mule-beetles drag seed-castings along the ruts. Hoverflies work the flower-heads up on the umbrellas, where each head is a stamping press turning out achenes at a fixed cadence — one cast, one cast, one cast, no rush orders, no shutdowns. Beetles walk the lines. And the lines are literal: pulse-vines sag pillar to pillar in catenaries like telegraph wire, carrying slow voltages through their tissue, with galls strung along them like insulators, and each gall clicks as a pulse passes. A message takes minutes to cross the basin. The message is always the same — rates nominal — and in the whole memory of the place, no other message has ever needed to be sent. Along the dry aisles, pollen sheds off a fine milling dust that ticks with faint static where it settles, the closest thing to electricity you'll hear here. Nobody hired any of this. The roles grew into being, and the fauna are staff and are also simply fauna; both readings are correct, and the place holds them without strain.

The birds sing fixed songs. A thrush-like bird up in the blades whistles the same three descending notes its line has whistled for ten thousand generations, because nothing in this environment has ever once asked it to change.

Now stand still and take the din apart, because the sound is the place's true population. On top, the hiss of transpiration, a high continuous wash like a steam main. Under it, the sizzle of thousands of metered drips striking drum-leaves — rain on a tin roof that never stops. Under that, the clack of pump-strokes from inside the pillars, eleven of them out of phase, a rolling percussion that never resolves into rhythm because it never had anywhere to go. Then the hum — the exact steadiness of a transformer, one held chord at one pitch, which on inspection is crickets. The peeper-shrill from the sumps, needle-thin. The wren ticking from the stone. And beneath everything, whenever the steady drift of air leans across the broken open mouths of old hollow stalks, a low pedal note like the largest organ pipe in the world. It is loud the way a loom hall is loud. You feel it in the sternum and in the stone and in the mat underfoot. To speak here you would have to shout, and there is no one to shout at. Silence is not a thing this place has ever contained; the emptiest second of its existence has been at full volume.

And it is empty. That is the strange luxury of it. For all its industry, the works are mostly hollow — near-void, almost nothing per volume. The pillars stand a city block apart; between them the aisles are unclaimed air from the green of the mat to the pearl of the sky, nothing at eye level in any direction. The plant fills the space the way a loom fills a hall: with motion, not mass. Matter here is thin — a film of animal life running over a plant body — while process is enormous. You can cross the whole establishment in ten minutes. Stand inside it, and it is a cathedral, or a turbine hall: the same architecture of few columns and much air, the same upward attention, the same sense that the emptiness is the working clearance, the room the sound needs in order to live. The light helps. It is flat, shadowless, diffuse through permanent high overcast, adequate for everything and dramatic about nothing — nothing here casts a decisive shadow. Day comes up and goes down over the works and nothing adjusts; you could tell time by the light, but time isn't in use here.

The rules, if you wanted them listed:

— Everything is alive; nothing is made. There are no objects here, only organisms. What look like fixtures are all grown: columns, plumbing, roof, wiring, silos, floor.

— Every rate is fixed and every ledger closes to zero. Production equals consumption, evaporation equals condensation, births equal deaths to the individual. The ant that dies on the trail is replaced mid-stride; the procession has been passing the stone, unbroken, for ten thousand years.

— Nothing grows. This is old-growth in the absolute sense: wood laid down and dissolved at the same rate, the mat never thickening, the pillars never widening. Everything moves; nothing changes.

— The weather is a feature, not an event. Eleven rain-rings, a fog rim, the sweat on the blades — run in closed loop, without schedule.

— The sound is the sound of nothing happening, at capacity, indefinitely.

Beyond the rim the plain resumes its near-silence, and the quiet out there has a pressure to it, like the flat after a waterfall. The works are the loudest thing for a day's walk in any direction.

Erosion, they say, is the story of things being taken away. This basin is the page where that story ends. The range is gone. What it left behind was a level, a dampness, a plain light — and into those, the green moved, and organized, and organized, until there was nothing left to organize, and then it stopped organizing and simply ran. A landscape at the end of its becoming: everything settled, everything loud, nothing finished, because the works do not finish — they run. Somewhere behind you, at this moment, a valve lets go of a drop. It falls forty feet and knocks. On schedule. Which is to say: now. Which is to say: always.
