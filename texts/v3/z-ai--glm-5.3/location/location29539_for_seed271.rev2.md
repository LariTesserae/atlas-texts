You come to it across low, warm country — pale limestone lying just under a thin skin of grass, thyme, and scattered juniper, the land so nearly flat that for a long time the only thing on the horizon is the horizon itself. The air is still and smells of heated stone and dry herbs. Water is scarce enough here that green survives only in the joints of things. Which is how you find the hollow before you see it: at the end of a hot afternoon, a thread of cool, mineral-smelling air moving across your ankles, as though the ground somewhere nearby were exhaling.

Then the land simply stops being land for a while. A bowl, perhaps forty paces across and eight deep, walled in pale rock gone soft-edged with age, one side deepening into a brow of overhanging stone like the eave of an enormous, patient roof. From the rim it reads as a garden — the floor is not stone-colored but green-grey, moss and pale mineral light mixed together, a small damp brightness sunk into the tan of everything else. The sky above it is a circle, and it looks, from up here, like a held breath.

You go down a slope of rubble that has long since finished moving. Every stone sits at its angle of rest; the upper faces wear lichen, the lower faces none, because the light fails before the slope does. As you descend, the horizon is subtracted from you in pieces — first the far flatness, then the junipers, then the sky itself narrowing overhead — and sound is taken too. The small noises of the upper world lose their edges, then their locations, then their existence. By the time you reach the floor, the loudest thing in the place is you.

At the bottom the air is layered like water. It is warm at your face and cool at your ankles, and in the lowest pocket of the floor — a shallow dish of bare travertine — it is something else again: heavy, slow, faintly sharp. This is the first law of the place, and there is nothing strange in it. The water that rises here spends itself building stone, and as it builds it breathes out gases it carried up from the dark; in the stillness of the bowl, the heavy breath settles to the floor the way sediment settles in a glass. Hold your hand at ankle height in that pocket and you feel the difference on your skin. A flame carried down would duck and gutter at your knees. An animal would turn around. Nothing has ever denned here, nested here, fed here — not because the hollow forbids it, but because the floor's air does the forbidding, patiently, without malice, in the voice of chemistry. The people who came here long ago knew this without knowing why. All of their traces are on the shelf, none in the dish.

The shelf is under the overhang: a ledge of warm stone at sitting height. And on the shaded wall beside it, the source of everything — a dark, slick seam where water emerges, not as a spring exactly but as a film, skin-warm, sliding down a staircase of stone the water itself has built. Terraces of travertine, pale as bone, each lip the ghost of an old waterline, each basin the size of a cupped palm, all of them overflowing so slowly that the overflow is invisible and only the shine proves it. Where the film dries at the edges it leaves a white line. Where it gathers, it enters the floor through a slot and keeps going underground — and you can hear it, faintly, beneath your feet: a voice in the stone's basement, going on without you.

Water comes to this place three ways and leaves one way. The seep, which never varies. The sweat — each night the walls and the travertine sweat, and by dawn every moss cushion is beaded, every terrace lip holding its bright beads, and by noon it has all dried again into the dim. And rain, rarely, arriving funneled down a few established lanes off the rim, smooth-worn channels that have carried the same storms for centuries. Nothing pools here. Nothing stands. Damp without puddles — and that, together with the bad air at the bottom, quietly dismantles the food chain at every link. No standing water means no larvae; no larvae means no insects; no insects means no lizards, and nothing for a bird to come down for. The seep water is mineral and chalky, nothing anyone would drink by choice. The soil is a centimeter of moss over rock. Only the first rung of life was ever installed here — light — and so everything alive in the hollow makes its own food from it. Moss in two shades of green. Liverwort like dark wax. A dozen small ferns of a single species, each standing alone in the seep's cool draft, each separated from the others by pale stone. A greenish algal film on the shaded wall. That is the entire census. You can count the presences; the place fits inside an inventory. It is not emptiness, exactly — it is countability, everything living placed at intervals, each one located by water, held by light.

The light is dim by architecture. The mouth of the bowl faces away from the sun, so daylight arrives mostly borrowed — bounced off the pale rubble, the white terraces, the circle of sky. Shadows are soft-edged; colors are folded down one step. For a few weeks of the year, at midday, one patch of direct sun crosses the floor like a slow animal, warming whatever it touches until the moss briefly exhales; the rest of the time the light is even, milky, and uncommitted. The hollow never looks like morning. It never looks like evening. It looks like the late afternoon of a day that has decided not to end.

On the ledge, the human marks are scattered and faint, the way footprints are faint in stone. Soot fans on the ceiling above the shelf, in overlapping generations — the oldest black and glossy, the later ones brown, softer. A low dry-laid arc of stacked stones, knee-high, three-quarters of a circle, one end simply thinner than the other, not fallen but abandoned mid-intention, every stone still exactly where a hand set it. A grinding hollow worn into the shelf, polished dark. Two potsherds pressed heel-deep into the mossy soil, their broken edges rounded by damp decades. One black chert flake, come from the black chert that flecks the local limestone — the rocks here supply their own artifacts. No bones, no midden, no wall of habitation. These were visits, not residences: people came for the cool and the water, stayed on the warm shelf, and left — as anyone leaves a place whose floor cannot be slept on.

And the sound. Mostly quiet, but furnished quiet. The seep's thread. The tick of drips letting go of terrace lips at their own slow tempo. The muffled voice of water under the floor. Above, once in a long while, wind combs the grass on the rim and never comes down; you hear it the way you hear weather through a wall. The bowl does not echo — the moss and the porous stone take your voice and keep it — so the quiet is not hollow but lined, like a held breath, and the moment you stop moving, you become the least event in it.

What you notice, finally, is that the place is finished. It began as a collapse — some older cave's ceiling letting go of the sky — but that was before any witness, and since then everything here has been arriving at equilibrium rather than going anywhere. Talus at its angle. Walls at their softness. Travertine at its full height. Even the human marks are not so much decaying as being incorporated — soot darkening into stone, sherds on their way to becoming soil. Erosion still files at the rim at a pace you would need a century-long photograph to see. The only changes left are the tick of water and the nightly sweat; a hair's-breadth of new terrace a year; moss a centimeter wider per lifetime. Come back in thirty years and you could not swear anything had moved except yourself.

Those are the laws here, all of them ordinary: water arrives three ways and leaves one; air sorts itself by weight, and the shelf is where a body is permitted; light arrives only borrowed; whatever lives here must feed itself from light and stone; and nothing is on its way anywhere. At dusk the hollow exhales its cool mineral breath up over the rim, the smell of thyme slides down in exchange, the sky-circle dims from milk to ash, and the seep keeps its small time. You sit on the warm ledge — the stone holds the day's heat well into the night — and for as long as you sit still, you are the only animal in the place, and only by the ledge's permission. The moment you make no noise, the place does not resume around you. It never noticed you were there. You are a small, brief weather event in a room that water and light have been keeping, tidily, without interruption, for a very long time.
