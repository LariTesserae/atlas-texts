You arrive the way everything arrives: down the hoist, in the freight, with the freight.

Half a kilometer of stone between you and the sky. The cage smells of floss-felt and machine oil, and the whole descent is a long exhale of cold, chalk-dry air rising past you, a draught out of a cellar that has never held a drop. Your ears fill and clear. Someone in the corner of the cage adjusts their breath-mask, not against bad air, but against losing the water in their own exhale — you learn that later, and then you notice everyone wears one, and then you notice your lips have already begun to split.

The doors open at the bottom station, and the first thing that strikes you is the silence. It takes a while to understand what is wrong with it. Every silence you have ever stood in had water somewhere in it — a pipe, a gutter, a throat. This one doesn't. There is a hole in the quiet exactly the shape of dripping, and you stand in the doorway of the cage longer than you mean to, listening to that absence.

Then you look up from listening, and see the Hollow.

Eight kilometers of floor under a ceiling two hundred meters high, held up by rock pillars the borers were careful to leave standing. No sky — a roof, mottled, faintly luminous with the mineral glow of the cut faces. And scattered across the floor below, ovals of true daylight, eleven of them, each a couple hundred meters across, hard-edged, glowing like windows laid flat. These are the dayshafts: vertical bores to the surface where heliostat crews track the sun and feed it down through mirror-chains, half a kilometer of optics delivering noon to the floor of the world. Between the ovals lies the Grey — planted dark, pale ground, lit only by the freckle-glow of lamp-bark and the distant wash of the Salt Floor's reflection. The town sits inside the largest cluster of light, a settlement of grown towers and woven halls and racked cistern-barrels, and it fits entirely inside its own noon. Noon here is not a time. It is a place. You can walk to it.

You walk to it on the mat.

The entire floor of the Hollow is one living weave — root and runner and dry husk, braided tight, dry as wicker, faintly springy underfoot, warm nowhere and cool everywhere. There is no bare stone. There is no soil in the wet sense; what passes for soil is mineral flour and ground dead matter, and it rises around your boots in a fine pale dust. The plants are the substrate, the pavement, the walls, the roof. Everything here is substrate before it is anything else.

And everything here holds water, because nothing else may. That is the first law of the place, and you feel it in your cracked hands within the hour: the air takes moisture from anything left open to it. Free water does not exist. Not in pools, not in dew, not in the deepest crack of the rock. The moment moisture is loose, the air strips it. So the water of this world lives only inside living things — banked in tissue, sealed under bark and wax and hide — and the plants are the aquifer, the reservoir, the vault. You drink by tap. Licensed tappers open cistern trees with augers the size of a thumb, and the sap comes slow and clear, cool and faintly sweet, tasting of clay and cucumber. At the Tapworks hall, grown barrels rack the yields and still-water is clarified, sealed, rationed, sold in flasks that people carry the way others carry heirlooms. The trees transpire a thin trickle of their holdings upward through the shafts as their heat-engine; surface condensers catch that vapor at the shaft-heads and pipe it back down as dew. The only rain in the world is in the pipes, and it is metered.

Once you understand the water, the industry of the place falls into place around it. The Mills eat the dead. That is not rhetoric — nothing rots here. No decay, no rot, no soft return: a fallen tallowback stands where it dies, hollows slowly, mummifies into something like lacquered furniture, and would stand for a century if the law allowed it. The roots will take a buried thing over years, doing slowly what bacteria would have done in weeks, but the floor cannot wait on that rate. So the dead are claimed, registered, carted to the Mills on electric drays, ground to meal, and returned to the mat as substrate — the circle of life run on conveyors, powered by dynamos spun in the updraft of the dayshafts. Wires run on living poles. The hum of the Mills is not heard; it is felt, a low continuous tremor in the weave underfoot, like a sleeping animal the size of a town.

The people are buried standing. Feet to the roots, in the gardens at the oval edges, posts among posts. Burial here is irrigation: your water returns on foot, slowly, through the roots that drink you. The graveyards do not look like graveyards. They look like young orchards, and they are tended like them, and a family will speak of an ancestor the way farmers speak of a well.

The animals never drink either. Tallowback herds rotate through the light cells in their hundreds — low, wax-hided grazers, hides so sealed against the air that rain on the surface world would bead off them if rain ever fell there. They chew sapwood and mat-edge sprouts; they are, economically, walking canteens, and herding is understood here as water banking. At the margins of the ovals, where light meets Grey, the cell-cats wait — long, flat, utterly silent, getting their moisture the way predators must: by taking it out of other bodies. Killing, in this world, is drinking. Dusk brings the dustmoth clouds rising off the mat like smoke deciding to be alive, carrying their freight of mineral dust between flowers — because the flowers here bloom metal. The ore orchards root deliberately over copper veins, and their blossoms open as filings, bright shavings that the moths trade between trees. The town's copper is farmed, not mined. The trees eat rock; the roots excrete films of acid and chew the ore bodies by contact; the metal rises in sap and grain, and a smelter exists here only at the scale of a kitchen.

And the trees remember light the way trees elsewhere remember years. Cut a stump and read it: laminae of bright wood under bright summers, thin dark bands under the seasons a shaft's mirror-chain failed. Because the ovals drift. As the sun's angle shifts through the year, the footprints of daylight migrate a few meters, and over generations the town re-cuts its bores, chases its own noon, and the floor keeps the record — bands of pale dense growth across the Grey where daylight used to land, a fossil map of two hundred years of wandering noon, legible from the hoofback if you know how to look. No one owns light. Everyone owns light-hours; the shaft-guilds keep the ledgers, and stealing a neighbor's mirror-hour is the local crime, tried harshly, because stealing light here is stealing rain.

It is cool. That is the other thing your body learns fast. The rock holds cellar-cold, eight degrees, and never had the depth to warm — the surface world above is a cold dead desert, drier than any country on any map, a place where the sky is a thief that steals moisture out of anything that faces it. That is why the town is here at all, under stone: rock is the only wallet that keeps. The ovals warm to a gentle twenty at noon; the ground-banks under them — stone slabs the builders laid to store the day — hold that heat into the lamp-hours, and at night the herds lie down on the warm slabs in great wax heaps, and the town's lanes were grown where warmth pools. People wear floss-felt, layered, the pale seed-down of the mat-plants spun coarse.

The quiet has a culture around it. Sound carries in the Hollow as if the ceiling were collecting it, and so no one spends it. Greetings are nods. The Tapworks signals shift-changes by light, shuttering a shaft in code — a bell would be obscene. Music exists as a whisper-trade in the felt rooms off the mill-lane, voices kept under the threshold of the mat's hiss. You will be shushed by strangers, gently, the way you would be steadied on ice. What sounds there are, are small and structural: the tick-tick of stone and mat contracting at dusk as the light dies, like a vast engine after the power is cut; the dry chittering of mat-ticks working the weave; the whisper of shaft-wind when a storm passes on the surface and leaks its pressure down the bores; and at the far rim, felt in the molars more than heard, the Worm.

Go to the Cut and you see what the place is.

The frontier is raw geology — faces the borers opened last month, sharp-edged, unweathered, dust still cutting-bright; the Hollow is a natural rift the founders found seventy years ago and have been enlarging ever since, a valley in the middle of being born. The Worm advances maybe three meters a day, and behind it surveyors drive stakes for streets that do not exist yet, drawn in lines of portable arc-lamps across black rock — the Nursery glowing under its own little false noon, seedlings in felt sleeves, the whole embryo city sketched in light on the dark. New shafts take a season to drill upward through the rock, and on shaft-opening day the town walks out to the new cell and stands together in the first noon it has ever had, masks on, saying nothing, for as long as the sun holds. It is the only ceremony here, and it is silence, and it is enough.

The Hollow breathes on a schedule posted at the Tapworks. Every six hours the Ventrow maws exhale — warm mineral gas from the deep works, carbon and sulfur, and the whole floor of the world rises a few millimeters and settles again as the mat drinks the breath and the pressure doors drift on their felt hinges. Dust falls then, slow weather, a snowfall of mineral flour that the mites clear off the weave in the hours after. The weather here is gas and pressure and light and dust. There is nothing else for it to be.

And at night — lamp-hours, the little moons burning on their towers down the lanes — one shaft is left open, capped with glass, aimed at nothing. The starpipe. Children climb to it to learn the sky the way other children learn a trade: useful, distant, owned. The sun, they are taught, is an employee of the town, and the sky is a rumor that pays wages.

Stand at the Cut at shift-end, with the Worm's tremor in your jaw and the staked streets glowing behind you in the black, and the place will finally explain itself. It is not a town in a cave. It is a seed that has been asked how big it intends to be, and has answered with stakes. Nothing here is finished; that is the local religion. And if you want to hear the one liquid sound in the world, go back to the cistern groves at the edge of noon, put your ear against a tap-hole in an old barrel-tree, and listen: the slow patient pulse of held water moving through held wood — a heartbeat the size of a reservoir, keeping the whole dry world alive by refusing, one liter at a time, to let the air have it.
