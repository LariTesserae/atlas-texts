You hear it a day before you arrive.

Out on the plain, when the last knuckles of the old range have sunk to green swellings on the horizon and the wind has nothing left to collide with, it carries a sound that is not wind: a low compound hum, threaded with thin whistles, swelling and subsiding with each gust but never stopping. It doesn't echo off anything. The plain is too finished to echo. The sound simply rides the air for a day's walk in every direction, and you walk toward it the way you'd walk toward smoke.

The plain itself is the oldest thing in this part of the world, and it behaves like it. It was worn flat over an almost unimaginable span — its mountains ground down, its valleys filled in, its rivers beheaded and lost — and then the slow collision of the world beneath lifted the whole finished surface intact, carrying it up into the high cold, where erosion nearly stopped for lack of anything left to do. What remains is a landscape in its final form: everything concluded, nothing petering out. Edges are crisp here. Boundaries are honest. The plain ends at a rim, and beyond the rim there is often cloud rather than sky — the weather happens *below* this place. Rain rarely reaches it. What reaches it is fog, arriving in the afternoons as a tide, pouring through a low notch in the rim and draining back out at dusk, twice a day, forever.

Half an hour's walk inside the rim, the fog tide meets the town.

There is no better word, though the word apologizes for itself, because nothing here was built and no tool ever touched it. A hundred and forty pipes — stone columns, knee-high to four meters, blunt-topped, worn to the rounded shoulders of old teeth — stand in rows too straight for chance. Three long ranks, an avenue aligned precisely with the notch so the fog pours straight down it, and a dense cluster where the pipes crowd shoulder to shoulder like townsfolk at a well: a square. Their walls are banded stone, layered in fine tiers like reef or fossil wasp-paper, and each band reads like a ledger entry of some patient ancient growth. No mortar. No chisel marks. No shells, no bones, no hearths, no trace of hands anywhere in the gravel. The three honest hypotheses — a warm-sea reef raised with the plain; a mineralized grove whose piths rotted out into flues; stone drilled by wind over time no one can write down — are all unsatisfying in different ways, and the local custom, if there were locals, would be to leave the question alone.

The streets, such as they are, are mostly emptiness. This is a town made of gaps. Between the rows the ground is bare, wind-swept stone gravel, and the space itself — dozens of paces of nothing between one presence and the next — is the dominant material. But the gravel is stratified. The town stands on itself: the rubble of its own eroded years is the pavement underfoot, layered generation on generation, and in that pavement run buried channels, pipe-linked to pipe, that still carry water. Every structure here is hollow, including the ground.

And it is *loud*. This is what the sound is: the wind coming over the plain finds each pipe's mouth and each mouth sings — the way a bottle sings, but with the breath of an entire plateau behind it. A hundred and forty flues, each pitched to its own length, and the lengths are all different because erosion is patient and uneven. Downwind of the square at full wind, you cannot speak. You can feel the chord in your teeth. The shorter pipes keen and whistle; the mid ones hold a ragged fifth; and under everything a gritty hiss — quartz flour, ground from the towers themselves, flung along the stone at the wind's waist. At midday the melt water wakes in the buried channels and you hear the town swallowing: air pockets knocking through the plumbing in rhythmic gulps, a percussion under the drone. Ears ring for a while after you leave a gust's path, and the ringing, you notice, is in the town's key.

The day has a shape, and cold holds the clock. Nights are cold enough that every mouth seals with a frost cap, and the town goes out light by light — trebles first, bass last — into a silence so complete it feels like pressure. Then the sun comes up over the rim, hard and thin-edged, the stone warms, and the caps crack off in a scatter-shot of pops — thermal percussion, pipes waking out of tune and *searching*, one joining another off-pitch until, as the sun climbs and a film of melt smooths each bore, the whole town slides into agreement with itself. That tuning-up is the morning; the full chord is midday; and at dusk the sealing runs top-down again, note by note, until the place holds its breath.

The fog tide arrives in the afternoon and everything changes key. Fog water beads on the pipe walls — you can watch a drop gather for a full minute at a band-line before it runs — and wet pipes sound lower, duller, more intimate; the loud clear-weather chord drops into a softer, darker one, and the whistles vanish into murmurs. This is when the town drinks. Every exterior is a condenser; every dawn the gravel is dark with it; the buried channels gurgle it away. In six wind-scoured pans between the rows — shallow eyes of black water — the night's frost-melt stands until noon, each one ringed in lacy ice at sunrise, a mirror with a lace collar, gone by afternoon.

And here is the strange economy of the place: the dead town still runs its town. The pipes harvest fog. The channels drain it. The rows break the wind and hold warmth in their lees. The loud pipes vibrate all day, and vibration shakes frost off — so the moss grows fattest on the loudest towers, and you can read each pipe's volume as a green coat. In the low runs between rows, black moss lies like wet hair, waterlogged and trembling; cushion plants form spongy hummocks that walk like shoulders underfoot; hairgrass stands in wisps whose seed-heads shiver visibly in the drone, as if conducting. On the north-facing shadows of certain pipes only — shade is the moist side; the sun side stays bare — a small fern grows, and it is the one truly saturated green in a world of gray and white, a green so cold and deep it looks wet even when it isn't. There is one flower: small, white, violet-veined, and it opens only when the chord drops into the fog key, so that on foggy afternoons the whole town blooms for a few hours and then closes.

The town's one citizen is a moth. White, powdery, its wings like flakes of frost, it lives nowhere else, and on warm afternoons it rises from the moss in loose columns and mates in the wind-shadows between gusts. Its caterpillars graze the moss; its droppings enrich the cushions; the cushions hold the water that feeds the moss that feeds the moth. A small, closed, entirely municipal economy — the town still performing its duties: water, shelter, warmth — for a citizenry that never knew there was a government.

At the deep end of the square stands the Throat: the largest pipe, half-collapsed, its bore wide as a doorway, too long and too open to ever seal. Its note is below hearing. You feel it in the sternum, a slow mass shifting somewhere under the audible world, and if you stand there long enough you begin to hum its upper harmonic without deciding to. On cold nights the moths gather in its mouth by the hundreds, powdering its rim.

And once in a while — always in the deepest fog — the town plays more pipes than it has. The chord thickens with voices that belong to no standing tower: the buried rows, perhaps, the ones erosion has taken below the pavement, flues sealed under their own ancestors' rubble, joining in anyway. No one has ever found one. The fog drains out at dusk and the extra voices go with it, and the sound never repeats in time to be caught.

The rules, gathered as well as they can be:

- Nothing here was made. Everything is grown, worn, or settled into final form.
- Water moves through this place only where life once was.
- Loudness is warmth, and the moss keeps the ledgers.
- Fog is the tide, and the town keeps two keys: a clear key and a fog key.
- Cold owns the clock. It opens and closes the town's mouths.
- Erosion, here, is audible. Every century takes a little height, and shorter pipes sound higher. The town has been rising in pitch for as long as it has been dying — a requiem sung on an ascending scale, and the end is not a crash but a whistle thinning past the reach of ears, and after that, a plain.

That ending is far off, and the place is at peace with it. It has outlasted its own mountains; it is in no hurry to finish. At night, when the frost has sealed everything and the fog has gone back over the rim, there is only the hard stars, the cold's clean nothing-smell, the Throat's slow invisible throb — and, for the first time since dawn, silence. In that silence your ears ring their small note, in the town's key. For those hours, you are the only pipe left open.
