The first sign of it is breath.

From a distance the plateau looks like any dry upland — scrub thinned to gray-green whiskers, gravel basins, dust the color of bone. But there are places where the ground exhales. Stand over one of the collapse pits at dawn and a slow column of cool air rises out of the dark, smelling of wet chalk and something faintly animal, like a stable built from stone. The mountain is hollow beneath you, and it is breathing on its own schedule, exhaling when the surface pressure falls, inhaling when it rises, keeping time for itself.

You go down through one of these pits. The descent is short and the change is total. The scrub ends behind you; the light ends with it. What remains is a coolness that holds steady, a dampness that clings to the rock in beads, and a dimness so complete that the eye invents shapes for a while before it stops.

The rock here is new. That is the strange thing, and it takes a while to feel it in the palm of your hand. The limestone walls still carry their fracture faces — granular, sharp, unlicked. Nothing in these galleries has been rounded. The breakdown boulders lie where they fell with their edges intact, like freshly struck flint the size of houses. The gravel underfoot is angular; every piece of it would cut a boot sole. Slicing into the walls here and there are ledges of dark chert standing proud of the softer stone like knuckles of a buried hand, because around them the limestone has only just begun to dissolve. There are no columns, no thick flowstone drapes, none of the softness caves earn over their long lives. The formations are only beginnings: soda straws a hand-span long hanging from the ceiling joints like blown glass needles, thin enough to ring if you were careless enough to touch one, and small white crusts of moonmilk on the damp walls, soft as paste, yielding under a fingertip like wet chalk. The cave is young. Its surfaces have their factory edge still on.

The first great chamber opens under a second skylight, and here the sparse green of the surface world sends a single root-curtain down into the dark. A fig has found the pit rim and driven roots forty feet down the shaft, splitting into gray cables that hang in the gray cone of daylight like a shredded curtain. In that one cone of light there is a garden — a few ferns, moss on the wet rock, algae staining the drip-runs bluish-green. Moths cluster on the roots by the hundred, packed wing to wing in rows, pale triangles breathing. This is nearly all the vegetation there is. The light cones are the only farms. Twenty steps beyond each one, the world goes back to rock and meat, and the roots reach down like supply lines feeding nothing but themselves.

Beyond the skylight halls, the system extends — farther than a day's walk, chambers and fissure-avenues and dry galleries, most of them empty. That emptiness is the place's signature. For long stretches there is nothing: floors of angular gravel sweeping off into blackness, walls that hold their sharp relief, air so still the dust of your passage hangs where you stirred it. In these stretches the only sounds are your own steps and the drips. The drips are worth listening to, because they are not random. Each seep keeps its interval — one every four seconds, one every eleven — with the patience of a metronome, and the big chambers hold the sound for two or three seconds after each strike, so a single drop becomes a small slow conversation. You could set a clock by them. In a sense, something has.

Water here is a held thing, not a flowing one. The floors are dry. The water stands in trays — potholes, hollowed ledges, shallow rimstone pools with shelfstone lips already building on their edges like white ice. On the still surfaces float calcite rafts, thin lattices of stone light enough to swim. Each pool holds its own small pale nation: blind amphipods working the bottom like fleas of glass, white isopods the size of fingernail clippings, flatworms in faint blush-pink gliding on the film of the underside of the water. In the largest pool there are fish — fat, translucent, eyeless, the color of nothing, drifting in slow circuits with no apparent purpose or urgency, and small blind snails grazing the pool rims in a band an inch wide. The water is cold and utterly clear, and the creatures in it have nothing to hide from and nowhere to go. They drift. They appear to have been drifting forever.

But the emptiness is a foreground, because the sound reaches you long before the source does. Through hundreds of meters of rock and still air comes something like surf — a broad, steady, granular roar, threaded with high ticking and chatter. You hear it in one gallery, louder in the next. Then the smell arrives, ammonia first, sharp enough to sting, and under it a sweet, worked, organic richness. Then the passage opens, and you are inside the vault, and the fauna of this place stops being a category and becomes the weather.

The vault is a domed hall big enough to hold a town square, and the ceiling is pelted with bats — free-tailed bats in numbers with no useful scale attached, millions of them, packed in shingled layers, the ceiling underneath them stained and pocked from generations of chemistry. The sound is the collective noise of that many small warm bodies shifting: a wash of wing-fabric like distant breakers, a continuous high chirring, squeals, the rustle of a colony turning itself over. The air is warmer here and thick with the particulate sweetness of them. Below, the guano has built cones like gray-brown dunes, sloping away in terraces toward the gallery floors, and the dunes are alive in themselves — the crackle rises off them like fat frying, and if you crouch you can see why: beetles like polished coffee beans in their tens of thousands, mites in drifting lawns, pale millipedes, bristletails flipping, springtails in moving frost. The guano floor is not a floor; it is a compost of beetles. Every second of the day something in it is being eaten or recycled. The mound, measured and measured again over the years by the few people who do measure such things, does not grow. It is consumed as fast as it is deposited. It is a landscape in perfect balance, a dune with a fixed shape, a ledger that closes every night at zero.

The bats leave at the same hour every evening. Not roughly the same hour — the same hour, pouring out of the skylights and pits in a braided column you can see from the scrubland above, a river of wings that takes hours to pass and sounds, from a distance, like wind through a narrow place. At dawn they return. Inside the vault the roar drops to a surf and climbs back to a storm and drops again, a tide with a clock behind it. Moths commuting along the root-curtains, cave crickets stridulating from the wall-shadows in chirrs that come from everywhere at once, and the drip-metronomes under everything — the whole cave is loud, but loudly in the way an engine is loud: exactly, repetitively, without event.

In the return galleries, the ceiling is starred. Blue-green points of light, hundreds upon hundreds, fixed in the black like constellations, each one a glowworm larva — a fungus gnat in its long second life — each one burning cold to lure small flying things onto the beaded fishing lines it has hung beneath itself. The light is faint; a match would erase it. But the eye adapts, and the vaults of that gallery hold enough of these little mouths to walk by, dimly, seeing silhouettes: the gravel bars, your own hands gone gray, the threads glinting with their tiny snares.
