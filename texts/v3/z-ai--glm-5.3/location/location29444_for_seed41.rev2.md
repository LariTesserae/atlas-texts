The hill is dry, and the hill is hollow, and the hill breathes.

For most of the year its scrub crackles. Dust stands in the air. Lizards do their arithmetic on the hot rocks, and the grass has given up on green. But under a fig tree on the hill's shoulder — a tree noticeably fatter than its neighbors, noticeably greener — there is a mouth. A throat of collapsed limestone, half choked with roots, exhaling warm air that smells of wet stone and cut fern, like a greenhouse left shut all summer. The hill breathes out through this mouth, twice a day, on a schedule older than anything living on it. You find the place by the smell of the breath.

You go down, and the world inverts. Ordinarily, descent means cold; here it means heat. The air thickens before the light thins — you feel the change at the throat, a humidity that drinks through the skin, a warmth rising from below with the patience of something enormous. Going in, you trade sky for warmth, and it is a fair trade. The rock under your hands has been rounded by ages of this damp; nothing here has a sharp edge left. Ten meters down the light is a rumor. Twenty meters down your eyes begin to negotiate.

The chamber, once they consent, is the size of a small church. Perhaps forty paces long, a vault fifteen meters over your head, its breakdown blocks settled into the floor like furniture no one remembers moving. The sinkhole's throat admits one needle of daylight, warped by the overhang and strained through the fig's leaves, and that needle is nearly all the light there is. The walls shine faintly wet — iron ochres, a manganese black like ink dried in stone, and everywhere the high sheen of condensation. The floor is warm as a flank. This is the first rule of the room, the one every visitor learns through their boots: the sun here is underfoot. The heat comes up from deep rock, from a warm spring that has been breathing its heat into this limestone for longer than the hill has had a shape.

The room makes its own weather. Warm wet air rises all day and pools at the vault, and the vault sweats. When the surface cools at dusk, the hill inhales — a long, slow draw through the throat — and the ceiling's sweat loosens. The room rains, lightly, from the top: a drizzle of single drops, each one finding its appointed place. Some land on moss and make no sound. Some land on stone and tick. One drop, at the far end, has been landing in the same spot long enough to have worn a cup in the stone lip the size and depth of an eye — a marble bath, ten thousand years in the carving. After it rains on the surface, weeks later, the room's rain quickens. The cave remembers storms that the hill has already forgotten. Nothing here flows; everything drips. There is exactly one pool, near the low dome at the chamber's end, body-warm and breathing — its level rises and falls a hand's width over the hours, as some far siphon fills and empties. A foam line on the walls shows you the tide's whole ambition. At its edge a warm seep spreads mineral thread-mats, white and faintly pink, and the air there carries the soft rotten-egg note of deep water. The room's water budget is small, and every milliliter of it is employed.

The needle of light moves across the floor like a slow clock hand. A moss bank leans into its path the way a cat leans into a hand, and everything green crowds the column: pale algae crusts, spike-mosses, a bank of moss so dense its spore capsules stand up from it like the populations of tiny cities. Filmy ferns veil the walls wherever a drip line runs — leaves one cell thick, translucent, more like green breath than like plants, able to live nowhere on the dry hill above and everywhere down here that the ceiling weeps. When the needle withdraws in the afternoon, the room does not go fully black. The dead wood carries foxfire — patches of fungus with their own cold blue-green light, dim as politeness, enough to read shapes by once your eyes have paid attention. The room gives you its information slowly, and only in exchange for patience.

And the fig keeps a garden of itself in the dark. Its roots come down through the throat in a chandelier — dozens of them, thick as a wrist to thin as a finger, drinking the room's humidity, feeding the great green crown on the dry hill. But here is the strange thing, the room's one true oddity: some of those roots, in the dark, have leafed. Pale, thin, almost white leaves, grown underground, that will never see the sky — leaves with nectar pores at their bases that the moths attend in slow orbits. Botany would want to argue about it. The moths do not. The fat fig on the dry hill is not living off the rain like its neighbors; it is living off this room, and the room, in the person of these root-leaves, seems in some quiet way to be keeping a garden for no return the ledger can show.

The room is full. That is its second quality, after warmth: fullness. Life here is layered to the point of near-touching, stacked in strata like the rock itself. On the floor: microbial mats, moss, a mulch of old leaves that fell down the throat decades of autumns ago, and snails — amber-shelled, thin as fingernails, in numbers that make the walls seem upholstered. You can hear one feeding, if you are still enough: a rasp fine as a fingernail drawn across paper. Springtails hop on the wet stone like spilled salt come alive. Pale crickets stand on the vault and the walls, antennae longer than their bodies, listening to the weather. Millipedes work the mulch. A big long-legged spider has strung its sheet under the drip cup and waits near the eye-shaped bath. A pale snake patrols the balustrade of breakdown in slow loops, checking ledges like a landlord, and takes a cricket without ceremony when the moment comes — quick, soundless, unhurried. Frogs live in the moss bank and say almost nothing; every hour or so one of them offers a single soft *toc*, like a knuckle on a table, and the room absorbs it. In the warm pool, eyeless fish hover at the cooler drip-inlets where the water carries more breath, and on the pool's floor caddis larvae walk about wearing cases they built from seeds and grit. In a high pocket of the ceiling, five or six bats hang folded like leather leaves, asleep, waiting for the dusk breath to go out and hunt the surface night.

Things happen here, but they happen softly. A moth drinks too long at the pool; its wing-tips touch; the water takes it; the surface rings twice; a fish rises like a decision being made. Springtails find a drowned moth and mob it within the hour. The snake moves; the crickets, all together, have already moved. When the hill inhales at dusk the bats leave through the throat without any sound you could name, and the room's air shifts around their going. Everything that falls in this room is answered by something that waits — that is nearly the whole of its law.

People have also been among those who waited. The marks are few and faint, and the room has been taking them into itself for a long time. High on the vault, a bloom of soot — many small fires' worth of dark, so old that calcite has grown a thin skin over it, soot becoming stone. A midden of snail shells, thousands deep, all cracked the same way. A ring of stones set around the warm seep, sized to hold heat and sitting bodies. A ledge above it polished by generations of sitting. One hand, in red ochre, printed rather than painted — a palm pressed to the rock and the rock keeping it, half-flaked now, five fingers still patient. A short row of red strokes beside it that might be counting, or trying to. A snail shell with a hole worn at the lip: stringing, or luck. Whoever they were, they came in the cold seasons, for the same reason the frogs live in the moss, and the room was their host, and it did not keep their names.

And it is quiet — quiet in a way that has structure. The vault should boom; it doesn't, because every surface is upholstered in moss and damp, and sound dies at birth here. What remains is a catalog of smallness: the four kinds of drip (onto water, a soft *tunk*; onto stone, a tick; onto moss, nothing; into the worn cup, a note). The snail's rasping. Cricket legs, like dry straw. The whisper of moth wings in the dark. The pool lapping its lip when the tide turns. The room's own breath at dawn and dusk, a sigh through the throat that takes half a minute. Your pulse, audible at last. The quiet is not absence; it is the room listening.

Nothing here is being made anymore. That is the final fact of the place, and it is not a sad one. The collapse that opened the chamber happened so long ago that its blocks are rounded, its edges softened, its dust long since become soil; the room is finished, achieved, complete, and has been for millennia. Stalactites grow at the speed of syrup. The drip carves its cup another micron. The midden becomes floor; the soot becomes stone; the leaves become mulch; the mulch becomes snails. Above, the hill cracks with dryness and the wind combs the scrub, and beneath it, unknown to the wind, the land keeps a warm green dark the way a body keeps a heartbeat.

When the light goes, the foxfire shows a little better, and the pale root-leaves turn toward it, like faces. The hill inhales. Somewhere, a snail feeds. The drip lands in its ancient cup, precisely.

The room goes on keeping itself.
