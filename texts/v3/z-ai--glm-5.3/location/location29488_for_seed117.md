# The Cistern Commons

A low country, barely above the water table — you understand this within minutes of arriving, because the ground tells you. Every footpath is a ridge of packed slag and crushed brick raised a hand's width above dark, tea-colored seepage. Step off the ridge and your boot sinks with a sound like a sigh. The water isn't pooled so much as *imminent* — it hangs a centimeter below the surface of everything, waiting.

The place is small. You can cross it in twenty minutes: a shallow bowl of land between two low moraines, maybe three hundred meters across, ringed by a crumbling embankment that someone, long ago, built to hold something back or in. Its boundaries feel deliberate. Inside them, the world is dense the way a tidepool is dense — every surface occupied, every niche claimed.

**The surfaces, first.** There is no plant life. Not a blade, not a moss, not a lichen — the ground is mineral and metal and glass. Old industrial slag lies everywhere in cindery heaps, weathered round, each lump a dull charcoal sponge. Rain — and it rains often here, a fine cool drizzle that never quite commits — doesn't soak into the slag. It beads on it, sits on it, weeps out of it. The heaps glisten as if lacquered. Where the slag has been in place longest, decades perhaps, water has leached it into soft horizontal bands, and the bands sweat continuously, thin rivulets running down the heap-flanks into channels between them, so the whole bowl ticks and trickles with tiny water-sounds. It is the loudest thing here, and it is quiet.

**What covers everything instead of plants:** a film. Not biological in any way you'd recognize — more like a mineral skin, a gelatinous sheen of silica and iron oxides and something faintly iridescent that grows in response to wetness the way moss responds to shade. It coats the slag in patches of ochre, deep purple-black, occasional startling teal. It has no cells anyone has found. It spreads. Where the film is thick, it drips — slow, viscous drips from the underside of overhangs, and beneath the worst overhangs these drips have built soft orange towers, centimeter-scale, like infantile cave formations. The drips count out a time signature for the whole place.

**The built things** are sparse and functional: a pump-house, brick, roofless, its iron pump still in its mounting like a dead animal in a trap; a run of ceramic pipe half-buried along the embankment, jointed and cracked, tree roots absent where tree roots ought to be — the pipe is just pipe, alone; a small brick cistern at the bowl's center, open-topped, filled with perfectly still black water that reflects the dim sky with total fidelity. The cistern is the heart of the place. Everything trickles toward it. Nothing overflows it. That is one of the local rules: the water here has a destination and discipline.

**The fauna** — abundant, and this is the strange part, because what thrives here is a dense small ecosystem running on the film and the leachate. Swarms of minute midge-like insects, but wingless, hopping in static-crackling clouds off the wet slag, their winglessness made up for by an electrostatic glide — they drift on the damp air's charge, sticking briefly to the film, feeding, popping free. Pale isopod-like scavengers in the channels, dozens per meter, working the drip-towers. Something in the cistern: a slow large shadow, visible only when the drizzle stills and the reflection clears, that rises once an hour or so and is gone. Nobody stays long enough at the cistern to learn its schedule. The rule of thumb passed between visitors: the hopping swarms thicken when the shadow rises.

**Atmosphere:** dim and close. The sky is a permanent pearl-grey lid, low enough that the moraine crests seem to almost touch it. Cool, not cold — the temperature of a cellar. Mist doesn't form and disperse; it just *subsists*, a permanent half-clarity in which the far embankment is a suggestion and the near slag-heaps are over-detailed, every bead of water a small lens. Light comes evenly from everywhere and casts no shadows, which flattens distance and makes the place feel smaller than its three hundred meters — more like a large room with an uncertain ceiling.

**Sound inventory:** the tick of seepage, the crackle of the hopping swarms (like distant cellophane), the drip-towers' slow meter, and every so often a deep single *thump* from underground — a pump somewhere below, or a water hammer in buried pipe, still cycling on some faint residual pressure decades after anyone maintained it. That thump is the place's heartbeat and its only industry. It keeps the water moving. It may be the only thing keeping the whole bowl from going stagnant.

The overwhelming impression is of a landscape in perfect, patient equilibrium with its own strangeness — a post-industrial tidepool, a machine's afterlife colonized by things that were never supposed to exist, all of them wet, all of them quiet, all of them going about their business under the low grey lid while the cistern keeps its still black counsel at the center.
