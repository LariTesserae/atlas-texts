You come over the Rim at first light, and the light reaches you before anything else — before the cold, before the smell of the place, before you have even seen it. The air up here is thin and hard, and the sun is low and circling, and the snow and pale stone throw the light back upward, so that everything stands lit from two directions at once. Shadows shrink to puddles. In the still air hang drifting crystals too small to see individually, and the sun enters them and comes out the other side multiplied: pillars, false suns, a ring the width of the sky. The sun arrives in flocks here. You learn quickly not to look at it directly, and then you learn that there is no such thing as looking at it indirectly, because everything is a reflection of it.

The bowl itself is small. That is the first thing the Rim tells you. It is a stone cirque, a mile from wall to wall, and from up here you can hold the whole of it in one look: the lake below, the village on its ridge, the bird cliffs, the frozen falls on the eastern wall. Nothing in this place is more than ten minutes' walk from anything else. Beyond the Rim is a plateau you will not think about, because everything that matters — every rule, every weather, every hour of the day — happens inside the bowl.

The walls are old in the way bedrock is old. Glaciers ground these hollows out so long ago that their work has stopped being history and become simply the shape of the world. The stone is dark, polished in long strokes, and mapped over with black and orange lichen in continents nobody drew.
