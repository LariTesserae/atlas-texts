**The Warm Dark**

Under the dry upland, below the level where anything happens, the stone holds a room.

Above, the upland goes on for miles: a desert of the same dark rock, rounded and bare, where it has not rained in the memory of any process that could remember. A fissure in that pavement opens into a rubble chute, and the chute gives onto the room — the lowest room there is. Everything that matters in this world runs downhill, and here is where downhill ends.

It is a small room, chapel-sized: forty paces long, twenty wide, a ceiling that obliges you to stoop where it decides to be low. The rock is basalt, dense and dark, and warmth is its principal fact. Heat rises through the floor from deeper stone at a rate that has not varied in the whole working life of the place. The floor is blood-warm under the flat of a hand — warmer than skin — and the air lies on you like the inside of a kiln with its door open: dry, mineral, faintly tasting of struck flint, emptied of every kind of wet. The sun of this place is underfoot. There is no other.

Darkness is nearly total. What light exists is made here, by the room's only inhabitants, and it is faint the way foxfire on an old stump is faint: a dim amber glow given off along two horizontal seams that run the walls like courses of masonry no one laid — one at knee height, one at shoulder height. It is enough light to know that a hand exists in front of your face. It is not enough to see the hand. What it does is mark the room's shape in faint lines, so that the dark is not an absence but a shape. The ceiling is a felt presence rather than a surface; the floor shows only as the place where the darkness turns soft. This light has no morning, no evening, and no memory of either. It has also never been seen. There is nothing here with eyes, and never has been. The glow shines for no one, and shines anyway.

The floor is stone-flour: basalt ground finer than sand, warm and soft as flour in a bin, lying at its finished depth — a finger deep along the walls, two fingers at the low center where it stopped drifting ages ago. Around the walls lie aprons of rubble, blocks that finished falling before that. Nothing here is mid-fall, mid-drift, mid-anything. The rock is worn, but not by water; there has never been water. It is worn by heat — eons of slow swelling and shrinking that flaked the edges off grain by grain — so the walls carry the smooth volumes of river stone with no river anywhere in their history. The familiar furniture of caves is absent too: no stalactites, no flowstone, no drip-mounds. This room never had water to decorate itself with. It is naked rock, polished by temperature alone. In the undersides of the rubble, white whiskers of salt feather out — halite grown in some eon when brine stood far above in the upland, and long since done growing. Dry salt. Salt in the perfect tense.

At knee and shoulder height the walls hold the only coolness in the world — not truly cool, only less warm — and on those two lines the stone is damp the way a window is damp at dawn, which is to say almost not at all: a film thin as breath, condensing where the warm air meets the slightly-less-warm rock, holding steady, never gathering, never running. That film is the entire water economy of the place. Everything alive here lives on it and within a hand's width of it.

What lives here is a crust. On the damp of the two seams grow patches of dark leathery felt — a lithobiont, chamois-textured, each a few hands across — perhaps twenty of them in the whole room, spaced out along the lines with bare warm stone, whole meters of it, between. Each patch is an island; the black floor is the sea between them. The felt is threadwork, mineral-harvesting filaments packed close, and the living edge is the part that glows. Its diet is short: it fixes carbon out of the trace gases of the air, it drinks the film, and it mines what it needs from the dust that sifts down the chute from the upland and is caught, grain by grain, on the damp of the seams — the same rate, as it happens, at which the crust mines it back into living felt. The room's books balance to the grain. And it runs on warmth. What light is to a leaf, heat is to this; the room serves no other dish. When warm — and it is always warm — the patches give off the faintest smell, like hay at the dry bottom of a loft.

The crust is also the only maker of things here, and what it makes is stonework. Its underside exudes a mild acid that etches the basalt grain by grain; every patch has undercut itself a shallow shelf, a private quarry, and behind each living edge the rock is drilled with holdfasts, borings the diameter of a hair. Where the patches are oldest, the seams have been deepened into troughs — rock dressed back and smoothed. Nothing constructed stands in this room: no wall, no tool, no mark of any maker with a body, and the floor's dust has never taken a print. The crust's etching is not construction; it is more like weather with an intent. Still, it is the room's whole built inventory — quarry, drill, dress — hand-tool work done by chemistry with nothing to hurry it.

No animal has ever been here. Nothing walks the floor, nothing watches the glow, nothing dies here, nothing hunts. The food web has four words in it: warmth, air, dust, crust. Nothing eats the crust; nothing pollinates it; it does not flower. Each patch grew until it reached the edge of its damp, long ago, and stopped, and its dead persist beneath its living edge, too dry to rot — so that every patch is its own graveyard and its own foundation, ancestor and footing in one felt.

The room has one voice. Air threads through the chute at a rate that has never changed, and the room answers with a note so low it sits below hearing most of the time, crossing into hearing now and then — a held tone, felt in the chest before the ear, the room's single sustained syllable. Otherwise, silence with a mineral weight to it. The flour-dust swallows sound; nothing echoes; the room keeps no noises. Nothing here has ever made a sound the room remembered.

The rules, then, are simple. Heat stands in for light. A film stands in for water. Dust stands in for rain. One note stands in for weather, and the past stands in for the future. Everything that was going to happen here has already happened: the corners were rounded until rounding ran out of corners; the blocks fell and stayed; the salt finished its bloom; the dust drifted to its final depth and stopped; the patches grew to the edges of their damp and stopped; the note found its pitch and holds it. There are no processes in this room, only positions. The room does not change; it maintains.

Sit still in it long enough, and your eyes give up looking for more light and accept the room's account of itself: a constellation of faint amber seams hanging in warmth, a black floor below, heat arriving from beneath at a rate nothing measures because nothing here needs it measured. It was like this before there was anything here to describe it. Describing it changes nothing. The room is not waiting — nothing here is — because waiting requires a future, and this place spent all of its, ages ago, buying the shape it's in.
