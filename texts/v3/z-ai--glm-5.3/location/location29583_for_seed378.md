You come up to it from below, from the Dry Countries, on the Lift: a counterweighted car that rises the last hundred meters of the escarpment without a sound. The counterweight is water. Everything here is water doing quiet work. As you clear the Brink, the plateau opens — a Shelf of white mineral terraces stepping away for kilometers under a hard, high, white sky — and the first thing you notice is not the heat, and not the light, though both arrive like hands laid flat on you. It's the silence. The Shelf gives back almost nothing. Your own footsteps go into the ground and do not come out.

The rock underfoot is sinter — silica laid down by hot springs over unguessable time, chalk-white, crisp, sometimes hollow. Walk where the guideposts stand. The whole Shelf steams faintly, all day, like a thing just taken from an oven and set down to cool. Except it never cools. That's the riddle of the place: it is high — high enough that the horizon sits far off and the sky goes deep — and it is hot, blood-hot, furnace-hot in patches, because the heat comes from below, not from the sun. The sun just supplies the light, and it supplies that in brutal quantity, doubled and tripled off the white ground until the sky-colored pools in the terraces look like holes in the world and the steam columns stand glowing like pillars of held light. Workers wear slitted wooden blinkers against the glare. Shadows here are ink-black and have edges you could cut with.

There are no plants. Nothing rooted, nothing green, no blade of anything. The water is too hot, too mineral, the ground too warmed through; the Shelf is a place where the earth forgot to be humble about its own heat. What grows instead is simpler and older: mats of thermophilic film along every waterline, staining the white terraces with bands of amber, rust, rose, and bottle-green. The locals call it the silk. It follows the temperature gradients like a litmus strip — scald-black at the springs, orange at the brood-line, green where the water has cooled toward blood-warm — and it is the entire base of everything alive here. There are no flowers on the Shelf. The gardens are microbial, and the animals are the wind.

And there are animals, in abundance — but pooled, the way water pools. This is a sparse country between its presences; the sinter flats between the springs lie bare as a page, footprints in nothing, and then you come to a warm run and it is crowded as a marketplace. Brine midges walk on the skin of the water in shifting veils; at midday they rise in vertical columns that look exactly like smoke, and the uninitiated keep mistaking life for steam. In the warm runs school pale bead-shrimp, translucent things that in this glare are visible mainly as shadows — you see the shadow first, then the animal that owns it. Cobblebacks — low, broad, stone-backed grazers with splayed silent feet — move along the flume-lines licking up the silk, and their feet are the silk's pollinators: they carry the film from pool to pool on their pads, seeding new gardens, doing for this vegetation-less world what wind and bees do everywhere else. Over the great spring cones, soarers hang. They never seem to flap. They ride the thermal columns rising off the vents, hundreds of meters of lift for free, and drop through the midge-columns with fringed, owl-soft wings. The Shelf never made song a good idea, so nothing here sings. The birds signal with wing-glints. The grazers flash pale belly-linings at one another across the white distance. The whole biosphere has moved its language from sound into light and heat — you watch two cobblebacks hold a slow conversation of shimmered bellies across four hundred meters of glare, and understand that you are the only thing in the valley that talks with its mouth.

Why the silence, the strangers always ask. Partly it is physical: the porous sinter eats noise; the warm dense air near the ground bends sound upward and away, so the Shelf sits in a permanent acoustic shadow, and the storms that pile up over the Dry Countries beyond the Brink perform their lightning in perfect dumbshow, enormous white-and-violet weather happening wordlessly miles below. Partly it is law. Sharp noise knocks the vents — a blow, a blast, a shout in the wrong place, and an exhaler can hiccup out of rhythm, and a crust that was settling can subside. So the founding rule of the Still — the settlement strung along the Main Spring, roof-chains dripping, spaced wide along the watercourses like beads on a wire — is nothing louder than the drips. The town's one bell lives wrapped in felt in a glass case, and it means one thing only: the ground has lied somewhere, a crust has gone, come to the shelters now. The bell is the loudest object on the Shelf and it is essentially a curse.

The town, the Still — the name is a triple pun the residents pretend not to enjoy — exists because the Dry Countries below are thirsty, and the sky above this plateau is full of water that isn't falling.
