There is a sea here with no water in it. The sea left a long time ago — long enough that no story remembers which direction it went — and left its floor behind, white as a held breath. The salt plain runs flat to every horizon, blinding at noon, cracked into polygons you can follow for days. Rain has not fallen here within the memory of the salt. Dunes of windblown crystal migrate along set lines like slow animals. If there is a driest place in the world, this flat is the finalist, and it is on the flat's lowest basin, where even the dust comes to rest, that the sound begins.

You hear it before you find it. Out on the white, in a line two hundred meters long, the salt is pierced by holes — the Mouths — and the flat breathes through them. It inhales all night and exhales all day, and the exhalation is not wind but voice: a churning roar that stands over the holes in visible columns of dust, audible a kilometer out, though there is no one out there to hear it. The Mouths are the only doors. The way down is a spiral stair cut into the wall of the largest one, its stone wedges polished round by centuries of feet, and as you descend the roar stops being something you hear and becomes something you move through. Sound here is not an event. It is a medium. The people who live below are to it what fish are said to be to water.

The stair opens and the world does something the flat above will never tell you: it turns green. A fissure canyon, four hundred meters wide and three long, sunk deep below the old seabed — everything in this country is downhill; the city lies lower than the sea itself ever stood — and roofed over with the salt itself, a ceiling tens of meters thick that the noon sun soaks into a glowing sky. The light below is white-gold and shadowless, bright enough to read anywhere, warm as a hand laid on your back, and through pinholes the first families enlarged by hand and fitted with clay tubes, shafts of direct sun fall like pillars, burning with slow drifting dust.

And the walls, floor, terraces: green. Deep, layered, waxy green — but a dry lushness, a green with no wetness in it, leaves like glass blades, sap that runs clear as lamp oil and burns in the lamps. The flora here does not drink. It eats the roar. The great bellwood trees, whose trunks ring when struck, and the drumfruit gourds, and the mats of waxgrass climbing the fissure walls, and the moss that grows only where the canyon's standing wave holds one steady note — all of them feed on the acoustic flood the Mouths pour down. Their roots take minerals from salt dust; their leaves take everything else from sound. The vegetation is the noise made visible: every loud corner is a thicket, every quiet corner is near-barren pavement, and you can read the acoustics of a street by how the moss has drawn it. When the wind above rises and the chord peaks, the drumfruit ripens in days.

The city fills the canyon the way coral fills a hull — thousands of rooms cut into the walls, streets along the ledges, rope bridges, galleries layered over galleries over older galleries, stairs cut into stairs, the whole built thing thickened and re-tuned across many generations with stone wedges, fired clay, rope, and a little cold-hammered copper for chisels. And nearly all of it is empty. The streets are swept and no one is in them. Doors stand kept, thresholds dusted, beds felted and aired, in houses where no one sleeps — the city was built for ten times its people and its people have been gently, slowly declining for longer than their records run. You can walk an hour and meet three people. You will hear all three coming; you will hear the whole city always; the emptiness here is measured in bodies, not in sensation. This is the opposite of a desert. It is a drought of people in a flood of sound.

Learn the sound's anatomy and you can live in it. At the bottom, the bed-roar, the churn of wind through the Mouths, brown and total. Above that, the canyon's held chord — one great standing tone you feel in your teeth and sternum before you hear it. Above that, whistling at every shutter, and rows of tuned clay bells hung on wires, each one deliberately shaved a hair off the chord so that it flutters — beats — at its own rate. Time below is kept by interference: the city's clocks are bells tuned slightly wrong on purpose. And in the swept plazas, the fine salt dust arranges itself. Where the standing wave crosses itself, the dust crawls into ridges, cells, rings, figures that shift each time the tuners shift the chord. Readers kneel at the plaza edges under breath-canopies, watching the figures reform. That is the writing — news, archive, scripture — and it is never touched by hand. To alter a figure would be the deepest crime below: forgery of the world's own voice.

The people number a few hundred. Their language is in the hands, the wrists, the cheek and chin; children are raised in the felt-lined quiet rooms at the canyon's dead ends, where a person can speak aloud, and they learn to talk before they earn the roar. Speech is spent like coin — one sentence in the Hush, a confession in a quiet corner, property being measured not in rooms but in the two luxuries: a light shaft of one's own, and a place where the tone goes quiet. Families inherit beams of sun the way others inherit fields. The tuners are the standing profession: they work the great stone pendulum dampers hung from the ceiling and the bellwood shutters at the Mouths' throats, and their law is that no new wall may be raised without them, because every wall changes the chord. Architecture below is composition. The city's eras are named for keys, and the city's name is not a word at all — it is a chord. It has had eleven names.

Water: there is exactly one kind, and it arrives once a day. Just before dawn the salt faces at the fissure's cold depth exhale their bitterness as a thin dew, and it is scraped — stone blades on chill crystal — into gourd cups, rationed by an office older than anyone's grandmother, bitter, mineral, warm with body heat almost before it is swallowed. As far as anyone here knows, this dawn-scraping is all the water in the world.

The rules of the place follow from its physics. Still days are famine: when the wind above dies, the roar thins, the green goes gray at the tips, and the people pray for storm. Storm days are harvest and danger together — the chord swells past safety, shutters slam, drumfruit fattens, the oil-jars fill, and someone usually has to be dug out of a collapsed terrace. The Mouths are never, never fully capped; there is a story taught to every child about a warden who sealed one Mouth to punish a district, and how in one season the district's green went to gray powder and its people went down the stair and out onto the flat and did not come back. And in the deep organ beneath the city — the fissure descends beyond reach into strata the tuners have never mapped — something sometimes answers. At certain tunings, a note arrives from below that no wind makes, felt in the chest rather than heard, and when it comes the resin moths rise in clouds and the dust figures tear themselves into new forms. The tuners' rule is one sentence: when the depths join the chord, let them, and keep one damper open.

The animals are few enough to be events. Resin moths, whose salt-glass wings shed a fine silk-felt for the quiet rooms, swarming only in the fruiting. Hush-geckos, pale and silent, whose gathering in a quiet room is the plain's only weather forecast. Salt-fleas in the leaf litter, turning the dead green back to dust. And the stitchers — nine small birds that live up in the Mouths, riding the exhalation — each one known to the city by name, and mourned by it, one by one, when the flat takes them.

The place is old enough to have stopped changing. The hollow neither fills nor deepens; the wind's deposits and withdrawals are exactly matched; the sweepers feed plaza dust to the gardens, and the gardens feed the storm to the oil jars, and the oil burns in the lamps, and the light joins the glowing sky. The people's oldest word for maintenance and their oldest word for prayer are the same word. Everything below is in its own state.

But go at dawn. Go when the wind above turns — the moment the plain switches from exhale to inhale and the great chord, for about a hundred heartbeats, falls to a whisper. That is the Hush, the only quiet in this world, and the city uses it the way other places use festivals: everyone speaks at once. Lovers say the thing they saved all night. A woman calls an apology across a plaza to a man who has been three streets distant from her for a decade. Babies hear their own voices for the first time and are stunned by them. Then the wind turns, the roar comes back in like a tide, and three hundred people fall silent together and lift their faces, and all the green of the canyon leans into the flood and drinks.
