You come to the Shelf the way everyone comes to it: by water, at dawn, when the whole island is steaming.

From a mile out across the marsh it looks like something that shouldn't be there yet — a grey back of stone lifting out of the wet, low and long, one continuous rock rather than a heap of them, four hundred paces end to end and nowhere higher than a granary roof. It is steaming because it is warm and the air is not. Cool mornings, the whole Shelf sits inside its own cloud. The cloud has a mineral smell to it, like a riverbed freshly split open, with a thread of iron underneath, and by mid-morning the sun burns it off and you see the place clearly: dense, dark-roofed, crammed shoulder to shoulder on the only dry footing for a day's punt in any direction.

The eldest of the town will tell you — unprompted, at the Warm Seam, while filling her ladle — that when she was a girl she fished over the exact spot where the square now stands. This is the Shelf's first strangeness and its founding fact: it is new. Within living memory this was open water. Then the stone came up — no flood, no shaking, just the lake-bed shouldering into the sky — and it has kept coming up since, a finger's width a year, more in wet years. The rock is young enough that it hasn't learned to be kind. Its edges are still knife-true; a careless hand on the rim opens like it touched a new tool. Every block quarried from the outside face is the color of wet bone and soft enough to cut with a horn pick, hardening in the air over a year or two. And every block is a page of the old lake's ledger: freshwater mussels, small snails, the occasional fish spine, all lying in the bedding like pressed flowers. Some door-lintels in the Crown were chosen for their fossils and set on purpose. The one with the fish's full backbone in it is touched for luck by people who would laugh at the suggestion that they are superstitious.

The stone weeps everywhere at once and nowhere for long. Cut anywhere on the Shelf and you strike seep within a forearm's depth; the rock is saturated through, still draining the lake it used to be. Water runs in lines that follow the bedding planes, so the seeps emerge in stripes, like the shells — the older masons say the stone remembers where it kept its water and keeps it there still. Nobody on the Shelf has a cellar. Everybody has a cistern: stone-lined, dark, cold, breathing a little under the floor of the kitchen. The town drinks its own geology.

And it drinks its own breath. At the crown of the island the Warm Seam rises — a spring that comes up warm as blood, iron-flavored, the one thing on the Shelf that never knows winter. Against the cool air this warmth makes weather. Dawn fog pools in the lanes until ten; every cold surface beaded by nine; dew fences — long tilted frames of reed-mat — stood out on the terraces overnight and wrung into jugs at first light by children whose specific chore it is. The drips that result are the town's music. Households know their drips by name, the way you know a dog. The Bell. The Gossiper. The Meter, which marks time so steadily that two lanes below set their breakfasts by it.

The town itself is the answer to a simple question: what do people do when dry ground is scarce? They stack. The Shelf holds four hundred people and does it by refusing to waste a horizontal inch. Houses share walls as a matter of course; the lanes between are a shoulder wide and run like drains — because they are drains, every lane floored with gutter-stone pitched to the common runs. There are three strata to the place. The Skirt, lowest, where the marsh laps at the old high-water notch: fish-houses, eel racks, the lime kilns, the stairs. The Lanes above, where most lives are lived. And the Crown, highest and driest, where the Warm Seam rises, where the square sits — and the square is no bigger than a threshing floor, serving all four hundred, because land this precious is not spent on emptiness. When the town assembles, it assembles like a crowd in a stairwell.

And then there is the Dry Way. Having run out of ground, the town built a second one overhead: plank walks slung roof to roof, bridge-rooms spanning the lanes, lofts that begin as one family's eave and end as another's porch. The Dry Way is how you cross town without wading the dripping lanes; it is where courtship happens, where laundry dries, where the swallows nest underneath in fecund rows. Nothing on the Shelf does one job. A wall is also a cistern's cheek. A stair is also a drying rack. A bridge is also a house's room.

Rank here is measured in dryness — in how many steps above the seep-line your bed stands. Marriages have foundered on mold history. There is a recognized category of dispute called a wet grievance, heard by the gutter-wardens, usually concerning a neighbor whose roof sheds onto another's path. The code is short and old: every roof drains to the common run; no one builds below the high-water notch; the rim is quarried only outward, never downward — the town must not eat its own footing. Water from the Warm Seam is shared by ladle and is never sold. Swallow nests are protected by law, because swallows eat midges and the midges are the only real predator on the Shelf. And no one sleeps off the rock. There are stilt-barns and reed-yards out on the marsh, plenty of them, and no one will tell you exactly why nobody sleeps there. They just don't.

The town speaks softly because it has no choice. Over open water a voice carries whole; in lanes a shoulder wide there is no privacy in sound at all. So the people of the Shelf have grown a half-language of the hands — lane-sign — a working vocabulary of forty gestures, finger-flicks and palm-tilts, for *coming through*, *your gutter's blocked*, *eels in the run*, *not now, she's sleeping*. Strangers find it eerie for a day and then find themselves using it.

For all that, the place is mostly quiet, and the quiet has grain to it. Drips, first — always drips, the island's own percussion. The tick of stone cooling after dark, a sound like a large clock losing patience. The soft clicking at the rim where the last freshwater mussels of the exposed bed gape and die back each season, like slow rain falling upward. The heron — a single heron, called the Banker, who owns the second-newest step and is regarded by everyone, without irony, as a taxpayer — knocks his dry croak off the water at dusk. Once or twice a winter comes the frost-silence: a clear night cold enough to stop every drip at once, and the town wakes into a hush so total that children run out into it laughing, to hear the quiet with their own ears.

The island's small ecologies are counted and known like family. Black moss mapping the wet lines on every wall — you can read a mason's age by how much of his work has gone dark. One seep on the north face, the green gang, thick with a fern colony that the whole town could point to and no one would dare pick. The eastern terrace's row of pollarded willows, brought as withies in the second year and kept cropped short like sheep — the only trees, and they are more like a fence that dreams. Vegetables in tubs of marsh-muck hauled up by rope: sorrel, fat hen, broad beans in the good years. The eels come through the runs in autumn and everyone's hands smell of them until the frost. Moths crowd the kiln-flues at night. That is the animal census, more or less: eels, midges, swallows, moths, the Banker, voles trapped by children for their skins. Something big moves in the deep marsh some nights — a single slow displacement of water, heard, never seen — and the town has agreed, without ever agreeing, not to have that conversation.

At the very crown, near the Warm Seam, there is a fissure the town has capped with a plank and a stone. They call it the Ear. Twice a day, if you kneel and listen — and the children do, on dares — you can hear water moving far below the bedrock. A slow push, a slow pull. There are no tides in the marsh. Nobody explains the Ear. The plank goes back on.

The Shelf's one true calendar is the Growth Post, a quarried pillar beside the stairs, notched every year at the spring flood, when the water stands highest and the measurement is honest. Because the island rises, the water is farther down each year than the year before — and so, every spring, the town carves one more step at the bottom of the long stair, down over rock that until recently was lake. The stair is the town's history, read top to bottom, oldest at the crown. The steps near the top are worn to butter by four generations of feet. The newest step is still sharp enough to cut.

Even rising, the island is being eaten — the town knows this too, without dwelling on it. The marsh works the western rim in frost and spate; wavelets undercut, slabs calve off in bad winters, and the quarrymen patch the wound with faggots and fresh stone even as they cut new ground off the east. Rising and eroding at once, and the only real question the town never settles is which is faster. It is a body in every sense: lungs of fog at dawn, a pulse of drips, growth at the rim, wounds that scab grey.

Perhaps because of this, the Shelf builds the way tenants build. The eldest saying on the island — the one they'll give you if you ask about the future — is that *the stone is only borrowing the sky*. The Shelf rose out of the water; someday it will settle back, and everything raised on it should be raiseable, unbuildable in a day if the day comes. So the houses are pegged, not fused; salvage rights are near-sacred; nobody pours their grief into foundations. And when someone dies, since there is no soil to bury in, the town does the reciprocal thing: the dead go out into the marsh weighted with quarried stone, the island giving back to the water what the water once gave it. Four hundred people live on the loan.

A child born on the Shelf this year will grow up thinking the island was always this size. An old woman at the Warm Seam can stand in the square — in the crowd, within arm's reach of six trades — and point at a door-sill and tell you she caught a perch there. Between those two facts is the whole place: steam at dawn, drips all day, the tick of cooling stone at night, and every year, somewhere below the last worn step, one more step down toward the water that remembers being everywhere.
