You hear it long before you can see it.

The plain goes on for days: white, level, mineral — cracked gypsum and salt pan to the horizon, bright as a struck match. Nothing grows. Nothing moves except heat shimmer and the slow grain-walk of dust. Water, here, is a rumor people tell about other worlds. The sun stands overhead like a hammer and the land is the anvil, and the silence of the place is so complete it becomes a texture, a pressure, a kind of weather. Travelers cross it with cloth over their faces and their thoughts ringing in their own skulls, because there is nothing else to listen to.

And then, hours out from nowhere, a note begins.

Not wind. Wind comes and goes; this does not. A single held tone, low and enormous, rising out of the dead flat with no visible source — felt in the sternum before it is heard in the ear, a hum in the teeth, in the boot soles. It grows the way a heartbeat grows when you notice your own. By the time you reach the low ridge of gypsum that breaks the plain, the sound is a chord, and the chord is the size of the sky.

Below the ridge, the world drops away.

**The Bell** is a bowl in the earth — half a mile across, a few hundred feet deep, its walls flared and scalloped like the throat of some colossal instrument, which is precisely what it is. From the middle of the floor you can always see the rim; the whole sky is a mouth; the whole world has walls. And everything inside those walls is *green*.

That is the first shock. The only green in a thousand miles of white, hidden below the level of the dry like a secret kept in a cup. The bowl floods with light — it is not a dim jungle but a luminous one, the canopy thin at the rim and thick toward the center, gold-green shafts falling through it, every leaf edge hard as a knife in the glare. The air is warm and smells of wax and cut stone and something faintly sweet, like a struck bellfruit.

The forest is lush the way a desert is honest: it has never touched a drop of water. Nothing here has. The dominant growth is the **chanter** — a hollow, jointed stalk that rises thirty feet on stems like organ pipes, its skin a turgid waxy green, swollen tight with sap. But the sap is not water. It is a clear cold spirit, light and faintly sweet, that would flash to vapor in the plain's sun if it ever saw the plain's sun. The chanters are sealed flasks, every one. And their roots do not seek water, because there is none to seek; they *eat the bowl itself*. Each root secretes a slow acid that sips the gypsum, and the bowl is drunk up through the forest over centuries — stone becoming leaf, the walls of the world softening into green. The masons of the town below have made a treaty of it: they plaster the foundations each year with bitter ash that the roots decline to cross, and the roots take everything else. Both sides honor the terms. Both sides have been honoring them for longer than anyone's grandmother's grandmother.

There are drumleaves — great taut skins of leaf that thrum in the resonance. There are **poppers**, bladder-pods that swell through the hot morning and detonate at noon in small bright cracks, scattering seed across the dust like a scatter of applause. There is the **caskroot**, a squat barrel of a plant, its trunk layered like a tun, holding forty liters of the clear sap that everything in this place drinks instead of water.

And all of it is singing. This is the second shock, and it never stops being one.

The wind above the Flat is constant, and the Flat is a lid. The wind pours over the scalloped rim of the Bell the way breath pours over the mouth of a flute, and the bowl beneath is the resonating chamber, and the thousands of hollow chanters are the pipes. The whole basin is one instrument, and it is always being played. The sound is not background. It is not ambience. It is *deafening* — a standing chord that presses on the chest, blurs the voice, swallows every small sound the world tries to make. Whispering is impossible here; the chord eats whispers whole. Shouting is worse; it's crude, a pebble thrown into a river. The people who live here — the **Bellfolk** — do not speak at all. They speak in palms, the quick language of hands on hands, and they speak in *taps*: knuckle-codes drummed on stone that travel down through the bowl's rock and rise, legible, out of the floor half a mile away. In the Bell, you hear people through your feet.

Even in dead calm — and there are perhaps three dead-calm hours a year — the bowl does not fully stop. A residual tone remains, a low hum that seismologists would call resonance and the Bellfolk call *the world remembering wind*. Total silence, here, is unknown. Except in one place.

The town sits across the bowl's floor and up its southern flank, and it was clearly built for a crowd that is no longer here. That is the third shock. The streets are wide and the houses stand far apart, each one alone in its stand of green, each one deliberately placed in its own small pocket of acoustic shade — a *beat*, a rest, a hole in the chord where the sound thins and a family can hear its own heartbeats. The gaps between houses are enormous, garden-choked, doors standing open onto rooms the forest has politely entered. A town built for thousands holds, perhaps, two hundred now, and the space between them is enormous: you can walk the whole settlement from rim-side to center and meet no one — and hear everyone. Taps rise out of the flagstones. Roof-drums carry greetings. The town sounds *full*. It looks almost empty. The Bellfolk find nothing sad in this arrangement; density, to them, is a kind of noise, and they are people who understand noise.

The buildings themselves are patient. Gypsum block and mud plaster, roofs of hide and woven fiber laced with cold-hammered copper from the green-stained veins in the bowl's walls. Every tool is a hand tool — copper adzes, chalcedony knives, bone awls, fiber rope. Everything is *layered*: generations of construction stacked and cut back and built over, old doorways silted down to waist height by centuries of drifting white dust, thresholds worn into shallow curves like sea glass. Nothing here is new. Nothing here is in a hurry. The whole settlement has the rounded, settled quality of a river stone — old walls half-eaten by patient roots and half-repaired by patient hands, in a balance so long-established it looks like peace and might actually be peace.

There is one street that is a staircase, and it climbs the bowl's inner wall to the First Notch — a chanter stalk taller than the rest, bearing the oldest tuning cut in the basin, remade and healed over and remade across more generations than the town has records. Because the forest is not left wild; it is *kept*. Every year, after the growth season, the Bellfolk climb with their copper knives for the Tuning Weeks, cutting mouths and notches in the new chanters, pruning, lengthening, closing bore-holes with wax. The town is not built beside the instrument. The town is part of the instrument, and the people are its tuners, and the chord is their ongoing work — a piece of music several thousand years into its composition. The houses have flute-shutters: open a shutter and your dwelling's note bends; a family's address, in the palm-language, is literally a pitch.

The day has a score. At first light the sun heats the eastern rim and the vents open and the low **Wake** begins. By noon the full **Great Chord** stands over everything — the hour of the poppers' applause, when the Bellfolk lie low, do quiet work indoors, press sap, bake the pale bladderpod bread. In the afternoon the caskroots run sweet and the tapping for loudwine starts — a draft of it and the chord sounds warmer, rounder, which the Bellfolk cheerfully admit is half the point. At dusk the air inverts, the pitch sinks by a whole step, the **Evening Cadence** comes through, and the chime-moths rise from the pipe bores in slow silver rivers — the only plentiful animal in the place, and the only one that matters: their mica wings keep the chanters' bores clean, and their wing-dust, shaken onto lamp oil, glows faint gold on the dark. Night thins the chord to a single held thread under stars, and the knucklebirds land on the topmost pipes — there are thirteen of them, gray and heavy, with bony calls like mallets; the Bellfolk count them every night and have counted thirteen for six hundred years, and find this reassuring rather than strange.

There is one strange thing. In the north quarter the old deep pipes cracked, long ago, in an event the town calls the Great Unclenching — a day when the chord is said to have faltered for the first time in memory. That ground has never sung since. The chord cannot enter it. It is the only patch of true silence in the bowl, a held breath of dust and shade, and the Bellfolk keep it as their garden of the dead — burials there, and also trysts, and also anyone who simply needs, for an hour, to be unheard. In a town where silence is impossible, silence is the only privacy. Lovers walk there. Grievers walk there. Almost no one lingers.

Because here is the rule the Bellfolk live by, the one travelers never expect: they do not think their world is too loud. For them, the hum is health — is heartbeat, weather, news, calendar, and god all at once. It is the *plain* that terrifies them. They call the white Flat **the Quiet**, and they regard it the way the rest of us regard graveyards: a place where sound goes to die. When a Bellfolk looks out over the rim at the silent, waterless, unlistening world, they see something obscene. When they come back down the staircase and the chord closes over them like warm water, their shoulders drop an inch.

At night the bowl glows faintly with moth-dusted lamps. The stone gives back the day's heat slowly, and people sleep on rooftops in the open, palms flat on the gypsum, reading the ground — because the taps are still traveling down there, the day's messages rising through the rock like weather, and a Bellfolk can lie awake under a sky that is nearly quiet and still be, through their hands, entirely in conversation.

The traveler who comes over the rim with wax in both ears hears nothing but roar, and calls the place cruel. The traveler who takes the wax out and stays a while begins to hear the chord change as the sun moves, begins to feel the taps in their boot soles, begins to understand that this hidden, hollow, half-empty bowl of green is not a broken place
