# The Lining

The Abrupt is three hundred kilometers of cliff that has not yet learned to be old. The range went up inside the last few thousand years — yesterday, on rock's calendar — and it shows in everything. Ledges end in edges. Boulders sit on their own broken blades. The dust is angular: pour a fistful through your fingers and it catches on the way down, because every grain is a fresh shard, untumbled, unrounded. Rivers never had their turn here. There is no water anywhere in the Abrupt — no seep staining a face, no green thread descending a gully, no cloud that rains. The clouds here are made of rock flour, and they drift like smoke.

The habitable world is far below, down where the air is thick and the depots cluster at the cliff's foot. Up here the wind owns everything. It is called the Scythe, and it combs the top two thousand meters of the Wall without pause, filing the peaks even as they rise — and they are rising; the lowland surveyors measure it in fingers per year. Six great cables, the Spokes, run from the depots up into the glare, and the baskets that ride them are the size of rooms, because everything this high must be imported: iron, reagents, felt, patience.

Nothing marks the way in. You could ride the Wall for a week with your face pressed to the rock and not find it. At dusk, if you happened to be watching the right kilometer at the right hour, you would see the seam betray itself — a stream of grey moths pouring out of a slot the width of a door, thousands of them, rising into the wind-shadow to graze on the flour the Scythe grinds off the peaks. Then pouring back in. The slot is called the Throat, and the moths are the only sign it exists.

Inside the Throat the wind is caught and killed in the Labyrinth — a kilometer of baffle-chambers, felt-lined, angled so that no straight path exists for sound or storm. The Scythe's scream arrives here as a pressure, a leaning of the air. In the Labyrinth's chambers, vane-wheels turn in the ducted wind, slow and greased and nearly silent, and dynamos hum under felt jackets, and the storm is milked three times a day for the electricity that runs everything deeper in.

Then the baffle-dark ends and you come out into the Room, and the Room is the world.

It is a vug — a hollow the size of a town, blown into the rising rock and lined, floor to dome, with crystal. Facets like the panes of a frozen sea, some the size of doors, some of barns, all of them pale and glassy and faintly luminous with the cold light of the vat-cultures set into the streets below. The dome is high enough to hold weather of its own and doesn't quite; instead it holds the city, stacked and hung and bolted to itself in shelves climbing the inner curve. The floor of broken crystal rubble is the Tumble, and the whole settlement — four thousand people, every ward, works, and market — would fit inside a lowland cathedral. You can cross the Lining in twenty minutes, if the galleries are clear. They are never clear.

The city is dense the way a fist is dense. Galleries overhang galleries; hanging streets of cast-crystal plank sway on silk guys; you hear your neighbor's cough through the chitin tile as if it were your own. Buildings are built of what the place exports and eats: iron brought up the Spokes, slag-wool carded from the foundry, tile cut from moth-wing shell, silk from the rack-farms, and pane after pane of cast crystal, amber-thin, that turn every wall into a dim window onto some other family's lamplight.

It is cool here, always, cellar-cool. The rock drinks heat faster than the furnaces can make it; people wear slag-wool coats indoors, layered over silk, and the moths crowd onto the warm exhaust ducts at night like grey weather settling. The light stays low by law and by nature — the ember-glow of the foundry ward, the pooled amber-green of the glow-vats, one lamp for every street, and in between, distances of prismatic dark where a facet catches a lantern and throws it, split, across three streets at once.

The Works are the heart, and the heart is a stomach. In the fixation towers, the air itself is taken apart and rebuilt with crushed rock and electricity into a nutrient paste — amber ribbons of it extruded all day, faintly sweet, smelling of hot dust and scorched sugar. This is the base of the food chain, and it is important to understand that the base of the food chain is a factory. Nothing grows here that was not made here. The vats of glow-culture ferment the paste into protein and light. Everything alive in the Room eats, in the end, from the Works' outflow — and the Room is very alive.

The walls crawl. Saltlice in the millions, small as punctuation, work the streets like a grey tide, eating what falls. Grease-beetles live inside the machinery itself, tolerated, beloved, polishing bearings with their bellies as they go. Tube-spiders spin racks of silk in the warm ducts. Jar-oxen — armored, dry, patient as furniture — haul the cut crystal to the cable-head on their rounded backs, and along the dome at dusk the glassmoths graze the growing tips of the facets, where the new crystal exhales a fine sweet dust. The animals of the Lining carry oil in their veins where other countries carry lymph. They never drink. They chew dust, lick paste, and pass between your feet in the thousands without making a sound you could hear past arm's length.

There are laws, and the first law is hush. A sharp report spalls a facet — a shout is a chisel, a dropped tool is a hammer, and a shard-rain off the dome can gut a ward. So the entire city is engineered toward silence: felted belts, greased gears, induction and wire-saws fed with grit, voices kept low by custom so old it feels physiological. The loudest regular sound in the Lining is ticking. Crystal ticks as it grows and as it cools, a small bright click like a fingernail under a thumbnail, and on quiet nights the whole dome does it — thousands of facets at once, the sound of the house getting bigger.

The second law is dryness. Nothing wet crosses the Throat. Pilgrims' rain-soaked cloaks hang outside on iron pegs like strange fruit; a drop of water is poison here — it clouds a facet forever, rusts the works, and rots the dry fauna from the inside out. Wet is rot, the children are taught. The air you bring in is scrubbed from your lungs by the Labyrinth's salt filters before it is allowed deeper. In the whole city there is not one thing that is damp, and the dryness has its own weather: static. Your hair lifts. Dust climbs your sleeves and clings. Copper ground-posts stand on every corner, polished bright by ten thousand hands, and everyone touches them, always, before touching anything else — because a spark is a chisel too.

The third law is that nothing is permanent, because the Room grows. Crystal cut on Tuesday is back by season's end; some veins surge overnight and swallow a gallery whole. You do not own a wall in the Lining — you rent the face, and the face evicts. The gauge-walkers measure every accessible facet each night with lead-glass rules, logging growth in fingers, and their Table settles the disputes. And once or twice a year, the Room shrugs.

Those are the surge nights, and they are the turbulence that everything here is built around. They come when the Scythe's rhythm falters and the pressure in the Throat drops fast — the Room inhales, the facets cool, and the ticking accelerates from scattered clicks into a rain-sound, then a hiss, then a crack. A vein fattens in hours. A ward evacuates in anthill silence — whole streets folding themselves up in the dark, moths and lice and people and jar-oxen flowing down the shelves like poured sand — and then the cutters come in with their grit-fed wires and spend the night in a hush of falling glass, and by dawn there is new space where a street used to be, raw and ringing and clean. The Night Exchange convenes at first light to auction wall-rights in the fresh room, and this is the closest the Lining comes to a bazaar: a hundred people, six lamps, claims on tomorrow being traded in a whisper, the whole economy a bet on which way the house will grow.

Deepest of all is the Bore, the great down-cut where the crystal comes fast and warm — warm, in this cellar of a world — and where the cutters work at the edge of what can be measured. The surveyors' numbers say the Room is still expanding, riding the same uplift that raised the Wall, a pocket of growth inside a growth. The city's dead go down there, given as flux to the deep cuts, and the Ancestor Facets hold them: faint figures in the far glass, blurred, like something seen through lake ice on a world that has lakes. People visit. They touch the ground-post, then the glass, and stand a while in the low light, and say nothing, which is the custom anyway.

At night the furnaces bank to ember and the vats dim to their minimum glow, and the Lining settles. Moths close their wings on the warm pipes. The salt-tide slows. Somewhere a jar-ox shifts its armor with a sound like a door easing shut, and far off through a kilometer of felt and baffles the Scythe is only a long sigh at the edge of hearing. Underneath everything, continuous, unhurried, the ticking.

The city is young, like its mountain. It lives hidden in the face of a cliff that is still rising, in a hollow that is still filling, and it is quiet because its whole existence is a negotiation conducted in a whisper — the soft lining inside something hard and young and growing, a chamber in the lung of a planet still catching its breath.
