# The Terrace of Weeping Engines

Stand at the rim and the city does not end — it descends. This is a highland place, a plateau lifted so long ago that its uplift is no longer an event but a condition, like the age of a stone. Somewhere far below the visible horizon, plains steam in the warmth; up here the air is mild, thick, and always on the verge of becoming water.

## The Water That Comes From Everywhere

Rain is not the main event here. The main event is condensation. The atmosphere is saturated to the point of laziness — mist that hangs rather than drifts, air you can nearly drink. Every morning, every surface weeps. The terraces of the city are cut with millions of fine channels, a capillary network cut into stone and brick and blackened iron, all of it engineered centuries ago to carry the weeping water somewhere useful. The channels are never dry. They glitter like a nervous system laid bare across the city's skin.

The rocks themselves seep. Water sits near the surface everywhere, a high water table pressing up through the plateau's joints, so that stairways glisten, walls stain dark with permanent damp, and the lower districts have simply accepted that their floors are shallow streams. Nothing rots here, though — because almost nothing here is alive in the way you'd recognize.

## A World Without Green

There is no plant life. Not a grass, not a moss, not a lichen. What covers the wet stone instead is a slow mineral bloom: a film, deep rust-red to violet-black, that grows where water and iron and warmth meet — not a plant, more like the stone sweating color. It spreads millimeters a year. The oldest districts are drowned in it, every surface plush with dark mineral velvet. The newest cuts of stone are pale as bone, and will not be beautiful for a hundred years.

Fungi hold the middle ground: pale, dense, shelf-like growths packed along the water channels, layered like shingles, near-touching, consuming what the condensation brings them. Whole alleys are walled in them. They do not move. They are patient the way geology is patient.

## The City

The settlement is vast and old enough to have become geology itself. It was built — and here is the strange part — by hands or by something like hands, long ago, and it has been rebuilt so many times that the construction is layered like sediment: a first stratum of carved bedrock, then brick, then fired ceramic, then iron, then steel, each era's materials still visible in cross-section where a street cuts down through an older one. Infrastructure-density beyond any planner's intent: pipes over pipes, a bridge beneath a bridge beneath a bridge, ducts and flues and condensation-gutters threading everything together into one enormous damp machine.

It is industrial, but an industry of one product: water. The city harvests its own atmosphere. Condensation farms — slabs of angled metal, kilometers of them, cold-water-fed — rake moisture out of the air day and night. The sound of the place is the sound of that harvesting: the endless ticking and trickling of drip-points, the deep slow churn of pump-halls, the hum of flywheels turning at almost imperceptible speed. It is loud the way a heartbeat is loud when you lie still at night — a great, room-filling, patient noise.

The engines are old and few of them are tended. They run slowly, deliberately, some completing a single revolution in a minute. Nothing here hurries. Change is measured in the migration of a stain across a wall.

## The Fauna

And it is full of animals — this is what no visitor expects. Overwhelmingly full. The absence of plants has not meant an absence of life; it has meant that life here feeds on the mineral film, the fungi, the condensate, the warmth of the machinery, and each other.

Grazers the size of cattle move across the terraces in slow herds, licking the iron-red film off the stones with long gray tongues, their hides the same color as the bloom so that a herd at rest is nearly invisible against the city's flanks. Swarms of small wet-winged things breed in the condensation farms and rise off them at dusk in columns you can see from miles away, thick as smoke. Things like pale flat crabs work the water channels in dense layered crowds, many bodies deep, filtering the flow. In the flooded lower districts, wide slow shapes move through the street-streams, visible only as ripples.

The animals have integrated with the machinery completely. Nests in the flues. Burrows in the warm brick behind the pump-halls. The grazers shelter in the engine rooms in cold snaps and nobody, or nothing, removes them. The city has become a reef, and the fauna its coral fish, and they are everywhere — every surface within reach is worn smooth by generations of bodies rubbing past.

## The Balance

What strikes you finally is that nothing here is in decline. This is a system in its own long-settled state: an old machine, an old wetness, an old abundance, all weathered into one another so completely that the boundary between city and terrain, between engineering and ecology, has dissolved. Erosion has already done its work and reached its agreements. The plateau, the mist, the mineral bloom, the slow engines, the drifting herds — all of it turns together at the same lazy rate, a vast warm humid clock, and it has been showing this particular hour for a very, very long time.
