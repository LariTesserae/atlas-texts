# The Table

From the valleys, you climb for two days through forest, then through cloud, and the ascent ends not at a summit but at a cessation — the world simply stops going up. What you have reached is the end of a mountain range. There were peaks here once; ten of them, high enough that the first rivers were born on their shoulders. Erosion had a million years to work, and erosion finished. What remains is a plane. Three thousand meters up, warm in the sun, flat as a threshing floor, running a hundred and fifty kilometers in every direction toward a horizon drawn with a straightedge.

The people of the valleys call it the Table. They are wrong about almost everything concerning it, but not about that.

The strangeness of the place announces itself in your first ten steps, because the ground gives under your feet like the deck of a boat. There is no soil here. There is no stone, either, not at the surface — if you dug anywhere on those hundred and fifty kilometers, you would find root before rock. The entire Table is one plant: a fig of a banyan habit that grows on stone and makes its own ground faster than weather can remove it. Trunks rose, toppled, layered, fused; aerial roots walked out and became pillars; the canopy, with nothing left to climb toward, collapsed sideways and became a floor. Where hollow trunks stand or lie, they have been rubbed smooth by rain and feet into halls and corridors. The bark underfoot is taut as a drumhead and warm — warm at midnight, warm in rain — because the mat's interior is composting, and all that slow digestion rises through the wood like heat off a flank. The whole Table is faintly, permanently warm, the way a sleeping animal is warm, and it smells of cut fruit and wet wood and something green that has been green for a very long time.

## How water moves here

Rain comes rarely, a few violent weeks a year. Rain is not how this place drinks.

Every morning the cloud sheet that lives below the Table rises with the sun, and for three or four hours the whole plateau sits inside the cloud. The fig is built for this. Its leaves are hooded and haired, needle-fine, combs for fog; droplets gather at leaf tips, run down ridge-roots, collect in channels the plant has raised for the purpose. The rivers of the Table are indoors — they run through the mat's hollows, thread along corridor floors, pool in the round chambers where the roots dip. It is a country that rains from its own leaves.

And this year, this particular year, there is a stranger mechanism, which the people have watched all their lives without naming: the loudest hours are the wettest hours. When the roar below peaks, the vibration shakes the fog loose. Water falls indoors, out of a sky that is not there. Sound makes rain.

## The roar

Because you should understand about the roar. It is why you have perhaps heard of the Table, and it is the true weather of the place.

Under the mat — in the fourth inch, the fortieth, the four-hundredth — the cicadas have been living for nineteen years. Nymphs, pale, blind, their mouthparts set into root, drinking sap from the one body that is also the ground and the mountains' bones. Nineteen years is a prime number of years, and the broods of periodical insects like primes, and — this is the part that is not fully understood — they keep a *shared* clock. They are, in effect, one enormous distributed brood, synchronized because they drink from the same root system. The whole Table keeps one calendar.

This is the year the calendar ends. They are coming up.

The night of the Rising, the floor softens with exit holes — thousands to the pace, mud turrets pushed up like tiny chimneys — and the nymphs climb anything vertical and split out of their own backs, and by morning the pale ones are hardening into adults, and the adults begin.

The sound is not a noise. It is a climate. It comes from everywhere, because there is nothing for it to echo off — no cliff, no ravine, no summit, nothing for a hundred and fifty kilometers that could give the roar a location. It is layered: a dry hiss underneath like the sea heard from far inside a shell, and over that a sawing, ringing whine that wavers when the thermals shift. You feel it in your sternum before you hear it. Standing water rings concentrically in pots. And because the broods on different patches of mat synchronize imperfectly, the roar travels in *waves* — you can stand on a hall's threshold and watch a wave of sound approach from kilometers away, visible as a moving shimmer of wings, a line of glitter rolling across the floor like wind over wheat, arriving, drowning you, passing on.

There are trillions. During these weeks the people do not speak — they could not. They use the hand-language, the sign-days, that every child of the Table learns before it learns words for its kin.

On hot days it rains upward, too: a fine warm drizzle from a cloudless sky, flung by ten million throats purging xylem. The sweat of the chorus, settling on your arms.

## Everything that eats

Nothing that can reach the Table misses it. Kites come up from the valleys in loose ribbons, a hundred birds wide. Bee-eaters and rollers arrive in a kind of gorged stupor — the predators get fat and then get bored; the emergence is simply larger than their appetite, which is the whole strategy. Ants herd the younger nymphs for their sweet excretions. Foxes grow round as badgers. Herons appear where there is no water, and stay for weeks, and nobody asks them why.

There is a wasp that drills the nymphs in their turrets, and the fig's own tiny wasp, the true tenant of nineteen quiet years, going about its ancient contract with the flowers. And there are the others — the ones the people will not eat, the hollow-bellied, whose abdomens have been quietly replaced by a white sporing body, who fly and sing perfectly well with a fungus where their insides were. The huskers knock those down when they find them and bury them at the rim. Not out of fear. Out of a courtesy nobody explains.

## The huskers

There are a few hundred people on the whole Table — that is the paradox of the place, that everything above knee height for a day's walk may be one hall, one family, a rack of drying fiber, a cairn at the southern rim where the plane finally breaks and the valleys begin. The emptiness is real. Everything that lives here lives in one of three places: under the mat's skin, in the mat's skin, or in the low thousand feet of air above it. Between those layers, the vacancy — and the vacancy is what makes the sound possible. Nothing stands in the roar's way.

The huskers' halls are not built. They are *coppiced* — grown, over years, by bending a corridor's roots where you want a doorway and waiting. Their tools are stone edges, bone needles, fiber, clay-lined fire-pits; their wealth is husk-meal and dried adults and the sugar of honeydew, traded down-valley in Loud Years. They keep time in the fig itself: every chorus year, the die-off manures the plant, and the ring the fig lays down that year runs fat. Split any old trunk and count back by nineteens — the wood remembers every Roar. A notch-stick planted at a child's birth; the child will be nineteen, thirty-eight, fifty-seven at the next ones. On the Table, time is not kept by the sky. It is kept in the grain of the ground.

## Rules of the table

Nothing here echoes. The people navigate by sun and by the fog's drift, never by sound.

The floor is the roof of the world and the ceiling of the ground, and it is warm, and you sleep on it like sleeping on the flank of something slow.

The quiet years are the same place wearing silence: fog-drip ticking in the hollows, the mat creaking as it cools at dusk, wind moving across a plane with nothing to strike. Nineteen years of that. Then this — the floor opening, the sky filling, the fog falling indoors, the horizon's straight line lost at dusk in a glow of sound.

If you stand at the rim at evening, you are standing on the last remnant of a mountain, held together by a plant, in the middle of a sound that took nineteen years to arrive and will take nineteen more to come again. The huskers say the Table is patient. They are wrong about almost nothing concerning it, but not about that.
