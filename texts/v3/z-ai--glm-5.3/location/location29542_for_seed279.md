You hear it before you see it. From the rim of the low hills, while the landscape below is still just a gray suggestion under a gray sky, the sound arrives — a wide, sustained roar with a floor to it, like rain striking a lake, like a waterfall heard through several walls, like the sea with the sea's rhythm removed. It does not swell or fade. It stands. The Tuners, whose marks are scattered thinly across this place, are said to have called it simply the Loudness, and the basin that makes it, Choirbottom.

The basin is a lake's abandoned bed, and it is young — the whole place has the rawness of something that finished becoming itself only a little while ago. Somewhere within the last few centuries, the water found lower roads out through the soft limestone underneath and left, draining down into a dark it chose for itself. What remains is a floor of pale clay cracked into vast polygons with edges so sharp they'll cut a boot sole, still glossy-wet along their seams. The cracks widen a little every dry season. Nothing here has been rounded yet by weather. Fracture faces show fresh, mineral-pale, as if the stone broke last week, and the spires — the spires especially — stand with the unnerving crispness of things that have not existed long enough to be forgiven.

Because the groves are the first thing you understand once you descend, after you stop flinching from the sound. Across the flats, in thickets and open ranks and near-touching clusters, rise hundreds — thousands — of tapering columns of cream-and-gray stone, some knee-high, some past a person's height, each one honeycombed, each one wet, each one glistening as though just pulled from the water. They are tufa, built the way tufa is built: warm mineral water rises along old fault-lines, vents up through the lakebed, meets air, and lets go of its lime. The stone precipitates around each vent in porous walls that thicken year by year while the throat of the vent stays open. So every spire is a pipe. Every pipe has wind across its mouth and water glugging somewhere in its throat. And the Loudness is the sum of them all — a standing chord, wind-bent, hundreds of notes deep, from a wet hiss like frying in the high registers down through fluted moans to a bass that is less heard than felt, a slow drumming in the ground you notice in your sternum and the roots of your teeth.

The density of the place is like this: you can see for miles, the whole vent-field spread flat to its ring of low hills, the spires thin as fingers and reeds against the mist, yet within a grove the pipes stand so close you must turn sideways between them, sidling past wet stone that sighs on either side of your head. Layer behind layer behind layer. It is a crowd that parts for no one, an open country you move through one squeezed channel at a time.

Everything is wet. Not pooled — filmed. The water here does not gather so much as cling. Every vertical surface weeps; condensation beads and runs on the spires in slow threads that find the old channels and follow them down; the clay flats wear a skin of damp that takes your footprint like mud without yielding like mud. At the basin's rim, where the ground begins to rise, the water table comes to meet the surface outright — seep-lines called the Weeps, glistening fronts where the hillside darkens and minute runnels braid down through fresh, sharp rills. The spring water comes up warm from its deep circulation, and the air is cool, and the marriage of the two makes the atmosphere: a permanent mist, standing in slow columns above the strongest vents like the smoke of enormous fires, rising when you'd expect vapor to sink. Light 0.7 shows itself as a gray-violet, shadowless wash under a low ceiling — day at its brightest is a long dusk; colors stay in the minerals, in cream and rust and iron-brown; your own shadow is a rumor. When true dark comes, the strongest vents hold a faint blue-green pallor in their vapor, less a glow than an admission.

Rain falls here sometimes, cold and thin, and you learn of it only by touch. It vanishes into the Loudness the way a whisper vanishes into a bell.

There are no plants. Not a moss, not a blade, not a seedling — the flats are mineral and the groves are mineral and the visitor's eye searches and searches for green and finds only, along the warm runnels, a dark slick film that most eyes take for stain. It is simpler than moss, older than moss, a mat of things that eat the chemistry of the springs. From this film a short and reluctant food chain hangs: in the shallow brine pans of the lowest flat, seasonal swimmers no longer than a fingernail, pale and eyeless, appearing some years and not others; and, very rarely, at the dim hours, a wader — a long-legged, porcelain-white thing that stands between the pipes and works the film with an unhurried bill, its proportions a degree or two off from any heron you've seen, its arrival soundless because the Loudness eats every sound. Almost nothing else comes. Most animals cannot bear the volume. The Loudness keeps the country empty, and this, more than the barren ground, is why the place feels held in reserve.

The strangest rule of Choirbottom is that its stone is alive on the only timescale it honors. The pipes grow. Finger-widths a year; a spire you could cap with your palm when the Tuners first raised their cairns now stands over your head. And as the walls thicken, the throats narrow, and each pipe's note bends downward — slowly, a step in a lifetime — so the whole chord is sinking, has been sinking since the lake left. Some pipes die outright, closing with a last gurgle and a thin mineral skin over the mouth. The Loudness is, among other things, a very slow diminuendo.

Which brings you to the scattered marks, the built residue at 1.2 and no higher: no machines, no metals, nothing that would count as a tool. The Tuners worked with hands and water, which is enough when the stone is this soft. Their cairn-caps sit on perhaps one pipe in forty — a selected stone balanced or seated in a vent's mouth to pinch the tone, steady it, lift it. Their path survives along the rim, set stones sunk into clay, re-laid and re-laid over generations as the spires crept. On certain pipe-rims there are patches polished dark and glassy, worn by skin oil over centuries of resting hands: faint marks in the most literal sense — the residue of touch as devotion. And alone on the rise at the basin's southern lip stands the Cell, a low corbelled dome of hand-fitted tufa knobs with a crawlway entrance and a single vent captured beneath its floor. Inside, the crawlway filters out the chord the way a throat filters a whisper, and there is one note. Just one, steady, warm, filling the little dark. Whatever the Tuners listened for, they built a room in which it could finally be heard alone.

Their doctrine, as much as anyone has reconstructed it: the chord is the water speaking; the Loudness is the sound of the lake leaving; when the last pipe fails and the basin falls silent, the water is finished withdrawing and the silence will be its signature. They did not leave because the place failed. They left following the falling note, or they didn't leave, or they are still somewhere below the flats where the water went, listening at the drowned ends of the pipes. No one is here to ask. The caps stay where they were set. The stone keeps taking the polish of rain.

Walk carefully and follow the rules the ground enforces itself: never cap a vent with your palm — the pull is real and the water is warm and relentless; step flat-footed on the polygons and never on a seam; touch the spires lightly, because pressure on a wet pipe can drop its note, and Choirbottom holds a grudge about pitch. Time here is not kept in days. It is kept in frequency — the locals-if-there-were-any would tell you their grandparents heard this country a full tone higher than you hear it now, and they would be right.

At the long gray dusk that passes here for evening, standing hip-deep in the grove with the mist columns standing too, you feel the truth of the place settle on you the way the condensation settles on the stone: this is a landscape in the middle of a single protracted event — a lake departing, singing on its way out, its exit amplified by the architecture it built for its own voice. The sound is not weather here. It is the fact against which all other facts are measured. And somewhere under the chord, beneath hearing, one more pipe's note has slipped half a step lower than it was last year.
