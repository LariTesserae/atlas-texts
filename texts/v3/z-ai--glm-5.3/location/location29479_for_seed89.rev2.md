Above the last sedge terraces the mountain stops pretending to be habitable. The rock turns black and wind-scoured, glazed with ice that never fully melts; the air is too thin for almost everything with lungs. For a day's walk in any direction there is no green thing, and no sound but wind working over stone. And yet on the east shoulder, on clear nights, a thread of vapor rises from the base of a cliff — a silver column you could cover with your thumb from a distance, which is to say, no one has ever covered it with a thumb. It rises from a rift, a slit tall enough to walk through, whose rim is crusted with dark, iridescent lichen. The only green for miles is just inside: a collar of algae drinking the gray light at the mouth, a hand's-breadth of photosynthesis, and then no more.

Follow the plume down and the mountain gives up its secret: it is still warm along one seam. The massif is an old, almost quiet thing, but a fissure of deep rock heat leaks upward through its ribs, and one chamber happens to sit over the leak. While the outside air falls below minus thirty at night, the room holds steady at the warmth of a sleeping body. Everything else about this place is a consequence of that fact. Heat is the law here.

The chamber itself is small — cathedral-sized, if a cathedral were carved by water and lit by nothing. Forty paces long, twenty wide, a ceiling no thrown stone could reach. The walls are scalloped, carved smooth by currents that vanished so long ago the scallops now only record which way an ancient flood ran. Everything that was ever going to fall has fallen: the breakdown boulders sit settled in their own sockets, dust-saturated, mossless, finished. There is one way in, and a tight fissure above where warm air escapes but bodies cannot. You could learn the whole cave in an hour. The ones who live here have had eight thousand years to learn it better.

Because the cave's defining material is not stone. It is animal. The smell arrives first — ammonia sweetened by the labor of beetles, warm fur, wet chalk, a faint sulfur note from the seam below. Then the sound. Then, mostly, nothing, because the dark does not give up its sight. But if you stand long enough for your eyes to surrender, the upper third of the walls and most of the ceiling resolve into texture, and the texture is woolly gray-gold monkeys, thousands upon thousands of them, sleeping in heaps. Frost beads in their brow fur where their breath has condensed. They sleep in contact, layered, an interior and a rim; through the night the rim trades slowly inward and the core drifts slowly out, a rotation no one enforces and no one cheats, so that no body spends the coldest hours on the outside and no body hoards the warm middle. The infants lie deep in the core. The old, who run hot, prefer the edge. Thirty thousand animals is not a number you can see. It is a number you can feel, the way you feel a fever. The heap breathes in a slow surf rhythm, and its combined heat is the room's weather.

Their breath is also the room's rain. Every exhale rises, meets stone colder than the lungs that made it, and surrenders its water. The ceiling sweats. Drops gather in the fur overhead and fall warm on the sleepers, who are built for it — guard hairs shed the drips down toward their neighbors — while along the walls the water routes through scallop-channels into three rimstone basins, drinking pools, each with its own mineral note: one chalky, one iron-bitter, one faintly sulfured from the seam. The drops ring as they land, each with a pitch depending on its size and its pool, so that all night the chamber plays a slow random music. The only rain for miles falls indoors, and it is made of breath. When a sleeper wakes dry-mouthed, it chews a wad of lichen to a pulp, presses the sponge against a sweating wall, and wrings it over its tongue — a tool that exists only while wet, is abandoned, and is remade next time from scratch. One wall, in addition, weeps snowmelt for six weeks each summer, a vein of cold water crossing a country of warm. And once a decade or so a storm rams water down the entrance sink and the floor floods to the knees. The floor is graded for it; the colony climbs the flood-ledges and waits a day. The flood is less an event than an appointment, one the cave has kept longer than any calendar.

The floor deserves its own weather report. It is guano, in places three meters deep, and it is in balance: what falls is eaten, fungal-processed, compressed, mineralized; the floor neither rises nor sinks in a beetle's lifetime, though in ten beetle lifetimes it rises a little. The beetles click like green wood in a fire, in the millions. Mites move across the hummocks in numbers that make the surface appear to shiver. Pale fungal threads bind everything, gardens without a gardener. And everywhere, drifted like gray snow, are the moths — because the plume is a highway. All night the cave exhales its warm breath through the slit, and moths from the valleys below ride the column upward. Some turn back. Some come in, meet the dark, and die here, and their bodies are the room's only import. Small chalk-white snails graze the fungus at the rims of the pools, species that exist nowhere else and have never seen light. In one alcove, threads of cold greenish light hang from the ceiling: the snares of a gnat larva, fishing for the moth-fall. They are the only lamps in the cave, and they are traps, and nothing here mistakes them for anything else. The green stops at the mouth. Inside, color gave up long ago. The palette is stone, chalk, fungus-white, and fur-gold, and the brightest thing in the cave is animal.

The technology, such as it is, belongs to the dark hours. A few sleepers wake hungry and work by feel. They know the anvil-steps — natural ledges, never altered — and the recesses where the hammer-stones are kept: river cobbles carried up from miles below, generations ago, each shaped by wear to a particular hand. A snail is set on the anvil; tick; crack. Mothers teach juveniles by leaving shells half-cracked, the way a door is left open. When a stone is dropped it sinks into the floor, and sometimes a flood or a beetle excavation returns it to circulation a century later. The stones have lineages, remembered in the hands that remember them. This is the whole of the technology: held, not built. Nothing in the cave is assembled. Nothing is made — things are used, worn, lost, and used again. If an artifact is an object shaped by intention, then the cave contains only intention shaped by habit. The monkeys have no name for the place. Names are not their kind of tool.

And it is loud — a strange thing to say about a room under a mountain, when outside the only sound for miles is wind on rock. The mouth is a bottle the mountain plays: wind across the slit makes a drone whose pitch carries the weather, and the colony reads coming storms in it hours before the clouds arrive. Beneath the drone, the massed breath in its surf rhythm; snores; the chirped double note, hup, hup, that sleepers exchange all night like a heartbeat distributed among thirty thousand chests — the sound of the dark being comfortable. Anvils ticking. The granular static of beetles. Moth wings buffeting in the dark. Drops ringing in the pools. The chamber's echo smears all of it into one continuous fabric, so that the loudness is not noise; it is climate. Silence is the alarm. If the huddle ever went quiet it would mean something large had come in, and nothing large ever comes in, because the entrance is small and the mountain is empty. Still, they keep the rule.

Other presences are few and settled in. Finches roost in the twilight band at the mouth and pay rent in stolen moths. A slender snake lives in the seam fissure above, born inside, and has never once felt weather; on warm nights it patrols the floor for beetle and moth, and the colony ignores it the way a household ignores a clock.

The rhythms close over the place in layers of slowness. Daily: before dawn the huddle pours down the scree to the sedge terraces on the mountain's lower apron, the only soft ground for leagues, and the room empties and keeps its own time in drips until dusk thickens the plume and they climb home. Yearly: births swell the numbers and the rim grows outward by a row of bodies. Century by century: the breath-fed flowstone creeps over the guano strata, a hair's width at a time, so that the wall is slowly laminating the colony into white stone with dark seams — an archive no one will ever read. Millennium by millennium: the seam itself is cooling, a fraction at a time. Someday the room will be merely cold, and the huddle will have to find another seam or become something else. That day is very far off. Nothing here is in a hurry. The cave finished falling, finished flooding, finished becoming itself, and what remains is maintenance.

Night, now: outside, the sky sits close and crammed with stars, the cold so clean it hurts; inside, thirty thousand sleep in a heap of their own weather while their breath falls back on them as warm rain. The plume rises into the starlight, visible from nowhere, since no one is watching, and the moths ride it up toward the one warm room on the mountain. Nothing in this place was built. The cave is an accident of deep water and deep heat; the colony is a consequence of the heat; the water is a consequence of the colony. The mountain breathes through its one warm room, and the dark inside is not empty. It is fully occupied.
