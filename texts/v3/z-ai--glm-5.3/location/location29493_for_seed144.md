You come up to it from below, and for most of the climb there is nothing to see — the land tilts upward through dry scrub until the scrub gives out, and then the road switchbacks through bare rock for a long time, and then the road ends, and there it is: a table of land so wide the far edge is haze, standing in the sky at a height where the air has been thinned like paint. The clouds you climbed through to get here are below you now. That is the first thing to understand about the plateau: the weather lives underneath it.

The land itself is old past argument. It's the laid-flat innards of an ancient volcano — pale welded ash, cream and oatmeal colored, fretted into shallow basins and long benches, everything worn round at the corners. Nothing here is young. The boulders sit balanced on their pedestals where they have been sitting for ten thousand years, the canyons are incised and then abandoned, and the whole tableland has arrived at a shape it intends to keep. Erosion is not a process here anymore; it's a finished argument. The plateau is in balance with its own weather, which is mostly the sun.

And the sun is serious. There is so little air above you that it can't hold heat; the light arrives unfiltered and does its work directly on the stone. By mid-morning the rock is too hot to put a hand on; by afternoon it hurts through boot soles. And yet the air itself never feels as hot as the ground — you can stand in shade, ten steps from blazing rock, and be genuinely cold. This is the plateau's fundamental trick: heat comes from below, not from around. The sun is a landlord; the air is not. Sweat evaporates off you before it can bead. Water boils early, sulking — the pumphouse kettle gives up well before it should, and tea never gets properly angry.

Because there are no rivers. That's the second thing. No streams, no creeks, no springs worth the name except a few mineral ones. What water exists comes sideways or from above, in installments.

The main installment arrives every evening, and it is the strangest habit of the place: the fog tide. The lowlands below stew all day, and at dusk their cloud sea rises — you can stand on the rim and watch it come, a slow gray flood pouring through the low gaps in the rock like water finding a sill. It fills the basins overnight to a depth of a few hundred feet, beads on every surface, and then at dawn it drains back down the way it came, retreating down the canyons with the sun behind it. On fog nights the temperature drops and the whole plateau sweats: the stones, the cables, the iron, all of it filmed with condensation until first light, when every surface runs with a skin of water that the heat burns off in an hour.

The people learned this a long time ago, and then, later, they industrialized it. First there were the old fog walls — dry-stacked stone fences, waist-high, lichened gray, built by whoever was here before anyone remembers. Fog condenses on stone, runs down, pools at the base. Those walls still work. They still drip on fog nights, standing in rows across the flats like the bones of an older idea.

The newer idea is the Condensery, and it is the biggest thing on the plateau while being made of almost nothing: hundreds of long racks of galvanized mesh on red steel frames, four stories tall, set in ranks downwind of the main fog gap. Fog passes through the mesh and leaves its water behind, and the water runs to gutters and the gutters run to cisterns and the cisterns run to a pipeline, and in the thin quiet of the night the whole array plays — each gutter has its own pitch depending on its length, so a fog night is a sparse, random plinking music, note by note, drop by drop. The drip crews walk the rows with lamps and can navigate the Condensery blind, by ear, like piano keys. Every drop is metered. There is a ledger. At the tramway head at the plateau's edge, tank buckets go down the wire to the thirsty towns below full of yesterday's cloud, and come back up with diesel, timber, flour. The plateau's fortune inverted, somebody will tell you: they quit mining the rock and started mining the sky.

The rock mining is still up there in the scars anyway — old adits near the black cinder cone they call the Cup, a stub of smelter chimney on the far bench, harmonica-ruined and rust-welded, abandoned so long ago it has become terrain. The spoil heaps of that era have a habit the animals know well: the ore was full of a hygroscopic salt, a mineral that drinks the air, and on fog nights the heaps weep.
