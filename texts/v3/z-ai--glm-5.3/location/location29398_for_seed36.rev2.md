# The Greenheart Cavity

Beneath a landscape of dry scrub and cracked hills — a land that gives no hint of what hangs below it — there is a chamber the size of a valley turned inside out. It opened recently, geologically speaking. The rock here is still being born: raw basaltic walls soft with mineral milk, unweathered, untested, wearing the wet sheen of a thing that has only just stopped being magma. No erosion has touched these surfaces. Nothing has had time to wear anything down.

## The Light

The chamber is bright, and this is the first wrongness — or the first wonder, depending on how you take it. There is no sun here, yet the space is lit like a greenhouse at noon. The light comes from the plants themselves. Every surface of the cavity is sheathed in a mat of luminous vegetation — a dense, layered photosynthetic turf that glows a warm, steady yellow-white from within its own tissues, as if chlorophyll here learned to do the sun's job directly. The glow is even, patient, and it does not flicker. It has not flickered, perhaps, for a very long time.

## The Architecture of Plants

Plant is not a feature of this place; plant is the place. The floor is not rock but a meters-deep living substrate, spongy and warm, threaded with rhizomes thick as a person's arm. Columns of swollen, woody-stemmed growths rise toward the ceiling — not quite trees, more like vertical organs of the same enormous body, their surfaces furred with light. Where the ceiling arches overhead, roots and hanging mats descend in curtains, glowing faintly, dividing the chamber into irregular grotto-sized rooms without ever closing anything off. The space is dense the way a lung is dense: every volume claimed, every cubic meter occupied by something green and warm, and yet permeable — you can walk through it all, through corridors the plants have made without anyone asking them to.

## Heat and Water

The heat rises from below, from the still-forming rock, and the plants drink it. It is a greenhouse held at the temperature of a fever — sweat-through-your-clothes hot, humid, close. Water exists here as condensation more than as flow: droplets gather on the glowing leaves and on the glassy young rock, hang, and fall. There is no stream, no pool of note — only a constant gentle rain of condensate, drops landing on the living floor with a soft, irregular percussion. That drip, and the almost sub-audible hum of the light — a sound you feel in your teeth more than hear — are the only sounds. Nothing moves. Nothing calls. The silence is thick enough to rest your hands on.

## Life, Sparse and Slow

Fauna is nearly absent. There are — maybe — small pale grazers in the depths of the mat, glimpsed once and never confirmed: something that browses the luminous turf and keeps it cropped to a perfect, even lawn. There are certainly spores, drifting on air that barely moves. And there is one structure, a single artifact in all this green: a rough dome of fitted stone near the chamber's center, half-swallowed by the mat, its purpose unknowable, its builders long gone. It is warm to the touch and the plants grow around it rather than over it, as if in deference.

## The Rule of the Place

The deepest rule here is stasis. This is a world in frozen equilibrium — a self-lit, self-heated, self-watering terrarium that reached its final form early and has simply continued. Nothing grows visibly. Nothing decays visibly. The condensation falls, the light glows, the heat holds. The embryonic rock beneath will not erupt, will not cool further, will not change. It is a landscape paused at the moment of its completion — a place that came into being all at once, hot and bright and green, and then decided, for reasons of its own, that it was done.
