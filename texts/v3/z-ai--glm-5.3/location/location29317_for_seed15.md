The first flood of the year goes under the city rather than through it. Up on the plaza the pavement is pale, sun-hot, empty at midday — and in the middle of it a square mouth opens in the stone, ringed with a rim so freshly cut that its edges haven't yet learned to be kind. The corners are still sharp enough to bite a palm. Down through that mouth, faint at first and then not faint at all, comes the roar of water falling into a room.

The descent is a staircase of dressed sandstone, thirty-one treads, each one chiseled and set by hand. The sound doubles with every turn of the stair, and the air changes with it: the dry heat of the plaza gives way to a thick, humid warmth that rises like breath from below, carrying the smell of wet dust, fresh lime mortar, and cut stone — the smell of a thing that was finished only weeks ago and has not yet had time to become anything other than itself. Under the pavement, in a hollow the size of a modest hall, the flood is arriving.

The chamber is small by the standards of what it does. Forty paces would cross it. The vaulted ceiling of fired brick sits low enough that a tall man could press his hand to it, and the whole room is swallowed — embedded in the city's floor like a held breath. There is nothing natural here, nowhere. The walls are brick and coursed ashlar, the floor is paved in fitted slabs, the benches along the walls are cut single blocks, the columns that hold the vault up out of the water are eight-sided, fluted by chisels whose marks still stand in the stone like handwriting. Even the darkness at the corners is a made darkness, the shadow of made things. The world in this room is entirely an artifact, and the flood is its first tenant.

Water already owns the lower half. The bottom steps of a second, interior stair vanish into brown-gold floodwater that trembles with the movement of the room. Along one wall, high up, the mouth of the inflow channel is a black arch from which a thick rope of riverwater falls in an unbroken column, hitting the pool with a sound like heavy cloth tearing, endlessly. The plunge churns a white crater into the surface. Silt-brown at the inflow, the water clarifies by degrees toward the far side, where it lies clear enough that the flooded paving below is visible — pale slabs, a drowned bench, the upper steps of the interior stair going down into green-gold murk. The room is mostly water now, and mostly emptiness, and this sparseness is not a lack but a design: wide spaces between the columns, long bare stretches of wall, one coil of rope, one wooden mallet left on the highest dry step. The emptiness is balanced against the water like ballast.

And the room is bright — bright as a courtyard, brighter than the alleys above. The open shaft behind the stair holds a standing column of noon sun that lands on the pool and shatters; the whole underside of the brick vault is netted with moving light, caustics crawling and folding like slow lace, so that the ceiling appears to be underwater even though it is bone-dry. Low in the walls, at what would be ground level if the room had a ground, a ring of small openings has been closed with panes of stone shaved so thin it has gone translucent — gypsum, alabaster — and these glow. Some hold light warm as honey, some white as paper, and where the sun finds their outer faces they throw soft shapes onto the water. The builders placed them knowing this. The room is one of the brightest places in the city and no one lives in it.

It is also one of the loudest. The falling inflow is the main voice, a steady roar that the hard, young surfaces refuse to absorb — nothing in the room is soft yet, nothing worn enough to swallow sound. Over and under it run the smaller processes: air being pushed out ahead of the rising water, escaping through a clay vent-pipe in the vault with a long, intermittent gurgle, like a jar being emptied. A wooden float-tally on the near wall knocking against its stop each time a swell passes. Somewhere behind the brick, the great sluice gate — cedar, pegged, counterweighted with stones in a rope harness — groans as the pressure leans on it and eases, leans and eases, in the rhythm of the flood. Drips from the vault find the pool with flat, percussive slaps. The room is loud the way a drum is loud: not because of what's happening, but because of what it's made of.

The marks of making are everywhere, because the water has only just begun to erase them. Red ochre level-lines scored along the walls, half washed away on the lower courses. String-guides scratched into the column bases. In a spill of mortar near the arch, four fingerprints pressed by a hand that steadied itself, and never came back to clean up. A rope knot, left tied. The chisel scars on the stair treads come in four distinct rhythms — four masons, four chisels, each edge slightly its own. The most recent thing in the room, older only than the water, is the gauge: a ladder of short horizontal cuts scratched into one wall from the paving up, and against this the water is climbing, hand-width by hand-width, counting itself.

That count is the rule of the whole place. Flood season puts water in; the level rises along the gauge, and when it stands at the uppermost mark the sluice will be winched and the arch closed, and the pool will wait under the plaza, warm as a kept thing. Dry season takes it out a thread at a time, through a clay delivery pipe that runs up and opens at a public tap in the plaza shade, where the whole city will drink this room without ever descending into it. The gatekeeper comes at dusk and dawn, checks the counterweight, reads the gauge, goes. The room is built to be drowned and drained a hundred times and to grow old doing it — but not yet. Right now it is on its first day of the only work it will ever do.

Life has barely found the place. A single green twig rides the plunge-water and was carried, spinning, out to the calm side. In a pocket of silt caught on the second step below the waterline, three pale seeds have already cracked and shown white rootlets; a film of green so thin it is more rumor than algae has begun at the sunlit edge. That is all. No larvae hang beneath the surface, no water-beetles row through the light, no fish — the room is too new; the water arrived before anything that lives in water could arrive with it. For now the room's only movements are water, light, and the settling of silt, which falls through the clarifying gold in slow veils, hour by hour polishing the water toward stillness.

The warmth is the flood's gift and the sun's. The inflow comes out of a heated landscape, and the vaulted stone still holds the warmth of the days it was built in, so that the chamber exhales heat from every surface — the water near the arch is warm as a bath, the air warmer than the plaza above, and the light itself seems to have temperature. If you sat on the top dry step at noon, in the roar, in the moving lace of light, you would be sitting inside something between a machine and a cradle: a room made entirely by hands, with hand tools, from stone and brick and clay and cedar and rope — a room whose whole purpose is to be full when the world above is empty of rain, and whose whole present is this first filling. It will never be this loud again. It will never again be this new. The water climbs to the third mark, holds, and climbs past it, and the room keeps counting.
