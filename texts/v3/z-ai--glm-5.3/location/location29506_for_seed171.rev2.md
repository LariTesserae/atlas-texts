You hear it long before you see it. From thirty kilometers out, over lowland flats that are still honest dirt and quiet sky, the Stand announces itself as a roar with grain to it — not surf, not wind, nothing cyclic or mindless. It has texture. A stadium applauding underwater. The rigging of ten thousand ships in a long gale. Fat spitting in a pan the size of the sky. It does not get louder gradually as you approach; it gets louder in steps, each step a new component surfacing out of the roar, until the roar has become a place.

The rim of the plateau is bare rock, and the rock is new — so new it still has edges. Feldspar glitters with unweathered faces. Nothing here has been rubbed round or browned by air; the minerals are sharp as the day the uplift cracked them loose, and they weep. Capillary water stands on every face in a cold film, sweating out of stone that hasn't learned yet whether it means to be dry. You are at two thousand four hundred meters, in air thin enough that shouting costs breath, and over the lip of the world the water leaves the continent in curtains: miles of it, pouring off the plateau edge in long white sheets, falling so far they become mist before they become rivers. The Weeps, the whole perimeter of them, feed a cloud bank that sits on the high land like a garment laid over shoulders.

Climb over the lip and you go inside the weather. There is no sky in the Stand. There is the Cloth — a continuous canopy surface a hundred meters up, a fabric of leaves so interlocked that it reads as one leaf the size of a sea — and beneath it the light comes down already used, green-filtered, secondhand, about as bright as a heavily overcast afternoon and never brighter. The air is six, seven degrees. Your breath fogs the instant it leaves you and joins the general fog. It is raining, gently, everywhere, and there is no rain falling: the Cloth condenses the cloud into drops the size of knuckles and drops them on schedule, so the interior has its own indoor weather, a permanent drizzle under a ceiling.

The ground is not ground. You sink into the Felt to the knee and never touch anything dead — every layer is alive, all the way down as far as you can push your arm, root over root over rhizome over moss, everything in contact with everything, no gaps anywhere. The water table is a hand-span below your feet. You could dig with your fingers and hit water in ten seconds. Nothing pools, because the Felt drinks faster than the Cloth drips, and so the water simply moves, through, down, always, quietly, at every point on the continent at once.

And the sound. The sound is the fact of the place. Break the roar apart with a long afternoon and you find its members. There is the creak: tension wood, millions of elastic stems loaded like masts and working like ship hulls, flexing in the fog wind, groaning at frequencies that arrive in your sternum before your ears. There is the crackle — the loudest component, a continuous detonation of small events: seed pods splitting ballistically, fiber sets snapping taut as stems thicken by the millimeter, and, everywhere, the click-click-click of cavitation, water columns under negative pressure letting go a thousand times a second like static on a radio the size of a landscape. There is the hiss and rush of condensation and seepage and indoor rain. There is the hum: the wind crossing the Stand plays it. Hollow stems, tuned tubes, flowers grown into trumpet bells and bottle throats — and they were grown for this, because in a world with no animals, flowers had no one to seduce, and so they became instruments instead. The Stand chords itself, and the chords change as the wind changes sector, hour by hour.

You cannot hear your own footsteps. You communicate with a companion at ten centimeters, shouting, and lose half of it. The crackle is felt in the teeth. You learn the roar has weather of its own: at night the fog thickens to soup and the drip becomes the dominant voice; at midmorning the fog thins on schedule and the Volley comes.

The Volley is the Stand's dawn event, and the network's signature. At the fog's daily minimum — the one window when the air above the Cloth is navigable — the whole continent launches its ballistic seeds at once. You hear the front coming for four, five minutes, a rolling artillery that approaches at the speed of sound across thousands of kilometers, every district detonating its mortar pods on cue. Overhead it passes as a crackle like the end of the world; behind you it goes on being thunder for an hour, receding along the rim. Nothing natural synchronizes like that. That synchronization is the technology, and the coordination underneath it — the thumps you feel through your boots, patterned, call and response, roots striking the Undermass in sequences that repeat with variation — is the Stand talking to itself about water, load, fog, and time, constantly, at volume, in the only medium that carries through a saturated continent: percussion, through the bodies of the plants themselves, which are all one continuous body.

There are no animals here at all. Not few — none. No birdsong, ever; that slot in the soundscape is not empty, it is filled with the Stand itself. No insect ever chewed a leaf in this place, and the leaves show it: understory foliage in perfect condition, unbitten, whole, which reads as eerie until you understand that damage here comes only from weather and from each other. Nothing eats anything in the Stand except its own descendants. The floor layers eat downward — roots mining the dead in place — and the sky grows upward, and you are in the middle, in the zone where death is not permitted to be visible. At floor level the light is so dim the plants have made their own: foxfire, a cold blue-green glow in rot-felt and bark seams, just enough to see by, the Stand lighting its own basement. Understory leaves are silvered underneath, mirrors angled to pass the weakened light laterally, neighbor to neighbor — light here is a currency spent carefully, used twice, spent again.

Go down conceptually, because you cannot go down actually: beneath the Felt lies the Undermass, kilometers of it, compressed generation over compressed generation, the true bedrock of this continent being not rock but ancestors. Cool, acid, oxygen-starved, it keeps instead of rotting; peat consolidates toward coal in real time — the Stand is manufacturing a seam the size of a landmass while you watch. This is why the erosion reading of the place is effectively zero and inverted: the Stand is not wearing away, it is depositing. It is anti-erosion. And it is far too thick for its age. The rock beneath is geologically embryonic, sharp-edged, newborn, and the mass above it should not be kilometers deep for a hundred thousand years yet. It is, because the Stand is very, very good at making land — that excess is the tell, the fingerprint of the engineering.

Walk the margins and you can watch land being manufactured. The Building Edge is a frontier of pioneer felt advancing meters a year over raw mineral ground: first the mineral-bitter mats, fixing loose gravel with mucus and patience; then the low squeezers, dying into the mat to fatten it; then the first real stakes, then the closure of the Cloth overhead, at which point the district stops being ground-under-construction and becomes the Stand proper. It is a reef, turned inside out to the sky. The youngest landscape you will ever stand on, alive before it is land. Rocks weep here, get colonized within days, and stop being rocks.

So the machine is this: grown, not built, and better than built — a hydraulic grid of negative-pressure xylem scaled to civil engineering, water lifted three hundred meters with pumps made of cell wall, valves and rectifiers and surge chambers all of them alive, replacing themselves as they wear. A nightly exhale of terpenes that nucleates the fog on purpose — the Stand tailors its own weather, wears its cloud as greenhouse and blanket, because a clear night at this elevation would frost its own lungs. A structural self-knowledge: the root network senses where the Undermass runs thin and routes load around it, pre-stressing its great stems like guy-cables over soft ground. A machine with no parts. Nothing here was ever assembled; everything was always organized.

The rules, as far as a visitor can derive them: there is no unused volume — every cubic centimeter of air, water film, and stem-interior is tenanted, and if you stand still too long your shoulders are seeded and the Stand begins treating you as substrate, because it has no protocols for an animal and simply files you as ground. All light is secondhand. All water is moving. And silence is impossible — the Stand is one continuous wet body, and a sound anywhere is a sound everywhere, and it is making sound everywhere at all times, because the deepest rule is this: the Stand is loud because the Stand is building. This is construction noise. Growth, at this density and pace, is a mechanical event, and a continent performing it does so at full volume, in public, forever.

Stay long enough — weeks, if your ears can bear the tuition — and the percussion begins to resolve. Less like talk. More like counting. The Stand appears to be reciting its own dimensions to itself, in sequences and restatements, saying the number of what it is about to be, a continent-sized embryo in the act of adding itself up. And at the rim, if you need quiet, there is exactly one direction it exists: off the edge, over the Weeps, out over the lowlands and their ordinary stars. Even there, you will hear the water leaving. It is not a forest standing on a continent. It is a continent standing on its own dead, still adding.
