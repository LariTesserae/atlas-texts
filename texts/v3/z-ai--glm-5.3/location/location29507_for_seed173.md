**The Pump-Hall**

You hear it before you see anything. Coming down the flooded access shaft — hand over hand along a ladder whose rungs have been worn thin and glass-smooth by water alone — the sound arrives through the water, through the steel, and finally through your own ribs: a low, doubled hum, two notes a fraction out of tune, beating against each other somewhere below hearing. The shaft delivers you under an archway, and the hall takes you in.

It is a nave, forty meters long and half that wide, and every millimeter of it is made. There is no rock face here, no natural wall anywhere — the vault is riveted plate, the walls poured concrete faced in buff-green tile, the columns cast iron set on granite plinths. This was a pump house once, buried deep to lift water from somewhere to somewhere else, and it drowned so long ago that the drowning is now simply its condition, the way being cold is the condition of the deep.

The sound is everywhere because the machines are still running. Three great vertical pumps stand down the centerline like columns before an altar, each one ten meters of bronze casing and iron shaft, each one older than any purpose you could name for it. Two of them still turn. Their impellers are hidden in their housings, but you can feel the rotation as a shudder in the water, and the two working pumps hum a fraction apart from each other, so the whole hall carries a slow beat, a throb about once a second, like something enormous breathing evenly in its sleep. The third pump is dead. It seized centuries — or millennia — ago, and it stands colonized and silent, its casing furred solid with mussels, its volute an apartment block for shrimp and young gobies. It has become the hall's relic. The living ones orbit it.

Dimness here is not absence. Three of the five service lamps still burn behind pressure glass in bronze housings mounted high on the columns, and their light comes down soft and greenish through the suspended silt, in cones, like light through cathedral glass that has been given too many centuries of weather. The lamp panes are crusted — not with mineral, but with life: a brown-green fuzz a few centimeters thick, which is very nearly the only vegetation the hall possesses. Fat little gobies graze these panes like sheep keeping a lawn, and the lawns never grow beyond the light, because outside the cones there is nothing green at all, only dark water and dark metal.

The current is the hall's sun. The working pumps draw water down through a gridded floor of intake channels and push it out through the three square mouths of a discharge manifold set in the far wall like the pipes of an organ, and because everything downstream of the manifold failed or flooded long ago, the water simply comes back — through failed seals, through bulkhead doorways, along the tile walls — and so the hall turns its own water in one long slow gyre, forever. Everything alive arranges itself along that gyre according to flow speed, the way a meadow arranges itself according to light.

And there is a great deal alive. The mussels come first, tens of thousands of them, laid in overlapping rows on the warm bronze of the pump casings — the housings run a degree or two warmer than the 9-degree water, and the mussels have found this out and hold it like a secret. They grow aligned with the current, all of them, every shell facing the same way, so the machine's geometry is written onto ten thousand small bodies. Amphipods swarm in the light cones like dust motes, in numbers that make the beams look grainy. Eels occupy the cable trays and conduit runs, threading the hall's wiring like a second nervous system. Pale crayfish patrol the drowned catwalks, cleaning whatever falls. A single ancient sturgeon owns the middle water, armored-plated, cruising the gyre at a stately tilt, patrolling a border only it can see. And down in the silted corners, in the intake sump beneath the floor grating, lives the catfish, enormous and pale, which everything else arranges its life around without ever acknowledging. Emptiness is a feature of the hall: the mid-water is almost vacant, mile-wide desert air between presences, so that each creature is separated from every other by a halo of clear space.

The hall keeps its own clock. Every few minutes the working pumps surge — the hum drops a key, the current quickens to a pull you'd have to swim against — and the whole congregation responds at once. Mussels open their siphons wide and strain the moving water. The amphipod swarms rise into the light. The sturgeon accelerates the merest fraction. Then a check valve somewhere in the manifold slams shut, and the sound goes through the hall like a struck bell — a boom you feel in your sternum before you hear it — and the water recoils, and everything closes, flattens, hides. Sixty heartbeats of stillness. Then the surge builds again. The fauna do not count days. They count surges.

Other sounds fill the space between: the tick and pop of snapping shrimp, a static-crackle from the colonized dead pump; the dry rattle of a loose catwalk panel vibrating in sympathy with the fundamental hum; the clink of mussel shells tapping each other in the current; the deep organ-roar of the manifold mouths drinking. Every so often a corroded junction high on the north wall loses its grip and throws one white spark — a flash that startles the entire nave into a single flinch — and then the dark returns, and the hum, which never flinched at all.

Against the crown of the vault there is trapped an air pocket, the hall's last undrowned room, a silver ceiling a few decimeters deep reached by nothing but bubbles. Bubbles rise steadily from the pump shaft seals, wobble up the columns, and join it, and once or twice a day the pocket gathers enough to shrug — a great burping boom rolling around the vault. Nothing lives up there. The eels visit it, though, and hang just beneath it, looking up, for reasons no one has ever established.

Understand what the floor is made of. The silt that blankets the tile — a soft, deep, gray-brown snow — is the hall digesting itself: concrete dust leached from the fretted walls, rust shed from rebar stubs that thin visibly decade by decade, paint flakes, shell hash from ten thousand generations of mussels. The amphipods graze the bacterial film that grows on the decay, the gobies eat the amphipods, the catfish eats the gobies, and the products of all that settle back into the silt to become substrate again. The building is the biome's bedrock and its food both. Everything here eats the hall, slowly, and the hall, being enormous, does not appear to notice.

It has finished becoming, that is the thing you feel most strongly here. Every corner in the place has been rounded off. The stair treads are dished. The stenciled letters on the switchboard — slate, brass knife-switches, one thrown and four corroded open — have been worn to ghost-relief you can read only by dragging a fingertip, and even then only one word survives: *INTAKE*. The hall was built to keep something dry, and it lost that war, or won it so long ago that the difference has eroded away like everything else. Its purpose drifted without anyone deciding: it no longer moves water for the sake of whatever waited downstream. It moves water for the sake of what now lives in it.

Late in one surge, a mussel loses its grip on the second pump's casing, drops, clatters down the bronze in a series of bright ticks, and lands in the silt. The whole nave pretends not to have heard. Then the manifold begins to drink again, and the mussels open like a congregation coming to its feet, and the two great notes slide back into their argument, and the hall — settled into the shape it will keep — goes on.
