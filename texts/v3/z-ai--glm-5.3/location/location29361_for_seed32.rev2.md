# The Interval Room

There is a place inside the great machine-world that no one made on purpose. It was left over — the negative space between two systems that were never meant to touch, a pocket of interior air maybe forty paces across, shaped like a lopsided hexagon, sealed off by walls that were built before anything else was built.

Everything here is made. There is no floor of stone, no soil, no grain of anything that nature laid down. The floor is a mosaic of salvage tiles — thousands of them, packed edge to edge without mortar, fitted so tightly that the seams are thinner than a hair, and no two tiles alike: glazed terracotta, brushed steel, cracked porcelain, slabs of some dead composite printed with faint circuitry like the veins of a pressed leaf. It looks like the inside of a drawer that held a civilization's pockets for ten thousand years. It was assembled by hand, over generations, by people who are no longer remembered, and then the door was sealed, and it became a room with no entrance and no witness.

The walls are the same — layered, dense, saturated. Conduits run everywhere, bundled and re-bundled, webbing the ceiling like roots of a tree turned upside down and mineralized. Everything presses against everything else. There is no empty corner, no bare surface, no resting place for dust. Density here is not a feeling of fullness but a kind of pressure: the room is at capacity, has been at capacity forever, and could not accept one more object without something surrendering its place.

Yet it is not crowded in the human sense. There are no people. There never were, not really — only the makers, briefly. There is no animal life at all: no mites, no spores of anything moving, no worm in the seams. What lives here is vegetable only, and barely that. From the crevices between tiles grow small pale plants — a sparse, deliberate scattering, maybe thirty of them across the whole floor, no bigger than a handspan. They are chlorophyll-ghosts, white and faintly green at the leaf-edges, drawing their water not from any rain but from a sealed micro-hydrology the makers engineered and then abandoned: condensation beads on the cold conduit pipes overhead, gathers, and falls in a slow fixed rhythm — one drop every eleven minutes, onto the same tile, where a channel in the mosaic carries it to the roots. It has fallen on that tile for ten thousand years. The plants drink and do not grow. They are in equilibrium with their own exact size, each one a perfect closed loop of use and renewal. Nothing blooms. Nothing dies.

It is cool — the pipes run cold, and the air holds that cellar-chill of metal in shadowless light. And the light is the strangeness here, or one of them: the room has no lamp, no window, no visible source, and yet it is bright everywhere at once, brighter than comfortable, a flat total illumination that comes from the walls themselves, from some phosphorescent compound in the salvage glazes that was charged once, long ago, and drains so slowly it will outlast the species that made it. The room glows from every direction, so nothing casts a shadow. Every plant is lit from all sides, its leaves shadowless, floating white-green in a light with no origin and no evening.

And it is loud. That is the room's whole presence, when you imagine yourself into it — the sound. The two systems it lies between both run, and both run forever, in a locked frozen equilibrium that has not varied in recorded memory. One side hums a deep subsonic drone, the resonance of some vast slow process. The other carries a higher, denser noise — a rushing, churning wash, like a wind tunnel or a frozen waterfall of air. Together they form a chord, a two-note drone that never rises, never falls, never pauses for breath, because neither source breathes. The sound is so constant it stops being sound and becomes architecture. The plants have grown into its shape; their stems are slightly bowed, all in the same direction, shaped by years of the standing pressure wave. The mosaic tiles nearest the loudest wall have been worn microscopically smooth on their upper faces — not by wind, but by vibration, ten thousand years of the room humming against itself, polishing itself, patient as a river.

That is the erosion here, and it is nearly finished. The room is in its own final state. It has been weathered by its own noise into a form it will keep essentially forever: settled, balanced, complete. Nothing new arrives. Nothing leaves. The drops fall. The chord sounds. The light glows. The plants hold their size.

The world outside this pocket may move, may change, may even one day end. The Interval Room does not know this. It is a made place with no maker present, a loud silence, a bright sealed dark — a small room of total occupation and absolute stillness, holding its one long note into a future that has no reason to be listening.
