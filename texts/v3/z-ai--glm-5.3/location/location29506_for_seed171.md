The first thing is the sound.

Not wind, not surf — this highland has no ocean and only the wind it makes for itself. And not voices, because there is nothing here that speaks. No birds, no insects, no calling of any kind. The roar comes out of the ground, out of the standing trunks, out of the air itself, and it is continuous, layered, and loud enough that you would stop trusting your ears within the hour and start listening with your sternum instead. The place is deafening the way a foundry is deafening, and like a foundry, everything it has to say is about work.

It is a plateau the size of an inland sea — thousands of kilometers of new land, and *new* is the word that explains almost everything. This ground rose recently and is rising still, a finger-width a year, lifted from below by forces still under construction themselves. The stone down there is raw: unweathered basalt, unjointed, uncarved. Not one river has had time to cut a valley into it. Not one canyon exists. There is no gorge, no ravine, no dry wash — the drainage of an entire continent-scale landmass has never once managed to concentrate. That isn't luck. That is the second thing you'd need to understand, and the sound of it is everywhere.

The plateau wears its own weather as a lid. Wet air from the lowlands climbs the outer slopes every day, meets the cool of the altitude, and condenses against it — a twice-daily tide of mist that pours up over the rim and lies across the land like a sheet of dim pearl. The exposed scarps at the edges weep continuously, condensate striping the black rock until the stone shines and runs. Water is not scarce anywhere here; it is *ambient*. And the reason it never carves anything is that it never gets the chance. The ground absorbs it before it can gather, because the ground is not ground.

That is the third thing, and the deepest. The surface of this plateau is not soil, not rock, not sediment. It is plants — ten meters in places, in others thirty, of interwoven living tissue: root woven through rhizome woven through stem, every layer occupied, every layer conducting, every layer alive. You could dig for a day and a half and reach nothing mineral. Walking on it is like walking on a lung. It gives underfoot, springs back, shifts its weight around your steps, redistributes the way a crowd makes room. The mineral world is a rumor beneath the mat, a floor no one has ever needed.

And the occupancy does not stop at the surface. Nothing here is bare. That needs saying plainly, because *bare* is a concept this landscape has never generated. Every trunk carries tenants, every tenant carries tenants, every leaf hosts a film of green so fine it reads as the leaf's own texture. Even the air is crowded — a permanent haze of drifting seeds and propagules, aerial plankton, so dense that on the rim, where the mist thins, the light diffuses through living dust. Breathe and you inhale the place's next century.

The light, what there is of it, is a bright patch of pearl behind permanent overcast, so high and so close overhead it feels touchable. Most of it dies in the upper canopy, drunk by the first three meters of leaves. Below that, the world runs on light the plants make themselves: a cold blue-green glow from roots and lantern-shaped structures, faint, functional, distributed like lamps in a workshop. It is not decoration. Photons are so scarce here they get banked, traded, routed — the bioluminescence is plumbing as much as anything, the visible trace of a light economy running underground. Day, in any meaningful sense, is a matter of volume, not brightness. The cycle announces itself in sound and temperature, and the eyes can sit this one out.

It is cool — a damp five to ten degrees, the chill of wet wool — except in the hearts. Some districts of the mat run hot: deep digestion chambers where the ground eats its own dead, since there is nothing else to do the eating. No animals, and no fungi either — this is a pure-plant system, closed all the way around, and decomposition is a job the flora does in-house. Steam curls up from the vents of those districts in slow ropes, and the mats around them run rust-colored and heat-loving, little climates clustered on their own exhaust. The whole plateau is a patchwork like that: cold ridges, warm hollows, wet bowls, drum-tight domes, a thermal geography made entirely of metabolism.

Now the sound, unpacked, because it deserves it. Under everything is the hydraulic hum — the transport roar of water moving through tissue at continental scale, a felt-more-than-heard drone you would eventually stop calling noise and start calling the ground's resting voice. Above it, the drum-leaves: hundreds of millions of cupped leaves tuned by shape and tension to amplify falling water, so that when rain arrives it does not patter but *peals*, a roar like a stadium crowd that has been given good news. Between rains, the pipe-stems take over — hollow trunks, resonant, each grown to a different note, and the plateau makes its own wind by heating and cooling its districts unevenly, so a slow chord moves across the land over the course of hours and changes key as the temperature does. Sharp against all this: the shriek of gas valves, metabolic pressure releasing through safety openings like steam whistles; the gunfire of turgor, stems snapping taut as they grow; the ballistic crack of seed pods throwing their offspring thirty meters because there are no animals to carry them. And beneath even the hum, every few hours, the boom of uplift — a bass note from the crust itself as the land rises another hair. You feel it in your teeth.

And then the chorus. Twice a day the whole system synchronizes. The hydraulic pumping falls into phase, the pipe-stems lock onto a shared fundamental, the drum-leaves all swing to the same angle, and the plateau *sings* — a sustained, structured, full-spectrum sound out of a landscape that contains no voice, played through shallow bowl-shaped basins the mat has grown over centuries, kilometer-wide amphitheaters of living tissue shaped with an acoustician's patience. Here is what the chorus is *for*, as near as can be told: sound nucleates rain. The standing waves the plants generate cohere the mist that lies over everything, collapsing it into droplets on demand. The rain here is not weather that happens to the place. It is weather the place calls down. When the chorus peaks, the fog deck lets go, and the drumming begins.

So this is a landscape that performs, daily, what we would have to call engineering — and it does it without a single artifact. No made thing exists anywhere on the plateau. No tool, no wall, no mark of a hand. The weirs that hold the internal rivers are living weirs. The water towers are swollen trunks. The valves are sphincters of tissue. In the deep mat, roots precipitate iron and copper out of the young stone and lay them down as conductive thread, and the buried ground is slowly, over centuries, becoming a lattice — a grid being built by gardeners that do not exist. The engineering is real. The engineer is not. Nothing here was built; everything here was grown, and the distinction, which seems so sturdy from far away, simply fails to hold weight when you stand on it.
