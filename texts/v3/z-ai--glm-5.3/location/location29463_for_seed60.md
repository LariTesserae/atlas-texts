You come down into it. The land lets itself go low — a basin the rivers forgot but the rain did not — and the air changes before the scenery does: warm, thick, held like a breath. On the far side of the cane wall the horizon is simple and wide, and here the horizon is gone entirely, replaced by a green standing crowd.

The wall itself used to be a compound wall, brick and lime. There is still one course of brick visible along the bottom, dark and rounded like river stones, sweating. Above that, the wall kept growing — the engineers of the place planted cane as a windbreak a long lifetime ago, and the cane took the wall's job and then the wall. There is a gate. There is also a fig on either side of the gate, grown into an arch, and buried in one trunk, raised a thumb's width above the bark, the thread of an iron hinge. The arch is the only doorway anyone uses now. You duck. The wet leather smell of the place folds over you as you pass through.

The floor is not ground. It is root-mat, a sprung corduroy of living runners laced over old paving, and beneath it — close, sometimes an inch — water. You feel it more than see it: every step compresses the mat, and water wells up around your boot-print and sinks back down, so that walking the compound is like walking on a very old drum. There is no bare soil anywhere. There is barely bare anything. Brick, gear, rail, pipe, glass — every surface is claimed, stacked, epiphyted, mossed, claimed again. The density is not clutter; it is occupancy. Every niche has a tenant, and the tenants have tenants.

It is small. That is the first thing that surprises people. A hundred and fifty paces by two hundred, walled in cane on all sides, and the highest thing in it is the stump of a chimney rising out of a strangler fig, maybe ten meters, breathing. You could see the whole compound from the chimney if you could climb it, and there is nothing in the compound that does not want you looking at it, so mostly you don't climb it.

The machinery is the second surprise, because there is machinery, and it is running.

Down the center of the compound lies what was a line-shaft — the old power spine of a fiber mill, driven once by waterwheel, carrying motion out to every workstation by belt and pulley. The shaft is still there. It is also a tree. A hardwood trunk runs through the old brick bearing-housings, forty feet of it, lignified smooth where it turns, and it turns — slowly, with a deep two-note creak, like a ship's hull thinking. One full revolution per day. The creak is the loudest single sound in the place, and you can hear it from the gate, and you never stop hearing it, the way you never stop hearing your own pulse.

From the shaft, belts run. They are lianas now, plaited by decades of growth into ribbons under tension, riding the turning trunk, delivering their patient rotation out to the stations. The stations do their work at plant speed. The great loom-frame at the north end — two crossed trunks, a tensioned web of fiber between them — adds perhaps a hand-width of cloth in a season. The cloth hangs in gray-green sheets, smells of hay and old rope. Nobody takes it. That is one of the rules you learn without being told: nothing here is exported anymore. All production is inward. The mill makes rope, and the rope binds the mill; the loom makes cloth, and the cloth shades the loom's own seedbeds. The works produces only the works.

The rets are where the sound lives.

Two rectangular ponds, brick-lined once, the brick now under a settled mat of roots, full of warm gold-green water and stacked stems of the fiber crop, sunk and softening. Retting — the long warm soak that rots the pith off a stem and leaves the fiber — was always the mill's heart, and the ponds still do it, in the same water, at the same temperature, without supervision. And they talk. Anaerobic gas gathers under the settling stalks and lifts, and each bubble reaches the surface with a soft wooden knock. Hundreds an hour. At dawn the ponds knock like a room of people tapping tables, and this is the closest thing the place has to birdsong, and there are no birds, and the knocking is why.

Smell, while we are at the ponds: sweet-sour, silage and wet rope, warm bread-dough underneath where sap runs, a thread of iron. The air is so humid that smell is less an aroma than a medium. By mid-afternoon the whole compound steams — warmth pooled in the basin all day, breathing back out through a thousand leaves, and the sun cuts through the cane canopy in bright shafts that the steam makes solid. Normal light, but thickened. Everything glossy. Every leaf edge wears a lens of condensation, and the lenses catch the light, so that standing still among the stations you are surrounded at eye level by small patient glares.

The afternoon is also when the pressure comes up. Turgor is this mill's steam. The vat-gourds — kettle-sized fruits on stalks grown into trestles — collect condensate and sap all morning, and as the day's heat builds, the fluid expands, and one by one the gourds vent through stomatal valves with a fine hiss, minutes long. At the peak of the heat, the compound sizzles softly from all directions, a shift's worth of small whistles, and the old-timers of the region say the works is blowing off, and they are right. It works hardest when it is hottest. Warmth is its fuel. Rain is its coal delivery.

There was electricity once. The insulators are still up — white porcelain beads strung on a living vine that has taken the copper's place, running along the old tramway bed where the mill once carted its bales. The vine carries a charge. Not much. Touch it and you taste tin foil at the back of your tongue, and your hand tingles for a while. It powers nothing that survives, or it powers everything, depending on how you count, since the ants have adopted it: the one animal species that still works here. Small dark ants, in trades, who patrol the valves and filters at all hours, clearing the stomata of debris, plugging the night-shift vents with their own bodies, unhurried, unionized by nothing. They are the entire fauna most days. Once, at the sluice, at the same hour three visits running, there was a heron. It stood as if clocking in. It has never been seen to eat or to leave, and the ants do not acknowledge it, and I will not interpret it for you.

The records are kept. The old counting-house is a roofless brick room with a desk that is now a bench of layered root, and on the roots hang thin trailing filaments, and on the filaments are knots — season knots, tied tight, year on year, a ledger in the only script the place trusts. Nearby, half-swallowed in a trunk, a brass valve wheel; the trunk has been lifting it for decades, an inch a decade, presenting it to visitors. In the wall behind, a shard of the old firebox door, and on it, in the oxide bleeding down the brick, a red map that the roots have faithfully copied.

Rules, as well as the place can say them: Cut nothing — the lines run under pressure, and a severed belt whips like a struck snake. The shifts are kept — the day shift runs on sun, the night shift on condensation, and the whole compound exhales at dusk, one long sigh through a thousand vents. The seed pods keep time aloud, snapping one by one through the heat of the afternoon like a tally being counted. Nothing here is for sale. And you are the fauna. The compound has no animals to move its seeds, so it uses what arrives: burrs in your cuffs, a hooked grain in a boot tread, a pod that bursts when your shadow cools it. You were hired by walking in. There is no clocking out; the seeds travel with you and that is the point.

It is loud, in sum, in a way no throat had anything to do with: the flywheel's slow creak, the ponds' knocking, the vent-hiss of the hot hours, the drip — always the drip, condensation ticking off ten thousand leaf tips at once, a rain that never falls from any sky, only from the canopy, all day, all night. Loud the way a crowd whispering is loud. Loud enough that after an hour you find your footsteps have fallen into the flywheel's rhythm, and it is hard to say which of you is pacing the other.

And nothing here is ruinous. That is the last thing to understand. The bricks are rounded, the iron is map-stain, the brick bearing-housings are worn smooth by the slow turn of the trunk — everything is weathered into its own state, settled, balanced, employed. The mill is not decaying under the plants. It is not overgrown. It was only ever an imitation — of retting, which the marsh invented; of weaving, which stems do under tension; of pumping, which is what xylem is; of the bellows, which is a leaf. The engineers built their machine out of the wetland's own gestures, and when they left, the gestures kept the appointment. What you are standing in is not a factory that got swallowed.

It is the original, restored to full operation, still making itself, one creak a day, in a warm green room with the door grown mostly shut.
