You come in through a hatch in the corner of the still house — a steel plate with a pull-ring, warm to the touch, hinged like a coin drawer. A ladder goes down two meters, bolted to a concrete pier. You descend out of the bright loud hall and the noise stays above you, like a lid, and you land with a crunch on washed gravel and enter the underfloor: the crawlspace sealed between a distillery's suspended production floor and its raw slab. Everything the plant is happens over your head. Everything the plant makes happen by accident happens down here.

The terrain, first. It is a plain of piers — pale concrete columns on a grid, form-tie holes open like pores, still sharp-edged because the building is only eleven months old. Above them runs a canopy of pipe: steam mains, condensate returns, lagged in foil and canvas, sagging in long deliberate curves like lianas trained along a wire. Between the piers, the gravel floor is the countryside — angular, unworn, a boulder field at miniature scale, every stone still carrying its quarry scar. A cable trench cuts the gravel like a canyon, floored with tray and silt. At the far end the trench descends to the sump: a round pit rimmed in red paint, a steel lid, a pump. That pit is the lowest point in this nation. The whole landscape holds less vertical relief than a staircase, and you cross it on hands and knees, because the sky here — the underside of the floor — is low enough to touch everywhere.

The weather is what the pipes decide. When the stills run, the mains sweat. The canopy rains. Warm water beads on steel, gathers, drops — a sound before it is a sight: chimes on pipe, patter on lagging, hiss where a drop finds a flange. The rain falls from overhead cracks and from the ceiling of pipes at once, a doubled rain. It threads the gravel into bright rivulets, cuts small braided channels already, and gathers in the low pockets into puddles the size of dinner plates. The trench runs as a shallow river toward the sump, and the sump pump swallows on a timer, so the river has a tide — a pulse every cycle, water rising and falling along the canyon like a coast obeying some small moon. Near the hottest joints, rain goes the other way: fog rises off the flanges, falls upward, and clings to your face like breath.

And the rain is not quite water. It carries the stills with it. The condensate that sweats through this room comes flavored from the botanicals worked above — faint juniper, a ghost of coriender and citrus peel, a sweetness like a bottle opened in another room. The puddles smell faintly of gin. Everything that drinks here drinks the plant.

Because the plant is young, the geology is young, and it is the fastest geology you will ever watch. Lime leaches from fresh concrete, dissolves in the condensate, and rebuilds itself on the pipes. White soda-straw formations hang from joint seams — months old, some already the length of a finger. A weeping elbow has grown a small chandelier of mineral. Efflorescence blooms across the piers like frost. There are drip-crusts forming under the loudest leaks, and a handprint on one sweating pier — someone steadying themselves months ago — has been varnished over, palm and fingers, in white mineral, a fossil already, of a living man.

Light arrives in strips and lozenges. At midday the hall's roof lights, then the hatch grating, stamp bright parallelograms onto the gravel — bright enough to read by — and along the expansion joints thin blades of sun slide like light under a closed door. The lozenges crawl all day across the floor, a nomad sky, and the ecology arranges itself along them the way a shore arranges itself along water: green films in the brightest, moss on the one pier-top that catches a noon blade, and two lone ferns standing in gravel like pioneers with flags. Elsewhere the world is dim gold, shadowed, the color of weak tea. At night, a glass block in the wall of the control room glows like a built-in moon, and under it, moths orbit.

The strangest fact of the place is the quiet. The hall above is thunder — pumps, milling, the clatter of the filling line. Down here the floor acts as an acoustic ceiling, and the roar stays up there, converted to rhythm you feel in your knees and palms: the heartbeat of the pumps in the gravel, the tick of contactors, the slow clank of expansion. What you hear, actually hear, is drips — a tuned percussion of ten thousand drops on steel and stone — the tick of cooling pipe, crickets ticking from the warm margins, and sometimes the dry paper whisper of springtails moving in drifts. When the loading door rolls open at the far end, the whole room sighs — pressure changes, drips swing on their threads of fall — the underfloor inhales.

And the life is packed. That is the true condition of the place: saturation. There is no empty square centimeter. Put your palm anywhere on the wet gravel and you have covered a village. The base of everything is a slick — yeast and bacterial films living on the aromatic condensate, pinkish weepings at the drain, slime blooms on lagging where spills soaked in. On the films graze springtails in uncountable drifts, moving like smoke poured along the ground; mites; fungus gnats; a few snails scraping green off the piers. Crickets sing from the warm margins of every puddle. Moths run a full life cycle here — caterpillars in the spilled malt dust beneath the mill joints above, adults drinking gin-rain from the puddle rims, some of them visibly unsteady. Cellar spiders in pipe crotches have hung ruin-webs already heavy with dust and moth wings. A gecko owns pipe six, thermoregulating on the hot steel, laying its eggs in a seam of lagging; some call it the inspector. Sparrows nest in cable tray three and commute out through the vent at dawn. Mice keep the country under the ladder rungs. The pit has a sea in it: someone from maintenance released feeder guppies into the sump months ago, and the water there holds a steady twenty-eight degrees, and eleven generations of guppies have now been born in it, multiplied, and died without ever seeing sky — a whole lineage native to a hole in the floor of a distillery. A young tortoiseshell cat — unowned, warm-seeking, tide-literate — patrols a fixed loop on the dry gravel and knows to the minute when the sump pump will make the trench sing, because that is when the crickets come down to drink.

Human marks are scattered through it like artifacts of a thin civilization. Stenciled pier codes, A7, B12. Welder's chalk: tallies, arrows, one rude drawing gone pale. A socket wrench dropped during commissioning, still bright, its chrome unscuffed. A plastic lunch bag being slowly fossilized in lime. Coffee rings on a pier cap. A wooden stool and an empty tin on a pallet in the driest aisle — "the office" — beside a paperback swollen to twice its thickness by damp, a bookmark of emery cloth maybe halfway in. Along the best drip-line, someone has set out a row of jar lids to catch the cleanest gin-rain, and someone empties them, and neither party has ever seen the other, and this arrangement has held for months. A seedling has come up under the stool — a tomato, probably from a fallen slice of someone's sandwich — growing leggy and earnest toward the noon lozenge.
