# The Terraces of Ilik-Vash

It is a small place. You could walk its full boundary in a quarter hour, and the boundary is real — a rim of black slate standing like teeth where the plateau ends and the world drops away into cloud. The hamlet of Ilik-Vash sits just inside that rim, high enough that the clouds are *below* it, a white sea breaking slowly against the cliffs all day, and above the clouds nothing but the most enormous, unforgiving brightness of open sky.

The light is the first thing anyone says about Ilik-Vash. It is not warm light. The sun here is a winter sun magnified by altitude and by snow — patches of old snow live in the shade of every wall, even in the warm months, and they throw their glare back upward so that the whole settlement seems to float inside a lit chamber. Shadows are hard-edged and blue. Faces ache after an hour. The forty-odd inhabitants go about with slit eyes and bone goggles carved with a single hairline slot, and their cheeks are burned dark as old leather.

## Water

The second thing anyone says about Ilik-Vash is that it weeps.

The plateau is a thick cap of stone over a heart of ice — old ice, blue ice, the kind that squeaks underfoot. The sun's ferocity melts the ice's skin all day, every day, and the meltwater cannot leave. It has nowhere to go. So it rises. It comes up through the joints of the paving stones, beads on the undersides of slate lintels, runs in threads down the faces of the terrace walls, gathers into shallow rills that cross and recross the lanes in braided silver. Every wall drips. Every overhang has its row of icicle-stubs in shadow, and hanging long glassy teeth in the cold hours. The people have stopped fighting this. Their town is built *of* the water as much as against it: the lanes are slightly channeled, the thresholds are stepped stones worn glass-smooth, and the great cistern at the center — a natural basin the first settlers merely ringed with fitted stone — fills itself, they say, without ever once being rained into.

At night, when the sun drops and the cold clamps down, the weeping stops and everything freezes. The rills become little glass roads. By mid-morning they are silver threads again. The whole town breathes water in and out on a daily cycle, like a slow lung.

## The Terraces

Because the land slopes gently toward the rim, the settlers — sheep-and-obsidian people, no metal to speak of, tools of knapped glass and bone and patience — carved the slope into terraces, forty-odd of them, each a few paces deep, each retained by dry-stone walls the color of storm. This is why the place feels dense, packed, layered: everything is stacked. The houses are stacked into the terrace faces themselves, half-dug into the slope, their front walls of fitted slate and their backs the living rock, so that walking the lanes you pass door after door after door, rooflines stepping down the hill in overlapping rows like shingles. Roofs nearly touch the terrace above them. You crouch to pass under some eaves. Smoke from the bone-fires finds its way out through little stone chimneys and immediately flattens and drifts sideways, thin and blue, lost in the brightness.

Between the terraces, almost nothing grows. This is deliberate and also unavoidable: altitude, cold, glare, and the constant freeze-thaw churn of the soil kill anything ambitious. A few mats of moss the color of rust. A lichen that grows in perfect circles, faintly luminous in the blue shadows. One heroic shrub, knee-high, twisted by wind into a permanent bow, which the townsfolk treat with the uneasy respect one gives a very old person. That is the flora. The people do not farm the terraces. They farm the wet walls.

## The Green Walls

Because the retaining walls weep constantly, their faces are the only fertile country in Ilik-Vash — and they are *intensely* fertile. The people seed them each spring with a domesticated hair-alga, a black-green filament they call *velvet*, which sheets the wet stone in dense, waterlogged felt. Into the velvet they press seeds of a small cold green, a mustard-kin with leaves like coins, which grows nowhere on earth except on walls that weep all day. The terrace faces are thus vertical gardens, forty shelves of dripping green stacked one above another down the whole slope, and this is what the plateau lacks in breadth it makes up in sheer stacked abundance.

The walls are also, quietly, the strangest thing here — and the town knows it, and does not fully discuss it.

The velvet is warm. Not sun-warm: *inside*-warm, warm at dawn, warm on the shadowed faces, warm when you press your palm against it on a freezing night. The meltwater comes up from the ice at barely above zero and pours down the walls measurably warmer, four degrees, five, and the geese who winter here (more on the geese) crowd against the velvet in the cold hours like cattle at a fire. Something down under the plateau — some chemistry in the old ice, some slow deep rot of mineral — is making heat. The terraces sit on top of it like a lid on a pot.

## Turbulence

And the pot does not always stay lidded.

This is the paradox of Ilik-Vash: a silent town on a boiling floor. Several times a day, without schedule or warning, the ground shudders very slightly and one of the melt-channels — the town knows each by name, the Long Rill, the Sister, the Judge — suddenly *surges*. Water pressure builds below, some ice-lens ruptures or some channel finds a new throat, and the little silver thread becomes for ten minutes or an hour a torrent, brown with rock-flour, loud as anything in the world, roaring down its channel, flooding the lowest lanes to the ankle, spinning the little water-wheels that the people — practical, uncomplaining — have built into every channel mouth, wheels that grind bone and grain the moment the water gives them leave to.

Then it stops as suddenly, the channel drains, the ice re-forms in the cold shadow, the flood-ice cracks and settles, and the enormous silence comes back down like a lid of its own.

In this way the town is rebuilt a little every day. The surges carry gravel that the people collect from catch-baskets and stack. The surges undercut a wall every year or two and the wall slumps and is re-fitted. The lanes migrate. On the oldest terrace, half-abandoned now, you can see lane-markers from three generations standing thirty paces apart — each marking where the water ran in its day. The people say the town is a conversation with the water, and that the water does most of the talking.

## Fauna

For all the sparseness of the plants, the animal life of Ilik-Vash is absurd, festival-crowded, and almost entirely silent.

The geese come first in number: a high, cold-loving race that does not migrate so much as *commute*, riding the updraft off the cloud-sea in the mornings in their hundreds, wheeling in total, uncanny quiet — wind at this altitude strips their calls away before they carry, or they have simply learned not to bother. They land on the wet walls and the rooftops and the snow-patches and graze the velvet and the coin-greens, and the people tolerate them with the weary generosity of those who have given up, and take an agreed quota of eggs.

Then the wetas: fat dark crickets of the melt-channels, thriving in the mineral warmth of the rills, hopping in slow arcs when the surge comes and riding rafts of flood-ice downstream with perfect fatalism. Then the wall-lice that keep the velvet trimmed, and the pale springtails that live in the snow-patches like living commas, and the one species of spider that has figured out the icicle-stubs and hangs its webs between them, so that at dawn the whole town is briefly hung with twenty small rainbows.

And the ice-birds. Three of them. A species of one cliff-face, the far one, outside the rim: white birds the size of gulls that hunt nothing anyone has ever identified, that fly into the weeping walls' warmest faces and press themselves against the velvet and shiver, and that sing — but only inside the meltwater, only underwater, so that if you kneel and put your ear to a flooded channel, you can sometimes hear a thin, high, bubbling song moving along beneath the stones. Nobody knows why. Nobody has ever seen them do it without flooding. The old people say the water is learning music.

## The Sound

Because that is the last thing to say about Ilik-Vash: how quiet it is, always, between surges. The brightness has no sound. The weeping is a whisper. The geese are silent. The forty people are not a talkative people; they communicate across terraces in a flat hand-language, arms out of doorways, greetings thrown silently like hats. What sound exists is water: a rill ticking over a stone lip, a drip finding a drum-head of ice, and beneath everything, felt in the feet more than heard, the deep subterranean *moving* of water under the plateau — the pressure, the melt, the warm current running through blue ice — like a huge animal turning over slowly in its sleep beneath the walls of a bright, small, crowded, patient town that has been built, by hand, out of stone, exactly on top of it, and that has no intention of moving.
