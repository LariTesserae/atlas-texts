The range is young. That is the first fact of this place, and everything else follows from it. The stone up here has not yet learned to be old: no soil has made itself, no rain has rounded an edge, the slopes are black basalt with the glass still on it, scree lying where it fell like the leavings of a kiln. The peaks tick. Quietly, at long intervals, a rock somewhere contracts as it cools and gives one small sound like a knuckle cracking. The mountains have been cooling for only a few hundred years. On clear nights they can be heard from the lowlands, a sparse clicking in the sky, and the lowlanders do not go up.

The valley between the newest peaks used to carry a river. Then the land began to rise — it is rising still, a finger's width a year, a hand's width in a lifetime — and the river's gorge pinched shut behind a tongue of fresh lava, and the water backed up and simply stayed. The oldest women of the town remember walking the ford as girls. Now they are born swimmers, and the ford is forty feet down.

The lake sits two miles above the lowlands, which is a strange place to keep a sea, and it is warm, which is stranger. The crust beneath it is too young to have gone cold. Hot water comes up through the fissures in the drowned fields, and the whole lake holds a blood-warmth, cooling toward the surface, warmer as you go down. Warm water over warm stone at altitude makes its own weather: a permanent crown of steam and fog stands over the valley, visible from a hundred miles off as a white cap on the mountains. The people call it the Pearl. From outside it is a wall. From inside it is a ceiling — a soft luminous mother-of-pearl lid a few hundred feet up, through which the sun arrives only as a rumor. Midday here is dim, pearly, shadowless. From below the water, midday is a green-gold hush, like being inside a lit stone.

Go under, and the valley opens.

The light shafts down in pale vertical columns that wander the drowned land as the sun drifts behind the Pearl — the people call them down-roads — and by that jade light you can see the shape of the world that was. Hedgerows run away into the green distance, kept and trellised now, because the town's rope-roads are strung along them. The old fields are meadows of tape-grass, blade after blade leaning toward whichever down-road is passing overhead. The orchards still stand, the original trees of the valley, leafless and soft-barked, whitened with sponge, hung with epiphyte gardens that the harvesters work like picking-beds. Higher in the water drift the mats — floating rafts of woven rhizome, red-rooted, that circulate the whole lake on a nine-year round, each one with a family's name and a small hut on it. Nothing eats much of any of it. That is the uncanny thing, if you have swum in older waters: the abundance has no grazers, no fry-clouds, no swallows of small fish. The warm deeps are thin with breath, the lake is young and cut off, and the animals simply have not arrived yet. The plants came first — carried up as seeds in the crops of the high-nesting birds — and the plants filled the valley to bursting while the animal world was still deciding whether to exist here.

The people are the exception. They didn't arrive; they never left.

The town stands on the drowned high ground — the ridgelines and hillocks of the old dry valley, chosen because they lie shallow enough to keep air. The houses are domes of great age, originally woven, and the lake has done what the lake does to everything woven and patient: the water is saturated with silica leeched out of the new rock, and over years it stiffens fiber into stone-lace. A basket goes rigid in a decade. A dead branch becomes agate-vein. A retired net, sunk and stitched into a wall, becomes the wall. The people call this the Cure, and they design for it — they weave thick what their grandchildren will want standing, and they say, without irony, that you weave for your children. So the town is built entirely of its own old soft work: domes of cured lace, weightless-looking, veined, honeycombed, golden in the green light. Nothing is thrown away. Everything is promoted into architecture.

You enter a house by diving under its lip and rising up inside — a sump door, a stone sill that holds the bubble in. Air is fetched down in clay lungpots by the young and strong, or bled in along long woven straws from floats above; air is the town's labor and its courtesy, and favors are repaid in lungpot runs. People speak of owing breath. The domes are small — big bubbles are unstable — so the houses crowd near-touching along the ridges, stitched to one another by rope-lines knotted with route-signs, and the whole settlement is one dense stitched fabric, the Stitch, roads of rope, marker-floats, weir-fences across the old lane-ends, cairns glazed white as enamel. At dusk the small hearths are lit and their smoke rises through the smoke-straws, and from the lowlands the Pearl wears a faint rash of orange stars at night, and the lowlanders have stories about that too.

Their old meeting hall still stands where it drowned, petrified to stone-lace, roofless, colonized, and it is now the archive: the town's records are woven and strung up inside to Cure, history that gets heavier every year, readable by touch in ten and by eye in a hundred. In the belfry hangs the old bell, and when a mooring line thrums against it — it happens a few times a season — the whole lake hears one soft note travel for miles, and work stops wherever it's heard.

For most of the time, though, the valley is quiet, and its quietness is layered like everything else. Rain falls on the surface as a vast whisper the depths listen to for days. The peaks tick. Once a month or so the young range shifts a half-inch and lets out a groan that is felt in the chest before it is heard, the mountain's voice, going on for a minute. Sound carries so far in the water that the town practices quiet as a politeness — a bell is an emergency, and real announcements are sung, the long-voice, a singer on a ridge audible two miles off through the green.

The animals there are, are known individually. The seed-bringing birds nest in the raw peaks, and the town floats mat-seed out for them on rafts to keep the old covenant — the birds seeded this whole garden, and the debt is honored. A few eels live in the lake, descendants of the river's own fish, called the river's remembering; when one crosses a meadow, work pauses. And there are the Nine: huge soft air-breathing grazers, boat-sized, slow as drifting logs, who work the deep meadows and surface at dusk with a sound like a bellows blown once. The day is considered done when the Nine breathe. When one dies, its shell becomes a boat, or a vault, and the town mourns a named individual, because in a fauna this sparse, every animal is a citizen.

The land keeps being born. Every few decades the uplift brings a black island up through the surface somewhere in the lake — raw stone, unmossed, unhallowed — and the town goes out to it, and the oldest woman steps barefoot onto it first and stakes a woven charm that will Cure into the island's first artifact. The rising water pushes the Stitch up the valley walls a house at a time, patiently, and the abandoned house-sites are left standing, cured lace in rows, so that the town's whole past can be read along the slope like a rope of knots. Once a year the Pearl thins and actual sunlight strikes the water for a few days, and everything seeds at once, in a rush the whole town swims out into.

They are a people with two tenses of making — soft and stone — and one virtue, which is patience, and they are right about the world, which is this: it is not finished. The valley is a child. The lake is the child's warm bath, the Pearl its breath, the ticking peaks its heart learning its beat. The people are what the valley dreamed while it was waking, and their work, all of it, is midwifery — tending a world that is still, in every sense, coming up.
