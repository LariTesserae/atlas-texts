# The Cistern House

It is a room the size of a small chapel, and it was built to hold water, and it has held water for so long that no one remembers it was built at all.

You go down to reach it — eleven steps of cast iron, each one worn into a shallow polished bowl by feet that stopped descending generations ago. The steps are warm. Everything here is warm. Somewhere above and behind the walls, the old district steam lines still run, and a fraction of their heat bleeds down through the brick and the concrete until the whole chamber sits at the temperature of a body just past fever. You do not notice the heat at first. You notice it ten minutes in, when your skin has agreed to it, and after that you stop noticing it again, and it becomes simply what warmth means.

Darkness here is not a condition. It is the medium. Your lamp makes a small wet room of visibility, and beyond its edge the chamber continues — you know this by the echoes, which come back from every direction slightly flattened, slightly soft, as though the walls have been upholstered in decades of mineral deposit. They have. The ceiling sweats. The steam pipes that pass through overhead — three of them, massively sleeved, crossing the vault like the ribs of some buried fish — condense their moisture against the cold-side air, and it gathers, and it drips. Not everywhere. In specific places, established places, patient places. Each drip has its own note and its own interval. One near the northeast corner falls every four seconds into a cast-iron basin that was perhaps once a junction housing and is now a perfectly tuned bell, a low *tong* that is the deepest sound in the room. Others tick against stone, plink into standing water, hiss onto the warm sleeve of a pipe and vanish instantly into steam so small you only see it if your light happens to rake across it at the right angle. The chamber is quiet the way a clock shop is quiet. The silence is a woven thing.

The floor is not level, and never was, and the water has found its answer to that. Thin films of it migrate across the concrete in slow braided sheets, following gradients so shallow they are more idea than slope, and they pool — a shallow dish of standing water here, a black rectangle there in the trough of an old cable trench, a long glaze across a floor channel that once carried lubricant and now carries rain from some grate above, arriving irregularly, in polite quantities, like a visitor who never stays long. The pools are warm. They are warm enough that they feel almost alive underfoot in dreams, though no one dreams here anymore except the maintenance divers who come twice a decade, and they say they dream of it constantly.

Vegetation has gotten in the way life always gets in: through a grate, as a spore, on a boot. It has found the two lamps — the maintenance lamps, wired and functioning on a circuit that has refused, out of sheer industrial stubbornness, to fail for ninety years. They burn behind frosted cages of salt and grime, amber, each one a dim perpetual twilight the strength of a candle behind cloth. And in that amber, in the dripline below the northeast pipe, a colony of ferns has established itself. Not many. A dozen plants, small, upright, dark green going almost black at the frond-edges, standing in a seam of grit that has accumulated on a ledge forty centimeters off the floor. They have never known a season. They have never known wind, or frost, or noon. Their world has two weathers: the drip and the not-drip. They grow slowly and they never die, or none of them has yet, and the oldest ones lean slightly toward the nearer lamp with the patience of things that have been leaning for half a century and consider it progress.

Everything else here is made. That must be understood literally. There is no ground. There is no rock face, no soil, no natural floor of any kind — only concrete, brick, iron, tile, glazed channel, expansion joint, and the accretions of these: rust in stratified blossoms, calcite curtains flowing down the walls like frozen batter, mineral wool spilling from old insulation in slow yellow weeps, paint in seven discernible eras peeling from the pipe sleeves in maps of nowhere. Even the dirt in the ferns' seam is manufactured — concrete dust, pipe scale, the exhaled calcium of the building itself, weathered back into a kind of soil by nothing but time and water. The room is consuming itself into arability, grain by grain.

It was a pump house once, or a valve gallery, or something with a name that had "auxiliary" in it. The machinery is mostly still present, and mostly still. There is a pump the size of a kneeling ox, bolted to a plinth, its housing furred in red-brown, its flywheel stopped at a particular angle that no one alive has seen it at any other. There are banks of gauges — dozens of them, small brass faces in steel ranks — and this is the strangest feature of the place, because roughly a third of the gauges still read. Not correctly, necessarily, but they read: needles trembling at faint pressures, registering the residual life of the steam system above, or their own trapped fluid, or possibly nothing but the heat's insistence that something be measured. They tick over very slowly, the needles, in movements you can only detect by looking away and back. The room is full of instruments monitoring a process that ended before the oldest fern germinated, and they have not given up.

Life beyond the ferns is rare and deliberate. There are springtails in the wet films — pale, near-invisible, moving like punctuation across the water sheen. There is a species of small blind moth, or something moth-like, whose larvae graze the mold that blooms in black patches behind the condensation lines, and whose adults sometimes rise in the lamplight in slow aimless arcs, wings soft as ash, living about a week in a world with no week in it. And there is, reportedly, one creature larger — heard by both of the last two maintenance divers, never seen — something that moves in the cable trench pools at long intervals, displacing water with a single unhurried *shhh*. Both divers agreed it was probably a rat, probably, and neither of them went to check, and both of them mentioned it first in their reports, before the gauges, before the structural findings. The room has decided to have a resident. These things happen.

Rules, then, as much as a place this size can have them:

The heat rises very slightly near the pipes and this is where the air moves — there is a permanent, barely perceptible convection through the chamber, warm air climbing the pipe runs, cool air crawling along the floor, so that a candle flame, if you brought one, would lean consistently toward the southwest wall as if in a draft from somewhere meaningful, which it is not.

The water never floods and never dries. The steam system above expands in the winter and contracts in the summer in slow yearly cycles, and the condensation follows it, so the drips quicken and slacken on a seasonal rhythm no one inside the chamber could ever detect, since it is all one long afternoon down here, and has been for ninety years.

What is dark stays dark and what is lit stays lit. The ferns grow toward the lamps; the mold grows behind the driplines; the moths orbit the amber at about a meter's distance, neither entering the glare nor leaving it, as if the light has a skin they have learned the boundary of.

And nothing is finished. That is the chamber's real character — it is not a ruin, which is a thing that has ended, but a *settled machine*, a place where every process is still running and every process has slowed to the pace of geology. The rust is still rusting, at its rate. The calcite is still flowing, at its rate. The gauges are still reading, at theirs. The drips are still falling on their four-second schedule into their iron bell, and if you sat in the dark for a hundred years — which the room would permit, which the room would barely notice — you would come back to find the ferns four centimeters taller, the stalactites a centimeter longer, and the needle of gauge number thirty-one moved, at last, into the next division of its face.

It is very warm, and very dark, and it sounds like a clock with water instead of time inside it, and the door at the top of the iron stairs is left unlocked, and no one comes.
