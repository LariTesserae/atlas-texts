## The Mill at the Top of the Sea

The place has no name, because there has never been anyone here to do the naming. Call it what it is: a mill the size of a continent, running at full capacity since before anything kept count.

It occupies the drowned crown of the world — a plateau three thousand kilometers across whose summit lies thirty meters below a surface that never breaks. No island, no reef-crest, no black fin of rock. The sea above it is a lid, flat and green-gold, and nothing has ever come through it from below or landed upon it from above. Around the plateau's rim, the seafloor gives up and drops — the Downfall, a drowned cliff-country falling away into blue-black silence. From anywhere on the plateau you can look out and *down*, the way you would from a mountain. This is the strange geography of the place: it is the high country of an ocean. You are always near the sky here, and the abyss is always at the edge of the yard.

The floor of the entire plateau is one plant. Not a forest — a single organism, a leaf the size of a country, its tissue stretched taut over the stone, green and slightly yielding under anything that walks. From this floor rise the organs, and here is the first thing to understand about their arrangement: they are not crowded. The great towers stand a kilometer apart, sometimes three, sometimes ten, with nothing between them but clean water and the straight fall of light. The mass of this place is not dense; it is *distributed*. The empty avenues are not emptiness. They are plumbing.

**The organs.** There are stacks — towers of iron-brown bark and collared glasswood that have never been built, only ripened — and they are exhalers. Each one vents a plaited rope of bubbles that rises without variation, hour into hour into eon, so that the water above them is permanently striped. There are mills: gizzard-organs grown around beds of grinding stone, ingesting shell-sand and shell-sand's makers, producing the fine chalk that flocculates downstream into other mouths. There are turbines — great wheels of silica-stiffened kelp-blade that turn in the fixed currents, not to make power for anyone but to pump seawater through their own feeding bodies, ranks of them receding down an avenue like a hall of clocks, all slightly out of phase, all exactly on time. There are vats: translucent fermentation bladders the size of municipal buildings, glowing faintly from the chemistry inside, gurgling at a pitch so low you feel it in the teeth. There are looms — long belt-fronds running nutrient mucus in one direction forever — and in the warm district called the Bath, a rank of true piston-engines: muscular columns sleeved in rigid silica, drawing and driving, drawing and driving, each stroke a fixed interval. The beat is that district's clock. Everyone who lives there can tell you what time it is without being able to tell you what time *is*.

And there is the Grid. Spires of conductive tissue stand at intervals across the plateau like an orchard of lightning-rods, holding a permanent potential between their skyward tips and the grounded floor. The water between them tingles. The field lines are legible to everything that swims — the fauna steer by them the way sailors once steered by stars no one will ever see from here. At fixed points the charge leaks in slow auroral flickers under the lid. Nothing is wired. Nothing was ever wired. The electrification, like everything else, is grown.

If you were somehow lowered through the surface, you would know the sound before you knew anything else. It is the loudest fact of the place: a chord. The stacks boom like flutes, the turbines whoosh and clack, the vats gurgle, the mills grind, the piston-sleeves knock, the Grid hums at a frequency the sternum understands. Add the crunching chorus of the grazers, the hiss of the vent-plumes, and underneath everything the permanent, far-off thunder of the storm at the rim — a hurricane that has been turning in one spot for ten thousand years. It is not an event. It is a landmark. All of this interferes and blends into one standing waveform, so loud it erases your own noise, so constant that it becomes a kind of rest. Silence does not exist here and never has. This is the place's one paradox, and it holds: the loudest thing in the world, because it never changes, functions as a silence. Travelers would sleep in it like a blanket. There are no travelers.

The light is an afternoon that has been going on for a very long time. The sun stands at an altitude it chose long ago, and the shafts it sends through the lid are permanent architecture — pillars of gold-green planted down the avenues, fixed pools on the floor that have names-by-function among the fauna, who hold them as territory the way anything holds anything here: exactly, and forever. There is no night. There is no season. The shadows are the oldest landmarks in the district. The weather, which at this scale would be a thing of systems and fronts, is instead a thing of *locations*: the Turning at the rim; the Rainwall, a column of cloud a league across that rains into the sea in one spot continuously, so that the water beneath it is deafened to hiss and the fish school along its edge like crowds outside a foundry; the fog-bank pinned over the vat-district like a lid's lid. Nothing in the sky moves on. The gradients here are spatial, never temporal.

The water is cool — a cellar's cool, eleven degrees, everywhere, always — except in the Bath, where the plumes stand like warm trees and the shimmer above them is a fixed feature you could navigate by. The materials are all of them living: cellulose worn smooth as old timber, silica set like glass, latex bladders, iron-tannin bark gone rust-orange, chalky drums, chitin plating. And every one of these is *finished*. That is the quality the place gives off most strongly after the noise: completion. The currents have been polishing the same corners for a million years. The towers are streamlined like whale-bone. Nothing here is half-built, nothing is going up, nothing is coming down. The plateau does not weather. It is *weathered* — the perfect passive voice, the state a thing enters after all its verbs are done.

The animals are the workforce. This is not a metaphor they would object to, if they could. Scraper-crabs work the stacks as fitters and cleaners, keeping the vent-collars clear; schools of courier-fish run fixed postal loops between districts, carrying chemical messages in their bodies, each route ancient, each generation trained before birth by the route itself. Great armored grazers ride the looms, cropping the belt-growth in perfect sequence, line-workers with ten-thousand-year seniority.
