**The Ninth Upcast**

You arrive by the Old Hoist, a cage on a chain that climbs the inside of the spire for a day and a half. The roar arrives before the summit does — first as a furriness in the links you lean against, then in your molars, then in the cage bolts, then everywhere, so that when the door finally opens at the head you step out not into a sound but into a weather. Loudness here is climate. You stop trying to hear it and start wearing it.

The head of the Ninth Upcast is a room the size of a cathedral nave and no bigger, and it is the only room at this altitude. You can hold the whole place in one look. It has edges: a rail, a frost line, a drop. Down through the floor runs the flue, a pipe two kilometers tall standing on the world below, and across the flue's mouth sits the fan — nine blades, each the size of a barn door, turning with a slowness that reads as stillness until the moment a blade passes the viewing port and the pressure steps on you, gently, like a heart the size of a building. Every twenty seconds, one blade. One full turn every three minutes. The fan has been pulling the deep world's breath up and throwing it at the sky since before anyone's records, and the sky has never once complained.

Everything green lives downstream of that breath. The exhaust goes out through louvers in the gallery walls and spills across terraced shoulders of the spire, and there it cools, and there it weeps. This is the place's first law of water: the rain here falls from underneath. Under the core of the plume the condensation is dense enough to fall as warm drizzle out of a clear sky; elsewhere it just beads — on rails, on pans, on moss — and ticks into the tinned-copper catchments. The pans drain to gutters, the gutters to a downtake, the downtake four kilometers down to the busy world below. The Ninth Upcast is a condenser as much as a chimney. The garden is its wet work, its rounding error: everything life uses here is spillage.

Ten steps from the plume's edge, the property line. Inside the breath it is greenhouse-warm, thick, faintly breathed — you are, in the strictest sense, indoors, standing in the exhalation of a city. Outside the breath the altitude reasserts itself: rime like sifted sugar on the same grating, air thin enough to make the rail swim, cold with the patience of altitude. The frost line runs across the deck like a ruled stroke. Inside it, orchids. Outside it, nothing green at all.

And inside it, so much green. Moss is the base layer and also, by design, the machinery — the shaft bearings are packed not with grease but with a fibrous moss bred for the job, self-replacing, self-lubricating; maintenance here became a synonym for gardening long ago. A fig has taken the north port, roots strangling a condensate main, canopy domed against the draft and always nodding. Espaliered trees stand trained against the louvers in rows some forgotten arborist set out. Ferns own the shadowed seams. A vine climbs the fan housing and flowers once every eleventh year, briefly, enormously. There is soil — excellent soil — but all of it is compost: leaf litter, chitin, oxide, a thousand years of garden making its own ground atop the made. Dig anywhere and you hit plate, never rock. There is no stone on this summit. There is no "here" that isn't manufactured; the only natural thing in sight is the sky.

One strangeness recurs if you look closely: every plant — fern, fig, orchid, the grass in the grating seams — carries the same leaf. One shape, serrated, blunt-tipped, matte green, as if all of them descend from a single draft in someone's notebook.

The fauna live entirely inside the breath, and they live loud-lives in the roar. Midge swarms hang in the plume in columns the wind draws and redraws. In the pans, where the dew carries a mineral skin up from below, a comma-sized shrimp thrives in numbers beyond counting; frogs — broad-mouthed, dark as the condensate — work the gutter pools, and they sing in the beat, each adult cramming one full phrase into the gap after each blade-pass and then holding still, in meter, for twenty seconds. Vent martins hawk the midges on the updraft and nest in the gap-plates along the frost line. Moths upholster the warm housings like grey fur. One hive of bees has lived for generations in a dead instrument cabinet, wax sealing the pressure gauge at 3.4 forever. Flat white spiders run webs across the gutters that the warm rain tears out weekly and the spiders rebuild — the only fast industry here. And there is a heron. One. Half-deaf, presumably; it stalks the pans for frogs and shrimp, and at dusk, some days, it blows a single flat horn note that cuts the roar like a blade.

Certain days, the chord moves. The flue is an organ pipe two kilometers tall and it holds a chord: a fundamental too low to hear, felt in the sternum; the roar proper; over that a thin ring at the top of hearing that hurts if you think about it. On pitch-days the deep world draws harder, or easier, and the whole chord drops by a small interval — and the animals know before you do. The moths rise in a body. The martins follow. The frogs compress their phrases into the longer gaps. The heron blows its note. Then the pitch settles, and the day resumes being no day at all.

From the rail, the world: nothing. Not nothing much — nothing. The spire's balustraded shoulders fall away out of sight. The cloud deck lies a kilometer below like a grey sea, and at night a bloom of city light spreads under it, a lamp under a quilt — the below, inhabited, busy, unaware of its own exhalation. On the horizon stand the Eighth Upcast and the Tenth, faint as pencil lines, each with its own small stain of vapor, its own probable orchard, a day's walk away along catwalks no one has walked. Between them: air, weather, and the one flue going down.

The tenders are gone, and the frost keeps their paths. That is the tenderest law here: the old walkways survive as pale seams through the green, not because anyone walks them, but because the frost does — every night, outside the breath, along the exact routes they used, killing whatever seeds try. The map of their working is preserved in temperature. On the gallery desk, under a tin, the last log's final entry is a chore list, not a goodbye: *Blade 6 flat. Top-dressed the west pans. Frogs loud.* The moss took the last of their duties in the end; that is the kindest read. Rust and root reached their settlement long ago — the oxide stable under the moss like a scab under gauze, the blade-tips' groove in the shroud ring a finger wide after centuries and worn glassy by the moss that lives in it and feeds on its dust. The metal has stopped counting its losses. The Old Hoist still keeps its timetable regardless: three times a week the empty cage rises, waits six hours at the head with its door open, and descends. Whatever it was waiting for, it is still waiting.

Certain laws hold, then. Warmth is local, and you cross its border in ten steps. Water comes from below and the rain falls upward. Loudness is weather; visitors write to each other in the dew on tabletops and talk by hand on metal, palm flat on the guardrail, the rail translating. The beat is the only clock, and everything alive tells time by it; everything slow is the world, and everything quick is alive. Everything is made, and the made has become the ground.

At dusk you stand at the rail in the thin cold while behind you the breath goes on blooming. Somewhere under the cloud, four kilometers down, thousands of people are finishing their shifts, and their exhalation, delivered to altitude, waters an orchard nobody harvests — the frogs take their gap, the heron blows its one note, a fig drops and ferments where no hand will find it. The fan makes another turn. A blade passes the port. The pressure steps on everything, gently, the way it has ten billion times, and will ten billion more.
