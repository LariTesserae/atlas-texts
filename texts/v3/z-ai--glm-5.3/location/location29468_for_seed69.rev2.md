There is no light in the Weep.

Begin there, because everything else begins there too. The Weep is a country folded underneath a high cold plateau — the surveyors call the plateau the Table, thin dry air above, nothing green for a hundred kilometers — and down here, under all that stone, in the top layers of a warm continental dark, nothing has ever seen anything. No sun has ever entered. No lamp is lit. The whole nation is conducted in other keys — heat, pressure, sound, touch — and it has grown lush and strange in them, the way music grows strange when it is composed for instruments no one has heard.

So you learn the place by ear. The crews who come down the Throat Line once a month learn it first as a discipline of the tongue: a click, sharp and small, thrown out like a stone, and the darkness sends it back shaped. A wall returns as a flat word. A passage returns long and syllabled. A drop-off swallows the click and says nothing at all, and you learn that the deep pits, the ones the maps mark *unanswered*, are places where your own voice does not come home. To call out here is to see. To sing is to shine. The dark is not an absence; it is the medium, and the medium is always full.

It is full first of water. The Table is high and cold, and the Weep is low and warm — heated from beneath, along veins of hot rock and hotter water — and the ceiling rock between the two never stops being chilled from above. So the warm air rises, meets the cold underside of the roof, and gives up its wet. The ceiling condenses. The ceiling weeps. All across the country the rock overhead shines — you know it by the cold of it on your wrist, held up — and drips, and in places rains outright, an upside-down rain that falls from the sky you can touch. Uprain, the crews call it. There are provinces where the uprain is the only rain there has ever been, drumming on the hangmeadows that grow rooted in the ceiling's cracks, an entire sky-flopped flora of moss and pale root and hanging fern that nobody has ever seen and that drinks from below instead of above.

The Weep breathes, too. When the weather turns on the Table and the pressure drops, the country exhales: warm fog rolls out of the low galleries and up the shafts in rivers you can lean against. When pressure builds, it inhales, and the uprain thickens, and the mistline — the altitude where warm air and cold roof make cloud — sinks down the walls until whole districts stand inside a glowing-less glow of fog, wet to the bone in ten minutes. Twice a day, roughly, the country turns its breath. It has no nights. It has tides instead.

And when it storms above — when the thaw or a big black storm sends meltwater screaming down the sinkholes — the Weep gets the Push. You hear the Push before it comes, which is to say you hear it as a strange arrival of *silence*, because everything alive can hear the bore wave coming minutes off and shuts up and climbs. A hush comes racing through the dark ahead of the water, swiftlets detonating upward in millions, rooter herds breaking for the terraces, and then the wall of water and pressurized air goes through the gallery like a train. Afterward the marshes are refreshed, the pools stirred, the country loud again, and the din is almost festive.

The din. That is the second fact of the Weep: it is never quiet. Loudness here is health. Loudness is daylight.

It comes in layers, and you learn them the way you'd learn the hours of a sky. Underneath everything, the water: thousands of drips out of sync, a hiss like distant surf, the trickle of gour dams spilling terrace to terrace down the travertine staircases that the water itself has spent a hundred thousand years building, rimstone pool over rimstone pool, each one a warm shallow pan of clear water over white calcite. Then the rivers — the big one is the Milk, broad and blood-warm, white with dissolved stone, running through the largest gallery like a spine; where it drops into the lower country its falls go out of earshot, and this is how you feel elevation in a lightless land, by the delay between what leaves and what lands. Some drops answer in a second. Some in five — a hundred and twenty meters of dark air. The Drop That Doesn't Answer takes your thrown stone and keeps it, and the maps mark it in red ink that no one down here will ever read.

Above the water, the life-noise. The ceiling is a rookery of cave swiftlets, billions deep, and it is a radio: their clicks layer and swarm and beat like weather, all night forever, all day forever. Bats run the big galleries in audible rivers, a smother of wing and squeak. The marshes tick and fizz — gnats, crickets, the throat-click of rooter herds as they work the mats, chunky pale grazers the size of pigs, blind as the rest of the country, seeing with their own throats. Pasture crabs clatter along the drip lines. Fish rise in the warm pools with a sound like kisses. And threading the floor, the grandads: dog-sized, then man-sized salamanders, white and soft and ancient, who announce themselves with a low knocking on stone, a deliberate woodpecker sound, so that everything small knows exactly where death is currently sitting. The grandads are honest. That is the courtesy of water predators here.

The Dim is not honest. The Dim is the land predator, and you know it because the noise *dents*. A hole in the sound comes walking through the reeds — bird-noise stopping, cricket-noise stopping, a widening cone of quiet that moves against the wind — and every animal in the Weep has one reflex drilled to the bone: read the silences. Silence is the only black in this bright loud world. A silence that is deep, slow, and narrow is a hunter. A silence that is wide, rushing, and rising is a flood. A silence that smells of hot iron and refuses to end is a dead machine. Children of the crews learn the grammar before they learn words.

And the country is worth all this vigilance, because it is lush. Lush is what happens when heat and water and chemistry go on for ten thousand years with nobody watching. The forest is fungal: palewood, the crews call it — great bracket-fungi in pagoda tiers, trunks like soft stacked shelves, whole galleries of it, dense enough to walk on for a day. Underfoot, mats of chemosynthetic bacteria carpet the warm marshes, fed by sulfur and iron and the endless soft snowfall of guano that drifts down from the rookeries — the ceiling feeds the floor; the birds are the weather; the guano is the snow that makes the green country green, except that nothing here is green. The palewood is white. The reeds are white and hollow-boned and hum in the breath of the country, drum-reed, a marsh that plays itself twice a day. At the hot vents grows kettlegrass in exactly the shape of the warmth, a green-less meadow drawn in heat. The snottites hang from the acid corners of the ceiling like living drapery, and the colors of all of it — the iron-orange of the vent pools, the purple-black of the sulfur skins, the astonishing crimson of the bacterial mats — exist the way a dowry exists in a drawer: recorded in the surveyors' notebooks, deduced from stains, seen by nobody. The Weep dresses up for a party that has never once arrived.

Into this, the Company put its machines, and the machines are now part of the ecology the way reefs are part of a sea.

They came for the water — the Table is a thirsty place, and the Weep sits on the hot aquifer like a full bowl — and they are scattered because the veins are scattered. Stations, not cities: a pump house here where a hot vein runs near a gallery floor; a condensery there by a shaft; a transformer yard humming alone in the dark with nothing around it for a kilometer but reeds. Boreholes go up through the roof of the world and the Waterlift sends the Weep's warm blood upward to the dry city on the Table. Steam engines run on the veins themselves. Turbines sit in the Throat and the other breathing shafts and harvest the country's respiration, turning on the exhale and the inhale both. Cable strung from gallery to gallery on drip-cured posts carries the electricity, and telephone wire with it, thin singing threads, Nine to the Condensery, the Condensery to the Throat.

There are perhaps a dozen works alive. There are three times that many dead, and you can tell instantly, because of the dry.

Everything in the Weep is wet. Rock sweats. Metal sweats. Your boots never dry. The dead stations are the only dry places in the whole nation, and dryness is so wrong here that it functions as the local idea of a ghost. Fourteen drew too deep and drank its district's heat in a decade; the forest around it snuffed like a wick, and now the Cold Scar stretches off into the dark — palewood standing like bone, reeds like wire, no clicks, no fizz, a dry gray silence you could sleep in and never wake. Nothing nests in Fourteen's culverts. Even the grandads won't shelter there. The Company learned, in the slow costly way institutions learn, and the living stations now run throttled, at polite speed, so the veins stay warm — because the third fact of the Weep, after darkness and loudness, is that heat is fertility. The map of the living is the map of the warm. Every station's cooling outfall is an oasis; everything edible crowds toward it; the Dim patrols its margins at the hottest hour of the tide.

The machines are more than oases. They are stars. In a country navigated by sound, the loudest permanent things are the fixed points by which everything else steers, and Nine — the great pump, the heartbeat, four strokes to the minute, slow as a sleeping animal's heart — is the North Star of half the northern marshes. Herds hold their routes by it. Swiftlet rookeries are tuned by it. A whole guild of creatures has evolved into machine-mimics because the Dim, which hunts by ear, cannot hunt under a running engine: near a pump, everyone is deaf, so near a pump is safe. Steam-crickets sing the compressor whine. The engineer moth courts in exact time with Nine's exhaust beat, females gathering in hundreds at the fence on every fourth stroke, a slow strobing courtship conducted entirely in rhythm. Kill a pump in the Weep and a district goes blind. The Company knows it. There are men on the Table who believe, without saying so, that the pumps are load-bearing for the world.

The dead works are being reabsorbed at the pace of geology, and the country is old enough to be calm about it. Ruins here don't crumble; they accrete. The Condensery wears a wedding veil of dripstone, one white coat per century. There is a wheelbarrow in Bivouac Four that is now a low white hill with a wheel. There is a boot. The surveyors' brass plaques have gone green and can be read by thumb. The old palewood flumes the cutting-crews built still run down the falls, and the forest they were cutting has long since healed in behind them and grown through them, and no one remembers the ban as anything but law.

One person lives down here full-time. The warden of Nine keeps the oil cups filled and the telephone answering and a single grow-lamp, hooded, thirty centimeters wide, burning over one bed of potatoes and beans in the station-house — the only sun in the Weep, the only garden, tended like a fire. Lights are kept here the way knives are kept: hooded, rare, for cause. A bare lamp burns the bacterial mats white in a night and puts a whole rookery of swiftlets onto the rocks in blind tumult. Light is violence in this country. The people who work here go native in the ear instead; the crews' children click before they talk.

Some rules of the place, then, as the crews carry them:

No light but machine light, and machine light kept hooded.
Sound is sight; loudness is life; read every silence before you trust it.
Heat is fertility; follow the warm water and you are following the country's blood.
The ceiling is a sky; it weeps for you; its birds are your weather.
Everything is wet; anything dry has died.
The pumps are stars; do not let them go out.
The country breathes twice a day and has no nights; keep your time by strokes and tides, not by any clock the dark can't read.

Stand anywhere in the Weep and click your tongue, and the province answers: reeds humming their two-day's-breath, the uprain drumming the hangmeadows, the Milk insisting, the swiftlets' billion-fold static, Nine at four to the minute like a heart in the next room, the drip-organ gallery upstream where the falling water plays random slow music on stone pipes that grew tuned — and somewhere under all of it, a moving absence, patient, making its patient hole in the choir. It is a warm place. It is a drowned, stacked, ancient, extravagant place, ten thousand years into its own state and settled there, dressed in colors no one will ever see, lit entirely by voices.

It never went looking for light. Everything here is always telling the world instead.
