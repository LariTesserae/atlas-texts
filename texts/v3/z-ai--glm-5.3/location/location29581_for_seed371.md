# The Mill Gorge

It is very high and very narrow, and these two facts refuse each other. You are above the line where trees stop growing — well above it — and yet you are standing in a green room.

The gorge is a fresh wound in a young mountain. The whole range lifted within living memory, geologically speaking: the rock is pale, unweathered, full of sharp edges and clean fractures, stone that has not yet learned to be round. Nothing here has been smoothed by patience. The walls rise close enough on either side that in places you could touch both at once, and they go up and up past the reach of light, so that the gorge is a slot of darkness cut into a bright, cold, killing altitude. Sky exists as a strip. A ribbon of grey-white hangs overhead like a lit ceiling in a room whose walls are made of mountain.

The moisture is not rain, exactly. Rain falls on the summit — you can hear it ticking and slapping on the rock high above — but down here the water arrives differently: as seep, as weep, as condensation. The walls are cold, colder than the air that pours down the slot, and the air gives up its water against them constantly. Every surface films with it. Threads of it run down the stone in thousands of fine courses, gathering into beaded lines, gathering into thin silver ropes, all of them moving. The sound of the gorge is partly this — a vast quiet hissing, like rain on a forest heard from under a forest — and partly other things, which we'll come to.

Because the strange fact of this place is what grows in the dark. The walls are hung with vegetation as dense as any tropical ravine: mosses in deep piled felt, liverworts in overlapping shingles, ferns leaning out from every ledge and seam, a turf of algae and small hardy plants cushioning every horizontal surface. It should not work. There is almost no light. But the young mountain is warm inside — it lifted recently, and it is still hot at its core, still cooking — and warm mineral water rises through the fractures and sweats out of the walls. The plants do not live on the sun. They live on the mountain's own heat and on the dissolved richness of the fresh stone, which has never been leached, which is still full of everything a root could want. It is a greenhouse with a furnace below and a roof of rock, and the green things have found it and filled it.

And they have filled it *completely*. Density here is not an abundance; it is a condition. The plants are near-touching, layered, stratified like sediment: bright green moss on top, black moss beneath it, ferns threading through both, fungi pale as bone in the deepest shade near the floor, everything holding water, everything dripping. To walk the gorge — and it is walkable, a single passage perhaps two hundred paces long, a place with boundaries, a grove-scale cathedral — is to walk inside a living wall on both sides. You touch leaves with each shoulder. Your sleeves come out dark with wet.

There are no animals. Not one. No insects, no birds, no soil-worms, nothing in the water threads. It makes the gorge quieter than it should be — a lushness with nothing moving *in* it, only the vegetation itself and the water. The ferns stir only when air moves them. It is a garden without eyes, and this is somehow the eeriest thing about a place that is, by every measure, entirely natural.

Now, the noise — because the gorge is loud, and it is loud the way a factory is loud. This is where the place becomes itself. The young mountain does not seep quietly. It works. Somewhere below the floor, superheated water sits in chambers under pressure, and it discharges in cycles: every few minutes, from vents down the passage and from fissures in the walls, geysers of steam and mineral water erupt with a percussive *bang* like a hammer on a cylinder head, followed by a rushing roar, followed by a drain-gurgle as it falls back. The rock acts as chimney and amplifier; the sound goes up the slot and ricochets. Between eruptions the gorge keeps other rhythms — the constant runnel-hiss of condensation, deep rock groans as the fresh mountain settles along its new joints, and, when wind crosses the top of the slot, a low organ-tone that rises and falls in the throat of the gorge like breath through a pipe. It is a mill that grinds nothing. An engine with no product. All of the industrial energies — pressure, cycle, exhaust, torque, heat — are here, embodied in stone and water, with no structure ever built, no machine ever assembled. The industry is geological.

And it is turbulent. The gorge is not in equilibrium; it is in the middle of becoming. Blocks calve off the walls without warning and crash down into the fern-turf, crushing one garden and opening substrate for another — this happens daily, sometimes hourly. Steam scalds a ledge and kills the moss white; within the same hour new seep water stains it green at the edges. A geyser vent repositions itself along a crack; the plants around the old vent die and the plants around the new one explode. Every surface is either newly destroyed or newly colonized, and the destruction and the colonization are the same process. The floor — where light never comes — is a boneyard of recent rockfall wearing a thin skin of fungus, and on top of that fungus, moss is already arriving as spore-wash carried down the water threads. The place is a constant, loud, simultaneous demolition and bloom.

Stand in the middle of it. Cool air slides down from the summit and warm mineral air rises from the floor, and you are in the seam between them, in a column of mixed temperature that moves like a slow river overhead. Steam ghosts past the ceiling-strip of sky. The hammer goes off again, somewhere up the passage, and the roar comes at you like something with a schedule. Moss drinks. Stone falls. Something green reaches into a gap that existed this morning.

It is a cathedral two hundred paces long, at the top of the world, with a furnace for a god, and the service never stops.
