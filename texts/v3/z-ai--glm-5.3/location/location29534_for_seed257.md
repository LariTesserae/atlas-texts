Everything here is underwater. Everything here is made.

Those two facts, held together, are most of what there is to know about this place, and the place has been this way for longer than knowing has had a shape. The world is a warm black ocean, and the ocean is built. No light has ever come down from above; if the water has a surface, it has never introduced itself. Nothing below has evidence of a top — only a direction, *up*, where things cool, darken, and thin out. All the heat comes from underneath. The world's interior is still young, still burning in the slow way that stone burns, and the water carries that warmth upward the way a body carries warmth from its middle to its skin.

The only high thing in the world is what would be called a mountain if there were anyone to call it, and it isn't one. It is a labor. It rises out of a plain that is itself construction — level under level of older structure, silted down to a horizon — and in all the ages the place has been settling, no one has found the first stone. There is no bedrock. There is only earlier work. The Mountain goes down as rooms, and that is the vertigo of this world: depth is architecture. The newest rooms are the deepest, and nothing has reached them. The oldest room is the highest, and nearly everything lives in it or near it.

The summit is where the making began. Whoever the makers were, they started at the top of the world and cut downward, which means the highest room is the first room — the world's first thought — and everything below it, level after level descending into the heat, is commentary. Erosion runs the same direction as age. The carving at the top is worn to ghost-grooves. The cuts far below, where the work ended or merely stopped, are still sharp. Somewhere down there a half-finished line breaks off mid-curve. The silt knows where. Nothing else does.

The last flight of the Great Stair is the only part most tenants ever meet. Its treads are dished — by feet, maybe, or by silt, or by the boring of sponges; after enough time all three read the same. Along the wall at hand height runs a groove, one finger wide, continuous, polished by touch. The makers had no light to read by, or no eyes to read with — the water doesn't preserve the difference — so their literature is a groove, and their sight was the pad of a finger. In a world without light, every wall is a book if it's carved.

A warm seep rises through a joint in the landing. The water there tastes faintly of iron and of something older, and it climbs the stairwell in a thread, which is why the last flight is the most crowded place in the world: warmth and minerals come up it like smoke up a chimney, and everything that can gather at the top of a chimney does. On the landing, half-buried, lies one lost tool — a stone abrader worn to a wafer, older than most of the rooms below it.

The summit room is the size of a small chapel: the kind that seats thirty, or that was never meant to seat anyone at all. It was cut from the crown-stone in one piece. Wall, bench, dome, and rim are a single continuous surface, so the room feels less built than uncovered — as though the stone had always contained this room and the makers only agreed with it. There is no door. The only opening is a round sky-hole in the dome — an oculus, though there is no sky for it to be the eye of — and since the makers worked from the top down, the sky-hole was their only entrance. To come into this room, you descend out of the cold. That has been the room's rule since before anything now living was born, and it has not been revised.

The stone of the room is warm to the touch, warmer than the water, the way a wall is warm in a house whose fire is on the floor below. A bench runs around the wall, dished at intervals like stations. Above it the relief climbs in processions — figures ascending or descending; the wear has made direction a matter of faith. Under the oculus, a ring of small cups is carved into the dome, evenly spaced, each holding a pinch of silt. The groove comes up the stair, crosses the sill of nothing, and ends at the bench, the way a sentence ends by sitting down.

Nothing in this world is plant. There are no plants anywhere and never have been. The food chain begins in fleece — white and rust-colored mats of microbes that graze the stone's warmth and the minerals in the seeps — and on that fleece everything else is founded.

The room is crowded the way a rush-hour train is crowded, if the train moved one notch above stillness and had been in service for ten thousand years. Floor to dome: silt to the ankle, warm, plowed into long furrows by sea cucumbers that work it like a field, a furrow to a season. Brittle stars knuckle through the furrows in handfuls. Sponges stud the lower walls, and some of them are borers — they live by eating the room, which makes them the erasers; and in erasing they preserve, because every groove they hollow becomes a shelter for something smaller. The walls are double-written: cut once by the makers, cut again by the sponges, and both hands were patient. Blind fish the color of the inside of a shell patrol the stair in circuits, whiskers first. Shrimp keep the world's accounts of sound. Anemones occupy the carved cups in the dome, one to a basin, tentacles raised up through the silt — and they fit so exactly that at some point you have to decide whether the room was built for them, or they were shaped, over ages, to the room. The room does not arbitrate. Crinoids stand along the rim of the sky-hole, arms open to the slow rising current: the room's hands, held out.

Deep corals continue the construction. Century by century they lay a film of new stone across the old tool-marks, so the mountain is still being built by tenants who do not know they are builders, at roughly a millimeter to a hundred years. The makers' work is the foundation of their successors' work, which is one definition of an inheritance. Another word for it is reef.

The rules hold whether or not anything witnesses them. All light is animal: the only illumination in the world is made by the animals themselves, in brief blue points — a shrimp's warning, a fish's courtship, the spark of something startled — a few times an hour at most, gone before anything can be sure what it saw, which may be the point. All heat is interior: the summit water is blood-warm, the depths approach scalding, and no animal crosses the line below the last inhabited level; past it the stone is bare and sterile, descending into heat and dark, which here are the same direction. All structure is made: *natural* is a word with no referent, though the tenants, having no words, are unbothered. All building is by touch: stone, shell, tooth, and cord of gut were the whole of the kit, and its marks are everywhere — the finger-wide groove, the abrader's cross-hatch, the cross-hatch worn soft again.

Time here is long, and it is spent rather than passed. The dishing of a stair-tread is an era. A coral's film is an epoch. The silt deepens by a hair per age. If the world has a history, it is legible only at these scales — and it is being reread constantly, by snails, in order, groove by groove down the processions. Or they are only eating, and order is what a mind supplies. The room keeps its counsel.

The quiet is load-bearing. Most hours contain the hiss of the seep in the stairwell, the sound of one's own blood, and, at long intervals, a single tick from the dark — a shrimp somewhere below closing its claw on the world's only news. Every few hours the ocean turns over, a slow convectional shrug, and the whole mass of it shifts with a pressure felt in the chest rather than heard: a note below sound, in the register stone must hum in, if stone hums.

What the room is for has not settled. The bench faces the sky-hole, and the sky-hole faces up, into cold and black, toward an answer that has never come — so one reading is vigil: the world's first room built to wait for a morning it had no way to be given. Another is simpler. The cups were always for anemones, the groove for snails, the bench for whoever was tired — a room built for residents rather than reasons. The room does not choose. It holds its warmth the way old houses hold the memory of a fire, long after; and once in a great while, through the sky-hole, a single blue spark falls out of the upper water — some small glowing thing drifting down like the first flake of a snow this world, warm to its core, will never get to keep.
