You hear the Shoal long before you see it.

From the dry rim of the basin, the sound arrives first — a weather of noise, rolling up the slope like something with mass. It is not one sound. Close under it is a bass hum with no source and no end, the note a continent makes when it is metabolizing. Over that, a granular click-layer, billions of small shutters and siphons and joints working out of phase. Over that, wind torn through a million hollow spars, a choir that changes key when the fog does. Over everything, bells — not signaling anything, just contributing. The dry highlands where you'd stand are silent by comparison, and the silence reads as absence, as a mistake. Nobody lives up here. Up here is the wasteland: too high, too dry, too quiet to support anything.

The city below fills the whole basin floor and runs to the horizon in every direction, a shallow warm sea of structure the size of a small continent. Its people call it the Shoal, and the name is exact — it stands in water barely above its own knees, and it has the density of a fish-run, every niche occupied, every surface claimed, no gap between presences that something isn't already moving into. The air above it is the city's own breath: a gold haze at 35 degrees, saturated, so that sweat is useless and every wall wears a permanent glossy film. Warm rain falls somewhere in it at every hour, but most of the weather is manufactured on-site — the decks exhale heat and moisture all day, and at dusk, when the upper surfaces cool across the dew point, the whole megalopolis weeps. Water beads out of the nacre in runnels and finds the grooves that a thousand years of dripping have cut into every vertical face. The Weeping Hour is the closest thing the Shoal has to a shared clock.

The city is grown, not built — though "grown" undersells the engineering, and "built" undersells the patience. The walls are aragonite laid down molecule by molecule under instruction: load-bearing nacre, oolite aggregate, flex-cables of chitin a meter thick, piezoelectric veins that make the whole structure a continuous nervous system. Nothing here is assembled and nothing is waste. This is a place with no plants — not a fern, not a moss, not a plankton bloom worth the name — and every job a plant would do is done by the city itself or by an animal. The base of the entire food web is a film, a chemosynthetic scum the color of weak tea, which the city cultivates on itself the way a body cultivates skin flora. The film grazes the mineral for what the mineral exhales. Everything else grazes the film. It grows thick on any surface left alone for an afternoon, and no surface is left alone for an afternoon.

The fauna are why the silence would frighten anyone who lived here. The shallows between the foundation piers are stands of siphon-worms — animals that do the work of reeds, standing in their thousands in the warm water, swaying with the current, snapping shut in rippling waves when something passes. On the flooded lower streets move the lickers: broad, tame, slow, wet-brown animals the size of cattle, grazing the film off the walls with tongues like squeeges. Their dung is the film's fertilizer; the film is their pasture; both belong to the city, and the city to them — the Shoal's gut fauna, and it knows them by name the way you know the bacteria in your own mouth. Above the streets the air itself is crowded, a vertical bazaar of mites and midgelike things at every scale, and through them cut the swifts — knife-shaped, warm-blooded, riding the standing thermals that rise from vent stacks, never landing except to lay eggs in gutters. Out on the warm sea, where the shelf runs knee-deep to chest-deep for four hundred kilometers under the city's outermost decks, something big filters. Nobody sees it. Everyone agrees on its existence the way coastal people agree on the tide.

Because water moves down, and light cannot be trusted to, the Shoal pumps its daylight after its rainwater. Mirror-gutters and heliostat shafts carry the pale-gold sky down through forty levels of city, and the shaft-mouths on the lower decks are the watering holes of this world — animals and people both gathering in the pooled light, holding out their hands to it. Depth is age here. Every level you descend is older, and the deep city drowns by degrees into the water table: the Sump, warm as blood, lit like a cathedral and only at intervals, rilled and fluted by a million years of dripwork, its columns furred with film, its air thick enough to chew. The city builds upward at exactly the rate it dissolves downward. That is the first law any newcomer learns: mass is conserved. Nothing is added here that is not subtracted somewhere, and the whole continent-scale balance is watched, tended, and balanced like a scale.

The second law: emptiness is temporary. Vacancy is an error state. Clear a surface and the film claims it by evening; clear a street and lickers gather; leave a volume genuinely unused and the city, which grows where use is dense the way a muscle grows where load is dense, will quietly dissolve the unused walls and spend them elsewhere. Presence is fertilizer here. Crowding feeds the place, which is why the place is crowded past saturation — density is not a failure of planning but the local form of nourishment, and the widest boulevards are the ones the city is starving.

The third law: the noise is law. It is the sound of everything functioning, and the Shoal reads it continuously — noise gradients for navigation, the Hum's pitch for the hour, dips and swells like weather. Quiet spots are either sacred or broken. Speech is a narrow-band art, pressed into whatever sliver of spectrum is open, but the intimacy of the place runs deeper than sound: parents talk to infants through bone, skull held to jaw, and two friends standing chest to chest in a market can conduct an entire conversation through pressure, through tapping, through the wet loud world against the skin.

And the markets are where the turbulence lives. The Shoal reorganizes itself the way a shoal of fish turns — all at once, from the inside, with no leader visible — and roughly every ninth day a district will simply change overnight: canals re-routed, a whole street-face dissolved and regrown, a staircase where the fish-stalls were. Nobody protests. Growth-code is the currency — patterns, templates, instruction-sets for rooms and tools and vessels, traded in sealed waxlike wafers at stalls that ring like forges — and the city files its own notices in advance, as efflorescence: a faint mineral bloom on the affected walls, weeks ahead, which everyone knows how to read with a wiped thumb. The city does not tell you what it wants. It grows what you use and takes back what you don't, and the negotiations are conducted in traffic, in film, in nutrients, in the pressure of nine hundred million lives.

Salt is what the water leaves behind when the Weeping dries — the Shoal's freckles, blooming white on every rill and windowsill, scraped up by hand by harvesters who work the dusk like farmers work a dawn. On the rim of the basin, at the very end of the descending road, a harvester finishes a wall and stands in the last thick light, wet to the collar, salt to the wrist. The hum below has not paused in nine thousand years, but it drops now, just slightly, as the first billion droplets condense out of the air — a hush you can hear from up here, a held breath the size of a sea. Then the Weeping Hour arrives everywhere at once, a soft detonation of dripwork, and the noise comes back up like applause.
