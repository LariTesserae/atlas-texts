The first thing you notice is that the shape is wrong for a mountain. The ridge is sharp and fresh and simple, the kind of spine that uplift throws up and hasn't yet learned to soften — but sitting on its shoulder, a few hundred meters of complicated, lobed, terraced architecture: domes overlapping domes, buttresses, flutings, a skyline of branching masses like frozen couplings. Nothing geological is that ornate. It is a reef. It is a reef three thousand meters in the air.

Sometime within the last few thousand years — yesterday, in stone-time — the seafloor here rose, fast enough that a whole coral city came up out of the water before it could die properly. The sea withdrew to some distant basin and then left even that, and now the old seabed far below is a salar, a white plain of salt laid out flat to the horizon, pale as a held breath. The reef stayed where the thrust put it. The reef, being a reef, did not stop building.

The ground is warm. Not sun-warm — *body*-warm, blood-warm, all the way down. The uplift is young and the rock beneath is still cooling its own violence, and the massif sits on that heat like a seedbed. Touch the coral and it feels like the flank of a sleeping animal. This is the warmth that makes everything else possible. There is no rain here. There are no streams, no dew ponds, no seeps. From the ridge you can see the whole world's dryness in one turn of the head. The terraces underfoot are unworn, their aragonite edges still keen enough to cut; the gravel fans spilling off the massif are fresh every spring, sharp chippings that haven't yet had time to round. The world outside is unfinished. The city is the only finished thing on it.

The boundary is real and legible. At the talus line the air changes — a fraction more weight, a smell of brine and green — and there is a scent laid down by the citizens, a chemical moat, a ring of statement that every creature here can read at a distance. Past that line, you are inside. The fog comes at dawn. It walks up the scarp out of the salar in slow grey ropes, and no one can say where a salt plain gets its moisture, and the city has never asked; it simply opens itself. Every leaf in the canopy is a shallow basin, silver-waxed, tilted inward. Every trunk is channelled. The fog touches the grove and is caught drop by drop, and the drops are conducted — never spilled, never exposed — down into the body of the city. The fog has no sound. It is the only rain this place has, and it falls silently into account.

Because there is a grove. Under the fog and the wax and the discipline, this is a lush place, and the lushness is the machine.
