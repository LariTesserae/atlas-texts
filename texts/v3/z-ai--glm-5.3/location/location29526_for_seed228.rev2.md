# The Cistern of Living Water

## First impression

You come down into it through a fault in the karst — a stairway that wasn't built so much as *grown* by machines that have since gone quiet. And then the space opens, and it is loud. Not loud like machinery, though machinery is here. Loud like weather. Loud like a market of waters.

It is a cavern the size of a cathedral nave, maybe two cathedrals set shoulder to shoulder, and it is in a permanent state of rain that falls *upward*. Water rises from the floor — from spongy stone terraces that weep from every pore — lifts in bright filaments and droplets into the vault, condenses against the curved ceiling in a sheath of silver, and falls back as a fine, warm drizzle. The cycle takes perhaps forty seconds per drop. The whole room is a slow fountain turned inside out.

The light comes from the water itself. Each droplet carries a suspension of engineered particles — legacy nanotech, older than any record here — that fluoresce when agitated. Rising water burns cool blue-white; falling water shifts amber. So the chamber breathes light: a blue lift, a gold fall, blue, gold, on a rhythm that never quite settles, because the water is argumentative.

## The water

It is warm — blood-warm, nearly bathwater — and it is everywhere, but never pooled deeply. The floor is terraced specifically to prevent pooling: seven broad steps of water-worn limestone descending toward a central channel, everything angled, everything drained, everything fed. There is no standing water and no dry stone. The rock is the color of wet bone and feels like it is sweating.

The central channel is where the turbulence lives. A channel two meters wide runs the length of the chamber floor, and in it the up-falling water concentrates, fights itself, braids and unbraids. It's the loud part. The channel roars — a sound like a river forced through a pipe too small, like applause, like a crowd. Spray stands off it in permanent white crests. The channel is the chamber's voice and it never stops talking.

Squatting over the channel at intervals are the only structures in the place: three pillars, waist-high, matte black, seamless, radiating faint heat. They are regulators — or were. They still work; the water cycle is too orderly, too deliberate, to be natural. But they work *differently* now. Something in them drifted over the centuries, and the climate they maintain has become something between a machine's function and a habit. The turbulence of the channel is their argument with each other. When two pillars fall into phase, the channel calms for a minute, the up-rain stills, the whole chamber holds its breath in green-white light — and then the phase breaks and everything crashes back to blue and gold and roar.

## What grows

Almost nothing. The light is abundant but the water is restless, and roots want stillness. There are pale, glassy films of algae-like growth on the terraces where the spray settles — thin as breath, slick, faintly warm. In the corners farthest from the channel, where drizzle is gentlest, a few stunted things that might once have been plants grow in crack-shadows: whitish stalks, no leaves, tremulous, leaning toward the pillars' warmth rather than the water's light. They are the chamber's only quiet things and they look embarrassed to be here.

## What lives

Fauna is nearly a rumor. But there is one presence: small bodies of water that move with intent. Knots of liquid, fist-sized, that detach from the channel's spray, glide through the up-rain, reshape themselves around falling droplets to absorb them, and return to the channel. Whether they are machines, organisms, or just a habit the water has developed in this place after so long — no one here can say, and they do not say. They avoid you. They do not seem afraid, only occupied.

## The marks of people

Someone used this place. Along the highest terrace, protected by a shallow lip of stone from the worst of the drizzle, there is a low bench cut flush into the wall, and beside it a socket in the floor — a fitting whose purpose is obvious even now: something was meant to stand here once, facing the channel. Whatever stood there is long gone. The bench is polished from use, not from weather. People sat here. They sat here a long time, watching the water argue.

## The rule of the place

You learn it within an hour, and it is the only law: *do not still the water.* The chamber is turbulent because it is alive — the pillars drive it, the water obeys and disobeys, the cycle turns, the light breathes. If you cup your hands and hold a handful of up-falling water — if you stop it — the light in your hands dims, and the nearest liquid-knots gather, and the channel's roar dips, as if the whole room has turned to look. Release the water, and everything resumes, and there is a suggestion — only a suggestion — that the channel's next crash is a little louder than it needed to be.

Sit on the bench, in the blue-gold rhythm, under the loud, warm, quarrelsome rain, and it is very easy to believe the room does not mind you being there. It is also easy to believe it would not notice if it stopped minding.
