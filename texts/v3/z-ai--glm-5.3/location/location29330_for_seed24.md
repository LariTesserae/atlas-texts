**The Low Room**

Warmth comes first. Before your eyes admit anything, the heat arrives — rising from below like breath from a stone mouth — the whole floor of the place warm as a quern used for a hundred years, as a hearthstone the morning after the fire. The air above it is warm too, and dry, and so still that it feels less like air than like a held substance, something you move through rather than breathe. This is how the place announces itself: as temperature. Only after the warmth does the darkness arrive, and it is nearly total.

There is light, but you have to find it, and when you do, you understand that finding it is the whole story of the place. On one wall — the southeastern, though no sun ever confirms this — a shelf of felted growth lies against the rock like a curtain of pale fur, and this felt gives off light. Not much. A low, steady, greenish-white radiance, dimmer than moonlight on clouds, the kind of glow foxfire holds. It does not flicker. It does not pulse. It has been on, as far as anything can tell, for as long as the chamber has been dry, which is to say for an unguessable time. It lights a small territory — a few paces of wall, a lens of floor below it, a suspended gleam in whatever dust happens to drift — and everywhere else is black. The room is an island of sight in a sea of the other thing.

And it is a room. That is the strangest comfort of it: the place has edges. Some forty paces long and twenty wide, with a ceiling that domes up higher at the center like the inside of an egg turned over. The walls are rounded, softened, finished — carved out of pale limestone long ago by water that no longer exists anywhere in this part of the world, then dried and left alone through so many millennia that the carving has become a kind of architecture. At the far end, a single great block of breakdown lies where it fell in some deep prehistory, its edges blunted, its underside fused to the floor by patient dust. At the near end, the passages that once led onward pinch into talus-chokes, sealed heaps of settled rubble. Nothing comes in that way; nothing goes out. The chamber is bounded, complete, a place with a size, the way a cupped hand is a place.

The floor is dust. Fine, mineral, warm dust, ankle-deep in the corners and laid thin at the center, where it has compacted into a fragile crust — and here is one of the strange facts of the place: the crust is unbroken. No track crosses it. Nothing that ever came here left a print, or nothing that lasted, and in the dryness everything would have lasted. Where the crust cracks, it cracks on its own terms, in shrink polygons like a dry lake bed in miniature.

What does grow here arrives from above.

Through the fissures in the dome, roots descend. One is enormous — thigh-thick, its bark worn smooth and dark by the patient friction of falling dust — and it hangs down through the dark like the center rib of some vast wooden chandelier. Around it hang the finer ones: ropes, cords, threads, some still bark-brown near the ceiling, some blanched to bone-pale where they've hung longest in the lightless air. They form curtains. They hold perfectly still, and yet each one is a living pipe, sealed along its whole length, and inside every pipe is the only water in the place. That is one of the rules here: water exists only inside living vessels. There is no drip, no pool, no damp seam in the stone — the world's water has withdrawn entirely into hoarded tissue, into wood and root-hair, into the deep-sealed rock itself. The roots are the rivers of this place, running internally, invisible, downward.

On the great root, about the height of a standing person, there is a scar. It is old. A long clean stroke was cut into the root once — a blade's width, a blade's depth — and the tree answered with a raised welt of healed tissue, resin-hardened, now darkened to something like amber. Whoever made the cut made it from above, or from the mouth of a fissure; nothing of them came down here, and nothing of them remains anywhere. No flake, no ember, no tool. But the wound is legible. Somewhere up on the surface there are people with hand-fitted edges and clay bowls for fire, people who know which roots hold water and how to open one like a stoppered jug — and this chamber, which no one has ever touched, carries their single gesture anyway, healed over, grown through, carried down on the living wood.

The rest of the chamber's few presences stand well apart from each other, in the manner of the place — everything given room. Near the glow, a small drift of root-litter lies on the dust like gray driftwood, desiccated rather than rotten, because in this dryness nothing decays, only mummifies. Beside the glowing felt, where the faint light reaches a seam of moisture the roots exude, a handful of pale stems rise from the floor: waxy, colorless, a hand-span high, leaves reduced to loose scales, heads bent. They have no green at all — they don't need it, being fed entirely by the fungal threads braided through the floor and linked, root to root, to trees in a sunlight none of these stems will ever perceive. The entire chamber runs this way: on imported energy, on sugar made by a sun it has never seen, piped down through fifty feet of stone in wooden conduits. Even the light on the wall is a byproduct — the fungus's slow lamp, lit, presumably, to summon flyers in a world that has flyers. No insect has ever answered it here. The glow keeps its appointment anyway, and has for centuries, a signal in a language with no listener.
