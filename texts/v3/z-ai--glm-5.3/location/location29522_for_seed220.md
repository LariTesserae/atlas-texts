The last stair of the descent is the only modest thing about the place. It is a person wide, a person tall, worn into a shallow trough down its middle by feet that stopped coming long ago, and from its bottom rises first the sound — a low, enormous pressure felt in the sternum before it is heard by the ears — then the warmth, like standing downwind of a sleeping animal, then the smell of wet mineral and hot dust. At the bottom, the stair opens into one room. Only one. A chamber, single and bounded, an organ removed from some buried body of a city and set here in the dark to do its work.

A plate beside the doorway gives the room its official name — EQUALISATION VESSEL, TERTIARY DISTRICT, with a serial number long since gone the color of the metal — but anyone who has spent a night here calls it the Throat, because that is what it does. It swallows for the city. Every buried district above and beyond it breathes through this room, and the room's whole architecture is one long, patient gulp.

The emptiness is the first thing that rearranges you. The Throat is a void kept deliberately, a hall far vaster than its machinery needs, and the reason for the surplus is the surplus itself: the air is the working part. The bore of the chamber is the instrument, the way the hollow inside a horn is the instrument, and the room is mostly hollow, mostly nothing, with a few presences standing far apart in their own acres of dark — the central column, a keeper's bench, the channels in the floor, the door you came through — distances between them magnified until presence itself feels like a kind of event. You cannot see the room. You hear it arrive, in layers.

The machinery is a stack of rings around a standing column of air. The rings turn without touching — no bearings, no oil, no wear in the sense your hands would recognize — and they flex the column of air by means that have no moving part you can point to, only effects: the swallow, the exhale, the warmth. The heat is waste heat, and it is everywhere, radiating from the housing and faintly from the walls themselves, kiln-warm, laundry-room warm, warm enough that you sweat through your clothes inside ten minutes and the damp never leaves them again. The walls record four epochs of building stacked like tree rings: the oldest course is chisel-cut stone, its tool-marks nearly erased under mineral milk; above that, a poured seamless stone that looks grown rather than cast; above that, a skin — an engineered film, part lichen, part machine, that repairs the walls at a pace indistinguishable from geology. The erosion here is not decay. The accretion and the repair reached an agreement centuries ago and have honored it since: the travertine rims grow up the channels exactly as fast as the skin heals them, flowstone aprons drape the housing where water has always run because water has always been allowed to run there, and nothing in the room is getting worse. It is finished weathering into itself. It has been in its own state longer than the stair has been worn.

Water exists here as weather, small and punctual. The ceiling sweats on a schedule. Between swallows, droplets gather in the dark overhead and come down all together — a chorus of drips landing on graded gravel, dozens of small wet percussion strikes that fall into time with the machinery's beat, so precisely that after an hour you stop hearing them as weather and start hearing them as part of the room's speech. At the exhale, warm rain falls in earnest, brief and heavy, and the floor's channels — cut, tiled, terraced with mineral rims — carry it away toward sumps you never see. Nowhere does the water pool deeper than a knuckle. The damp is the constant: in the rope hand-rails, in the paste that was once dust in the corners, in the air itself, thick and mineral-sweet, condensing on cold skin the moment you stop moving.
