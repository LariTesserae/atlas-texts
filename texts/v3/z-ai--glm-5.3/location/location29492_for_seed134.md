# The Foundry Shoal

The mountain is young. You can tell because nothing has learned to soften it yet.

It rises from the abyssal plain in a single geological shrug — a seamount barely ten thousand years old, its summit plateau stopping just short of the surface, capped by perhaps forty meters of green-gold water. On that plateau, light still arrives. It arrives in slanted cathedral columns that swing slowly with the swell, and everything below is arranged to receive it.

There is no plant life. Nothing here photosynthesizes in the way a forest would understand it. The plateau is mineral — basalt black and raw, knife-edged ridgelines of pillow lava that cooled too recently to accumulate sediment, glassy vents where the rock tore itself open and froze mid-gesture. It is a small place. A swimmer could cross it in four minutes. But no one crosses it in four minutes, because the shoal is *full*.

## The Industry That No One Built

Here is the strange fact of the Foundry Shoal, the thing that every visitor must reconcile: it is industrial, and nothing in it was constructed.

The fauna have converged on mechanism. This is not metaphor. The dominant presence is a colonial organism — call it the loom — that grows in rectilinear frames across the basalt, thousands of interlocking chambers of chitin and biogenic silica, and within each chamber a membrane pulses on a slow four-second cycle. Drive, exhaust, drive, exhaust. Each colony is an engine the size of a dinner table; there are thousands of them, near-touching, layered two and three deep across the plateau floor, so that the whole summit hums with their combined output. The water itself is the crankshaft. When the tide shifts, all the looms shift with it, a phase-lag rolling across the shoal like a wave passing down an assembly line.

What do they produce? Mineral lace. The looms filter the young rock's dissolved metals and extrude them as filigree — copper-red traceries, manganese black, brittle geometric growths that look exactly like heat-exchanger fins and function as nothing of the sort. The plateau is slowly burying itself in ornament. The water is dense with it, the water is dense with *everything* — swimmers, drifters, grazing clouds of glass-shelled grazers that hang in the light columns like punctuation.

## The Sound

It is loud here, in the way the sea is loud: a total, pressurized saturation with no silence anywhere in it. The looms contribute their deep hydraulic throb. The basalt ridgelines, still cooling at their cores, tick and crack — a staccato of thermal stress, tiny hammer-blows from the mountain's own foundry heart. Over everything rolls the distant surface weather, transmitted down as a slow enormous drumming, forty meters of rain or swell arriving as pure rhythm.

And threaded through it, the fauna: stridulating shrimp that sound like struck flint, the grazers clicking their silica shells in synchronized barrages, something in one of the deeper ravines that emits a tone so low and sustained swimmers feel it in their sternums before they hear it. Nobody has found what makes it. It has not changed pitch in recorded memory.

## The Rules of the World

The shoal runs slow. Every process here operates on patience: the looms pulse four times a minute, not four hundred. The mineral lace accretes a millimeter a year. The light columns swing with the seasons rather than the hours. Fauna here live long, move deliberately, and die in place, their frames becoming substrate for the next colony — which is why the plateau's density keeps climbing, layer over layer, an archive of engines built on engines.

The single rule that everything obeys: *nothing is wasted, and nothing is hurried.* Young rock, old water, and between them an economy of bodies that discovered the machine independently, without ever once needing a mind to design it.

You hover at the plateau's edge. The looms throb. The light columns lean, and lean, and lean. Forty meters above, the surface drums its slow applause on a world that has never once looked up.
