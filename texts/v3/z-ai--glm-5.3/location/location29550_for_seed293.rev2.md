You come to it across a marsh that has finished being anything else.

The horizon in every direction is water-flat, a plain of dark sedge and tea-colored pools so old it has stopped changing — nothing grows tall here anymore, nothing new establishes, the whole expanse settled into its final shape the way a river settles at the end of its delta. Quiet, too. Wind, water, one heron-colored thing standing a kilometer off, patient as furniture. You can walk an hour in this silence and start to believe the world has run out of events.

Then you crest the low rim of the Kettle and the sound hits you like weather.

Inside the basin — a bowl perhaps four hundred paces across, rim to rim — it is *loud*, a full-throated, layered, municipal loudness, and it begins and stops at the rim with no transition at all, as if the quiet outside were a wall you walked through. Warm air, warm as a greenhouse, rises past your face. The light inside is green-gold and bright, ordinary daylight strained through a living ceiling. Below you, the city.

It is called the Old Organ, and it is one plant, and it is also a town, and it is also — this takes longer to accept — a machine running a physics your body understands before your head does.

**The floor and the stones.** The basin floor is a woven mat of roots, warm underfoot, warmed from below by the Organ's own composting heart. The water table sits *above* the ground: sheets of shallow black water lie in the low places and move along channels the Organ maintains, and the air above them is never quite clear of mist. Scattered across the floor stand eleven rounded stones, worn to nubs, the only remains of a mineral city that stood here before the Organ germinated — the Lime City, digested ages ago, its calcium folded into the plant's bones. The stones weep continuously. Water rises through them and runs down their flanks in slow threads, leaving white trails of dissolved mineral, and birds drink from the bowl-shaped hollows their tears have worn. The Organ will not grow roots over the stones, and it will not grow across certain dead-straight lines that connect them — the old street plan, kept the way a body keeps a scar. The water channels follow those lines exactly. The citizens call them the Streets, and the Streets have names — Milk, Bell, Broad, Tanner — that the Organ maintains not in any language but in scent: each channel's water carries its own faint signature, so that a fish or a wasp can tell where it is by taste.

**The pillars and the rain.** Rising from the floor, wide apart, with long empty distances between them — that sparseness is real, the place is mostly space — stand the great buttress trunks, a dozen of them, each hollow, each a pipe. Their bases have melted-looking flares, flows of wood gone soft and re-hardened a million times, because the Organ has been at this for geological ages and everything about its chassis is rounded, finished, final. Up high, the canopy closes into a dome, and the dome *rains indoors*. Warm air condenses against its underside all day, and the drip is not random: the leaves at the ceiling's inner surface are shingled in graded sizes, tuned, so the condensation falls as a slow, steady, quietly melodic rain onto the floor — thousands of drops an hour, each landing on a pitched leaf, the whole basin faintly ringing with it like an instrument being played by its own breath.

The Organ has a heartbeat. Deep in the central trunk, hydraulic pumps drive sap up the pipes, and every second or so the floor flexes — *whomp* — a pressure you feel in your feet and sternum before you consciously hear it. Between heartbeats, everything else stacks: the hiss of ant traffic, the wet sawing of wasps enlarging their apartments, the drumming of birds, frog choruses in organized keys, and the constant descending scales of the resonant scale-insects, whose domed shells they vibrate in chords.

**The citizens.** The fauna are the Organ's hands, mouths, couriers, and data-lines, and they know their jobs the way you know your mother's voice:

- *Knucklebirds* — heavy, olive, flightless, taller than your waist — stride the open floor between the pillars, and when one wants something it walks to a buttress root and drums a specific rhythm. A minute later a tone comes back through the wood, and a nectar tap opens, or a bridge-print grows across a channel, or nothing happens because the request was denied. The birds argue. They are the most visibly *municipal* of the residents.
- *Gall-wasps* live in the Galleries, a district of programmable swellings on the south pillars, where the Organ grows apartments to specification — chambers sized to families, with sap plumbing and warm walls — in exchange for pruning, defense, and fine assembly work on the daily prints. A wasp's address is legible in the gall's scent to anyone who cares to read it.
- *Ledgerfish* run the Streets, shallow-water couriers with bright flanks, carrying sealed pollen pouches between districts. Their flashing patterns along their sides are read at each lock by ants.
- *The ants* are maintenance, millions strong, in gray chemical livery that the Organ can update: today's scent-tasks, today's closures. They are the anthill energy made visible, streams of them opening and closing channels, plugging, patching, ferrying.
- *Bell-frogs* sit at fixed stations and announce: shift changes, wage-tap openings, the arrival of rain overhead, the presence of guests. They are the city's clocks and its alarm system, and they take the job seriously.
- *Glasswing midges* live outside, in the flat marsh, and are summoned in storms at each shift change — pollinator weather, glittering static that pours in under the dome, does its work at the flowers, and pours out again.

Payment is nectar, sap, warmth, lodging, and light. The wage-taps run on schedule and the queues are honest; the one enforcement the Organ uses is the sun — its canopy has lens-wood skylights it can aim, and a cheat finds a hard bright circle on the floor following him around all day, drying him, until his account is settled.

**The tech.** Nothing here is mineral and nothing here is dead. What the planters left, ages ago, was not a device but a *method*, and the Organ has been running it so long that it has worn grooves in the local laws of matter:

The Organ *speaks in beams*. It shapes sound the way it shapes wood, and it can address an individual — a bird, a fish, you — with a voice that arrives inside the chest rather than the ear, pitched and accented precisely to your hearing, so that a knucklebird hears knucklebird and you hear something uncomfortably like your own language spoken by something with no lips. It uses this constantly, for warnings, for wages, for the sort of civic nagging that makes a city function. This is half the loudness: a city where the buildings never stop talking to the citizens.

The Organ *keeps its dead as music*. In the flooded vault under the central trunk, where the water is cold against all odds — sap supercooled by some chemistry that shouldn't hold — it grows panes of root-glass, and in the glass it stores days. Touch a pane and you get a dead bird's morning, its tune, the pressure of its feet on the mat. Every resident has a signature chord, held in the Archive. When one dies, the body goes to the compost notaries, the nutrients are tallied — nothing here is wasted, but nothing is quite thrown away either — and at dusk the Organ plays the chord once, into the floor, through every pipe.

That moment is the only silence of the day. Everything stops. Midges hang in the air. The indoor rain seems to hold its drops. Ten seconds, maybe fifteen. Then the city resumes at full volume, and this is somehow the most municipal thing about it: grief, but scheduled.

The Organ *prints*. Every dawn, from the great meristem nozzle high under the dome, it manufactures the day's short-lived organs in a soft rushing spray of growth — flowers, door-flaps, bridges, cups, light-lenses, and things harder to classify. Water, in its sap, can be *hardened*: cups and panes of standing water that hold their shape for hours, ring when tapped, and slowly evaporate back into ordinary liquid. The windows on the floor are made of this — hard-water lenses lying about like thick coins, magnifying the warm black streams beneath them.

**The flush.** Understand the turbulence, watch a flush. Some mid-mornings, without warning, a wave of flowering rolls across the floor — a thousand blooms opening in sequence with small crackling pops like applause from a huge audience, a wall of scent arriving after the sound like thunder after lightning, midges boiling in from the rim, birds running, fish flashing up every Street, the ant streams rerouting in real time, the dome raining harder in sympathy. It is total, it is deafening, it is over within the hour. By evening the petals are eaten, the hard-water cups lie melting, the day's bridges are softening back into the mat, and tomorrow's print will begin again. This is the ancient paradox of the place: its *form* is finished — nothing about the chassis has changed in a hundred thousand years, the geology is done, the Kettle is as smooth as a river pebble — but its *metabolism* never settled. The land stopped eroding and took up metabolizing. Stillness at the bottom, riot on top, and both of them, somehow, the same stability.

**The Attendance.** And then, mid-morning, most days, the strangest work of all. From the print, along with the flowers, come figures. Plant-grown, sap-filled, green-veined, roughly the size and gait of people. There are never more than a few. They walk the old street lines. They inspect the Teeth, counting them on soft fingers. They check seed inventory at the Vault door. They pause at the weeping stones as if waiting for someone to come out. The birds groom them without fear; the wasps ignore them; the whole city treats them as staff.

They are the planters. Or the Organ's memory of the planters, rehearsed in green tissue, day after day, from root-glass panes that are themselves copies of copies. The figures are not quite right — the faces are what a very old organism remembers of faces — and they wilt by dusk, softening, slumping, sinking into the mat, reabsorbed, printed again next dawn. You may walk with one. Everyone does, sometimes. You may not help one: interrupting its round makes it wilt faster, and the citizens regard this the way you'd regard tripping an old man. The rounds are the point. The rounds are all that's left of the reason.

**Rules, as far as anyone has collected them.** Nothing sleeps all at once; the day runs three shifts. All work is paid; the wage-taps open on the bell-frogs' word. The Streets obey the dead city's plan, and no living thing crosses the scent-lines at the stones. Don't dig — a hand's depth and the ground weeps for hours, and the ants will come plug your hole and you will smell like debt for days. Don't assist the Attendance. And if the gray Assessor comes over the rim — the long-legged, heron-stance thing that walks the perimeter perhaps once a year, taking notes nobody sees — do not feed it, follow it, or answer it, and the Organ itself will lower its music while it passes. Nobody remembers who made that rule. It is as old as the Teeth.

**Dusk, and under.** In the vault, below the cold water, something big turns over slowly in the dark — the Warden, an animal older than any record, possibly a founder's companion, possibly something else that decided to stay. In the deepest shelf of the Archive there is one pane that does not hold a day or a tune. It holds a seed. Not the seed stocks — those are tallied and current. A single seed, near-finished, that the Organ has been printing toward for longer than the marsh has been flat, and cannot quite complete, and will not discard. Nobody asks about it. The bell-frogs, who announce everything, are silent on the subject by some agreement nobody made aloud.

Then a knucklebird dies of old age near the Tanner lock, and dusk tolls: one chord, played through every pipe, and the entire Kettle — the whole loud bright civic day of it — holds still for fifteen seconds. Even the Attendance, halfway down the Broad Street line, stops, and stands soft and green among the pillars, waiting, with the patience of something that has been remembering for a very long time.

Then the night shift starts up, louder than you'd expect, and from the rim, if you climb out, all of it is gone at a single step, and the ancient marsh lies flat and quiet in every direction, keeping its own counsel, one enormous finished body with one small loud heart.
