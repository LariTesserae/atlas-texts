# The Shelf of Singing Moss (Where Nothing Sings)

The plateau exists the way a held breath exists — briefly, and under strain. It rose out of the mountain's shoulder within living memory; the oldest grandmother in the settlement remembers her mother's stories of the ground *cracking upward*, a shelf of pale, sharp-edged basalt pushing free of the slope with a sound like a giant's knuckles. The rock has not yet learned to be old. Its edges cut. Its columns still bear the slick, glassy skin of new stone, and in the three generations since its emergence the Shelf has risen another arm's length, quietly, insistently, so that the lower stairs of the village are always in the process of being buried and the upper stairs in the process of being built. The people measure their history in stairways abandoned.

It is a small place. A person of reasonable legs can walk its boundary in a quarter of an hour, and the boundary is real — a raw cliff-rim on three sides, dropping into the fog country below, and on the fourth the mountain itself, still steaming faintly from its recent labor. Everything the world offers these people fits inside that rim. Nothing else is theirs, and they do not pretend otherwise.

## The Wet Dark

Light arrives here the way rumors arrive: diluted, secondhand, late. The Shelf sits inside a permanent collar of cloud, and the sun, when it can be found at all, is a pale coin behind oiled cloth. It is cool — the kind of cool that lives in the fingers, not the wind — and the air is so saturated that a hand held still for a minute gathers a fine pelt of condensation. It does not rain here. Rain would be an event. Instead, water simply *materializes*: it beads on the black rock, it swells on every moss-frond, it beads along the cables of spider silk that span the ravines like harp strings strung by something patient. The silks are the village's aqueducts. Each dawn the people walk the thread-lines with clay cups hung from yokes and harvest the night's condensation from the webs — the spiders tolerate this, having been paid, in the way of things here, by being left the moths that fall.

And it is lush, absurdly, unreasonably lush for so young and dim a place. The moss came first, riding the fog up the cliff faces, and it now covers every horizontal and most vertical surfaces in a deep, springy, silver-green pelt an arm's length thick. Where the moss touches a basalt column for more than a season, small gardens erupt: fog-orchids with translucent petals that drink cloud directly, liverworts in rust and bronze, strangling vines that have not yet decided what they intend to strangle. The vegetation is young too, and enthusiastic, and slightly wrong — the orchids bloom in winter and sleep in whatever passes for summer, and several of the mosses are faintly, faintly warm to the touch.

## The Village, and Why It Is Quiet

Perhaps thirty families live on the Shelf, in houses of lashed timber and woven vine-wall, raised on stilts above the moss because the moss will otherwise take the floor for its own. There is no metal here, no wheel, no kiln that works in this damp. Tools are stone, bone, the astonishingly hard silk of the great moths, and fire is a guest that must be petitioned — the fog takes unprotected flame within minutes, so every hearth lives behind curtains of oiled hide and every family keeps a *flame-tender* whose only duty is to keep the flame from being reclaimed.

The village is sparse by choice. The houses stand well apart, connected by plankwalks, with wide dark intervals of empty moss between, because the people know something a visitor learns within an hour:

**Nothing here makes noise, because something here eats noise.**

Sound does not carry on the Shelf. It does not echo — the young rock would echo beautifully, but it is muzzled under its moss pelt, and the fog is soft as wool. Voices drop to a whisper within days of arrival, not from custom but from a sense, quickly acquired, that loudness *drains*. The people gesture richly. They have a hundred signs. When they must speak, they put their mouths close to an ear.

The reason moves through the moss at dusk. They call it the Listener — a pale, low-slung thing the size of a hog, all soft edges and no visible features except the vast, many-folded surface of its body, which is not skin so much as an *ear entire*. It grazes on vibration. It eats footfalls, birdsong, the crack of splitting rock, the hum of insect wings. Whole stretches of the Shelf fall silent when the Listener passes through — not that they were loud before, but there is silence and then there is *its* silence, a dense, pressurized quiet you feel in the teeth. The village endures it, works around it, leaves offerings of struck stones at the plateau's edge. No one has ever heard it. That is rather the point.

## The Overwhelming Others

And yet — the Shelf is *crowded*. The fauna is the true population here, and humanity a thin, quiet garnish upon it. The moths come up from the fog country in slow blizzards, wings the size of two hands, and they are the village's light: their abdomens glow with a cold blue-green luminescence, and the people keep *living lamps* — cages of woven silk, each holding a drowsy, feeding moth, hung at doorways and carried on staves through the permanent dusk. A lamp must be fed. A lamp will die in nine days. Every lamp in the village is a small grief on a schedule. There are moths the color of ash and moths the color of bruises, and on fog-thick nights the air above the Shelf is a slow drift of green-blue sparks, hundreds upon hundreds of them, and the children stand on the plankwalks with their mouths open.

Below the moths come the spiders, exploiting them. Below the spiders come the fog-cats — lean, soundless things the people have never entirely seen, only deduced from the geometry of what disappears. There are beetles the size of fists that farm the moss, pruning it in neat hexagons for reasons unclear. There are birds, but they have *given up singing* on the Shelf — a predator that eats sound selects against song in a few short generations, and so the local birds now signal by flashing the pale undersides of their wings, a population of semaphore flags chattering light across the dim green. Evolution here is happening at a sprint, because the ground itself is new, and every arriving species is improvising.

## The Active Hush

Do not mistake the quiet for stillness. This is an ecosystem at full gallop, conducted silently: the fog breathing up the cliff face every afternoon like a tide that never quite crests; the water beads fattening on the silk-lines and falling, each fall making a meal for the moss and a non-sound for the Listener; the plateau itself rising, a finger-width a year, cracking its stairways and feeding fresh stone to the lichen pioneers; the moths rising at dusk to be caught, harvested, milked for light and silk and finally eaten; the village climbing its own slow staircase of rebuildings. Everything is in motion. The motion simply has the volume turned down to a whisper, and then down further.

Stand at the cliff-rim at full dark — as dark as this place gets, which is a green-black luminous gloom starred with moth-light — and you can just make out, far below in the fog country, other plateaus, other shelves, other soft blue constellations of lamplight. The world is young here and rising, and getting populated from below, one silent village at a time, and the only price of living in it is this: you may never again be loud.
