Stand anywhere on the valley floor and look up, and you will see a sky. It is pale, even-toned, bright enough at midday to throw soft-edged shadows off every trunk and stone — and it is stone. The ceiling of this place hangs perhaps a quarter-mile overhead, furred across its whole underside with a living crust of luminous mats, microbe and lichen grown into one continuous pelt that waxes and wanes on its own long schedule. The people here simply call it the sky. When the mats cloud and thin, the light dims toward dusk. When they thicken, it brightens toward noon. Nothing else marks the hours. Nothing else needs to.

The valley is wide enough to have weather, and far enough across that crossing it takes a strong walker most of a day. From the terraces on the eastern wall you can take in nearly all of it at once: the fen floor spread out below, the young river braiding through it in silver threads, the town grown onto a low rise in the middle distance, and the far wall hung floor-to-ceiling with green. The air between is never empty. It carries mist the way other air carries nothing — a visible medium, cool and slow, and everything you look at is seen slightly through it.

The walls tell the history in horizontal lines. A few centuries ago this whole hollow was a lake, dark and pressurized under the mountain, its waterline furred with glowing mats that had nothing else to live on. Then some throat of rock gave way downslope and the lake ran off in a single season, leaving the mats stranded overhead like a drowned sky, and leaving behind a drained basin of mineral-rich silt. The old shorelines still ring the walls like stains in a bath — you can trace them for kilometers, flat ledges of pale rock now colonized by fern. Everything below those rings is new. The river is young and cutting fast, leaving bluffs of raw scalloped stone and gravel bars still sharp-edged, unworn. There are no stalagmite forests here, no slow sculptures; the water has not yet learned its channels through the rock, so instead of percolating downward it sheets down the walls in seep-lines, hundreds of vertical silver stripes striping the cliffs, each one stitched green.

The water table is at the surface. Everywhere. Dig anywhere with your heel and the hole fills. The floor is a fen, spongy and braided, its streams split so thin and spread so wide over moss that they move without a sound — you can stand ankle-deep in current and hear nothing but your own pulse. And every day, at dusk, the Weep comes. The light dims, the ceiling stone cools, the warm wet air below loses its grip, and the whole sky begins to drip: not a roaring rain but a fine, dense, universal weeping, millions of soft taps landing on moss, which is to say landing on silence. The Weep is a rain you feel on your shoulders before you hear anywhere at all.

The cold is constant, the way cave cold is constant — a chill like a cellar in early spring, never freezing on the floor, never warm. The light gives almost no heat; it is cool light, overcast light. Breath barely fogs. What makes the lushness possible is the steadiness: no winter ever arrives, no dry spell, no dark season. The sky shines every day, the water never leaves, and so the green never stops. Growth here is not fast but it is absolutely unceasing, a slow tide.

And it is packed. Nothing in this valley is empty, and bare rock is a temporary condition. The moss runs knee-deep to shoulder-deep across the whole floor — walking anywhere is a silent wading, your legs disappearing into a green fathoms thick, and every footfall swallowed whole. The first trees grew up in that mat within decades of the drainage: fast, soft-wooded things with shallow sprawling roots, since no root needs to chase water that rises to meet it, their trunks tilted and buttressed and each one so layered with tenants it is nearly hidden — moss on the bark, liverwort on the moss, fern on the liverwort, a curtain of hanging epiphytes binding neighbor to neighbor, glow-lichen seeded through all of it like frost that lights itself faintly blue-green in the dim hours. On the walls, where the seep-lines stripe, the ferns stand so dense they hold their own soil. The air itself is occupied, mist-droplets riding spores, spores riding midges.
