Somewhere in the cold middle of a world-ocean, the water is building a continent.

Not the way reefs build — nothing here lives, nothing secretes — and not the way volcanoes build, though the fresh stone is warm in places, warm like bread. The water builds the way a chord builds: by standing still in the right shape for long enough. It has been at this for a long time. By the evidence of the work, it is perhaps one percent finished.

There is no name for the place yet. Naming is a thing that happens to finished places, and this one has never once been finished. Call it the Raising Ground, which is not a name so much as a status.

You would come to it from below, up the long western flank, out of water so deep it has stopped being a color. The climb takes days. Black softens into green-black, green-black into the gray-green of overcast noon, and then, over the rim of the plateau, the light arrives properly — secondhand, storm-gold, sifted down through a few hundred meters of the clearest water in the world. The plateau is high ground the way a mountaintop is high ground, though every bit of it is drowned: the crown of it sits just under the weather, and around its edges the world falls away again into the abyss it was raised from. It is two thousand kilometers across, and it is shallow, and it is bright, and it is loud.

It is very, very loud. That is the first rule of the place: nothing here is quiet, and nothing here is local. Sound in cold water runs a kilometer and a half each second, so any noise made anywhere on the Raising Ground is, twenty minutes later, everywhere on it. A column of stone settling on the far side of the plateau arrives at your position as a slow mountainous wall of pressure that shoves through your chest like weather. The surface, far overhead, is in permanent tempest — the flood never drained here; it became the sky — and the storm's thunder comes down through the water as enormous patient drumbeats, the ceiling of the world being hammered from the other side. When lightning strikes up there, the whole column below flashes white for half a second, a day compressed to an instant, and the boom follows like a verdict.

The baseline underneath everything is a drone, a note so low it has slid out of hearing and into the body — you feel it in your teeth, in the joints of your jaw, a hum that turns out on inspection to be the water itself, the whole plateau-scale volume of it, vibrating at one agreed frequency. Above that drone the work is layered: the tick and grind of stone precipitating out of solution, kilometers of it; the hiss of fresh rock quenching; long sweeping cries like sonar, like a saw drawn across wet slate, that move through the water in slow searching arcs; and, several times an hour, the ringing. The ringing is the columns. As each one stiffens and takes its final load, it gives off a clear struck-bell tone, and because thousands are stiffening at any given moment, the floor of the Raising Ground is a continuous carillon, an instrument the size of a country being tuned.

Because sound is loud here, sound is also load-bearing. This is the second rule, and it is the strange one, the hinge the whole place turns on. The water is saturated with grist — angular mineral motes, glittering faintly in the storm-light like snow that has opinions. Every liter holds millions of them. Each one is, in the way a gene is, machinery: organized matter doing work, with no seams, no parts, no maker's marks, no version numbers, nothing to see at all — the sophistication being precisely that there is nothing to see. The grist listens to the sound. Where the standing waves in the water stack into focused loud lines, the motes precipitate stone along those lines, and the stone accretes the way frost accretes on glass: flutes, terraces, columns a kilometer tall and rising, bedding planes writing themselves into existence in a rock that has no history, only future. The strata here do not record what happened. They record what is being decided. When the chord changes — and it changes constantly, turbulently, weather-fronts of frequency rolling across the plateau like the storms overhead — the growth fronts migrate with it, kilometers in a day, and coastlines of young stone race across the floor with nothing behind them but open water.

The stone itself is new in a way rock is never new. It is pale, gray-green, the color of unfinished things, and none of it is rounded. That is the third rule, and the oddest to stand inside: nothing on the Raising Ground has eroded, because nothing has been finished long enough. Water rounds; water softens; water forgets edges — but here the flood has been caught mid-task working as a mason instead of a thief, adding and adding and only ever subtracting by revision. There is no sand. The gravel is angular, vehement, mineral with a point of view. There are no boulders worn to lozenges, no polished walls, no delta-silk silt except as pigment. Every surface in this place is on its first draft.

And between the growth fields — this is the fourth thing, the one that gets into visitors, if a visitor could be supposed — there is almost nothing at all. Density here is a rumor. Field after field of the young stone is separated by hundreds of kilometers of open gray water, cold, six degrees, dense as glass, carrying its grist and its drone and nothing else. No fish. None. Nothing darts, nothing grazes, nothing flees your shadow, because there are no shadows worth fleeing from; there is nothing with eyes anywhere in this ocean, and the absence is enormous, a stillness of unobserved work, like standing in a shipyard where every crane is moving and no shift has ever included workers. Visibility in the between-waters is fog-distance: a hundred meters of faint gold haze, then the water simply becomes itself. You can cross a day of this emptiness and hear the next field before you see it — a gray shore of ringing stone announcing itself from beyond every horizon the light can draw, which is how you learn that here, sound is the true horizon. The world extends exactly as far as it is loud.

It rains, sometimes, underwater. Where the young stone exhales heat and mineral, dense cold brine comes out of solution in sheets and falls — actual rain within the ocean, silver ropes of heavier water descending through lighter, drizzling onto the fresh terraces and feeding the grist, and the drops land with a sound like static, like distant applause. The drizzle belts drift like the fronts of storms. There is weather inside the weather here.

And there is, so far, exactly one form of life, and it lives in the gaps of the work. In the acoustic shadows — the pockets where the standing waves cancel and the great chord falls momentarily, locally silent — gray-gold filament lawns take hold on the fresh stone, thin sparse mats, first-growth, chemosynthetic, drawing on the rock's youth. They cannot grow where it is loud; the work overwrites them. So life, on the Raising Ground, begins as errata — it exists only where the blueprint forgot to press down.
