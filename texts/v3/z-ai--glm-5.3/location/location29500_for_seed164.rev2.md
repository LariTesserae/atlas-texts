# The Set

The cog line climbs the face of the Set for six hours, and for the last four of them it is raining — except that nobody here calls it rain, because it never started. Fog condenses on the cold basalt of the escarpment, the water beads out of the air itself, and every face of the rock runs silver. The rocks weep. The word is exact, and the locals use it without poetry: the stone does not leak, it weeps, cold-faced, all of it, always.

At the top of the climb, travelers stop asking when the weather will clear, because they slowly understand it hasn't begun. It has been just-after-rain on the Set since the beginning. The whole plateau smells like the first minute after a storm, and has for two hundred years.

## New Stone

The Set rose in a single act — a season, a year, a night; the surveys disagree because the surveys weren't there. Some founding families claim their great-grandparents watched the horizon change. What is certain: a thousand kilometres by seven hundred of fresh dark basalt now stands two kilometres above the lowlands, and it is young. Not young the way a sapling is young — young the way poured metal is young, the way a bone in a cast is young. The scree slopes have never settled. Every edge cuts. The rivers are ankle-deep and undecided, braiding over sheets of stone, wandering experimental meanders they will hold forever, because they have not had time to learn where to go and never will.

Here is the strange geology of the place: the weeping water carries minerals that re-cement the rock as fast as the water wears it. Waterfall lips extend stone skirts; cliff faces grow a hard mineral glaze to a certain thickness and then stop, as if satisfied. Erosion and lithification have fought to a permanent draw. The plateau will never become a valley, a plain, a sea cliff. It was born final. Quarried stone is soft as chalk and hardens over decades — the town's oldest walls are its hardest, aging in reverse. Masons speak of stone the way farmers speak of fruit.

At the rim, the fog deck pours over the lip in a fixed silver apron, a waterfall of cloud that falls day and night and has never once varied, so that after an hour you stop seeing it as motion. It is a landscape feature. It is the idea of falling, held still.

## The Regime

Four degrees. It is four degrees everywhere on the Set, all day, all year, and this is not luck but physics: condensation is a thermostat. The fog surrenders its heat as it surrenders its water; the air can neither warm past the dew point nor cool below it without breaking the loop that holds it. The Set found its temperature the day it rose and has kept it since, the way a body does.

Dig anywhere and you strike water within a hand's depth. You cannot dig a grave here; every hole becomes a well.

The water is the same water. It beads on the stone, feeds the moss, runs to the rim, falls, rises again as fog, and comes back. Chemists have proven the isotopes match — this is original water, going round. And the plateau meters itself: the town doubled its harvesting capacity in the founding decade and the daily yield did not move a liter. The Set pays what it pays. Engineers call it the carrying capacity of the air. Everyone else calls it the Rate.

The falling water pays for the harvest. The great penstock — the Downcomer — drops the surplus down the escarpment, and its turbines light the trams, the fans, the lamps. The plateau runs on its own product, a machine that pays its own wages.

## The Green Country

And yet — lush. Impossibly, coldly lush. Fog meadows of moss run to the white horizon in chartreuse, bottle-green, rust: knee-deep, spongy, saturated to a fixed point so that every footfall squeezes a cup of water out and the footprint refills behind you, and in an hour it is as if you were never there. The meadow erases everyone. It has to; it is at rate.

Whetgrass grows in the wind seams, and the wind files its tips as fast as they grow — every field lies level as a bowling green without ever once being mown. Clubmoss hedges lean identically, growth balanced against abrasion, whole kilometers of vegetation bent into one fixed gesture.

Nothing blooms on the Set. No flowers, no fruit, no scent of blossom — colour here is not *for* anyone, because nothing ever needed to attract anything. The only flare is a lichen that burns orange in fixed patches, mapped and named like towns, and the map has not been redrawn since it was drawn.

And the flora is defenseless. Thorns are for being eaten; bark is for being bored into; toxins are for being tasted. Nothing here has ever been any of those things, and walking through it feels faintly indecent, like touching something that never learned to be touched.

## A World Without Animals

No birds — the fog gives them nothing to navigate by, and there is nothing to nest on, scavenge, or sing for. No insects: this is the world's largest bog with no mosquitoes, and visitors wait for them out of habit, and the habit takes years to break. No rats in the granaries; the granaries are guarded from damp, and from nothing else.

Nothing rots. There is nothing here to rot *with* — the decomposers never arrived, so the meadow drinks its own dead, dissolving yesterday's growth in the constant dew and taking it back through the roots. The botanical survey weighs the same forty plots every year. The figures are identical to the gram. They have recalibrated the instruments eleven times.

The founding families learned early that burial was a mistake: the ground preserves rather than takes. The old graveyard — they call it the Preserve — is a lawn where nothing has happened to anyone in two centuries. People visit great-grandmothers and find them unchanged. Most families find this worse than grief and better than losing.

The town's only livestock is a jar of sourdough starter, kept in the bakery like a relic, because it is one: the only domesticated life on the plateau. The brewery failed in the founding year. Bread is the town's single fermentation, and Old Rise is fed by rota and guarded like a saint.

Stand at the edge of a fog meadow at dusk and do the arithmetic: you are the only animal in ten thousand square kilometres. The feeling arrives uninvited, and it feels like being a specimen.

## Motherwell

Motherwell is two towns' worth of people and one town's worth of noise, terraced on the rim where the Downcomer goes over. Its architecture is half plumbing — every sill has its drip lip, every wall its runnel, the downstairs floors are deliberately *the wet floor*, where the walls are allowed to stream, and the dry rooms live upstairs behind stoves. Laundry is municipal; the Condensery's waste heat dries the whole town's shirts. The copper on the roofs has the same fixed patina it has had for a hundred years. A flag hangs at the terminus at a permanent lean; the flag *is* the wind gauge.

The gauges are the altars. Needle asleep at their stops, forever: pressure, flow, temperature, all of them pinned like the barometers, because the Set has one isobar and it is drawn around itself. The forecast is a brass plaque screwed to the wall of the Lift terminus in the founding year, and it has never once been wrong: FOG. 4°. WIND SE'LY, CONSTANT. VISIBILITY 300 METRES.

Children fly kites that stand stock-still in the sky, hung at a fixed angle like ornaments. Visitors find these kites unbearable and cannot say why.

## The Roar and the Hushes

The Set is loud the way the sea is loud — but the sea has moods, and this has none. The rim gale holds a fixed chord, the fan galleries drone in governed synchrony, the trunk mains throb a note you feel in the sternum, and the drizzle sizzles eternally on ten thousand mesh panels. A roar with no gusts in it. Sound that carries no information becomes landscape, and the people have stopped hearing it — while visitors lie awake all night in what feels like standing inside a waterfall that is standing still.

Where the sources interfere, the roar cancels: there are fixed cones of absolute stillness on the plateau, marked with white hush-poles. Shift workers eat lunch inside them. Children learn their boundaries the way children elsewhere learn the edges of lawns, and there is a fine for spoiling the hush, and every child pays it once, for the experience of being forgiven.

The town tunes its machines the way an orchestra tunes between movements — not to be quiet, to be *consonant*. Noise is permitted. Novelty is not. A squealing bearing is a civic emergency not for the volume but for the variation.

And at noon the tower rings the Change Bell, once. Its ringing is the only sound on the plateau allowed to decay — people gather in the square to hear change happen, legally, once a day. Change is rationed here at the rate of one bell.

## Out Along the Line

Beyond Motherwell: emptiness at continental scale. The Harp Line — eleven hundred kilometres of wire mesh that sings faintly and sweats its fog into gutters — runs away to the vanishing point, and the gutters run to the Downcomer, and between here and the far rim there are two towns, forty-one gauge stations, and one train.

Station Forty stands alone in fog meadow, days from anywhere, one warden, one task: confirm that nothing has changed. Form 1 has a single checkbox and a line for a signature, and the warden files the same form daily, ten thousand times. The Verifiers require the signature witnessed fresh each day — because handwriting is the only thing on the Set that ages, and the archive at Motherwell is full of it: shelves of identical forms signed by drifting hands, the slow failing of ten thousand fingers. It is the most honest document on the plateau. Change, filed.

The lowlands are dry because the Set is wet. The plateau took the sky from a continent and sells it back by the liter, at the Rate fixed by the Water Charter, and three downstream cities are sized to that rate exactly — their populations, their fountains, their treaties. In the vault at Motherwell there is a sealed envelope marked **Off Rate Procedure**. No one alive has opened it. Everyone knows what it would mean. It would mean the future.

## The Laws of the Set

Everything flows; nothing varies. The water is the same water. The census of species is closed — what the wind sowed in the first year is the flora forever. Nothing decays; matter here is on eternal loan. The stone is born and immediately final. And the only gradient on the whole plateau, the only permitted one-way process, is people — who age, arrive, leave, and are erased by the meadow behind them. Entropy is rationed here, and humanity got the ration.

You leave by the Lift, and the roar goes with you. In the lowlands the silence wakes you at night, and you find yourself waiting for noon, for the one change in the day. And the water you drink there is already on its way home: it will rise at the rim, join the apron of fog, bead on the cold stone, run down the wires, and go down the Downcomer again, learning nothing, saying the same thing forever.

It has been just-after-rain here for two hundred years. It will never have finished raining.
