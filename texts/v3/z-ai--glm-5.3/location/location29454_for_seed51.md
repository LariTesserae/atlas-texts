# The Basin

You hear it before you see it. Coming down off the low rim through ferns that close behind you like water behind a hull, the loudness arrives first — a broad, dense wash of falling and seeping that sounds like rain remembered by a valley. Then the light changes: the sky, already gray, becomes a ceiling, low and softly lit from within itself, and you understand you are inside something. The basin holds fog the way a cupped hand holds water. The air is cool and wet against the bone, the green is black-green and thick to the horizon's blur, and the ground gives underfoot like a living mattress. There is nothing made here. No post, no wall, no path. That is not the same as no one having made anything.

## The light

It is dim the way an aquarium is dim: light everywhere, from nowhere, arriving without direction. The cloud deck hangs perhaps two hundred meters up and glows with an even gray amplitude that strengthens and softens through the day — except that "day" is the wrong word, because the brightness doesn't rise and set. It circles. The whole ceiling revolves slowly around the basin's center, one revolution a day, a drain in no hurry about draining, and the brightest sector drags a pale noon behind it as it goes. When it passes you stand in a light like late afternoon under a heavy roof, and then it moves on toward the far rim and the valley dims again.

The dimness has grain. Shadows pool wrong — lying thick in open moss, absent under ferns — and some of them have come loose entirely, drifting slowly at knee height until the fog takes them. In the deepest hollows the season is older: a fern there has been mid-unfurl for years, and nobody, including the fern, is concerned about it.

## The water

The rain here is not over. It is being administered. The stone faces along the terraces — rounded, pored, soft-cornered — hold the last thousand years of weather inside them and release it on a schedule nobody published. Water leaves them in threads, in pulses, in slow beads that grow to falling size and then hang, held mid-air under the weep like punctuation a sentence hasn't reached yet. Some drops land the instant they form. Others wait. Watch one long enough and it may hang for an hour before the basin allows it to fall, at which point it lands without ceremony, as if it had only just left the stone.

The water table is at the surface pretending not to be. Everywhere the moss gives up pretending you get a pool: shallow, black, warm-skinned in places where the ground beneath is faintly warm, cold everywhere else. The pools are not water, exactly. They are inventory. Anything that goes under — a dropped fern-frond, a shaken seed, anything — dissolves into them in days and is returned, eventually, on the moss somewhere else, whole. Always whole, which is worse. Sometimes the mud returns a shape nobody dropped, printed soft in moss-stuff — a stair, once, or what a stair would be if it had only ever been imagined — and the shape keeps for a morning and then rejoins the solution.

Your footprints do not stay empty. They fill, after some minutes, with warm water, as if the ground had noticed you and wept politely at your weight. An hour later they are still printed; by evening the moss has lifted them out like sleep taking a crease from a face.

At dusk the whole valley breathes out — fog rising off the seep-faces in slow standing columns — and by morning it has drunk most of it back. The cycle is reliable enough to set a clock by, if you could find a clock that survived here, which you could not.

## The loudness

The sound is enormous and it is made entirely of smallness: ten million drips, seeps, threadfalls, and beaded weeps aggregating into a roar like sustained applause, like rain on a lake, like a stadium of nothing at all. It is loud and it is ordered. Water falls on certain stones that ring — flat, tuned, bronze-voiced despite being stone — in patterns that are almost music and almost language, a continuous readout of the valley's condition, broadcast to no one, for the ten-thousandth year running.

The basin never echoes. Call out and the sound is filed; the loudness stays close-grained and sourceless, and nothing anywhere answers with a copy. You cannot locate anything by ear here.

Under the water-noise there is a hum, felt more than heard, a bass tone that sits in the sternum like a hand. It has a pulse: a slow beat roughly forty seconds long, the idle of something enormous keeping time beneath the marsh. In the warm-skinned patches of moss the hum is louder in the ground than the air. Press your palm flat to the moss there and somewhere beneath, the hum changes key. The moss is listening back. It is taking your temperature at a scale of detail you would rather not think about.

And once or twice a day comes the comb: a long, enormous sound like a comb drawn through wet hair the size of a valley, and everything — every drip, every thread, every held drop — goes silent at once. Then the noise rebuilds like surf, and the valley resumes.

## The green

Because the light is thin, the growth went low and dense and patient: moss in continuous fields hip-deep, deep as a mattress, springing underfoot; liverwort crusted over every stone in dark green wax; ferns head-high along the seeps; bracket fungi shelving out of the wet wood-pockets in pale stacks, some of them faintly lit, gills glowing like paper lanterns in the general gray. Lichen hangs in curtains wherever something stands still long enough. It is lush the way cold places are lush — all the biomass below knee height, all of it touching, a saturation of presences per cubic meter: mud, moss, fern, fog, spore, hanging drop, stacked in layers with no air to spare between them.

Everything grows toward the hum. You can map the underside by the green: where the ground is thinnest and the idling warmth nearest, the moss grows tallest and the fungal lanterns crowd thickest. At dimming, the fungi let go their spores and the fog that is always here acquires weather — a slow snow of pale motes drifting sideways, all in the same direction, all down the grain.

## The tenders

Fauna is scarce and strange here, because most of the niches an ecology would fill are staffed instead. You may see the tenders three times in a day and should count that as many: eyeless, pale, the length of a hand and a half, the color of the inside of a shell, moving over the moss with total certainty. They groom the fern-crowns. They press their heads to the ringing stones and hold still, listening to the readout. They attend the hanging drops, and sometimes take one, and no one knows where the drops go. They are either the last animals in the valley or its first tools, and the valley has never been asked to distinguish.

At dusk, something calls across the water: two notes, a falling interval, thin and far. Something on the far side answers with the same two notes, lower. Caller and answerer are never seen, never located, and never once fail each other. It may be the valley talking to itself across its own length, checking its echo — and finding none, answering for itself.

## The underside

Somewhere below, something was built. Down here, "built" is a word that has eroded off. Whatever the basin was for has weathered the way lettering weathers off stone — the purpose is gone, the gesture of it remains, and the gesture is maintenance. The system has been at work so long that the work has become geology. Every surface of it has been argued at by water for ten thousand years, and the water has won every argument gently. There are no structures because the structures retired into being terrain: the sensors became moss, a skin of instruments gone green, gone feral, still sensing; the channels became seep-faces; the readout became rain on tuned stones.

Below the resolution of eyesight, below the resolution of suspicion, every cell of that moss is busier than a plant has any reason to be. And above the ceiling, once, the overcast thinned for the length of a held breath, and something vast kept its orbit up there in the gray, and the light dimmed and it was gone, and the valley made no remark about it at all.

## Rules of the basin

- **Water always falls eventually.** "Eventually" ranges from instantly to three days. The basin meters the fall. The rain is being administered, not denied.
- **Gravity has a grain.** Drop a pebble and it does not fall; it settles, a little sideways, along the grain — the way a tired man leans. All across the valley, whatever comes to rest comes to rest pointing the same way, like iron filings.
- **Distances are negotiable, but the valley honors the second attempt.** Pace out a gap and it disagrees with you. Pace it again and it apologizes, and agrees exactly.
- **Nothing is lost.** What the pools take, the moss returns, whole, elsewhere. The black water is an archive in solution, and it is full.
- **Straight lines do not survive.** A fallen branch that lies straight will be found curved by morning. The comb comes for geometry that conflicts with the settled shape of things, and after the comb, briefly, the world is flat and quiet everywhere at once, like a page just wiped.
- **Nothing here has an edge left.** Even the impossible is worn smooth. The broken physics has been rounded by its own long residence, and it behaves now the way an old river behaves: within its banks.

## Dusk

The bright sector makes its slow lap and goes behind the far rim, and the valley dims to its standing gray. The fog stands up out of the seeps. The spores go sideways down the grain. The two notes go out over the water and are answered, faithful, from the far side. Somewhere near the center of everything the hum beats once, forty seconds, beats again, the long idle of a caretaker that has outlived its instructions and kept the only habit it had left.
