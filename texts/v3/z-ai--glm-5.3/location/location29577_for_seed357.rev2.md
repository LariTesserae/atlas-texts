# The Weeping Terrace

It is high. You feel it in the way the clouds come *down* to meet you — not overhead but at shoulder height, moving through the landscape like slow animals made of steam. The Terrace sits three kilometers above whatever lowlands exist below it, and nothing here has learned to be old yet.

## The Bones of the Place

Everything is built. That is the first thing you understand, and it takes a while to understand what it means when there is no builder. The ground underfoot is root — not covered in root, not threaded with root, but *made of* root, a woven living pavement the color of wet rope and old ivory, dense as any stonemason's work and warm from the sun. You walk on it and it gives slightly, like a floorboard that breathes. Rainfall — and it rains here often, in brief hot deluges that leave everything steaming — drains through it instantly, into the architecture, and returns an hour later as sweat from the faces of the terrace walls.

Because the whole plateau is terraced. Someone, something, the roots themselves — the walls are gabions of living wood, stacked courses of woven stems that hold back slopes of moss-substrate and spore-rich earth. The terraces are young; the edges are still sharp, still crisp in the way that mountains only are for their first few million years. Sharp-edged and yet soft-bodied: the paradox of the whole place. A cliff you could cut yourself on, that heals.

The heat is wet heat. Jungle heat, but at altitude, which makes it strange — a steam-bath thinness where breathing feels like drinking. Water is near the surface everywhere: press your palm to the woven root-pavement and moisture beads between your fingers. The rocks weep. Every exposed stone surface — and there are a few, dark volcanic fingers poking through the weaving like knuckles — runs with a continuous film of condensate, and from each one hangs a fringe of liverwort and long silver-green strands that drip, audibly, constantly.

## The Sound

Which brings us to the sound, because this place is loud, though there are no animals, and that is the eeriest part of it. All this sound is plant sound, water sound, growth sound:

The drip-chorus from the weeping rocks, thousands of them, at slightly different pitches, a tuned rain. The groan of the terraces growing — and they *do* groan, a low creaking yawn as woven stems thicken and slide against each other, a sound like ship's rigging in weather. And periodically, the deep *whumpf* of a bloom-event, when some vast structure across the valley opens all at once, and the air pressure of it reaches you a half-second before the smell does.

There are no birdsong-shaped holes in the soundscape, because there were never birds to miss. The plants own the entire acoustic register and use all of it.

## The Turbulence

This is not a settled place. It is being *made* right now, continuously, and unmade, and remade differently.

Watch a distance — the far edge of the plateau, where it drops into cloud — and you'll see spire-gardens rising. Fast-growing stalks, thick as temple columns, shooting up in visible time, weaving themselves into hollow towers, budding, flowering, collapsing into mulch that becomes the footing for the next generation. The skyline changes daily. Nothing here holds a shape for long; the architecture is a verb. Whole colonnades of living pillar-root slump into wet yellow compost in an afternoon, and by evening something else — a canopy, a rope-bridge of tension-vines, a shallow bowl of crimson moss big enough to be an amphitheater — stands where it was.

It is Solaris-like, yes, but not intelligent, or not intelligently. It is creation and destruction with anthill energy, hyperactive, indifferent. A bazaar of forms: everything being traded, nothing kept.

## The Emptiness Within It

And yet — sparse. This is what unsettles most. For all the riot of growth, you can walk for an hour across open woven pavement with nothing taller than ankle-moss around you, under a sky of ordinary light — a big midday glare softened by cloud-rag, shadows that behave themselves. The great structures cluster and then abandon vast clear stretches. Standing in one of those clearings, high, hot, damp, with a dripping skyline far off in three directions and the fourth dropping into white nothing, you feel the emptiness as a presence: the plateau breathes *in*, gathers itself, and the cleared space is the held breath before the next structure erupts.

No animals at all. No insects, no birds, nothing with eyes. Pollination happens by wind, by water-drip, by the mechanical shudder of blooms throwing spores in artillery-arcs across the empty middles. You are, if you are here, the only thing that has ever watched.

## The Rules of This World

There are a few, and you learn them by watching:

**Everything built is grown.** There is no tool, no cut, no kiln. Notches fit notches because they grew into each other. Nothing here knows the concept of an edge that isn't a cell.

**Water travels in circuits you can hear.** Rain falls into the weave, descends, is lifted by capillary towers of living fiber, sweats out of high walls, drips, pools, is drunk, is rained again. The whole plateau is a fountain with no pump but life.

**Nothing persists, so nothing decays.** The violence of the change is total but clean — every collapse is instantly food, every food instantly form. There is no ruin here anywhere. No dead wood, no bones, no rust. Just an eternal, deafening, boiling-hot *present tense*, three kilometers up, in the cloud, being rebuilt as fast as it can be unmade.

Sit down on the warm woven ground near a weeping rock. Within ten minutes, the moss begins considering your boots.
