The upland sits so high that it has stopped receiving the world's weather and started manufacturing its own. Fog comes up out of the ground here rather than down out of the sky — steam, mostly, off rock that is still warm from being made — and it hangs at the level of the plateau's shoulders like a lid the place has built for itself. The light under that lid is dim even at noon, a long-dusk gray with no visible source, the kind of light that makes distances uncertain. The cold comes through clothing at the seams. The air is thin enough that a hard walk leaves you breathing like a fish, and thin air carries sound grudgingly, which is one reason the place is so quiet. The other reasons are more interesting.

The rock is new. That is the fact everything else hangs from. The massif emerged a few hundred years ago at most, and it is still emerging — adding itself in finger-widths per year, outpacing the weather that would otherwise be wearing it down. Erosion has not failed to arrive here; it has simply been given more work than it can do. So nothing is rounded. Lava lies in fields of black rope and fields of black rubble, neither one weathered, both sharp enough to cut a boot. Ash plains fill the lows, sifted and unsifted, glassy underfoot. There is no soil, because soil takes time, and there is almost no lichen, because lichen takes even more. The streams have not learned their courses yet — drainage is provisional, braids wandering the flats looking for valleys that don't exist, water unassigned. At night the newest flows snap and pistol-crack as they contract in the cold, the sound of a job site after the crews have quit, except no crew ever came.

Everything weeps. This is what happens when warm rock meets cold air: every cliff face runs wet, every overhang drips onto gravel that never dries, hoarfrost combs the grass in the low spots while the heated ground steams bare ten paces away. Rain almost never falls, but it is always almost raining — the fog condenses on any surface cool enough to accept it. Step off the sinter and the ash squelches; a bootprint fills. The water table is nearly the surface, and better than near it: the water is moving. Melt and seepage sink until they meet the heat, then rise again through cracks, mineral-charged, to be re-issued as springs. The ground is plumbed. It is a circulatory system with magma for a heart.

And this is where the place starts to resemble something it isn't. Along the fracture lines the fumaroles stand in rows like stacks, hissing day and night. Mudpots churn in their pits like vats, thick, gray, smelling of eggs and struck matches. One geyser keeps a schedule: a long quiet interval while the chamber refills, then discharge, then quiet again — a compressor cycling, a valve operating. The works never close. They produce steam, sulfur, silica, and new ground, around the clock, and they are wholly unbuilt: no catwalk, no ladder, no plate stamped with a maker's name, no door, no light burning over a door, no door to burn it over. Not a fence, not a nail, not a human footprint anywhere on it. Even the electricity is homegrown — the plumes carry their own charge, fine ash rubbing fine ash, so that near an active vent your hair lifts and your scalp prickles, and on the darkest nights a soft crackle of lightning happens inside the steam where almost no one could see it.

Mostly, though: quiet. The hiss is local — walk two hundred paces from a vent and it's gone, and what remains is drips, and the far-off mutter of cooling stone, and nothing. The muffle of ash and snow eats hoof-clicks. Geese cross the overcast in skeins you hear only when they're nearly overhead — a soft horn-note thinning with altitude. When the wind drops entirely you can hear the whole works idling together, a low combined breath under everything, steady as a held note, and then a puff of breeze and even that is gone.

The palette is narrow and industrial by nature: charcoal rock, bone-white sinter terraces building themselves millimeter by millimeter in the outflows (deposition is the only construction here, and water is the only builder), sulfur-yellow vent aprons, rust-brown sedge in autumn, and along the hot channels a strange bright inventory of orange, red, and green — the thermophilic mats, bacteria living at temperatures that would scald a hand, the first permanent residents. They are the base of everything. After them, mosses at the warm margins. After the mosses, sedge and cotton grass in the seepage flats where hot runoff pools against cold ground — the only soft country, spongy thermal marshes the size of playing fields. Scattered grass tufts hide in sheltered ash hollows. On the oldest flow, a haze of first lichen has started, barely, like a rumor arriving of forests several centuries out. No trees. Nothing woody anywhere. Step thirty paces from warm ground and life ends as abruptly as a shoreline.

And yet the place is thick with animals, and the reason is geography. This saddle is the lowest crossing for a hundred miles, so the great migration comes through it — has always come through it, a route older than the rock it now crosses, the herds simply walking over ground their grandmothers never stood on. The caribou come in single file, strings of hundreds, and they follow the heat. Warm ground is bare ground; bare ground is walkable ground when everything else is snow or thigh-deep postholing. So their trail runs along the fracture lines like a service corridor, a path braided into the ash by generations of hooves and by nothing else — the only line on the land not drawn by water is drawn by feet. In the mats, small flies breed in numbers that beggar sense, larvae living in water too hot for anything else; when disturbed they rise off the warm channels in visible clouds. Snow buntings and pipits work the vents for them. Raven pairs hold territories along the steam. A gyrfalcon stitches the margins of the herd. Foxes patrol the warm line all winter, because along it the snow never settles and the lemmings and voles stay active year-round in pockets of heated ground — whole small mammals living through an arctic winter on thermal subsidy, awake in January in a hole where the floor is warm. From a height, if the fog permitted a height, the abundance would resolve into its true shape: a blank page, with writing only along the seams. Lines, knots, threads. Between them, void, kilometers of it.

The rules of the ground, if you wanted them written down: heat is the currency, and every living thing here pays in it or starves. Footing is provisional — the crust over vents and hollow lava is a promise the rock does not always keep, and you trust color over texture: clean white sinter holds, thin steaming ground does not. Weather is manufactured locally and obeys the vents' schedule more than the sky's. Change runs at ecological speed — a spring can die and another open within a season; the terraces grow while you eat lunch; geology here happens fast enough to notice. And nothing is named. Nothing is built. The only structures are the ones the water is raising, and the only paths are the ones the herds have paid for in hoofbeats.

The works run all night. The geyser keeps its interval in the dark. The fog rebuilds itself as fast as it drifts apart. The herds move through the black from one warm pocket to the next, steaming faintly, breath hanging in the dim like a second, lower cloud. Underneath, the mountain adds its finger-width; on the oldest flow, the first lichen extends itself by something less than that. Production with no consumer. Goods delivered to no one — ground made ahead of any need for ground. Somewhere out in the fog a valve opens on schedule, and no one hears it, and the route keeps moving over the rock, older than the rock, doing what it has always done, now crossing a place that is still being made.
