You hear the hall long before you reach it. The stair down is cut into the root-bole of a fig so large that on the surface it passes for a hill with a tree on it, and the rock around the stair is dry as biscuit, warm as a hearthstone, scaled with rust where iron staples once held a handrail. Somewhere around the fortieth step the sound arrives — not all at once, but in layers, the way surf arrives. First a thud, slow and enormous, once every four seconds, felt in the arches of your feet before you hear it properly. Then, under the thud, a roar: continuous, soft-edged, dense. A river held indoors. A stadium heard from outside its walls. By the time the shaft ends at an iron door gone orange with oxide, the roar has stopped being a sound and become a medium, something you are about to walk into.

Inside, the dark is not dark. It is lit, the way a banked fire is lit — a low amber everywhere, sourceless, warm-toned, moving. The light is animals. The hall swarms with moths — tallowmoths, the ledger would have called them, bred here by the billion for the fat lantern-grease rendered out of their bodies — and every one of them glows, faintly, ember-faint, a candle seen through horn. Individually they are almost nothing. Together they are weather. The far wall of the hall is a conjecture; twenty paces out, shapes drown into dusk, and the vault above dissolves into braidwork of roots hung with silk. What light exists belongs to the swarm, and the swarm moves, so the light has weather: bright fronts pass across the machinery like cloud-shadows in reverse, dim fogs gather in the corners, and the whole hall pulses, gently, on a four-second cycle. On every engine stroke the moths brighten. Between strokes they bank themselves down.

The terrain underfoot is not stone. Stone is down there somewhere, but the floor of the hall is a lawn — a pale, blanched, ankle-high sward of fine white rootlets, the fig's own feeder-roots, cultivated here generations ago as fodder and never decultivated since. It is springy to walk on, warm from below like a stable floor, sweet with sap, cropping down to stubble in long mowing-lines where the grubs graze and greening again behind them at a pace you can almost watch. The walls are living wood, ribs of root the size of church buttresses, furred with silver liverwort. The ceiling is rhizome lattice, and between its braids hang decades of silk: cocoon after cocoon, layer over layer, grey-gold and sagging, insulating the warm pipes like a rookery. You are standing in a factory inside a tree inside a hill, and the tree, being the size of a hill, has not noticed.

The Works are laid out like a town, though the town is one hall. There is Vat Row — a rank of six copper render-vats, wide as baths, now upholstered in moss and filled with sleeping cocoons. There is a gantry crane gone to oxide, its wheels locked mid-turn, its ladder colonized by a vine that has no memory of vines. There is the Packing Room, a niche where boxed tallow once went up by winch, its walls now curtained in silk so thick the doorway has narrowed to a shoulder's width. And at the south end stands the boiler, and on the boiler's riveted chest a nameplate reads DILIGENCE, and the Diligence is the last citizen still at work. It fires every four seconds. The thud you feel is its heart. Its flue runs the length of the hall beneath the sward like a buried summer, and the sward grows fat on that heat, and drinks from a brass sap-tap weeping into its bed, and is watered — this is the part that takes a while to believe — by the swarm's own breath. The air of the hall is thick with exhaled moisture. It rises, meets the cold return-main, beads, and rains down over the fodder-beds on a schedule. Thirsty, you drink at the machine, from the basin where the condensed breath pools, along with a colony of blind white shrimp that have never known another water.

The spacing between all this is generous. That is the strange mercy of the place: for all its industry, the hall is mostly air and lawn. The presences — vats, gantry, boiler, the one brick arch that once led to the rest of the district before the rest of the district fell in — stand well apart, town-like, with open pale meadow running between them. The volume in between belongs entirely to the moths. Pound for pound they outweigh the machinery. The air has texture; you feel wingbeats against your cheek like warm sleet. When you walk, the roar doesn't stop — it opens around you, closes behind, the acoustics of a crowd parting.

The loop that runs the place is simple, closed, and nobody designed it. Heat from the Diligence grows the sward. The sward feeds the grubs, which graze in regiments — a sound like fat quietly sizzling, like a thousand pages turned at arm's length, all night and all day, there being no other kind here. The grubs spin, pupate on the warm pipes of Nursery Lane where the industry once ran incubator rooms, hatch, burn bright for a season, and die. And the dead fall, and they accumulate on the grating above the boiler's hopper, and the old screw-conveyor — installed to carry husk and chaff — draws them down. No one converted the boiler to this fuel. The grubs taught it by dying. The fire has not gone out in longer than memory, because there has never once been a shortage of the dead. The hall runs for nothing now, produces nothing, ships nothing up the shaft. It runs because its parts agree. Everything here finished changing a long time ago and began continuing.

Small laws hold the place together, and they are strict:

The engine keeps the time. The hall knows no sun; its day is some twelve thousand strokes, its seasons measured in hatch-surges, when the cocoons open by the million and the roar doubles until a shout at arm's length is inaudible.

All light is animal, and therefore all light is provisional. Shadows do not lie still here; they migrate with the swarm, and the far end of any view is a rumor.

The water is breath. It falls where the pipes are cold and the moths are thick, and nowhere else. The deeper galleries past the sealed arch are dry as paper — a draught out of that dark will make your lips crack in minutes.

Nothing checks the moths. There are mites on them like dust; there are crickets chinking in the brickwork; there are two enormous albino geckos, blind and pampered, who live beside the boiler door and eat moths by the dozen, and their eating is a rounding error, a ritual, a tax too small to notice. The only real limit on the swarm is the growth rate of the lawn. Scarcity, here, has exactly one face.

Silence is the alarm. If the roar thins, something has come through the arch. The moths do not pass the bricked doorway, and neither, if you are wise, do you.

The humans left traces, spare and legible. A foreman's office with a liverwort lawn where the desk was. A coat on a hook with a forget-me-not gone woody in the breast pocket. A chalk tally of hatch-days on the brick by the ledger shelf, ending mid-column, in the middle of a word that might be *Friday*. On the walls, rows of iron lamp-brackets with their sockets empty of wicks and tallow — and in each socket a roost of moths, packed and glowing. The lighting system stocks itself now.

Stay long enough and you will notice the hall shrinking. It is being upholstered. Every hatch adds its layer of silk to the walls, the ceiling, the vats; every year the chamber loses a hand's width to its own products, and the doorways narrow, and the gaps between the machines fill in. Given centuries — and there is nothing here to prevent centuries — the hall will close like a wound around its machinery, a solid mass of silk and chitin and boiler-brick and root, all of it warm. The whole chamber is a cocoon in the making, and what it is making is itself: an industry, a tree, and an animal population, sealed together into one chrysalis underground, running on its own breath and its own dead, loud as surf, dim as a banked fire, and lit — every dim, warm, moving, beating inch of it — by animals.
