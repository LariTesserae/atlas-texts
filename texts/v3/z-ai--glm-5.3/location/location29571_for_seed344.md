**Blowout Hollow — the K-4 vent**

You hear it long before you can see it, and that is the point of the place: it hides. The upland around it gives no warning — gravel benches, pale dirt, shadscale and greasewood standing a body-width apart as if each had asked the others for distance, heat coming up off the ground in a shimmer that makes the far ridges swim. Sparse country, dry country, quiet country. And under all of it, from five kilometers out, a rumor. A steady roar with no river behind it. From two kilometers, a waterfall that isn't there. From one, a jet that never passes overhead and never fades. There is no landmark to aim for. You navigate by ear, downhill, into a fold in the plateau, and the fold deepens into a shallow ravine, and the sound rises around you like water coming up a well shaft, and then you are at the lip.

Three summers ago this draw was another groove of scrub. Then the casing on well K-4 — drilled to 1,740 meters, capped, walked away from — cracked along its cement, and the earth found the easier way up. The blowout ate a crater into the ravine floor, boiled the shrubs where they stood, and opened a pocket of wet, roaring, mineral-white country inside the dry one, a hundred meters of it, walled in and invisible until you're inside it.

The threshold is a wall of warmth and then a wall of steam. The dead shrubs mark the boundary like survey stakes — killed in place, every leaf dropped, the wood bleached bone-grey, standing in the fog of the plume. And here is the first quiet strangeness: the flies stop at the lip. A wet place like this, in this heat, should hum with midges; there are none. No birds cross above the plume. No lizard skitters. Within the ring of white rock, nothing lives, and the loudness — which you would expect to be full of voices, since it sounds like a crowd — turns out to be made entirely of steam, stone, and steel. It is the loudest place for miles, and nothing in it has a throat.

The crater itself is twenty paces across, bitten through fresh rock whose break-faces are still sharp-edged and dark, unweathered, young. From its middle rises the stump of the casing — a meter of scabbed steel pipe with its bolt-holes weeping rust — and out of the pipe comes the steam: a white column thirty meters tall, bending, tearing apart, rebuilding itself. The sound comes in layers, and once you start hearing the layers you cannot stop. The broadband roar of the jet itself, a hiss the size of weather. Underneath it, a drone — the half-kilometer of buried pipe acting as the world's largest flue, resonating somewhere below the bottom of a piano, a note you feel in the sternum and the fillings more than hear. Around those two constants, the percussion: mud pots down-slope going *bloop* on their slow bells; condensate drumming on sheet metal; and every so often a rifle-crack as a slab of the young rock, cooled and heated and cooled again, spalls off in a shard that skips down the terrace like thrown slate.

At the crater's rim you cannot converse. You could put your mouth against someone's ear and they would catch a vowel. After ten minutes your ears surrender and the roar becomes a room you're standing in; you stop flinching; you begin to hear the room's furniture. That is when the place lets you look around.

It rains here. That is the pocket's second strangeness, and it is perfectly real: the steam column climbs, meets cooler air above the hollow, condenses, and falls back — but inward, a private rain dropping out of a clear sky, hitting the white ground with a ticking hiss. Every surface weeps. The crater walls run with warm rivulets that stain the fresh rock orange-brown and grey. The flats between the features are flooded gravel, ankle-slick, printing your boots in mud that steams. You are wet and hot at once, and stay that way — sweat can't dry, condensate beads on your sleeves and then the condensate itself warms, and your glasses fog and will not clear, and the sun, where it comes through the plume, is a white coin behind waxed paper. The light is ordinary daylight, but the steam files it flat: no shadows anywhere in the hollow, only glare off the wet white ground — brilliant enough that you find yourself squinting in a place with no sun in it.

Downstream of the crater, the water builds. The outflow sheets across a series of terraces of geyserite — young silica, stepped like poured candle wax, every lip and rim still knife-edged, none of it softened yet by a decade of weather — and gathers in a pool the color of milk dropped into sky. The pool is a hand's depth of nearly-boiling, sour water, too hot and too acid for anything to grow in it. Its edges are where the only life appears: stringy films, white first, then a dirty green in the cooler rills, bacteria threading the boundary where scalding becomes merely warm. The pool has a tide. Over about a day and a half it rises and falls a hand-span, with no respect for the sun or the moon, on some deep pressure-breath of its own — the whole pocket exhales on that schedule, the drone deepening and lifting, the column thickening and thinning. A meter of the world's plumbing, breathing.

The industrial remains are scattered across the far side of the hollow like something set down and then dropped. Four concrete stubs with their anchor bolts, one sheared clean. The derrick lying down the slope in four lattice sections, warped, its climbing ladder still bolted on, its legs already blooming scale. The doghouse — the drillers' shed — half-collapsed, plywood blistered and grey, its roof drummed endlessly by the interior rain; inside, a bench vise, a mug skimmed over with silica, logbook pages fused into a brick. A core rack holding sixty meters of stone in ordered cylinders, rows of it, like the pipes of some buried organ that the well has become. A stenciled crate on skids, sunk to its axles in white sinter, holding a turbine that was never assembled, for a small power plant that poured its slab and got no further — the bare concrete pad sits a few hundred meters off with the pipeline stub marching toward it and stopping. A hardhat on the ground, filled with hot water, already two centimeters deep in stone.

That is the local rule of preservation: nothing rots here, and everything is buried. The silica-charged water coats whatever stands still — wood, wire, cloth, steel — in stone
