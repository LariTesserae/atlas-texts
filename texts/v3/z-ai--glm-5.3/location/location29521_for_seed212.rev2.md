# The Greenhowl

It began, perhaps, as a river delta. No one is certain, because the delta keeps rewriting itself, and there is no one to be certain — no one at all. This is a place without observers. That fact shapes everything.

## What the place is

The Greenhowl is a city that is a forest that is a single argument between growth and gravity, conducted at volume. It occupies a low warm basin — you would call it a floodplain, except the water never fully arrives. Moisture comes instead as condensation: every night, warm air rising off the basin meets cooler air sliding down from somewhere, and the whole city sweats. Beads of water form on the undersides of leaves the size of doors and fall in fat audible drops all morning, which is the closest thing this place has to rain, and the closest thing to a clock.

The substrate is plant. Not "mostly plant" — there is no ground here that is not root, no stone, no soil older than the roots that hold it. Dig anywhere and you find white tuber-mass packed like marrow, then more tuber, then a floor of living wood that flexes slightly under pressure. The horizon itself is vegetal: the basin's edges are not hills but ramparts of braided trunk and folded bark, still sharp-featured, still young — everything here is young, the whole city is perhaps a century old, and it has the unfinished, urgent look of a construction site, if construction sites were also demolition sites, also the demolition crews, also the lumber.

## The roar

This is the defining fact. The Greenhowl is deafening, and the sound is not wind.

It is growth. Every structure here is built of gourds and swollen stems that inflate visibly — a pillar takes a week to go from knee-height to full height, and it creaks, pops, and groans the entire time, and there are a hundred thousand pillars. Culms snap upward with a report like cracking knuckles amplified across the valley. Pods burst. The dome-vaults that serve as ceilings are membranes stretched taut and tightening, and they thrum like drum heads being tuned by a lunatic. Seed-towers collapse daily with a sound like a hill falling over, which is exactly what it is.

And beneath all of it, continuous: a subsonic churn, the sound of the root-floor flexing as the whole city shifts its weight. You feel it in your sternum more than hear it. Standing here, you would stop being able to distinguish your own heartbeat from the place's. It is easy to imagine the city has one.

## The city, as such

There are streets. There are plazas, aqueduct-galleries where the condensation is channeled in woven living gutters, even something like districts — a quarter of tall buttress-flats, a quarter of low dense vaulting, a sprawl of gourd-dwellings each the size of a cottage. No one built any of it. The geometry emerges the way crystals emerge: the plants that grow in street-like patterns are the plants that don't get crushed by the ones that do, and over decades the city has been selecting itself for navigability, for load-bearing, for rooms.

And yet it is unmistakably urban. Not a metaphor of a city. A city. There are load paths and redundancy and redundancy's failure; there are choked intersections where three trunk-columns compete for one airspace and one wins while the others rot around it in place, still standing, still functioning as walls. There are ruins — districts that grew too tall, collapsed in a night, and are now being eaten by the next wave of colonizers, which grow out of the ruins in the ruins' exact footprints, so that the city remembers itself. Whether it knows it remembers is one of the open wounds of this place.

## Where reality frays

Here is the difficulty, and the reason "weirdness" is not too strong a word:

The plants are not behaving entirely like plants. They grow too purposefully. A stem will begin, mid-growth, growing *toward* something — and when you trace the growth line back, the thing it was growing toward is a gap where nothing has grown yet, an absence the shape of a future building. Then, weeks later, the building fills the absence. The plants are building toward their own intentions before those intentions exist, and the city is consequently haunted by its future: empty plazas that will clearly be full, full corridors that will clearly be gone, doors that are already opening slightly on rooms that haven't finished deciding to be there.

Some nights the condensation falls upward. Not far — a few handspans, a slow reversal, drops rising off the root-floor and hanging in the air like the beginning of rain played backward — and the roar drops with it into a register that seems to be saying something, a long low mutter in a grammar of pressure-changes. It is not speech. It is what speech would be if speech were a plant's idea, heard secondhand through a membrane wall.

And time is not reliable in the collapsed districts. A ruin can be observed young and old simultaneously: fresh sap at the fracture face, deep rot in the same wound. The city does not resolve this contradiction. It holds it, the way a poorly healed bone holds its break.

## The absence

There is no fauna. Not one insect, not one bird, nothing that eats anything. The ecology is a closed loop of the plants consuming themselves — the living streets, the dead districts, the colonizing sprouts, and back again, a system with no outside. Pollination happens by concussion: the nightly collapse of a seed-tower hurls pollen across whole quarters in a blizzard that catches the light and settles in soft warm drifts. The place fertilizes itself with its own catastrophes. Every disaster is a wedding.

This is why the sound never stops, and why the silence, if it ever came, would be the true event — the moment the argument ended, the growth concluded, the city finished.

There is no sign that it will be finished. If anything, the roar is getting louder. The Greenhowl is not decay and not bloom; it is the permanent violence between them, a city at the exact instant of becoming, sustained for a hundred years, and it shows no interest whatsoever in arriving.
