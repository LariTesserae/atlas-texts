You hear the valley before the land admits to anything. Out on the approach flats — dry, dun-colored, scattered with black glass pebbles — there is a roar with no visible cause: low, level, total, the sound of a great works heard across a fence line. The throb of blowers. The hum of a boiler under permanent load. Then the plain tilts, the horizon steps down, and there it is below: a wide sunken basin, several miles across, breathing steam from two dozen scattered throats. The source of all that noise, running steadily, as it has always run.

The basin is the lowest country for a long way in any direction. Every slope in the region eventually turns toward it, and two thin rivers actually arrive, sliding down off the dry uplands with a mineral brightness in their water, warm at the touch even where the current is quick. They come in and never leave. What the rivers bring in, the valley exhales as vapor; what they carry dissolved, it keeps. That is the arrangement here, and it has been the arrangement for so long that the place has finished becoming what it was becoming. It is not forming. It is not decaying. It is simply in operation.

The vents are scattered across the white flats with a machine-floor spacing — far apart, each alone, hundreds of paces of blank crust between one and the next, though no access was ever planned and no one ever planned it. Most of the throats are ringed with built-up lips of geyserite, stone the steam itself has cast, cream-colored and fluted like poured wax. Around certain vents the sulfur has bloomed out of the ground in yellow filigree, castings in place, delicate as ornament on a stove. The largest vent — the one whose voice carries all the way to the flats above — is better than a meter across, and its roar holds a pitch. It is not noise exactly. A tuned ear, or a tuned anything, would find the note down there beneath the roar, steady enough to calibrate against, and it would find the same note tomorrow, and in ten thousand tomorrows. The other vents chuff: a piston-slap of steam every few seconds, apertures polished by their own exhalation, intervals that do not drift.

The steam columns never lean. Weather moves overhead — clouds run through, sun crosses, wind will push dust along the upper flats — but the valley's own air sits still, and every plume stands vertical, plumb-straight, all day, columns of white standing over the flats like smokestacks that were never raised and never will be, because nothing here was ever built and nothing here ever fell.

Underfoot, the ground is sinter: silica laid down film by film by the hot water over uncounted time, snow-white, chalk-porous, faintly ringing under a step, like walking on a poured floor. Lay a palm on it and the warmth comes up through the crust; hold still and you may feel a slow breath of vapor rise from a pinhole, as if the floor itself were exhaling. In patches the iron has come out of the water instead, and the terracrust goes rust-red, scabbed and mill-scaled. There is black basalt under everything and there are the obsidian pebbles worn round on the flats, glass from old fire, cool on their windward side at dawn and beaded with dew.
