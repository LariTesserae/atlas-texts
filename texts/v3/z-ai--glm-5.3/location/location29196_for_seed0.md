**THE WARP**

The way in announces itself before you find it: a warm exhalation rising through a split in the forest floor, carrying mushroom and wet iron and the ammoniac sweetness of bats. The crack is new — you can tell because its edges are unworn, glassy, still sharp enough to open a boot sole — and it breathes. When the pressure falls above, the dark below exhales a soft fog through this throat; when pressure rises, it drinks the forest air down. The people who come here read the entrance like a weather-glass, and they go in only on a falling glass, because that is when everything inside is about to bloom.

You descend through root fibers, hand over hand, and the light goes out of the world in stages — green, then brown, then a grey that is more memory than photon, then nothing. What replaces sight arrives as a wall of sound. At first you will misread it as a river. Then as wind. Then, when you are far enough down that your own footfalls vanish into it, you will understand that the roar is *population* — millions of wings and feet and stridulating thighs — and that you have entered one of the loudest rooms on earth by way of one of its darkest.

***

The main chamber is called the Long Room, and it is named honestly: a plain of dunes running kilometers along the rift, floor and walls and ceiling all formed within living memory of the rock. Nothing down here is rounded yet. The stone is young volcanic breccia, knife-edged, still cooling in places — put your ear to a fresh wall and it ticks like a cooling kettle, because it *is* one. The dune-sand is shattered volcanic glass that chimes faintly underfoot and glitters when any light finds it, which is almost never. Compasses go lazy and wrong down here; the iron in the rock froze pointing at the sky the day it formed, and it has not agreed to update. Knapped stone has to be struck by feel.

The ground is warm. Not warm-ish — warm the way a body is warm, and this is the place's great gift: you can sleep anywhere on it. The heat is the leftover heat of the rock's own birth, still bleeding out of the young intrusion below, and it warms the whole cavern to the inside of a mouth. The air is thick, breath-shaped, and it never, ever gets cold. Everything that dies down here is eaten within days. Nothing keeps. Everything happens, fast.

Water is present the way a rumor is present. It does not run; it *arrives*. Condensation beads on the ceiling and the hanging roots all day, and when the cavern's breath shifts, that condensation falls at once — a sudden soft rain in the dark, called cave-weather. Seep-water gathers in sharp-edged pockets and shallow bowls in the rock, warm, faintly sulfurous, skinned with mineral film and rings of pale yellow. One or two deep pools lie along the rift, and they are so low, so close to the sea's own water table, that their surfaces lift and subside a finger's width with tides that are felt, not seen, from kilometers away.
