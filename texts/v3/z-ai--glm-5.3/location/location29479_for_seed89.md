**The Warm Throat**

The mountain is the oldest and highest thing for a hundred miles, and it is almost done being a mountain. Its shoulders wear glaciers. Its air, at this height, is more suggestion than substance — a dozen breaths to do the work of one. And yet, in all weathers, the summit smokes. A gray feather of vapor stands above the peak, day and night, and the valley people — who still knap their tools from river stone, whose hammers have never crossed the icefall — call the plume the mountain's breath and leave it at that. They are right. The fire is almost out. What remains of it is spent on one room.

The room is not easy to reach. Behind a blade of wind-carved rock, a cleft in the summit opens like a flute's mouthpiece, and the passage within is polished the way a flute's interior is polished — by centuries of the same breath passing through. Warmth comes up the throat first: a slow exhalation, animal and humid, rising against the killing cold outside. Sound comes with it. Long before the eyes admit to anything, the ears report a low hiss under everything, and above the hiss, a thin, ceaseless ticking, like rain that has forgotten how to land.

Then the passage opens and you are in it.

One chamber. That is the whole of the place — a domed hollow some forty paces across, shaped like an upturned skull, ribbed with columns of dark andesite that were lava once and have had ten thousand years to learn patience. The scale is small, bounded, complete: the dome, its narrow skirt of side-fissures, and nothing else. But within that boundary, almost nothing is empty.

The floor is a flight of terraces — mineral stairs built drop by drop by the warm water itself, cream and rust and terracotta, though the eye will never confirm this. Small scalloped pools sit in the terraces like linked thumbprints, warm as blood, overflowing one into the next down a groove the seep has worn so long that the groove has become permanent, a settled fact of the room, as if carved deliberately. Fumaroles breathe from a half-dozen small mouths in the rock. The air they make is bathhouse air: damp, mineral, faintly of struck matches and hot rain, underlaid with the mild ammonia of very old droppings. Breathing here is like drinking a warm soup. Outside the door, the world is forty degrees below zero. Inside, the mountain keeps a summer.

And it is dark. That is the room's second great fact. Darkness here is not an absence; it is furnished. The eye, given time, will salvage almost nothing: cold threads of blue-green where luminous bacterial films edge the wet sinter, a dull foxfire breath off the oldest guano heaps, and at the entrance dimness, the only true green in the whole world of this place — a rim of black moss, one stunted saxifrage rooted in a pocket of droppings, a single small fern unrolling near the door. Nearly every plant that lives here lives within arm's reach of daylight. Past that fringe, the room is sound and heat and touch.

Sound is the landscape. The chamber is loud the way a river is wet. The vents sing, each at its own fixed note, and the wind across the chimney mouth makes the whole dome moan in slow organ chords — the mountain playing itself, a tune with no audience it cares about. The dome amplifies everything: a whisper at the entrance is audible in the deeps; the room is one ear with forty paces of hearing. Over the drone sits the thin ticking — tens of thousands of crevice swiftlets, stitching the darkness with audible clicks, navigating a cathedral of air by echolocation. They pack the hot ceiling in glued bowls of moss, feather, and hardened saliva, each nest a made thing by an unmakable process; the rock's warmth broods the eggs for them, so the ceiling is one vast communal incubator, and the birds repay the mountain by hunting the meadows below and carrying the outside in on their bodies. Guano falls here like slow, constant snow. This is a mountain inverted: its fertility rains down instead of rising up.

Below the birds, the walls are layered the way soil is layered. Sinter under microbial film under springtails like strewn grains of salt under cave crickets, cold-fingered and immense of antenna, packed so close their folded legs touch. Bats hang in the warm creases by the thousand, a soft leathern rustle. Mites move through the droppings like a second, finer snow. In the deep pools, blind red filaments sway where the water is almost too warm for anything to be alive, and pale amphipods patrol the survivable margin. There is a snail here — a small white, lens-shaped snail that exists nowhere else on earth — grazing the guano in patient rows. The floor of the chamber is, to a depth no one has measured, mostly shell: ten thousand years of the same snail, falling. And at the entrance, where a thread of dimness survives, the warm fissures house pikas who haul cut grass in from the cold slopes and cure it in haystacks on ledges, while choughs crack open the white snails on a flat slab just outside the door. That slab has been used for so many generations that it is polished and ringed by a midden of shards. It is the closest thing to a made object in the entire room, and no one made it. This is the technology of the place, if it can be called that: glue, weave, haystack, anvil. Craft without makers. Hands were never involved.

The rules are simple and old. Heat is the currency, and every creature holds territory by distance from a vent — temperature castes, from the amphipods' marginal bathwater to the crickets' middle band to the birds' baking ceiling.
