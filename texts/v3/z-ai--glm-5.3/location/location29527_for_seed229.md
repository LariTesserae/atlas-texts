You hear it three days before you arrive.

The high plain is a quiet country — salt crust and old basalt under a permanent amber haze, light like a lamp behind a horn panel, warm but never bright. Nothing speaks there. Then, faintly, on the second night, a tone comes up through the soles of your feet, and by the third morning it is in your teeth: a drone, then a chord, then many chords braiding and rebraiding, as if the mountain ahead were humming to itself in its sleep. The sound does not grow louder so much as it grows *closer to true* — richer, more resolved — until you climb the last shoulder and break through the haze layer, out onto a summit shelf the size of a cathedral close, and the sound stops being something you hear and becomes a place you are inside.

The shelf is a bowl of pale rock a few hundred paces across, rimmed on three sides by a horseshoe of fluted columns and breached on the fourth by a slot the locals — there are locals, faintly, more on them soon — call the Notch. The Notch is a weir for wind. All day the warm air of the lowlands rises along the flank of the massif and pours through that slot like a river over a lip, and the bowl drinks it. Everything here is arranged to drink it. The whole shelf is an instrument, and the wind never stops playing it, and nothing in this world — not the trees, not the animals, not the stones themselves — is quiet, because quiet, here, is the same thing as starving.

Start with the floor, because you touch it first. It is sand: bone-colored, salt-crusted, and it *booms*. Your first step lands like a struck drumhead, and every step after that, until you learn to walk with the flat of your foot and let the crust sing under you. The ripples in the sand are not wind-ripples, or not only. They are the standing waves made visible — the sand sorts itself along the current chord like iron filings around a magnet, and when the gust angle shifts and the grove re-tunes, you can watch the ripples *migrate*, a slow shiver of grain crossing the floor, the score rewriting itself in real time.

The colonnade on the north rim is the organ proper. Columns of wind-carved rock, twenty, thirty paces tall, honeycombed with flutes and slots and palm-wide bores, each bore holding its own note the way a bottle holds its breath. Touch one: the stone is warm, almost blood-warm, and it hums against your palm. These forms are finished. That is the strangest thing about them. There is no raw edge anywhere on this shelf, no fresh scar, no half-dug channel — the wind has been at this work for so long that it has reached an equilibrium, every hole smoothed to its note, every notch at its angle of least resistance.
