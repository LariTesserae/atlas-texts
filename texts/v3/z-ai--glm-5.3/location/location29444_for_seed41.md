Above, the plateau gives you almost nothing. Thin grass gone the color of bone, a wind that has run out of everything it could carry, sky pressed flat and white with heat. The only tree for a mile stands alone in a shallow depression, and beneath its roots the ground opens — not a cave mouth exactly, but a throat. A sinkhole, soft-edged, ferned around the lip, wide enough for a body and a barrel of light. It breathes. Warm air rises out of it in a slow column, and the tree above leans its whole crown toward that breath, because the breath is water.

Go down. The descent is a groove of stone, polished to a dull shine by feet — uncountable feet, over a time nobody counted — with the fig's roots for a handrail, roots as thick as arms, running down into the green dim below like cables into a machine that runs on nothing.

The room at the bottom is small. Thirty paces would cross it. The dome overhead is ribbed like the inside of a bell, dark at the crown where no light has ever reached, and the light that does arrive — a single shaft falling from the sinkhole's throat, traveling all day across the floor like a slow hand — leaves everything outside its beam in a green-gray dimness, the light of a closed eyelid. It is hot down here. Not the surface's baked glare but a wet, kept heat, the heat of a proofing oven, of a greenhouse at midnight, of a held breath. It rises from below — you feel it through your boot soles, in the stone, in the water — and the dome is a lid, and the heat cannot leave, so it has stopped trying. The walls sweat. Drop by drop, on a clock no one winds, condensation gathers on the cooler stone near the opening and lets go.

The rock itself is cream and caramel, limestone softened and settled into lobes and terraces, flowstone poured down the walls in frozen drapery, older than impatience. Nothing here is being carved. The water that made this room has finished making it; it arrives now as vapor and seep and drip, and leaves the same way, and the formations grow so slowly that growth and stillness have become the same word. Along the floor, the seep has built a staircase of tiny rimstone dams, basins nested like rice paddies in miniature, and each basin holds water so clear it looks like a thickness of air, water warmed from beneath until it is — you know this the moment your hand goes in — exactly the heat of blood. The inside of a wrist, held under.

And it is green. Shockingly, given the dryness above, but the green has edges, and the edges are honest: moss lawns and filmy ferns grow exactly where the shaft's light travels across the seasons, and stop exactly where it doesn't, a boundary as clean as a shoreline. In the beam's territory the growth is nearly tropical — ferns waist-high, liverworts in black-green plates, algae silk on the wet stone, seedlings of the fig sprouting from seeds the mother tree drops down her own roots. The fig is the whole story, actually. The room is younger than the fig. Some root, decades or centuries ago, found the crack in the plateau and followed the warmth and the damp downward, and the crack widened, and a lid of rock fell in, and light entered a place that had only ever held heat. After that the room had two fortunes — warmth from below, sunlight from above — and life, which is a patient accountant, invested both immediately.

The fauna is the room's crowd. Look once and the place seems still; look again and there is barely an unoccupied surface. Bats hang in the dome's dark, a small colony, thumb-sized, wings folded like smoked paper, utterly silent. Their droppings fall to one side of the floor in a bronze, fine-grained cone that smells faintly, sweetly of ammonia and minerals — and this cone is itself a city: beetles like moving crumbs, pale millipedes, mites in dust-cloud numbers, white threads of fungus stitching it all back into soil. On the damp walls, thousands of lentil-sized snails graze the algae film, spaced like constellations. Cave crickets, translucent, antennae longer than their bodies, stand on the flowstone ledges with the stillness of carved things. A gecko the color of milk works the wall seams at dusk. Sheet webs of nearly invisible spiders sag between the fig roots, and in the midday beam the midges congregate in a trembling column like a thought the room is having.

The pools have their own population — glass shrimp, black tadpoles, and frogs the color of wet leaves, who speak rarely: a single wooden *tock* every so often, as if time needed reminding. And there is a snake. Thin as a bootlace, patient as the stone, it keeps a schedule at the second basin, and the frogs keep a different schedule, and this arrangement — predator and prey sharing one warm room at different hours — is the room's oldest treaty.

Humans came too. The evidence is almost nothing, which is the point of it. The polished groove of the path, worn by generations of bare feet until foot-wear and water-wear became indistinguishable. A fan of torch soot on the wall at shoulder height, soft as breath on glass. A few stacked stones that might have been a bench or might have been a shelf, fallen now into a shape geology will accept. And on a panel of moonmilk — a deposit soft as wet chalk — someone long ago drew a single line with a finger, following the crack in the rock behind it, a river for a river. Beside it, pressed flat into the mineral paste: handprints. A large one. Then a small one. Then another small one. Whatever happened here — and the warmth suggests it plainly, the blood-heat water, the sick and the cold and the laboring brought down into the one place the plateau kept kind — it happened bare-handed, with fire, with nothing else. No tool ever entered this room. The only thing people built here was wear.

The room has rules, and it enforces them without drama.

Heat is wealth, and it stays. Light is rationed, and everything grows within its ration. Water does not flow here; it seeps, arrives as breath, departs as breath and wings. Nothing is in a hurry, because the room has finished becoming. What dies falls where the light isn't, and the small workers fold it back in. And — this is the rule you feel first — the room keeps no echoes. The soft stone, the moss, the damp, all of it swallows sound the way the pools swallow light. A drip lands with a tap felt more than heard. The bats leave at dusk in a quiet ribbon, close along the wall, wings making only a paper whisper. A gray fox comes down the worn path at the same hour some evenings, drinks at the coolest basin, and leaves without spilling a single sound. Your own voice, if you try it, comes back to you wet and near, as though the room were smaller than it is, and then not at all. It is a place where listening is easier than speaking, and after a few minutes you stop speaking.
