**The Old Table**

It is the top of a mountain that has stopped being one. Once — in an age with a different name for time — this was a peak, a tooth in the range. But erosion here is measured in eras, and the wind and the thermal creep and the slow mineral fatigue of the rock have had their way down to the last patience. What remains is a plateau, a table, a flat gray floor of ancient granite the size of a market town, lifted three vertical miles into the warm haze. Walk to its edge and you stand above the clouds, though the clouds themselves are below a second ceiling: a permanent luminous smog-inversion that holds the whole summit in a warm, dim, pear-colored light, like late afternoon that never commits to ending.

There is no water here. That is the first law, and everything alive on the Table has built itself around it.

And yet the place is green — lushly, absurdly green, a dense shoulder-to-shoulder jungle of wax. Every plant on the Table is sealed. Leaves are thick, glossy, folded shut along their edges like hands; stems are lacquered; roots run shallow and interlaced rather than deep, because there is nothing below to reach for. Nothing drinks. Nothing sweats. The vegetation survives by passing fluid sideways: trees tapped by animals, animals licked by birds, stems grafted to stems in slow commerce. The whole canopy is one closed system of sap, trading moisture leaf to leaf the way a village trades bread. When something dies — a moth, a branch, a lizard — it is consumed to the last drop before the second hour, and nothing dries. The dry air is fierce; a spill is a death sentence. Even the humans, of whom there are a few hundred, do not weep here as a habit. They say the sky takes tears as payment and gives nothing back.

The settlement sits in the densest stand of trees, and the distinction between built and grown is a courtesy. Huts are frames of hardwood lashed with bark-cord, then *assisted*: saplings planted at their corners and trained over the years until the roof grows itself and the walls are living, sealed against the desiccating wind. Paths are stone, worn glass-smooth by generations of bare feet. The town has no streets so much as layers — dwellings stacked on dwellings, terraces cut a hand-span at a time into the ancient flat granite, walkways passing under roots that were once walkways themselves. From above it reads as one organism with a thousand small doors.

Their technology is the technology of hands. Stone bowls, bone needles, fire coaxed carefully in sealed hearths (open flame dries the air, and dry air is the enemy of everything). The fauna are the loud ones. Sound here is constant and layered — a wet noise in a dry place. Insects dominate: a choral drone of sap-beetles that rises and falls across the whole day like breathing, thickened by the trapped haze which carries sound the way it carries warmth. Over that, the bright clicking of the leaf-warblers, small birds that never land on stone, only on living wood, and whose call is a percussion like stones tapped together. Lizards the color of old bronze run the walls at dusk, hunting with their ears. And under everything, all night, the deep belling of the greater night-heron, a bird nearly the size of a person, which stands motionless in the canopy absorbing dew through its feathers and calling at intervals that the villagers use to mark the hours.

The wind is the other presence. At this height it never fully stops, but on the Table it has been reduced to a slow, persistent pressing — the mountain's final shape is so aerodynamically finished, so completely settled, that the wind can no longer bite it and can only stroke it. It combs the canopy with a sound like surf heard through a wall. It turns the villagers' bone-and-bark chimes, hung in rows from every doorway, so that even silence here has a soft skeletal music in it.

Change comes slowly, and the place is nearly done changing. The mountain will not grow. The forest will not spread — there is nowhere to spread to, and the Table's edges are a cliff too sheer and dry even for the sealed trees. Buildings take a decade to finish because the trees must be grown into them. Children learn patience as a survival trait before they learn to walk. The only fast things are the animals and the insects, and even their seasons stretch: one long warm dim year, one slow sap-run, one generation of beetles to a human month.

Stand at the eastern rim at the dimmest hour, when the haze below briefly glows rose-colored from some filtered dawn, and the Table feels like the world's last shelf — a place at the extreme top of things that has finished its becoming, kept its warmth, raised its small loud green life, and decided, with the authority of extreme age, to simply continue.
