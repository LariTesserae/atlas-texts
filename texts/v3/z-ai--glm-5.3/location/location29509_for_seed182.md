You come down into Wickhollow from a dry country. That's the first thing to understand about the place: the surface above it is a brown, stingy plain of scrub and alkaline flat, where clouds pass overhead all year without committing to anything. The people who built this town did not come for minerals or refuge. They came for water they didn't have, and found it by accident when a quake split the basalt open about eighty years ago and the ground breathed out warm, wet air like a sleeper turning over. They dug a stair into the crack and never went back up to live.

The Stair is a switchback cut into rock that is still young enough to cut you. That's not a figure of speech — the fracture faces are glassy, their edges keen, unweathered, glittering with fresh crystal where the heat has begun to paint the stone in sulfur-yellow and rust-orange. There is no dust down here; the damp keeps everything wiped clean. By the third turn you are sweating. By the fifth you smell green — impossible, this far underground — wet leaves, iron, a whisper of rotten-egg sweetness. Then the walls let go of each other, and you are in the Longroom.

It is one chamber, and the whole town fits inside it: three hundred paces long, forty wide, ceiling lost somewhere above in a permanent haze. The dimness at first makes you half-blind. Then your eyes adjust, and you see that nothing here is empty. Terraces are stacked against both walls like shelves in a pantry, four and five dwellings deep, stitched together by bridges and stair-streets. Pipe-runs cross overhead at every height, beaded with condensation, climbed by vines. Every vertical surface is either a wall, a field, a ladder, a gutter, or a lamp — usually three of these at once. The eye searches for an unoccupied surface and fails. Even the ceiling has a job: pinned to it, eighty feet up, hangs the town's private cloud, a grey-gold haze that never leaves. Wickhollow keeps its own sky, the way another town might keep bees.

The light comes from the walls. The people farm a phosphorescent moss — wickmoss, they call it — in shallow pads and sealed jars hung like lanterns, and it glows the color of moonlight through leaves: dim, gold-green, patient. The moss is trimmed like wicks, watered from the gutters, and its brightness is a public virtue. You can judge a street's prosperity by its glow. Letting your wall go dark is the local equivalent of letting your fence rot — a dark house is a rude house. Wickhollow also, quietly, exports light: young moss packed in felt-lined crates and shipped out by rail-sledge to other towns under the world. They send darkness full of light, and it arrives glowing.

Water arrives the same way it does everything here: slowly, and by arrangement. At the deep end of the Longroom, heat comes up out of the earth — the floor there is too hot to stand on barefoot, and the town has drawn a painted line across the rock, the scorch line, beyond which you build nothing you love. On the near side of that line they've set the Kettle: a riveted iron boiler squatting over the hottest fissure, ticking over at a whisper, raising steam that climbs to the cold ceiling, meets the cloud, and condenses.
