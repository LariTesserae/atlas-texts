# The Primary Datum

You come up the last ridge expecting more country beyond it, and there is no more country. There is a plain.

Not a flatness like a lake bed or a plateau — a flatness like a *level*. The surface runs true to within centimeters over distances that take days to cross, and it keeps going past the horizon, and the horizon here is so far and so clean that you can see the faint arc of the world in it. Everything on this continent is high. That is the first thing the place tells you, and it tells you in a strange grammar, because high is a relative word and there is nothing left to be high *relative to*. The plain sits four kilometers above a mean sea level that was assigned, surveyed, and benchmarked — and then the sea was subtracted.

Altitude here is a memory. Every measurement is taken from a body of water that is no longer anywhere.

## The Finish

The ground underfoot is a single piece. Not stone in sheets — one continuous mineral, blue-gray shading to black, fused and dense, ringing faintly when struck. Its surface has the quality of a machined part: the soft haze of wind-polish, a varnish like the skin on old metal, and a flatness that light doesn't waver on. If you fracture it, it breaks with the curved shell-mark of flint, and the exposed face is the only fresh thing for kilometers.

It was either the hardest layer the world ever had, or it was made. Probably both. The wind spent an unthinkable number of years sand-blasting this land, and erosion here did not stop — it *finished*. It took everything removable, down to the last gravel, the last grain, and when nothing loose remained the wind found it could take nothing more. What you walk on is the terminal hardness of a planet: the surface past which erosion cannot go. Machinists have a word for achieving a surface by wearing it with abrasive until it is perfect — they call it lapping. This continent has been lapped. The locals, if there were locals, would call it the Finish.

Nothing on it rattles. Nothing shifts. Underfoot is forever.

## The Dry Sea

The ocean is what happened here. Something — the project, the program, whatever word you want for an engineering that current physics only partly describes — took the sea apart. Not drained it: *dissolved* its purpose. The water itself is gone, drawn off into the machinery or the sky or space, but the ocean was never just water.
