# The Levels

You hear the Levels an hour before you reach them. The sound arrives ahead of the land the way the smell of rain arrives ahead of a storm: first a pressure behind the eyes, then a hum you feel in the sternum and the bones of your jaw, and only at the end of that hour does the fog thin enough to show what has been making all that noise. It is a grey plain, flat to every horizon, going on past any distance you could be confident about, and the first thing you understand is that the noise is not coming from anywhere in particular. It is coming from everywhere, continuously, from the land itself.

The Levels are the floor of a sea that retired so long ago its sediments have forgotten being under anything. Underfoot is shell-lime and gravel, and a good third of the stones are simply that — shells, nacre gone pewter with age, the entire previous cast of life ground down into footing. The mountains that once shed sediment into this basin are gone so completely that even their rubble has been rounded into lozenges. Relief is measured in finger-widths: a stone rib protruding like the spine of a sleeping animal, a gravel bar, a stillrun — a channel with so little gradient left that the water moves with the patience of someone who knows exactly how long they have. Nothing descends anywhere. The land has almost finished being land.

The air is cool enough to show your breath in the first hours of the day, and the standing water stings. Fog is not weather here; it is the medium. It thickens and thins on a daily rhythm the way tides move, and the light never fully turns on. Noon is a pale authority overhead, a brightness without source or direction, diffuse through standing haze until nothing casts more than a smudge of shadow. You find your way by wind on the face and by ear. This is not a hardship. It is simply the local sense, and the local sense is hearing. Everything smells of wet chalk and cold iron, and under that, faintly animal — a stable for things that have never been named.

The water comes out of the air. Scattered boulders called weepers stand a half-morning apart across the flats, each colder than the fog around it, and the fog condenses on their windward flanks until the dark stone shines and runs, weeping steadily all day, every day. Each weeper is a water tower, a slow well. The drip fills pecked bowls in their tops, overbrims down the flanks, and feeds a halo of wet ground around each stone — and the halo is where everything lives. Between weepers the flats are nearly sterile grey. Deaf ground, half a morning of it, sometimes a whole day. The world here is not evenly spread. It is rationed out stone by stone.

Now the noise. Its foundation is a drone: wind crossing the hollows water has carved into the ribs, and entering the old solution-caves beneath the shell-lime, plays the land like a stopped organ, a broad chord that shifts key when the wind veers and swells when the fog pushes through. Over the drone, the percussion of water — millions of drips on stone, into cavities, onto living film, arrhythmic but utterly constant. In every pool, a fizz, which I will explain. Overhead, something like sleet. And moving through all of it, at dawn and again at dusk, the slow bright ringing of the herds.

Three facts make it as loud as it is. The land is hard, so nothing absorbs. It is flat, so nothing blocks. It is empty, so there is nothing standing between you and any of it. Distance is a rumor here; a knuckleback snapping three miles off arrives at your feet. The Levels are the largest small room you will ever stand in. Plugs are useless, because much of it comes through your bones. The sound has weather, and the sound has hours, and you will learn the hours by ear within three days and never unlearn them: the Loud Morning, when the fog is thickest and the drip peaks and the herds cross the ringing pans; the Plenum, the full midday roar; the Roll Call at dusk, when each kind signs off in a fixed order as if agreeing to; and at midnight the Swallow, when every swallow-hole drains into the rock at once, a long glottal gulp passing from horizon to horizon — the only hour the whole surface can be heard at the same time, and therefore the hour when every animal drinks, because it is the only time anyone can be sure nothing is missing. After you leave, your ears ring for two days. Silent rooms will feel like held breath. You will not forgive a quiet street for a week.

There are no plants. Not few — none. No stem, no leaf, no green worth the name. What you mistake for moss in the half-light is the bloom: a dark, wet, continuous skin on every stone that stays damp, one cell-layer thick in places, thicker where it has had centuries. It does not photosynthesize; the light here would not allow it. It eats the stone and the fog's dissolved chemistry, a patience-based metabolism, and where the bloom grows, everything else can. Green is a texture here, not a color. In the deeper pools stand the mat mounds — layered domes the bloom's ancestors built, a lamina every few years, the oldest structures on the Levels and the oldest kind of structure in the world. They exhale a bubble a minute, roughly. The oldest voices in the valley are also its slowest percussion.

The animals crowd in on that single thin economy, and they are abundant the way stars are: between the points, nothing at all; at each point, everything. The grazers are the bells — shelled, palm-size to melon-size, nacre flanks gone the color of the gravel — and their herds are called carillons, because they ring. There are pans where the stone rings true; struck, it says a note instead of a knock. The bell-herds cross these deliberately, single file, and for the minutes it takes, the whole country is a slow glockenspiel. The bells are also the gardeners: grit in their gut carries bloom from weeper to weeper, so every herd is a seed-cart and every migration route a planted row.

In every film of standing water sit the knucklebacks, small crustacean-things that snap cavitation bubbles to stun their prey — that's the fizz, a sound like fat in a pan, audible from miles. Glasswing midges swarm above the pools like driven sleet, each one clicking to hold the swarm's shape, and dish-faced waders stalk the fringes picking them out of the air by ear alone, heads swinging like searchlights that hear. In the darkest pools a lurker called the wick hangs a single cold blue lure — the only light on the Levels that isn't fog. And there are the false-knockers, the only liars in an honest economy: small predators that counterfeit a drip-sound or a bell's footfall to draw the thirsty within reach.

The apex of it all you will almost certainly never see. The Hush is flat the way the land is flat, wide as a door and low as a doorstep, and it hunts inside loudness, steps timed to the chorus peaks, body pressed to the ringing stone. You never hear it coming. You hear everything else stop. A bell quits grazing mid-mouthful; a pool's fizz drops out; the stillruns go dumb; and the silence spreads, animal to animal, faster than the Hush itself can move, because quiet here is contagious the way panic is. The hush-wave passes over you like the shadow of a cloud, only faster. Then somewhere out in the fog one knuckleback snaps — a single loud, defiant, terrible snap — and the whole valley floods back in. Silence on the Levels has a shape, and the shape is low and wide and hungry.

Three laws hold the place together, and everything is born knowing them. First: loud is safe, quiet is dying — in a fog-world the crowd is the hiding place, and every animal advertises itself because only the drowned-out survive. Second: nothing is far — sound makes one room of this country, so behave at all times as if overheard, because you are. Third: water lives in stone, and so does everything else — life is a fringe condition, and the flats are barren by definition.

There is one exception, which is its own law. The Mute is a pan on the eastern side where the gravel gives way to fine damp ash, ground so soft and so dead it eats sound whole. Standing in the middle of it you hear only your own blood. Footfalls go silent as they land; even the fog seems to fall straight down instead of drifting. No animal crosses it. The old path runs straight to its rim and stops. The last pecked water-bowl sits at the edge, half-finished, its tool-marks still crisp after all this time. Whatever the Mute is, someone decided against finishing the bowl.

Which brings us to the listeners, because someone built here. Small people, pre-metal: pecking stones, clay, fiber, shell. Their works persist in fragments across the flats. The stake-combs — fishing weirs driven into the stillruns so long ago the gravel has mineralized them into grey ribs standing in rows. The ear-walls, low arcs of stacked stone, parabola-built, now broken into beads across the ground. The horn-pipes, clay whistles sunk into the banks with their mouths toward the prevailing wind; the wind still plays them, and no one maintains them, and no one has for a very long time. Fog-bowls pecked in long lines along the migration routes. And one building: a round corbelled house, roofless, with a throat-passage through its wall. When wind crosses the throat, the dome makes the long note — the lowest sound on the Levels — and everything near it goes still to hear it, the only silence the animals trust.

The ruins are the best instruments in the country and the animals know it. Choristers perch on the ear-walls, where the amplification is worth fighting for. Knucklebacks spawn to the pitch of the horn-pipes. The dome's rim is the most contested territory on the flats. The living play the instruments of the dead; it may be the only inheritance here. The builders called the place Earthmouth, and they came here, one must assume, to listen. What they believed, the pecked record suggests: that stone keeps every sound it has ever held, that erosion is the earth saying itself slowly, that the dead go into the ground and come out of it eventually as sound. There are no graves. There are no middens of any size. Whatever they came to hear, they heard it, or it stopped, or the same patience that takes everything here took them too.

Some of what the Levels produces has no owner. Every few days a roll-pulse crosses the whole country — every animal striking together for a few beats as the wave passes, a valley-wide tick, faster than anything travels, and nobody knows what starts it, and nobody needs to. There is an empty pan on the north side that knocks in a pattern for hours; nothing lives in it. And on still nights, under the dome, people used to claim the long note carried syllables. Do not put much weight on that. Do not put none, either.

The last thing to understand is that the Levels are not ending. They are deepening. Erosion finished with the surface ages ago — settled it into a final shape, and everything since has been rearrangement — but it continues underground, where the water hollows the shell-lime into galleries and the swallow-holes go down and down toward a sea that waits beneath the thin floor of the world. The first cast of life lies under your boots. The second cast still performs at the edges of stones. What is left to be made of this place will be made downward, of water and sound.

If the listeners were right that stone keeps sound, then the Levels are well past being a place and well into being an instrument remembering its whole life at once. The end of a landscape, it turns out, is not silence. It is everything the land has left to say, said all together, for a very long time, to anyone willing to stand still in the fog and be a listener.
