You hear it before you see it.

Half a day of climbing through hollowstone — a dark, bubble-pocked rock the wind has been carving for longer than anything on this world has bothered to keep count — and the sound arrives around the third turn from the top. Not a roar. A chord. A held chord, wide as weather, that comes up through your boots before it reaches your ears. The loose scree of the path is singing along with it. So, faintly, are your teeth.

Then the rim, and the place.

The first shock is how small it is. The shelf that makes all that noise would fit inside a cathedral's footprint: two hundred paces by three hundred, flat as a table, ringed by pinnacles of wind-fluted stone, and past the pinnacles — down, and down, and down — the sea.

There is no water on this world, and that has to be said plainly, because everything else follows from it. The ocean left ages ago; the story the valley folk tell is that the planet sweated it off into the sky and lost it. What stayed behind when the water went was the ocean's ghost: a sea of fog, warm and mineral-heavy, brine-thick, glowing from within by the labor of uncountable small bright things that live in it. It drinks like warm oil. It tastes — one cautious experiment on a fingertip will tell you — of metal and overripe fruit, and faintly, subtly wrong, which is why nobody licks the stones for pleasure. Nothing here drinks. Nothing sweats, nothing cries. Every living thing on this shelf anoints itself instead, with the film the floods leave behind: the gloss, warm and oily and faintly luminous, beading on stone in slow fat drops that crawl rather than run.

The light comes from below. That takes getting used to. The fog sea glows like a lamp the size of a horizon, and the shelf floats in its uplight, while overhead the sky is a pearl haze through which the sun has never once been seen distinctly. Shadows here point the wrong way — off the tops of things, upward, toward a sky nobody has ever seen a star in. At the rim the great plants stand black against their own glow, lit from underneath like figures held over a fire.

Walk out onto the crust. It is banded like lacquered wood, layer on layer — cream, rust, jade, a hairline of green copper — and every band is one flood: one occasion on which the sea rose and left its sediment to dry. Kneel and count them and you will lose count before you lose interest. The surface feels like nailed leather over dried honeycomb; it hums underfoot when the chord swells. Here and there it is pocked with old wax droppings gone hard as beads, and flecked with native copper weathered green, and bored into flute-grooves by wind older than the stair you climbed. Nothing on this shelf is being worn down. It is being worn into itself. Deposit by flood, grazing, and scour, the crust holds a steady thickness — a skin in balance, growing a centimeter a century, old enough that "old" stops meaning anything.

And the plants.

There are perhaps a dozen of them, and you can stand at the rim and see every one at once, because they are spaced like conspirators — each vast trunk far enough from every other that no voice crowds another. That spacing is not poverty. It is acoustics. Each one is a pipe: a hollow, silica-stiffened column the width of a wine barrel at the base, taller than three men, sheathed in wax bark, opening at the top into a great downward-facing bell lined with green velvet moss — because the light comes from below, the leaves have folded inward and down, and the chlorophyll lives in the bell's throat like carpet on a cathedral ceiling. Vines and gourd-gardens crowd their shoulders; spore-bells hang like lanterns; epiphyte cushions colonize every horizontal inch. The lushness here is vertical. A dozen plants, and each one a rainforest standing on a single stalk.

They earn their voices by dying correctly. A stalk grows fat in flood season, then cracks in the dry weeks, then hollows as the pith flakes away — and the hollow rings, and the wind finds the aperture, and now it is an instrument. The oldest and thickest is called the Deep Organ by those who name things here; its note is the floor the whole chord stands on. You feel it in your sternum more than hear it. At arm's length, over that drone, you cannot hold a conversation. People hum to each other here, or sign with their hands.

Someone built here, once, and maybe still does. A spiral wall, knee-high, dry-stacked from hollowstone chunks. A fallen lintel of bone so big it had to come from something the size of an ox — indeed from the oxen, the shaggy wax-fleeced grazers that wade the floods, their footfalls landing like muffled drums, their bellies providing the bass rumble the pipes are too proud to make. A rack of flutes made from cut reeds and loom-ox bone, pinned with hammered copper, left facing the wind; blow a short motif on the rack and you have left a message, which is how the tuners wrote to each other, since they could not speak over the chord. The rim itself is bored: hand-drilled flues through the pinnacles, angled so the wind crossing them sounds a set of low whistles underneath the grove. Somebody tuned this mountain. Their one recorded rule, cut into a flat stone in a hand-sign alphabet you can decode by watching the place work, is: take no pipe the grove still plays.

And on the rack today, a fresh-cut reed, its gloss still tacky.

Because the sea breathes. That is the turbulence here, and it is everything. At intervals no calendar predicts — twice in a day, or not for weeks — the fog sea swells, and its surface climbs the mountain, and the flood comes over the rim like slow white rapids, a meter deep, warm, reeking of metal and fruit. Lean birds called front-runners skate ahead of the front on its skin, piping the alarm. And then the shelf becomes a bazaar. Shuttermoths arrive in millions, their wing-clatter like applause on tin. Chime-finch flocks tinkle overhead in running figures. Carillon-hornets crawl into the velvet bells and come out powdered, each one humming, absurdly, the pitch of the plant it serves. Loom-oxen wade in, grazing the gloss off the crust with sounds like someone stripping wallpaper. Spore-bells pop in volleys. The chord sags flat as the heavy vapor fills the pipes — the sea leaning its weight on the key — and in the flood's glow you can watch the grove growing, a stalk gaining a hand's-breadth in the time it takes you to notice you're watching.

And in the flood, the fog stands up.

Not everywhere. Not often. But you will likely see it once: the vapor gathering itself into a figure — a heron, a school of fish-shapes swimming through air, once, famously, a kneeling shape holding what looked like a cut reed. The figures hold for a minute, sometimes less, and collapse without drama. Whether the sea is remembering, or mimicking, or luring, or whether these are simply its own large grazing creatures seen too briefly to be understood, no one has settled. The tuners carved the kneeling figure into their rimstones more than once, so the sightings are old. Nothing about it predates you. Nothing about it explains itself.

Then the sea leans back. The fog pours away over the rim like a tide reversing, and leaves a hush so complete you can hear your own blood for the first time in hours. Glitter settles out of the pearl haze in a slow dry rain, powder catching the uplight as it falls. The pipes shed their heavy vapor, the warm wind drains back down the mountain, and the chord sharpens again by degrees — the sea pulls it flat, the warmth pulls it sharp, and the argument between the two is, in the end, the music.

Understand the rules of the place and it stops looking like a garden and starts looking like an economy. Every species here owns a frequency the way species elsewhere own a niche: the finches of this shelf trill half a note higher than their grandmothers, because the Deep Organ has flattened with age and every voice had to move to stay audible. The hornets hum their employers' pitches. Small sexton-crabs live in the pipes, scouring debris, sharpening the apertures — the grove stays in tune partly because something is paid, in gloss and pollen, to keep it so. Loudness is fertility here: a stalk that sounds well is a stalk that gets visited, and the seed rides away on the audience. Turbulence is metabolism. The creation and destruction — grow, crack, hollow, sing — is not decay; it is digestion, the whole shelf eating its own weather and turning it into music at a centimeter a century.

Stay until the haze dims to ember. The sea below goes dark except for the slow lanes of light that move through it like streets seen from a great height, and the drone sinks with the sea's long inhale, and it becomes possible, for the first time since the rim, to hear a single one of the great pipes by itself — a sound like a held breath that has been held for a thousand years. If the flood comes at night, the white rapids glow as they climb, and the whole shelf fills with light from the inside out, every shadow pointing at the sky.

The entire place fits in one glance. You can see all of it at once — every plant, every wall, the whole bounded room of it, floating above a luminous sea with nowhere further to go. And you will never finish hearing it. In the morning the reed on the rack is gone, and the rack's flutes are singing a phrase nobody cut yesterday, and the knee-high spiral wall has one more stone in it than before.
