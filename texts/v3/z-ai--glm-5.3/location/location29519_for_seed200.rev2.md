The heat arrives before anything else — not the heat of a sun, because there is no sun here, but heat from below, rising out of the ground the way warmth rises off a sleeping animal. The air that enters the lungs is already body-temperature and saturated, thick as a greenhouse at closing time. Breathing feels less like taking in air and more like agreeing to share it with the place.

Overhead is an unbroken ceiling of pale cloud, glowing faintly amber-grey, lit by nothing visible. It does not move. It has never moved. It casts a dim, directionless light — an eternal overcast hour, late afternoon just before rain, held there without ever tipping into rain. Shadows are soft or absent entirely. There is no morning here to come out of and no evening to fall into.

The land beneath is new. New in a way you can feel with a hand pressed to it: mud soft as fresh bread crumb and warm to the wrist, gravels that have never been tumbled round, pale sands still sharp-grained and unworn. Where stone shows — along the low steaming ridges at the valley's rim, in benches above the pools — it is young enough to mark with a thumbnail, faintly warm, as if recently alive. The geology is in its first sleep. Nothing has compacted. The mud has not begun to become stone, and won't; the stone that exists has not begun to be anything else. The valley has no history yet, only ingredients.

And it is low. The whole basin sits barely above the warm water table, so that water is less a feature of the place than a condition of it. Kneel anywhere and push a hand into the ground and hot water rises around the wrist within seconds. Pools lie scattered across the flats at their exact and permanent levels — never higher after any "rain," because there is no rain; never lower, because nothing ever drains them. A river threads through the marsh, slow, wide, blood-warm, spreading through the reeds rather than cutting into anything; it deposits exactly what it lifts, so it never builds its banks and never wears them. Where the hottest seeps breathe, columns of steam stand up from the ground like slow pale pillars, leaning only under their own heat, and stay standing. There is no wind at all here — none, ever — and so the steam has nowhere to go and simply remains, a permanent fixture, like the trees.

Nothing is dry, though nothing is rained on. Water condenses out of the warm-saturated air onto every cooler surface and slides down it. The rocks weep. Every stone face is beaded and streaked with mineral tears — soft white and rust-colored trails from the constant weeping, trails that never widen because the weeping never varies. Every leaf-tip carries a permanent bead, and the beads fall, each from its same tip in its same order, a soft irregular patterning that goes on all the unchanging hours of the light.

The vegetation is lush the way a first generation is lush — everything growing fast, then arriving, then holding. The trees shot up early and stopped at their height; the canopy has been exactly this tall forever. The leaves are enormous, paddle-sized and paddle-shaped, lacquered deep green because wet, every one drawn out to a drip-tip that never once dries out to stop dripping. Ferns and giant herbs crowd the hummocks; reeds stand in the shallows in ranks; floating mats of rooted vegetation lie on the pools, held together like woven rafts. Moss films every trunk and every stone. There are flowers, and they hold their bloom indefinitely — open, vivid, unwilting — and fruit hangs in all stages at once, always, because there is no season to sort them into. On the ground the leaf litter lies at one fixed depth, always this depth, always this particular soft wet stratum, because decay consumes it at precisely the rate it is added. The ground never rises. The canopy never lifts. The whole green architecture is a chord, struck and sustained.

And the place is full — packed, layered, saturated with presences. Begin with the air itself: between the mist-ceiling and the canopy hang the swarms, columns of midges and gnats standing over each pool like grey pins pinned into the air, each column over its own spot the way a marker stands over a well. Dragonfly-analogues thread through them in slow circuits. There are no days or nights here to divide the animals into shifts, so everything is awake at once — all the birds, all the insects, all the waders and swimmers and creepers sharing one continuous crepuscular hour. That is why it is so full. Nothing takes turns.

In the warm water the fish stack in layers by temperature tolerance, dark shapes hanging motionless in the black-green depth; along the bacterial seeps some of them carry a faint cold glimmer along their flanks, a pale light that seems misplaced in a world this warm, and it is always there, never brighter, never gone. The mudflats shine with mats of heat-loving growth banded by temperature around each vent — scald-white nearest the water, then orange, then green, then dark — a fixed rainbow, painted and repainted identically forever. Wading birds stand in the shallows, necks moving in slow arcs. Big warm-blooded grazers stand chest-deep in the channels, mowing the vegetation in lanes that remain lanes because they are always being mowed the same way. Snakes lie in the branches with the exact stillness of branches. And the litter layer is never still: the whole ground surface moves gently, like the top of a slow boil, with beetles and crabs and small amphibians in numbers too dense to count, going about lives that consist entirely of holding position.

The sound is loud in the way saturation is loud: not a shout, but fullness. Underneath everything is the insect drone, a wide band of cicada-like song that never starts and never stops — it was already going when you arrived and it is a floor, not an event. Layered over it, the frogs: clicks, booms, whistles, purrs, in overlapping rhythms so fixed they sound rehearsed. Over that, the birds, calling the same phrases into the same dim air. Through it all, the drip of the leaves, the hiss of the seeps, the soft slosh of the big bodies shifting weight in the shallows, the tick of small shrimplike creatures in the warm water. Together it makes a single sustained chord that never resolves. You stop hearing it, and then it returns all at once, the way your own pulse does, and you realize it has been the place's heartbeat the whole time.

Here are the rules, so far as the place keeps them. The heat comes from below and always will; the water cycles in place, condensing, dripping, running, evaporating, never imported and never lost; the light has no source and no schedule. Growth everywhere exactly equals decay, birth exactly equals death, at every scale from the bacterial film to the tallest tree. Nothing migrates. Nothing floods. Nothing sheds what it does not immediately replace. The land is too new to erode — the processes of wearing down have simply not engaged, the way a wheel not yet touching the ground has not yet worn — and so it holds its first shapes: hummocks still hummocks, deltas still shapeless, gullies never formed.

Which is the strange thing about the place, once you have been in it long enough to feel it. Nothing here changes, and nothing here stops. It is a wheel spinning perfectly, quickly, in place — all the motion of a living world, none of the travel. The drips fall from the same leaf-tips in the same order. The swarms hang over their same pools like specimens pinned by a careful, absent hand. A long-legged wader stands at one particular spot in one particular pool, and whether it has been the same bird standing there for a hundred years the landscape does not say; it keeps no count.

The valley is an hour old. It has been an hour old for as long as it has been anything. It is a landscape caught in the middle of being born — mid-creation, mid-sentence, the ground still soft, the stone still warm, the first generation of green at its full and final size — and held there, mid-breath, indefinitely, by an equilibrium so exact it feels less like an accident of nature and more like a decision the world once made about this place and has never yet seen fit to revisit.

If anything here could accumulate memory, the mud would be the keeper of it. But the mud forgets as fast as it is written on. Everything that happens has always been happening. The chord plays on. The steam stands. The light does not change, and under it, everything is already home.
