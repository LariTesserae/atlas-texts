You come down into it through the light. For the first stretch the sun still holds — shafts, glitter, the ordinary business of open water — and then it dissolves. Not into darkness. Into pearl. The water here is clouded with a living haze, spores and drifting filaments fine enough that they never fully settle, and the sun overhead comes through it scattered from every direction at once: a sourceless mother-of-pearl glow in which nothing casts a real shadow. Noon is a bright patch in the ceiling, a silver window the size of a town. Night is when that window dims to ink, and then you learn that the ground glows — the fissures, faintly, like banked coals.

The water is warm. That is the first strangeness, the one your body argues with: blood-warm, everywhere, thick as breath. It tastes of iron and sulfur and something faintly sweet, like cut stems. You move through it the way you move through silk. The warmth comes from below, from the land itself, and the land is the second strangeness — because the land is not finished.

The floor of this sea is a highland, still being born. A young volcanic plateau — call it the Rise — hanging a few hundred meters under the light but high above everything around it, so that from its rim you look down long darkening slopes into blue-black distance, the way mountaineers look into a valley. Only the valley is cold. Swim twenty body-lengths down the flank and the warm silk turns to chill, then to winter; the warmth is only a skin, laid over the high ground by the heat still rising out of it. The people here fear the cold the way desert people fear the deep night, and they give their dead to it.

Everything about the rock is new. The crust lies in fat pillow-lobes and cracked plates, grey-blue and scummed with pale bacterial felt. There is no sand. There are no rounded stones, no pebbles, no worn edges anywhere — the sea has not had time to invent smoothness here, and won't for a hundred thousand years. Every surface is angular, fresh, unworn. A single smooth stone is a treasure; the town keeps a few, traded down from some drowned elder range far older than this one, and they are handled like relics.

Where the crust is youngest — the birthing grounds, on the plateau's seaward edge — the skin still steams. Warm mineral smoke stands up out of the fissures in slow shimmering columns, a rain that falls the wrong way, upward, wavering. From a distance the whole field looks like a range of pale fountains, and the town's children learn early to read them: a strong standing column means a fat vein of heat below, a faltering one means the vein is wandering, moving on. Sometimes the ground's skin splits entirely. There is a drumming through the rock, then a hush, then everyone comes to look at the new acre, still soft as porridge, too hot to touch, steaming its first smoke into the water. When the Rise grows, it groans — a long low note that travels the whole plateau, stone to stone, and the people feel it in their chests and mark the day. Their ground is also their bell.

Because the land is warm, it is gardened. The gardens are the reason the water is dim: they spore, endlessly, and their pollen is the pearl haze. Nothing in them eats light. They eat the heat. Ribboned, roped, matted things — white, cream, bone, faint amber, rose where the warm veins run closest to the surface — root themselves along the fissures and spread in tiers, mat over ribbon over rope, a forest laid on its side and stacked to the ceiling of visibility. The gardeners dowse for heat with their palms flat to the ground, following a vein by its warmth the way other farmers follow water, and they plant the way others plant: in rows, in patience, in inheritance. Wealth here is not land. Wealth is warmth — a fat vein under a family's plot, a hearth-vein that has held its place for four generations.

The town sits along the heat lines like iron filings along a magnet's field. It is close-built, terraced, dense: walls of stacked young basalt mortared with mashed spore-mat, roofs of woven ribbon hung to shed the slow snowfall of drifting spores, doorways curtained with fiber. The streets are lines — rope-roads, guide-cords strung wall to wall through the pearl haze — and you travel them hand over hand, and you are never out of arm's reach of something.
