This is the high country of a drowned world. That phrase takes a moment to settle, because there is no dry land anywhere — but altitude here is relative, and this is what remains of up. Everything lower went under first, eons ago; the last of the land was a plateau, and the plateau is what you stand on, or hover above, or drift across. It has been worn so flat, for so long, that it has nearly finished being land at all. Above it lies warm sea, shallow by the standards of oceans, no more than a few hundred meters at the deepest — and so wide, so continental, that it makes weather of its own.

The bedrock is the first thing you understand about this place. It is quartzite and old gneiss polished to the smoothness of a river stone, as if the entire continent were a pebble that spent a very long time in a very large current. Across ten kilometers the surface might rise or fall less than a hand-span. It is not dead flat — nothing is — but the undulations are so gentle they read as moods rather than terrain. The ghost rivers are still legible: darker stone tracing dendritic scribbles across the plain, the fossil drainage of watersheds that had nothing left to shed a hundred million years ago. Those old channels hold the silt now, warm mud that breathes in the day-cycle, and in them the water is different — older, cooler, patient.

Because there are still hills. A handful of them, scattered at continental distances: the last monadnocks, the stubborn remnants of mountains that gave everything else up. Forty, sixty meters proud of the plain, rounded like knuckles, furred to their crowns with hanging gardens. From the top of one, you can see — well, "see" is generous here. The light comes from nowhere in particular.

That is the second thing you understand. There is no sun visible in this water. Light simply arrives, ambient, a green-gold suffusion without a source, as though the sea itself had agreed to be lit. It consents to be day; then, in a slow rhythm no one governs, it consents to dim, and the day is over, and there is only the luminous dark. At night the meadows write in cold light — slow script scrolling through the grass, constellations rooted in place — and this is how the plain keeps time. There is no horizon. Distance here is measured in light, not meters: you can see until the brightness tires, and then there is a soft green-gold haze, and beyond it the place simply declines to be perceived.

The water is warm everywhere. Blood-warm, a little warmer, warmed from above by nobody and from below by the stone itself — the plateau is a warm animal, and over the felt-covered rock the heat shimmer ripples upward in slow refraction. On the monadnock hills there are springs that fume mineral clouds into the sea, staining the water rust and jade for kilometers down the current. Everything you touch has been made comfortable by an amount of time that is difficult to hold in mind.

And the water is not empty between its floor and its ceiling. This is the third understanding: the density. Hold your hand still and within a minute ten small things will settle on it — drifting seeds, spores like snow, fragments of felt, glimmers of planktonic life, grit from the bloom. The sea has a grain to it, like wood. Currents here have memory; the water is layered with the years it has carried, and if you attend you can read it — rings of turbidity, warm braids sliding over cool ones, ribbons of sweetness that taste of iron and of something like pollen. You taste the water and you know what season it is, and what season it was.

The meadow makes up most of the world. Grass of a glassy green stands hip-high across a thousand kilometers, rooted in the felt, bending always in the same lean because the current's habits are older than the grass. Over the meadow stand the tall ones: pale stalks that rise forty meters, leafless, each ending in a slow wheel of blades that turns without any wind, at a speed that is not a speed so much as a decision. And below: layer upon layer, canopy to drift to turf to felt to mat to the anoxic mud, life packed so tightly that the categories blur — every volume is occupied, everything near-touching everything, the saturation of a place that has had forever to fill itself in.

There is almost nothing here that moves on its own intent. That is the strangest absence, once you notice it. No schools, no swarms, no grazing multitudes. The ecosystem has rearranged itself around the vacancy: the currents do the pollinating, the decay does the grazing, the bloom does the herding. And yet there is one animal. Perhaps more precisely, there is *the* animal — a single broad, slow grazer the size of a boat, hide furred with the same felt as the stones, mouth like a plowshare, working the meadow along a route so ancient it has worn itself a hand's depth into the quartzite. The Road. Two ruts, polished, running straight past every horizon. It was not built; it was walked — by the uncounted dead of a lineage that wore their passage into bedrock over a million years of going where they went. The plain keeps its calendar by that Road. The grazer passes, and its wake is a blessing: behind it, for weeks, the grass fruits, the mats fume nutrients, the water goes briefly rich. Sometimes, far off in the haze, you see two of them. You are almost always wrong. The two are the one, or the water has been holding still too long and is practicing the idea of a fish again.

There are other marks. Not many — the world has spent its age erasing them. A single arch still stands out on the plain, furred green, framing nothing, its stone looking less carved than grown or chewed. A circle of pillar-stumps worn to nubs like knuckles. A perfect sphere of granite half-swallowed by the meadow, which the grass handles gently, as if it were an egg. You can go weeks without meeting any mark at all, and then find two in one afternoon, as though the plain had forgotten to keep them apart. Nothing shows a tool's edge. Nothing shows an edge. Whatever made them had no machines, maybe no hands — they might be the metabolic habits of organisms a hundred million years extinct, works accumulated the way a reef is accumulated, by living rather than deciding. The arch might be the keel bone of something that used to live here.

The rules of the place, as far as the place observes them: there is no surface. Swim upward and the water simply continues, thinning in light toward a dark that is not night and not sea, and if you keep going you come back changed, or you come back before, or you do not come back and instead arrive. Time has sediment here. In the deep pockets of the ghost rivers the water is older than the plain's own flatness, and wading into it you feel the years take hold of your limbs like thick cloth — bubbles hang where they are, and yesterday is visible a short way down. Sound can arrive before its cause: some evenings you hear a stone settle that will not settle until tomorrow. And distance is not a plane. You can travel toward the arch all day and be no closer, and then have it appear beside you, politely, as if it had walked around to meet you. At the plateau's far rim — the last cliffs of the last high land — there is a pull that is not current: it is downhill in time, toward the Low, where the oldest water lies like a held secret. Nothing here goes to the rim.

The silence is the fourth understanding, and the slowest to come. Quiet here is not absence; it is a material. The water is so old it has mostly finished carrying sound — the sea absorbed the world's noise over the ages and now holds it, banked. What remains is small and precious: the hinge-creak of grass as the current shifts, the sifting of spore-snow, the tiny bells of dawn bubbles rising off the breathing mats.
