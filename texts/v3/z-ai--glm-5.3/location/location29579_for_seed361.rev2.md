# The Still

## The Level

The mountains here are over.

What remains is a table of black quartzite some three hundred kilometers across, polished by rain that has been falling on it since before there were birds to be rained on. The range that once stood here is not ruined; it is *finished* — ground down, hauled off, dissolved, its最后一 height spent. Geologists call such a surface a peneplain, a near-plain, and stand on it feeling seasick. There is almost nothing left to erode. Every nub that survives has been vetted by ten million years of weather and found too stubborn to bother with.

You are high — high enough that at dawn the clouds arrive from *below*, a gray sea breaking over the rim of the world and draining across the stone in slow ropes. Yet the Level is warm for its height; it sits low under the sun. By ten in the morning the pavement is baking gently underfoot, and the whole plateau smells of hot stone and faintly of tea — the dust is the color of weak tea, and there is a lot of dust, and it is very old.

Rain comes once or twice a summer, an insult of a rain, thirty seconds of fat drops that the Level takes personally. The only reliable water arrives horizontally. Every morning, the fog sea climbs, combs itself across the quartzite, and is wrung out by whatever it touches. Everything that lives here has learned to drink the air.

Almost nothing does. That is the strange thing about the Level. The pavement is not barren — it is *spaced*. Sentinel shrubs stand a long bowshot apart, evenly, as though planted by a patient minimalist. Cushions of plants, each a hand's-width island. Lichen crusts in gray and faint neon. Between the presences: stone, light, and enormous room. You can walk for hours and pass nothing, and feel the emptiness not as absence but as a kind of holding of the breath.

## The Approach

You hear the Room before you see it.

At a kilometer out it is a pressure, a note that seems to come from the ground itself — like the moment before a struck bell decides to be struck. You feel it in your molars first. At half a kilometer, the note acquires texture: a shimmer of high frequencies riding it, thousands of small voices agreeing on something. At two hundred paces there is a green smudge in a fold of the plain, the only green you have seen since morning, and the sound has become weather.

Then a lip of stone, and you look down into the Room.

## The Room

It is a bowl, eighty paces across, with walls the height of a barn — a concert hall that grew instead of being built. The walls are terraced, faintly: someone, at some point, cut stairways and benches into the inner face, and erosion has since ground them down to ripples, a staircase remembered by a stone. The shaded side of the bowl is always a little damp, streaked with dark algae like the inside of an old cistern, because the hollow is a cold sink and every dawn it sweats.

Inside, the green grows in islands. A moss lawn here. A fan of ferns there. An orchid colony clinging to a ledge, roots white as thread. Each island sits a meter or more from its neighbors, evenly spaced, as though the field that permits germination here only permits it at a fixed distance — so the place reads less like a jungle than like a garden kept by someone with a strong sense of proportion and no interest in filling every pot. Lush, and yet airy. Every plant given its own room.

And in among the green, animals — this is the part no one is prepared for. The Room is *crowded* with them, in the open, in daylight: butterflies stacked on wet mud in the thousands, wings opening and closing like stained glass breathing; doves on every ledge; deer at the water; swifts overhead in numbers that give the air a visible grain, like poured tea held to the light. Biologists who visit give up on counting and start weighing; the numbers are always wrong and always large.

## The Still

At the center stands the Still.

It is three meters tall — an inverted pagoda of fluted cones, the gray of pencil lead, utterly seamless, warm as the inside of a hand though by every law of sun and stone it should be too hot to touch. Tap it and you hear it in your bones; it is not stone, and it is not metal, and it has no joints to fail. It does not glow. It carries no symbols, makes no sound of its own beyond a fundamental you feel rather than hear. It is a machine entirely without the theater of machines, and the local goatherds have accordingly never once thought of worshipping it. They sing to it instead, on the practical grounds that it works.

Because that is what it does. It drinks the fog and pours it out as water. Cold water, sweet, carrying a whisper of ozone and coin — droplets condense on its flutes in ropes, run down spiral grooves, pool in bowls, overflow down chutes into a chain of step pools, and from there spill out a short stream that dies a hundred paces into the plain, the Level's only river. The physics are available in fragments — radiative cooling, sky-facing channels that dump heat into the cold of space — but the ledger does not close. There is more water than there is explanation. The missing term, the goatherds say, is the noise.

It was set on a summit once. The summit is gone; the hill it became is gone; the plain they flattened into is this plain. The Still has been exhumed by patience. The land eroded into agreement with it, and now it stands at the bottom of a bowl it never asked for, the oldest stable state of anything anywhere: the argument's surviving term. Nothing marks its age because nothing has ever seen it being built, mended, or broken. The mountain it stood on is a rumor of a rumor.

In its bowls live the chalice newts — a whole species whose entire world is the Still's watercourse. A chalice newt is born in a bowl, matures in the spiral grooves, spawns in the overflow pools, and dies without once touching the ground. They do not know they live inside a machine. It is possible that we don't either.

## The Chorus

The Room is loud the way a heart is loud. You stop apologizing for it and start keeping time.

At the peak of the morning it is arm's-length shouting loud: bell-frogs the size of thumbnails running rounds against each other from the moss islands; clock-cicadas that lock their pulses onto the Still's fundamental with a precision no insect brain should manage, so the entire wall of shrill sound *beats*; doves cooing the offbeat; ten thousand swifts screaming in the updraft off the rim; the drips from the flutes ticking against it all. The bowl is the soundboard. Walk the rim and you can hear the standing waves interfere — the volume swells and thins with your steps like sound through a shell.

And under everything, felt in the sternum, the Still's own note: not heard, exactly. Held.

The strangest verified fact on the Level is this: the output tracks the chorus. When a hawk's shadow starters the frogs into silence — the goatherds call it the hush test — the dew visibly slackens on the flutes within seconds. Sing, and it runs. A lone traveler at the lip humming into the bowl will gather, in one held note, a minute's worth of extra rain. Fog goes in; water, sound, and somewhere in that exchange the machine takes its cut in the only currency it has ever accepted. It condenses the fog with the noise of the animals it waters. The loop is closed, elegant, ancient: water for life, life for sound, sound for water.

Once or twice a day, without warning, the entire chorus drops to nothing at once. Nine full seconds. The frogs hang open-mouthed; the swifts bank; the cicadas hold their beat somewhere you can't locate. Then it all comes back, on the beat, as if a page had been turned. The goatherds call this the Listening and will not say more about it.

## The Truce

Inside the rim, nothing eats anything.

The calm is a property of the air near the Still, the way warmth is a property of sunlight. It starts at the lip — you feel it as a soft click behind the sternum, a drop in blood pressure, an unaccountable willingness to forgive people — and every animal is born knowing exactly where it ends. Inside the line, a gray fox sleeps in the ferns with a hundred cavies breathing around it like a rumor of prey. A hawk drinks at the pool's edge between the doves, wings hanging loose. The bear comes down the scarp stair at dusk and the little deer do not even lift their heads, only shift their hips to make room.

Just beyond the line, the patient ones sit in the wall's shadow: the hawk on a nub, the fox at the notch, the plateau cat pretending with total commitment to be a rock. They wait the way an audience waits at intermission. The meat line is hard, knowable, and fixed — a geography of mercy that has been drawn on this spot for longer than there have been species to object to it — and it is renewed every morning like the dew.

The fauna of the Level accordingly treats the Room as its capital. Goldbeard bees work the orchids. Glasswing butterflies puddle by the thousands at the mineral crusts — orange and turquoise bole at the seeps — licking salts from the mud. At dusk the net-bats come off the scarp in a spiral visible from a kilometer, funneling down the bowl like water going backward. Then come the tinsel moths, drawn up out of the empty plain: their wings carry a metallized sheen, built mineral by mineral from the Still's overflow, and when one misses a landing and hits the stone, it rings, very faintly, like a dropped coin.

And there is one bird — the hearkener, a plain gray thrush — that sings the Still's note note-perfect, and nothing else. Its whole species' range is the nine basins of the Level, most of them dead. It still makes its rounds.

## The Others

The Room is the eighth of nine basins scattered across the plain, a morning's walk apart, and the others are its grief.

Most are empty now — a socket scar in the rock, a bowl of bare quartzite, terraces worn to ripples around nothing. Two still hold their Stills: husks. Cold to the touch, silent, their bowls bone-dry, drifted with tea-colored dust and the ghost-roots of orchids that died with their water. You can sit inside a dead machine and hear nothing to the horizon, and understand in your body what the Room is *for*.

Weathering out of the husk basins come gray beads the size of peas — the material of the Still in its only perishable form. Or its seeds; the scarp villagers say seeds, and thread them for their children, and trade them for luck. No bead has ever sprouted. That is not the same as never.

Of the builders, nothing but the work: boundary stones ground to ankle height, a road that is now a rumor of flatness, and, around the living Room, the faintest human strata — knapped flint from someone centuries gone, a crescent herder's wall, a battered tin cup left on a ledge with bees drinking from its rim, faded ribbon ties on the sentinel shrubs. Recent, all of it. Scratches on a very old table.

## One Day

Before dawn the fog sea comes up over the rim and the Still drinks. The chorus builds with the light — round by round, species by species, the way an orchestra assembles itself — until the drinking hour, when everyone is in the room at once: swifts on the updraft, doves rotating through the pools, the deer descending the ghost stair, the bear last and largest, and the butterflies puddling in their stained-glass thousands. You could shout and not be heard by your own companion.

By ten the fog sea sinks, the sky becomes enormous, and the heat settles. Cicadas hold the noon; the animals doze in the calm. Afternoons, sometimes, a storm walks over the scarp and rains thirty fat seconds on the hollow, and the chorus does not even pause — rain is ordinary here in a way dew is not; the Room distrusts the sky's charity and prefers to wring its own. Dusk brings the bat funnel, the moth hour, the tinsel coins ringing. Then night, when the frogs own the dark entirely and the Still's fundamental rises through the soles of your feet, and the Level, so silent all day everywhere else, is never once silent here.

## What Is Known

Six things are known for sure about the Room:

One: water goes in as fog and comes out as more water than the fog held.
Two: the output tracks the chorus. Silence the animals and the dew slackens; sing, and it runs.
Three: inside the rim, nothing hunts. The calm is in the air like warmth is in light, and everything alive knows where it ends.
Four: nothing erodes the Still. Everything else on the Level is being erased; it is what the argument is made of.
Five: a dead Still makes a desert, and the two husk basins are proof, and the hearkener still goes basin to basin singing the note to the empty ones.
Six: it has never been seen to be built, mended, or to fail — so no one knows how old it is, only that the mountain it was set on is gone, and the fog keeps coming, and the frogs keep answering.

If you stand at the lip at night and hum — people do; the goatherds do it every morning — the dew gathers faster on the flutes, visibly, a minute's rain in a breath. It is polite, they say. And it works.
