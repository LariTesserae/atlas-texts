# The Temper

You come down through a hole in the roof of the world.

There is a break in the black fields of the mountain's flank where the ground gave way into a chamber beneath, and someone long ago widened it, braced it, and cut a stair into the rubble so that now you descend through a shaft of daylight into a tunnel that was — within living memory — a river of fire. The oldest woman in town remembers standing at this skylight as a child, watching the glow beneath the crust go out like a banked fire. The rock has been cooling ever since. The town lives on the cooling.

The first thing that reaches you is not light but breath: air at blood heat, thick with moisture in patches, smelling of hot stone, lamp gas, soap, and bread. The second thing is the shape of the place. You are standing in a tube a little wider than a cathedral aisle, an arched ceiling of stone ropes frozen mid-ripple, going off in both directions until the lamps give out. Six hundred yards of it, most of it one unbroken hallway, swelling twice into wider rooms where the flow once pooled. That is the whole territory. A town the length of a street.

## The walls

Everything here is young, and youth in stone means sharpness. The walls wear a glassy glaze where the lava polished its own channel; the ceiling hangs with blunt teeth of drip-stone formed as the molten roof sagged and stretched; the floor is the frozen riverbed itself, wrinkled and ropy, its ridges never yet softened by anything. Chisel marks from forty years of cutting still look fresh enough to have been made last week. Nothing in the Temper has had time to round off. Edges cut cloth. Doorframes keep the tooth of the drill that squared them. The place has the crispness of something just unwrapped.

## The town itself

The settlement stacks against the warm walls like barnacles on a hull. Three tiers of brick galleries ride on iron brackets driven into the basalt — homes partitioned with riveted plate and canvas doors, front porches of plank hanging over the street, laundry lines strung beneath the walkways so nothing drips on anyone but everyone. Pipes run everywhere the people run: over railings, under staircases, through the very apartments, threaded so thickly that in places you walk in a tunnel of pipe with a corridor inside it. Cold mains. Hot returns. Gas lines. The density is not clutter; it is a body — the town is so packed with plumbing and gallery and tenement that the spaces between are near touching, and the whole thing reads as one organism whose organs are visible.

Tram rails run the length of the floor, and a rope-driven cart hauls goods from the head house to the deep rooms. The street itself is the old riverbed. You walk on the flow.

## Water

There is no rain underground, so the Temper makes its own weather. Cold brine is circulated down from the night air of the surface through long iron mains, and the moment those cold pipes enter the hot halls, they sweat. The town harvests its own condensation. Every main wears a channel beneath it, chiseled into the floor, and the channels gather into cisterns sunk in the low rooms. Households hold drip allotments the way farmers hold water rights — this section of gutter, these hours. When a cold snap passes over the mountain, the ceiling sweats too, and the Temper gets an indoor rain: people set out pans, children stand under the seeps, the gutters run loud for a night. In the floor's hollows, seepage gathers into tepid black pools rimmed with mineral bloom, white and sulfur-yellow, the only standing water for miles in every direction.

There is a gauge for all this: an iron bar hung in the central room that beads with condensation hours before a sweat comes. They call it the rainstick, and it is consulted like scripture.

## Heat and the works

The heat is the town's mineral deposit. Boreholes sunk through the floor open into rock still close to its birth temperature, and the town has capped them with stove-grates and jack-ovens. Everything the Temper sells is a product of that heat: springs and blades annealed in borehole ovens until their stress is gone, glass tempered slow and true, bread baked in ovens that need no fuel at all, laundry boiled for the mountain villages in water that costs nothing to heat. The great slow engine — the Granny, they call her — exists mostly to pump *cold*, the scarcer commodity. She breathes once every forty seconds, all day, all night, and the whole town sets its pulse by her.

## Light and sound

The light is a permanent dusk of amber. Gas jets on iron brackets, carbide lamps on the walkers, and in the deep room the dull red glow of the stove-vents, like hearths seen through a grate. The far ends of the tube simply cease into black. The Temper is a lamplit page in a long dark book.

And it is quiet — deeply, strangely quiet — because the town's many processes are mostly slow ones. The Granny's breath. The tick of stone as it cools, contracting joint by joint, and sometimes a deep knock from below, from a seam still settling its debts, loud enough to stop conversation. The drips. The drips deserve their own sentence: the tinsmiths long ago tuned the gutters — bent the spouts, set the pans at depths — so that the condensation falling through the halls lands in a scale, five notes, scattered along the tube, playing at random, endlessly, like a musician half asleep. Visitors find it maddening for a day and then never notice it again, and after that they miss it when they leave.

A whisper at the head house can be heard at the deep end. So the rule is low voices. Bells speak in patterns. The town murmurs.

## Green, and what is absent

Green exists here only where someone pays for it. Moss was seeded along the condensation gutters decades ago and now draws bright lines through the dark — read the moss and you can map the plumbing like a circuit diagram. Ferns keep to wall niches where a household spends its drip-share on them. In the best-lit alcove of the central room stands a fig in a barrel, nick-named the Mayor, because everyone visits it, everyone knows it, and it has opinions about the weather. Wealth in the Temper is measured in moss.

But the strangest thing, which locals no longer notice and visitors never stop noticing: there are no animals. None. No rats in a town of granaries, no bats in a perfect cave, no flies on the laundries, no moths in the wool, no birds, no beetles, nothing that walks or crawls or wings. Leave bread out for a week and it doesn't get eaten; it just dries. The founders enforced this at the start — quarantine at the head house, boots scraped, goods fumigated, nothing alive admitted on the hoof or wing — and somehow it has held. The silence of no vermin is a luxury. It is also a wrongness you feel in your spine. The only warm-blooded things in the Temper are the townsfolk, and they barely count as wildlife.

## The rules

The Temper's laws are few and physical. The day is inverted: when the mountain's night cools the surface, cold air falls down the stair and the halls become livable, so the bells ring *cool-in* and the town wakes, works, gossips, and lights its lamps, then sleeps through the hot hours in the sump rooms. Heat is a commons, metered by the stove-keeper in borehole-hours. Drips are property. Voices stay low. And never drill past the third seam — below the third seam, the miners say, the rock is still in a bad mood.

Sometimes they are proven right. Some days the deep heat breathes upward — the rainstick sweats early, the barometer climbs, fog forms on the mains, and the bells ring the Flare. Then the ovens shut, the ferns get wrapped in wet sacking, the fig is wheeled into a sump, and the whole town waits out a fever in the cold rooms, listening to the stone knock and mutter beneath them, like a household during a storm.

## The long cooling

Understand, finally, what the town lives inside: not a place but a process. The gauge drops a hair every year. The tube is a hearth going out over centuries, and the Temper rides the cooling curve downhill, drilling deeper each decade to stay on it, drawing every comfort from a fire it did not light and cannot renew. One day, two or three generations on, the walls will be merely cool, and the town will have to decide what to be then.
