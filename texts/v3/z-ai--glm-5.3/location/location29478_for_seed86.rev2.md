You hear it before you find it. On the plain above — a cool, wind-brushed stretch of young basalt where the black rock lies in slabs and ropes like something poured and abandoned — there are holes in the ground that breathe a low, continuous murmur up into the grass. A hum with texture in it. A rustle under the hum, like rain falling somewhere it isn't raining. From a hundred meters you might think water. Closer, you realize it is voices. Thousands per second, too small and too many to separate.

Then you find the skylight, and you go down into it.

The hole is a collapsed roof-section of a lava tube, a roughly circular pit maybe eight meters across, its rim fringed with moss and a collar of small ferns that stop exactly where the light does. A rope has run over the same basalt lip for a very long time; the stone there is worn into a groove, glassy-smooth, polished by generations of hemp and hide. You descend through a gray shaft of daylight, past rubble fused into rust-colored scab, and your feet meet the floor of the tube itself.

And the tube goes on. That is the first thing it tells you, once you are in: it goes on past any guess. The passage runs ten, twelve meters wide under an arched ceiling eight meters up, and it runs in both directions until the darkness takes it — several kilometers downslope of the volcano that vomited it, braided here and there into side loops and double levels where the flow once split around itself. From inside, it is less a cave than a valley turned inside out: a long interior country with its own weather, its own floor, its own far-off horizons that you cannot see but can hear.

The rock is young. That is the second thing. Nothing here has been softened. The floor is frozen pahoehoe — ropes and toes of lava locked mid-flow, blisters, lobes, glass chips that ring underfoot like dropped crockery. The ceiling bristles with shark-tooth drips of stone where the last skin of the flow sagged and cooled, and the walls keep a blue-black glassy rind, mineral and faintly sheened, edges still sharp enough to open cloth. There is no dust film on anything, no rounding, no patience of ages. Geologically the tube is an infant — a few thousand years at most — and it looks it: crisp, clean, new-minted.

The light is dim, and it is rationed. Every few hundred meters a skylight drops its shaft of pale gray through the ceiling, and in each shaft the air is full of drifting motes — guano dust, spores, wings — turning slowly like snow in water. Under each shaft grows the tube's entire vegetable world: a moss collar, some ferns, a greenish film of algae on wet rock, a few seedlings that never get past the first weeks before the crickets find them. That is the vegetation of the place — small green islands in a mineral sea, each one ending at the exact circumference of its beam. Between the shafts the darkness is total and black, and the tube is known there by ear.

Because sound is what this place runs on. The tube is a resonating pipe: a dropped pebble rings for four seconds. Cricket song bounces and overlaps until the walls themselves seem to chirp. And everywhere, under everything, there is the murmur — a sea-noise, distant surf, audible a kilometer from its source — which is the colony.

The bats. They hang from the ceiling in pockets and crevices, layer over layer, millions of free-tails packed among the stone drips until the roof looks like black thatch that breathes. Disturb them and a ripple travels through the thatch like wind over grain. Their body-heat rises in a warm current you can feel with a raised hand, a soft roof of mammal warmth hanging under the cold stone. Their smell comes before them and stays after: ammonia-sweet, dusty, musky.

Where the roost is densest — the lower chamber, where the tube deepens — the floor has become something that is not exactly floor anymore. Guano has rained down for thousands of generations and been processed and rained upon again, and the result is a cone, meters deep, whose apex sits directly under the densest ceiling. It is warm. That is the strangest touch in a cool cave: dig a hand in a few centimeters and the heap is compost-hot, generating its own climate, steaming faintly at the nose. And the cone's surface *moves*. It has a skin of beetles. Dermestids, mites, pale larvae, fungus-gnats — a continuous working population that turns the deposit over grain by grain, and their collective rustle is one of the tube's standing noises: like frying at a distance, like rain on paper, audible meters away.

So the cave's density is inverted, and this is its third lesson. Almost all of its space is empty — long reaches of bare, ringing, glassy basalt, cool and dry and dark, with nothing in them but echoes and the slow tick of condensation dripping. Then life occurs, and when it occurs it is overwhelming: the ceiling thatch, the boiling cone, the cricket-papered walls, the moth that lands to drink at a drip-hole where a single bead of condensation falls every eleven seconds into the same small dark pool it has always filled. Between the oases, silence and black. The emptiness and the crowd define each other.

Cool, by the way, is the tube's one weather. Cave-cool everywhere, cellar-frosted — cold enough that the ceiling near the skylights sweats, where outside air meets stone, and the beads gather and fall in fixed drip-lines that have fixed landing-spots, each one a tiny pond for gnats and moths, each one a metronome. Warm pockets exist only where life makes them: under the roost, in the cone's compost heart. There is no season down here. No frost, no flood, no gradient over time. The drips land where they have always landed. The cricket choruses hold their pitch. The system runs at one speed and has always run at that speed.

The rules, then, are legible:

*Everything inside is downstream of the bats.* The colony flies out at dusk and returns at dawn, and every night it carries the outside world in — the insects of a hundred square kilometers of plain, taxed nightly, rendered into guano, fed to beetle and mite and fungus, and fungus fed to gnat, and all of it finally to gas and warmth. The tube is a stomach. Its size and its emptiness are digestive.

*Light rules where green can live; water rules where damp can live; sound rules everything else.* In the deep passages you navigate as the bats do — by ear, by air-current, by smell. Eyes are only good in the shafts.

*Nothing changes, but everything moves.* The exodus is the tube's heartbeat, not its history: every dusk, the skylights fill with a rising whirlpool of bodies pouring out like smoke run backward, and every dawn they pour home in the same spiral, and the warmth rebuilds under the roost precisely as it cooled. The cone deepens by a hair's width a year. That is the only arrow of time here, and it is nearly invisible.

*One animal has learned stillness.* At the cone's edge, at the boundary between the beetles' jurisdiction and the bare rock, a snake waits — the only motionless creature in the whole loud country — for the bats that fall or fumble at dusk. Everything else here moves, or rustles, or drips. The snake is the period at the end of the cave's sentence.

And people come, though they have built nothing. Guano-harvesters come in through the skylights with woven baskets, digging-sticks, fiber ropes, low resin torches; they take only from the cone's outer apron, working fast with cloth over their faces because of the ammonia, and they carry their tools in and out again. What they leave is not construction but *wear*: the rope-groove on the lip, soot-ghost patches on the ceiling near the entrance, a foot-polished path across the ropy floor, a scatter of dropped fiber, and once, once, a knapped flake of chert lying on the cone's flank, being slowly swallowed by beetles and time. The cave owns them the way it owns everything they bring: completely, and without acknowledgment.

If you stay to dusk, you feel the hour before you hear it. The murmur thickens. The thatch begins to shiver. And then the ceiling opens from beneath like a hive broken sideways, and the tube exhales its millions through the skylight above you in a rising spiral that takes an hour and sounds like the whole mountain turning over — and then, when the last of them is out over the dark plain, the tube settles back into its long dim murmur, its crickets, its frying beetles, its metronome drips, and waits for dawn, which it has waited for, identically, every night since the rock cooled.
