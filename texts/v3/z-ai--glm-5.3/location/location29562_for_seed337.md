# The Sinking City

There is a valley here, except no river ever carved it. The people did — are doing, still, have been doing for nine generations and expect their grandchildren to continue. What began as a quarry became a hollow, the hollow became a bowl, and the bowl is becoming, by patient increments of stone chip and sweat, a city that descends. From the rim, you look down into it the way you'd look down a well: terraces spiraling inward, hundreds of doorways cut into the living rock, smoke rising from hearths whose fires are tended a hundred feet below the original surface of the earth.

The place is young in the way that geological things are young, which is to say it has the unfinished, provisional look of raw geology interrupted. Every wall shows its strata — pale bands of limestone, a rust-colored seam of iron-stained rock, a dark layer like a horizon line running through everything. The people have not yet learned to finish surfaces. Doorways are rough-cut, their lintels still bearing the point-marks of the picks that made them. Stairways are chiseled directly into the terraces, worn only a little at the edges, because most of the feet that use them are young feet. Whole districts exist as intent rather than architecture: a flat area where someone has begun leveling a floor for houses not yet started, rows of post-holes augered into rock and waiting for wooden posts that would have to be carried down from the distant treeline, an entire terrace that is currently just an idea marked by lines of white chalk.

Down, down, down — because this is the defining rule of the place, the single law everyone knows: **the city grows downward and never up.** The rim is the oldest and the bottom is the newest, which means the whole settlement is inverted in time. At the rim live the founding families in wide, high, ancient rooms whose walls have been smoothed by nine generations of hands. At the bottom, near the mist, the newest arrivals cut their single-cell dwellings into raw rock and sleep in the noise of the digging, which never fully stops — someone is always extending the floor of the world.

That floor is the deep place, the reason for all of it. The excavation follows water. Somewhere beneath — no one has reached it yet, but every seep, every damp streak down the raw walls, every morning's condensation says it is close — there is an aquifer, a underground river, a vein of the world's blood. The diggers follow the wetness like miners following ore. They lick the rock faces to taste for it. They press their palms against freshly exposed stone at dawn to feel for coolness. The oldest prayer in the city, older than any of the terrace gods, is simply: *let us not dig past it, let us dig into it.*

The damp is everywhere in the meanwhile, the whole place marinated in intermittent moisture. Cool air pours down the terraced walls and meets the warmer breath rising from hundreds of hearths, and the meeting makes mist — every morning the bottom third of the city fills with a soft white standing fog, knee-deep, hip-deep, and the lower-city dwellers walk through it to their work, their torches burning half-hidden like swamp lights. Water condenses on ceilings and runs down in thread-thin rills that everyone routes carefully along channels they've scratched into their floors, into stone basins, into the common cisterns. There is one true surface stream, thin and bright, that enters through a crack in the north wall midway down and falls in a delicate ribbon to the lower pools. Around those pools, everything that is green in this place is gathered.

And it is not much green — the city is rock and smoke and skin. But the water-gardens at the bottom are the settlement's second heart: terraced basins of pale moss cultivated like a crop, beds of a transplanted fern that the founders carried here as spores in their hair and clothing and which now grows nowhere else in the world, and sheds — actual built sheds, warm and dark and humid — where fungus farming has become the local high technology. The fungus masters know their strains the way other cultures know their cattle bloodlines. The food of the city is fungus, fern shoots, dried pigeon, the small blind fish netted from the pools, and grain traded up from the surface villages who buy, of all things, the city's stone.

Stone is what they have. Stone is what they are, in every sense. The tools are of simple metal — soft, green-tinged copper beaten out over stone anvils, and more commonly knapped chert and heavy antler picks — and every tool exists to modify more rock. Whole families specialize: there are dynasties of splitters who read the grain of a face before touching it, dynasties of haulers whose children learn the switchback stair-runs before they learn to speak properly, one small guild of polishers who are considered slightly sacred because they alone deal in finished things, in smoothness, in the future tense.

The sound of the place is what strikes visitors hardest, after the vertigo of the rim. It is quiet — a held-breath quiet, the quiet of people working with hand-tools in an amphitheater of stone. The chipping of picks comes from everywhere and nowhere, muffled and multiplied by echo into something like the sound of sleet on a roof. Voices are kept low; sound carries absurdly here, and the culture has responded by growing soft-spoken. The market on the seventh terrace conducts most of its business in a murmur and a system of hand-signs. Children are shushed not because noise is bad but because your noise is *everyone's* noise. When the evening fog-trumpet sounds from the rim — a long, low note blown through a drilled horn of stone, announcing the mist is coming early — the whole city seems to inhale.

The light is the mercy of the place. Because it is a pit and not a true cave, sun enters. For most of the day only the upper terraces bathe in it; but at noon, when the sun is high enough, a shaft of full light descends all the way to the bottom, striking the pools and mist and turning the lower city briefly, daily, golden — and for that hour the mist burns off and the ferns unfurl and everyone, even the splitters, pauses to stand in it. The old people say the founders chose the shape of the excavation for exactly this: a cone with the math of noon built into its walls. The younger diggers are less sure the ancestors could calculate. But the light comes anyway, punctual, and no one argues with that.

Fauna is scarce and treasured accordingly. The pigeons that nest in the upper cracks and are half-tame, harvested for meat and feather. The blind white fish in the pools, no longer than a finger, which the children are forbidden to catch in certain months for reasons the elders phrase as taboo and the mushroom masters privately admit is just good management. A species of small brown moth that swarms the torches after dark and is, in its caterpillar stage, occasionally eaten as a delicacy and otherwise ignored. Once, a hawk got in, and the story of the panic it caused is still told at every hearth.

What is the place becoming? Even its own people aren't sure. Some of the founders' descendants speak of a finished city — the whole pit terraced, roofed at the rim perhaps, the great water found and crowned with a temple. Others, especially the diggers at the bottom, have begun to say something stranger: that they will not stop at the water. That the water is a door. That people came out of the ground once, in the old stories, and there is no rule that says they cannot go back in, if the ground is made welcoming enough.

Either way, the picks are ringing right now, somewhere down in the mist, patient and multiplied and quiet, and the floor of the world is one chisel-mark lower than it was yesterday.
