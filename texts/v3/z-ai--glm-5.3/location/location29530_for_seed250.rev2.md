The sea is high. Not mountain-lake high — continent high. It lies on a tabletop of young crust, so far above the planet's datum that the air over it is thin and the light lands hard, shadows with edges like cut paper. On clear days the horizon bends visibly away from you, and the cloud towers of distant storms assemble below your eye level, rising over open water the way a city rises two streets away. There are no lowlands in view. In most directions there is no shore at all. The water goes on for thousands of kilometers, and its only land is the land it is making.

You notice the temperature before anything else, if you come to it from above: an autumn chill riding on air that smells of nothing but water and stone. No pollen, no leaf-oil, no animal musk. The sea holds about eight degrees, and the sky above it a little less, and the chill is honest — it has no wind-chill tricks, no frost. It is simply the cool of a place that has not yet learned to be warm for anyone.

The water is glass-clear, and it is not empty — it is *loaded*. Every liter carries a dissolved freight of calcium and carbonate and finer mineral dust held in a suspension too perfect to be natural. Forty meters down, the sunlight still stands in columns, and through those columns the sea is snowing. White grains fall in slow veils, too evenly spaced, too well sorted, drifting down onto the shelves where the actual work of this place is going on.

Because nothing here erodes. That is the first rule, and everything else follows from it. This is a sea with its arrow reversed. The shelves and terraces of pale aragonite grow out of the floor at the speed of coral or faster — a centimeter a day, a finger's width in a week — and the fresh stone is warm to the touch. Setting gives off heat. Newborn shelves steam faintly in the cool air where they break the surface, and if you put your ear to young rock you hear it tick and chime as it cools, like engine metal. Old stone, generations older, is cold and quiet. Age here is a temperature.

In the lee of the growing shelves, where the current slows, the sea plants its groves. They look like white saplings, thin as wands, branching, rigid — but they are not plants, because nothing here is alive yet. They are accretions, mineral growths sown in rows so straight you want to believe the current made them and cannot quite. These are the sparse vegetation of the place: groves of stone-weed on a sea floor being built, standing in drifts of falling white snow, with nothing to graze them, nothing to bend them, no insect on them, no fish among them. Nothing in this entire ocean has learned to eat.

And below the sunlit zone the water goes navy, then ink, and the shelves march down into trenches where the sea is four kilometers deep and the pressure makes the cold feel like a hand. Here, in the dark, runs the second half of the project: resorption zones, where stone that failed is unwritten. A shelf that grows too fast, too greedy, founders under its own weight and sinks, and the deep water — chemically hungry, tuned to dissolve — takes it back apart, molecule by molecule, and lifts the freight upward again in slow cold rivers to feed new growth somewhere else. The sea drafts and redrafts. It builds a thousand shelves to keep a hundred. Creation and destruction are not in balance here; destruction is simply construction's other hand.

Above the surface, the stirring mechanism lives: weather. This sea is large enough to make its own climate, and it does, relentlessly. Warm patches of newborn stone heat their own columns of air; squalls are born over them in the morning and spend the afternoon crossing open water, and by evening the anvil tops stand on the horizon in ranks. The rain, when it comes, arrives as a wall you can see approaching — a gray continent moving at walking pace, and then it is on you, and the roar is enormous. Rain on water is one of the loudest sounds in nature, and here it falls on three thousand kilometers of water at once. The top meter of the sea churns white. Thunder rolls unbroken for a minute and a half, no hills to stop it. Underwater, the storms are a muffled drumming felt in the sternum, and beneath that runs the deeper sound: a permanent infrasonic hum, basin resonating against basin, the whole ocean a bell that no one struck.

The rain is feedstock. That is the second rule. It falls saturated — the clouds have been seeded with the same freight the sea carries — so every storm is a delivery, every squall a truck arriving with materials. Where the rain lands, deposition accelerates; the shelves under storm tracks grow measurably faster, and their terraces step upward in ridges that record the weather like tree rings, if trees kept diaries of thunderstorms.

The third rule: the engineering is in the water, and nowhere else. There are no machines. No structures, no cables, no lights, no hulls, no buoys, no birds, no boats — the surface of this sea has never been broken by a fin or a keel. But the chemistry is too coherent to be chance. The saturation never slips, not in the trenches, not in the gyres. Deposition happens exactly where the currents slow and nowhere else, and the currents themselves are steered — by salinity gradients tuned like wiring, by temperature differentials doing the work of voltage, by mineral loads so precisely layered that the whole ocean behaves as a single circuit with no components. Somewhere, or somewhen, there is a plan with a tolerance no instrument you own could measure. The technology of this place is indistinguishable from oceanography. Perhaps that is the definition of the technology.

The strangenesses are small and they are all the same strangeness: intention without inhabitants. The groves sown in rows. The snow falling in veils too regular for turbulence. The rain landing a half-degree warmer than the sea it falls into, the way a courier is warmer than the doorstep. The sense, standing on a newborn shelf at dusk, that you are being scheduled around.

And the emptiness. That may be the hardest part to convey. Density here is almost nothing per volume — the shelves are rare, presences spaced a day's travel apart across open water, and between them the sea is vacancy stacked on vacancy: the vacant sky, the vacant light-zone, the vacant dark. You could drift for a week and meet one shelf, steaming gently in the cool air, already sown with its first white grove. The ecologies of this world are entirely chemical: supersaturation as the sun at the bottom of the food chain, nucleation as the pioneer, the shelves as reefs built and waiting for tenants who have not been written yet. This is not a dead sea. Nothing here has died. It is an unbuilt one — a house framed, plumbed, weather-sealed, its fields fertilized, and no tenant in it anywhere, because tenancy is a later chapter.

Storms end the way they began, from the inside out. The wall of rain thins to silver, then to scattered drops loud as stones on the new terraces, and then comes the sound underneath everything: the ticking. Thousands of square kilometers of young rock, cooling and setting and growing in the after-rain, all of it ticking and chiming at once — the entire sea floor ticking like a bed of embers, the whole place a verb. Somewhere in the deepening dark, out past the last visible shelf, something new breaks the surface with a sound like a held breath released, and steams, and stands up into the cool air for the first time.

The world is being written here, in stone, at the speed of weather. The silence when it finishes a sentence is the loudest you have ever heard, because it isn't silence. It is the sound of the next line being mixed.
