Come up the rack railway from the lowlands and the cloud deck swallows you at the mid-station, a thousand meters below the rim. The carriage lamps come on at noon. When you top out onto the Shelf, the light doesn't return — it only changes grain: from the grey of being inside a cloud to the grey of standing above one, inside a bowl nine kilometers wide, filled edge to edge with made things, all of it glistening, almost none of it still.

This is the Gathering Works. It sits at the altitude where clouds are not a ceiling but a tide. Below the rim, a sea of stratus goes on for a hundred kilometers, lapping at the peaks; above it, the basin holds a second, thinner grey that presses down like a lid you could touch with a ladder. Twice a day the lowlands heat up, the deck rises, and fog pours over the southwestern gap — the Notch — in a slow spill that takes an hour to cross the floor and another three to be drunk. Fog is not the weather here. Fog is the crop.

Look down and you'll notice you haven't stood on ground since the border. The works has no natural substrate; soil is manufactured in the digesters — spent filter-moss rotted with willow chip into a black loam that exists nowhere outside the basin — and it only ever arrives in beds, trays, and gutter-nurseries bolted to something else.
