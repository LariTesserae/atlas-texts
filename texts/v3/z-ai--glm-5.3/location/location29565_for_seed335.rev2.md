# The Sump

It is the size of a large room and you would walk past it. That is the whole trick of the place — the Sump sits in a fold of low ground where a moraine slumps into a black fen, and until you are three steps from its lip, it is just more reeds, more gray sky, more of the long wet cold that this country is made of.

Then the ground drops away, and you are looking down into a collapsed bowl maybe forty meters across, floored with standing water the color of steeped tea. Below the water, unmistakably, machinery.

Not ruins in the ordinary sense. Nothing built here, nothing bolted and abandoned by hands — the params insist on that and the params are right. The machinery *was exposed* here, and recently. A flood two springs ago — snowmelt dammed behind an ice lens, then released all at once — scoured out this hollow in a single violent night, stripped the peat away like wet paper, and left the bowl with its walls still raw and vertical, young, knife-cut, trickling. The watercourses along the fresh faces haven't found their patience yet. They run straight where older streams would have meandered.

And under the peat, all along this coast of the fen, there has always been *this*: a lattice of metal the size of a factory floor, canted at ten degrees, half-drowned now. Vessels like tanks without entrances. Cylinders in ranks. Conduits thick as a man's waist, running from cylinder to cylinder and down into darkness below the waterline. No seams visible. No hatches, no writing, no wear that says anyone ever touched it — it reads less like a ruin than like a *cast* of industry, a mold something industrial was poured out of. The flood didn't break it. It just uncovered it, the way a wave uncovers a shipwreck, and the water came in through the same gap and pooled on top, and stayed.

The metal does not rust. It *weeps* instead — everywhere on the exposed faces, slow beads of clear water push through the surface and crawl down to rejoin the pool. In the cold dimness (the sun gets over the rim perhaps two hours a day this season, and even then arrives tired, diluted through cloud) the beads catch what light there is and the whole lattice glitter-chimes, faintly, constantly, wet bells in the fog.

Because it is not silent. This is the loudest thing in the fen and it is not even loud, exactly — it is *close*. A deep cyclic throb under everything, once every eleven seconds, felt in the shins before it is heard, like a pump prime that never resolves into running. Then a long hiss of gas or steam escaping somewhere below the water, rising as a thermal column that shivers the mist off the pool for a moment. Then silence, then the throb again. The birds have learned the rhythm and build their whole day on it.

And the birds are the other half of the place. The bowl is young, wet, warm-side-of-cold from whatever runs beneath it, and absurdly rich — algae blooming in the shallows, midge clouds thick enough to taste, sweet decaying matter in every fresh-cut wall. So it has been *colonized*, fast and dense, this pocket ecosystem running at triple speed. Reeds standing in the shallows where they shouldn't have rooted yet, rooted. Moss racing down the raw scar walls in green drips. A heronry — eleven nests — hanging on a conduit framework over the water, stacked like apartments, because the lattice offers better perch-footing than any living branch in the fen. The herons fish the pool, which is boiling with small dark fish that came in with the flood and now breed year-round in the machine's warmth. Snipe drum over the rim at dusk. A fox has her kits in the gap between two of the great cylinders, dry, sheltered, precisely kit-sized, and she moves through the Sump like a landlord.

They have all simply ignored the fact of the machinery, the way animals ignore a cliff or a boulder. It is terrain. Its throb is weather. Its warmth is a property of the ground, like a spring. And there is no one, anywhere, who claims it. No road leads here, no path even — the fen itself is empty country, hours of nothing between presences, and this pocket inside it is the emptiest-feeling of all despite its crowd, because the crowd is all small life and the *big* life — whoever made all this metal — is absent in a way that feels more like *not yet* than like *gone*.

Near the middle of the pool, where the water turns from tea to ink, one conduit ends in an open flange, down-facing, and from it, in the dim hours, a faint orange light moves. Not shining. Being *carried*. Something down in the lattice is walking the pipes with a lamp, or glowing, or both, and its passage is what makes the throb hesitate, some nights, by a half-second.

The herons don't care. The fox pauses, then goes on. This is the place's only rule, and everything here obeys it without question: the Sump is occupied, its occupant is patient, and the surface world is not invited in — which is fine, because the surface world has not noticed it exists.
