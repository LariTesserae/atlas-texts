**The Unnumbered Room**

The last numbered floor is SL-3. Below it, behind a condenser bank whose coils breathe warm air into the cold, there is a hatch with the stencil **SL-4** and nothing after it — no digit, just painted space where a digit will go. The crawlway beyond is a throat of cable bundles, fat and braided like roots, and the deeper you go the colder and quieter and dimmer it gets, until the only sound is your own cloth against the duct walls and the far, floor-shaking pulse of the presses, felt more than heard, like standing inside something with a slow heart.

Then the duct opens, and there is the room.

It is small. Four paces long, two wide, ceiling low enough to touch with a flat palm. It is not on any map because it is not done — the building does not draw its rooms until they exist, and this one is still being born. It budded in a gap between two assemblies, a pocket of void the greater structure left behind, and some printer was dispatched to make a room of it. That was a long time ago. The room is a runt. It grows slower than its siblings, in stolen moments, and no function has been assigned to it yet. It is a blank — a key not yet cut, an organ with no duty, waiting in the body of the works.

Everything in it was made. That is the first rule of the place, and you feel it immediately: nothing here was quarried, grown, or weathered into shape. The walls are sintered steel panel, galvanized, still showing the fine orbital grain of the sintering head. The floor is grating, and below the grating run drain gullets and a warm conduit bleeding heat in from the plant above. The ceiling is a dense layered map of pipes, cable trays, brackets, valve bodies, expansion joints — near-touching, everything at arm's reach, the whole room so packed above shoulder height that you move under it like a wader under low branches. The air smells of ozone, wet steel, and — near one seam — warm plastic, the new-toy smell of a polymer cooling.

That seam is the newest thing in the room. Follow it with your eye: a horizontal line running the length of the inner wall, slightly proud of the old surface, layer upon micro-layer, the texture oddly organic — a stippling that resembles nothing so much as lichen. This is the printer's work. A small gantry rides rails in the corner, a caged amber inspection lamp swinging from its arm, and when the building above goes still enough — between press strokes, during the lulls — the extrusion head comes alive with a sound like a pencil shading, patiently, and lays down another stratum. The room is growing a new inner skin over the old. Millimeter by millimeter, the rooms of this plant mature inward. Given decades, a finished room closes like a hand. The building ossifies from the inside; void becomes tissue; the rooms are temporary organs. This one is at the other end of that life — soft-shelled, incomplete, its purpose undetermined until the final bead is laid and the numerals are stenciled on. Its number will be its christening.

Put your palm on the curing seam and you feel the only warmth in the place — a low fever held in the wall, a vein of unfinished heat in a cold body. Everywhere else the room is near freezing. The dominant feature of the cold is a refrigerant line that crosses the ceiling on its way to somewhere more important, and it wears frost the way a log wears moss: white ferns, fractal, plumed, growing measurably in an hour, dying back when the compressor cycles off and the line warms a degree. The frost garden blooms and collapses on a schedule nobody set. It is one of the two gardens in the room.

The other is real vegetation, and there is exactly one patch of it: a shallow tray bolted to the wall under a small nozzle, holding a square of dark green-black velvet — moss, inoculated from an aerosol canister whose lot number is still stenciled on the tray's edge. Even the green here is manufactured. Every twenty minutes the nozzle gives a click, a ten-second hiss of fog, and the moss darkens with drink, and the cold green smell of it rises — the only organic odor in the room, cold and mineral and faintly sweet. Nothing eats it. There are no mites in the moss, no spiders in the corners, no gnats above the drain, no mice, nothing with a spine or a shell or a leg. The fauna of this place is zero, and the absence is audible: the silence of a world without scurrying. The moss has the whole room to itself. The drip has never been drunk.

And there is drip. Water here is borrowed from the air. Warm process air bleeds down through vents from the plant above, meets the chilled shell, and gives itself up: every bolt head holds a bead, a bright lens; the underside of every pipe wears a wet fringe; condensation creeps in shining maps across the galvanizing. It gathers, hangs, lets go. One droplet in the whole room has found music — a funnel in the corner, and the drops fall into it with a bright plink, counted, patient, a clock made of ceiling water. The rest slides into the gullets and goes away into the dark to be used somewhere else.

Once an hour, the room rains. It lasts thirty seconds. When the big compressor upstairs finishes its run and the pressure equalizes, the whole ceiling exhales at once — every held droplet releasing together, a fine shivering fall of water onto the grating, a whispering drumroll, and then done. Dry again. The room goes back to its beads and its single plink. This is its weather: damp, then dry, then damp, on a tide it doesn't control.

The light is sparing, the way a nursery light is sparing. The printer's amber lamp makes one cone of color in the corner. Through a louvered grille high on one wall, corridor light from the plant above falls in dim stripes across the floor grating — the room is striped like something in a cage. A run of sealant along one joint glows faintly, a low blue horizon line ringing the walls. A few indicator lamps sit among the pipes, green and amber, patient as icons. It is enough light to see the whole room by. It is not enough light to make the room feel seen.

The sounds, catalogued, are few, and all of them small: the drip into its funnel; the tick of expansion joints as the cold tightens the metal, like guitar strings cooling; the transformer hum at the floor of hearing, felt in the molars more than heard; the pencil-shading whisper of the printhead when it works; the click-hiss of the mist; and — coming up through the whole structure, muffled by tons of building — the far thud of the presses, the heartbeat of the parent body, and the pipes answering it with a little resonant shiver. Every so often a valve somewhere exhales, a short steel sigh, and the hatch at your back flexes inward a centimeter and eases out again. The building breathes. The room rides that breath.

Its economy runs entirely on byproducts. Heat is bled off from the plant's warm air. Cold is borrowed from a refrigerant line merely passing through. Water is condensed from exhaust that was headed elsewhere anyway. Light falls through a grille meant for ventilation, not for eyes. The moss is watered by a nozzle that serves no process listed anywhere. The room lives on scraps of intention — and one thing, possibly, more than scraps. Because the printer, laying its lichen-textured skin over the old wall, does something curious around the moss tray: it leaves a niche. The new wall steps back, four centimeters, exactly the footprint of the tray, with a recessed ledge above it and a small channel that aligns — after enough layers — with the nozzle line. Whether the machine was told to garden, or whether the building has simply been growing rooms long enough that it has developed habits, no one is positioned to say. The plant does not explain itself. It buds; it fills; it numbers. Somewhere up on SL-2 there are finished rooms full of clattering function, and every one of them was once like this — a cold pocket, sweating, half-born, unassigned, waiting to learn what it would be for.

Stand here and put your palm back on the curing seam. Under the cold skin of the wall there is new warmth; under your feet there is the building's pulse; above your head the frost ferns are opening and the ceiling is quietly filling its beads for the next little rain. The printer whispers through another layer. The plink counts. The stencil on the hatch holds its blank, patient as an unpulled trigger, for the digit that will complete this room and begin its long, slow closing.

You are standing inside something that is still deciding what it is.
