# The Steading Plain

You come up onto it all at once — a plateau lifted clean out of the lower haze, bone-pale, warm before you touch it. The light is enormous and dry and slightly too honest: shadows lie down under each stone with edges like cut paper, and distance itself is legible. Forty miles off, a single dark-green smudge resolves, even at that range, into a crown of leaves. There is nothing in between but ground. That is the first lesson of the place: how far apart things are allowed to be.

The stone underfoot is young. Not new-looking — actually new, in the way a casting is new when it comes out of its mold: flash still on the edges, seam-lines running across the slabs in long tooling-marks, as if the rock had been poured and nobody poured it. No rain has ever taken a file to it. No river has been commissioned to cut it. Where the plateau finally gives up at its rim, the escarpment drops in one unbroken lip, and along that lip you can find the gully-stubs — the first centimeter of a thousand future canyons, begun at a finger's width per thousand years, and holding there, permanently. The only weather here is light. The sun does what weathering gets done, and it does almost none of it, because everything the light falls on is too recently arrived to be worn.

The warmth is not entirely the sun's doing. Stand still long enough and you understand the floor is heated from beneath — the rock holds a heat like the side of a kiln an hour after firing, constant, tireless, older than this morning. Night comes and the plateau does not cool properly; the ground keeps its temperature the way a banked stove does, and the air above it stays mild while the stars harden.

Walk out from the rim and within an hour you cross the first of the vent-fields — ranks of chimneys, ranked like organ pipes, hundreds of them, standing in rows too regular for comfort and too regular for accident. They are fumaroles, except that word arrives with you and doesn't quite fit either. Each one breathes on a slow tide, an inhale of a few hours, an exhale of a few hours, and the exhale is warm and mineral and faintly sweet, like the inside of a new kettle. They do not hiss. Nothing here has learned to hiss. What they produce instead is felt rather than heard: a pressure in the sternum, a great slow idling under everything, the sensation of standing on the crankcase of something vast that is running at ten revolutions per day and has been running since before there was anyone to stand on it.

Around the vent-fields the ground has built its own factory floor — terraces of pale mineral laid down in shelves, step after step, tiled like a works, the deposit growing on the far edges of each shelf and wasting away from the near ones at exactly the same rate. A ledger balanced to the gram. Nothing accumulates. Nothing is made. Nothing is consumed. If you stand on those tiles you are standing on production with no product, throughput with no destination, and it is only habit that makes you reach for the word *industry* and then find nothing in the landscape for the word to attach to. No wall. No path. No shard of anything worked by a hand. The machinery here is simply how the ground behaves.

And yet behave it does. The groves confirm it.

Each stand of forest sits exactly where a vent-field exhales its warm mineral breath, and each stand is *lush* the way nothing in a waterless country has a right to be — domed canopies of deep, almost black-green, trunks plated and enameled like tanks, leaves shaped like trowels, waxy to the touch, so glossy they look dipped. Cut one (nobody does; there is no one to) and it weeps a clear sap that is not water — a warm, light oil smelling of resin and hot coin, beading on the blade and sealing itself in the sun. Every tree is a sealed vessel. Each one carries its entire hydraulics inside its own skin, a closed cycle, a terrarium the size of a building, condensing and raining privately onto itself forever. The world is dry; the trees are full. They have simply never agreed to share.

The grove floors are ankle-deep in leaves that fell decades or centuries ago, and none of them have rotted. There is no water to rot with. There are no insects, no grazers, no birds — nothing with a mouth lives here — and so every leaf on every tree is entire, un-nibbled, perfect, a perfection with no one to please. The leaf-fall lies resin-sweet and preserved, the forest its own archive, and it thins and vanishes into the stone at a rate that precisely equals the rate of falling. Balanced. Again balanced. When afternoon heat peaks, the seed-pods split with a sound like a knuckle cracking — the loudest event for miles — and the heavy brass-bright seeds roll out downslope until the ground wants them. Wind does the rest. Pollination was never anyone's job, and so the flowers, when a tree bothers with them, open their wax blossoms for nobody at all, and never wilt on any schedule you could call a schedule.

The quiet between all this is not an absence. It is a latency. Leaves knock together in the rare wind with a small dry clatter like fingernails on china, and that is nearly the whole of the audible world. The vents work below hearing. The wind arrives already quiet because there is so little for it to push. And at night, as the top millimeter of rock lets go of the day, the whole plateau ticks — *tick*, a pause, *tick* — stone counting itself down in degrees, a clock that measures nothing, striking the hours of nothing. The place is an instrument that has never once been struck, and it holds that readiness in the air the way a hall holds the silence before a downbeat.

Time, then. This is the part that takes the longest to accept.

The plateau is rising. You cannot watch it; you can only read it in the seam-lines, in the gully-stubs, in the raw unfacetted edges that nothing has ever rounded. A finger's width in a thousand years, pressure from below, the whole tableland being pushed up out of the world's interior like a slow piston on its outstroke — and it has been rising forever, and it will rise forever, and it will never arrive. This is a landscape permanently mid-birth. A labor that holds. Everything in it is under construction and nothing in it will ever be finished: the terraces build and unbuild, the vents breathe, the sap circulates, the seeds roll, and every process runs at a steady state so exact that from any vantage, at any hour, over any lifetime, the place is identical. Not frozen. Worse and better than frozen: *running*, at rates too slow or too cyclical to register as change. An engine with no product. A heart that beats because stopping is not what hearts are.

If you stand at the rim at the bronze hour, with the light flat along the terraces and the groves burning green against the pale stone, you can feel the whole arrangement breathing under you — the inhale gathering across miles of bare rock, the exhale warming your ankles at the nearest chimney — and you can notice, finally, the strangest thing about it: none of it needs you. Not the industry, since there is no product to witness. Not the lushness, since there is nobody to please. The machinery was not built and will not be attended, and it runs whether anything ever stands on it or not, and it has never once been late, and there is no such thing here as late.

The plateau breathes out.

Nothing anywhere notices. That is the whole of the sound.
