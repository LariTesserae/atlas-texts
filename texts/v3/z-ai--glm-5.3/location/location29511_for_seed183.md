**The Loud Country**

You hear it a day before you arrive. Not with your ears — the sound is still too far, the basin still below three ridgelines of the lower world — but through your boots, through the stone under your boots, and eventually through your teeth. A low, continuous roar with a note inside it, one held tone like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass the size of a valley. The country announces itself through the earth's own bones before it consents to be seen.

Then the last pitch, and the rim, and it is all there at once.

Below you lies a stepped bowl perhaps four miles across, a day's walk to cross, and every foot of it visible from up here — which is the first strange gift of the place, because the openness is total, and yet the land below is *crowded*. Pale terraces descend in long benches toward a central floor, and the benches are packed with standing stones: hundreds of pinnacles, near-touching, mature and columnar, ranked like the pipes of an organ built by no one. Wardens call that field the Choir. Between the pipes, the pavements shine wet. Everything shines. The stone is brighter than the sky above it.

**Terrain, light, air**

The rock is a laminated pale stuff — board-rock, the wardens say — laid down in thin sheets you can read like pages at any broken edge, though what's written in them no one has deciphered. The terraces are old in a settled way: edges softened, curves found, nothing raw. The place is not being worn down so much as *being itself*, a landscape in stalemate with its own weather, and the stalemate has held so long it has become the landscape's character.

The elevation is extreme and the sky shows it — enormous, thin, close. The air carries almost nothing; the lower world hangs under a haze you can see the whole shape of. Walking is breath-heavy work in a chill that never lifts. The temperature is cool and damp, a cellar's coolness at high altitude: your breath fogs faintly all day, and the glare gives no heat worth the name. Light at 2.6 means light here does not merely fall, it *stands*. It comes down hard from a white sun, and comes *up* harder off the wet pavements, doubled in every pool, so that shadows are knife-edged and the world looks overexposed, like a place with one layer of reality removed. Above the far wall of the basin there is sometimes a second sun — a bright seam where the sky's fabric is thin, glowing faintly along its stitch. The wardens call it the Seam. You do not look at the Seam at dusk.

Over the wet stone there is a shimmer, but it is not heat shimmer. It is the air thinning.

**Water**

Water is everywhere near the surface and nowhere in a hurry. The benches weep along their seams. Overhangs stream at dawn with condensation gathered overnight out of the thin air — by mid-morning every lip of stone has a silver beard and a drip-line below it. Between the laminations the water rides under pressure, so that when you step on the pavement it *sweats*, pearls rising between the pages of the rock. Rills braid across the flats in networks too complex to be accidental-looking, and collect in shallow pools by the hundred, most of them rimmed with low stacked-stone walls that were clearly set by hands.

The water is clear, mineral, cold, and empty. Nothing swims in it. Nothing grows on it. Each pool holds two suns and, at odd moments, at noon, a pool will briefly hold stars — the sky's underside, reflected from the wrong side of things.

The water is also loosely ordered in time. A pool will fill an hour before the rain that fills it arrives. Near the deep drums, drips fall slowly, hang, consider. Once, watching a rivulet, you see it run briefly uphill and do not fully trust the seeing, and the country does not ask you to.

**The sound**

The roar is constant and it is *loud* — loud enough that speech is a fantasy except with your lips at someone's ear. It has layers. The top layer is wind: air moving across the mouth of every pinnacle in the Choir produces a sustained organ tone that rises and falls with the weather, a chord without a chord-change, sometimes for hours. Under it, percussion: the drip-lines, thousands of them, striking pools in arrhythmic but dense patterns, like rain on a thousand tin roofs. Under *that* is the bass — the deepest layer, felt more than heard, in the sternum, in the jaw. It comes from the thin places, the membranes where the world's fabric has worn through and only a film of air remains stretched across the gap. The wardens call these places drums.

Echoes behave badly here. They come back altered, or late, or *first* — a shout's return arriving before the shout, faintly, as if remembering it. The rule of the Loud Country is that its sound precedes it. At dawn you will hear, very faint, far away, tonight's ringing — arriving early. At night, after everything has stilled, the day comes back: the drips you heard at noon return out of order, all night, the landscape slowly playing back its own daylight.

**The wool**

There is no green. No grass, no lichen even. No birds — and the sky's emptiness is not gentle absence but *vacancy*, a thing you notice as a pressure. No legs, no wings, no eyes. The only borderline presence is what the wardens call the wool: a grey mineral film that grows where water stands and where the sound is steady. It is not a plant. It may not be alive. It lies on the pool rims and the drip-lines in soft strata like wet felt, and it does one thing that keeps it ambiguous: it grows *in bands* — ridges and grooves laid down in patterns that match the standing waves of the local note. You can read the pitch of a drum in the grain of the wool beside it, the way you could read years in a tree, if trees existed. In bright light the wool curls, slowly, protectively, though from what it is not clear anyone knows.

It is the only soft thing in the country, and so it is everything: the wardens cut and twist it into cordage while damp (it sets hard as horn), press it into gaskets and windbreak seals, and wear it as visors against the glare.

**The marks on the land**

Human marks are scattered and small, and every one of them is load-bearing. A single hut of fitted slabs on the third terrace, roofed with wool under weighted stones — one building in four miles, lived in by one or two wardens at a time, because the loudness unstrings people and no one stays long. Along the far wall, a green vein of native copper, cold-hammered by hand into pegs, and those copper pegs are driven into the seams of the drums — dozens of them, green as pond water, each one humming faintly with the membrane it holds. Cairns are strung in straight lines across the benches, lines called chords, connecting drum to drum *through* the quiet places; follow a chord-line and your footing is safe and your walk is short, no matter what your eyes tell you about distance. Beside each drum, rows of tally-notches cut into the board-rock, generations deep: a record, notch by notch, of the pitch drifting.

Because it is drifting. That is the quiet work of this place. The note is very slowly rising.

And there are the deaf hollows — small pockets, a dozen of them, scattered, where sound dies utterly and the silence has a texture like pressure. They are marked by deliberately broken cairns. In a deaf hollow you can hear your own blood. The wardens rest there, sleep there, weep there, and rotate back down to the lower world before the roar finishes unstringing them. A few, over the years, have chosen not to go down. Their cairns stand on the chords, unbroken, and the country does not object.

**How the country behaves**

Learn the rules or the rules will learn you:

*Sound arrives before cause.* Trust your ears about the future, not the present.
*Distances breathe.* The far wall is nearer in the morning and farther at dusk; walk the chords, not the sightlines.
*Stone wears both ways.* Erosion takes the benches down, and along certain seams — if the pitch is right — the stone grows back, laminations accreting like rings. The terraces are a thousand-year argument between wearing and healing, currently in balance. It has the settledness of an old couple.
*Gravity hesitates at edges.* Scree sometimes climbs. Drips sometimes wait.
*Silence is a resource.* Spend it where it's marked.
*Never hum.* A human voice adds to the standing wave, and the drums are already tighter than the wardens like.

**Cycles**

The country runs a daily engine. Dawn: the weep — condensation streaming off every overhang, the whole basin glistening at once, the coldest hour. Midday: glare and dryness, the wool curled tight, the pools shrinking to rings, the wind-chord at its highest. Dusk: the wind reverses, the air rolls over like something turning in sleep, and the sounding happens — every pinnacle, every drum, the whole four-mile bowl ringing *one note together* for three or five minutes, the pools shivering into concentric rings, the water on the pavements standing up in fine ridges, the note arriving in your chest before it arrives in your ears, and the Seam above the far wall glowing briefly along its stitch like something on the other side pressing a finger to the fabric.

Then night. One deep bell, sometimes, from the deepest drum at the basin's center. Then the playback: the day's sounds returning, out of order, until dawn.

At the center stands the wardens' finest work — the listening arch, stacked slabs with a wool-lined seat beneath it, positioned at the one place where all the chords cross. Sit there at dusk with your hand flat on the wet stone, and you feel the sounding through your bones before you ever hear it: the country keeping its own time, loud and bright and empty and cool, lifeless and yet running, a landscape with no living thing in it that is nonetheless *in motion* — and you understand what the tally-notches are for, and why the pegs must be kept tight, and why a note that is slowly rising is the only prayer this place has left.

When you leave, the roar goes with you for a while, in your sternum, days after — and then one night, faintly, early, you hear tomorrow's ringing arrive. The country, remembering you forward.
