# The Stillwater Works

It is the size of a market square, maybe three of them — you could walk its perimeter in a quarter hour, and its edges are definite: a moss-grown retaining wall on the north, a canal cut on the south, and beyond both, the fog does not so much end as become uninterestingly thick.

Inside: a dense, low settlement of machinery. Not a factory so much as what a factory becomes when no one remembers it is a factory. Water is the government here. It rains most afternoons — a warm, patient rain that seems to fall out of habit rather than weather — and between rains the air condenses on everything, so that every surface shines faintly, always, as if just licked. The stones weep. This is not metaphor: the older masonry has developed seeps, thread-thin runnels that have carved grooves down the walls over so many centuries that the grooves now look intentional, like decoration, like a script nobody can read anymore.

The warmth is the warmth of compost and cooling pipes. Something under the district's floor — old boilers, or the ground itself — exhales a steady tepid heat, and the water on the surfaces never quite cools enough to be unpleasant. Touch a wall and it is blood-warm. The whole place is blood-warm.

## What stands here

The construction is layered, industrial, and irreducibly old. Iron gantries furred with moss. A brick gasometer, sunk slightly on one side, its telescope crown permanently half-collapsed and now hosting a hanging garden inside its bell — ferns in the dimness, ferns on ferns. Pumps. Rows of them, brass-and-cast, house-high in some cases, still going. That is the strange thing: the machinery has not stopped. Nobody feeds it, nobody tends it, but the water pressure in the deep aquifer is enough to keep a dozen ram pumps nodding their slow heads, and those pumps drive the rest. The district runs on the patience of falling water.

Every vertical surface is colonized. Every horizontal one holds a centimeter of standing film. Vegetation — 1.9 lushness compressed into a tenth of a hectare — grows not in fields but in towers: liverworts over moss over algae, ferns out of gutter-joints, a single enormous fig-like tree that rooted in a clogged flue and has since split the flue, the stack, and the roof it belonged to, and now stands there like a landlord. Its roots run down the outside of the pump-house like rigging. Underfoot, duckweed mats the flooded tramway so completely that you take the first three steps onto water believing it is lawn, and the fourth step tells you otherwise — but only knee-deep, warm, and smelling of green iron.

## The sounds

Loud, but the loudness of a place with no animals: all of it is mechanical and hydrological. The district has a chord. The ram pumps provide the bass note — a deep, unhurried *chunk... chunk...* about one beat every two seconds, felt in the sternum more than heard. Above that, water in the grooves: a continuous silver hiss of ten thousand small runnels finding their ways down walls. Add the condensation-drip from every beam, each drop striking at its own tempo, and the flap of the loose iron sheeting on the gasometer roof when the afternoon rain comes. There is no birdsong, no insect whine, no scraping of claws. No animal life at all, not even midges, not even in the standing water — and the standing water is everywhere, and it is still and unworrying because nothing lives in it and nothing ever has. It is the eeriest thing about the place. Water without mosquitoes is water that has decided to be mineral.

## The light

Dim, and dim in layers. The canopy of the great fig eats most of the sky; fog and permanent light rain take more. What gets through arrives in a few grand columns — one through the collapsed gasometer bell, one down a stairwell shaft, one through the broken clerestory of the old engine house — and in these columns the district performs. Steam lifts from the warm stones into the light and you can watch it, slow as smoke in a photograph. The light moves across the day like everything else here does: gradually, ceremonially, without hurry.

## The rules

Change happens, but slowly, and mostly in the direction of continuation. Rust advances a fingernail a year. Moss dams its own gutters and then drowns its own dam and then grows again. The pumps were balanced by whoever built this place into a self-correcting harmony, and the water table rises and falls by centimeters across seasons, so the district breathes: in the wet months the lower gallery floods and the ferns stand to their waists in dark glass; in the dry months (there are two, barely) the water retreats and leaves the brick singing and the moss silver.

Erosion here has finished its argument. Nothing is being worn down into new shapes anymore — every groove is grooved as far as it means to go, every channel has found its bed. The place is in balance the way a stopped clock is not, but the way an old married couple is.

If you stand very still on the tramway duckweed — you will sink slowly, imperceptibly, into it, warm to the hip, held like a hand — the chord of the pumps settles into you, and the strange thought arrives, and keeps arriving every time you visit, which is the one law of the Stillwater Works that is not written anywhere:

The machinery is not abandoned. It is simply that its maintenance schedule is longer than the human one. A pump arm will be replaced by a root, someday, over a few hundred years, and the pump will not notice the difference.
