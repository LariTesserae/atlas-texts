**The Bole**

Walk out from under a crown at mid-morning and you enter the plain, and the plain is the whole paradox of this place at once: a bark-steppe running flat to a shimmering horizon, wide open, almost empty of anything you could point at — and deafening.

There is nothing here to see and everything to hear. That is the first law you learn on the Bole, and you learn it through your teeth, because the sound arrives through the ground before it arrives through the air.

**The land itself**

Underfoot, the world is bark. Plates of it, charcoal-dark and polished by ten million years of rain and footfall, lying like flagstones for hundreds of miles. The seams between plates show paler, younger wood, and the color of the ground tells you where you are in the continent's slow biography: near the rim, everything is tan and green and slightly swollen with new growth; a thousand miles in, the wood has gone almost black, dense as ebony, worn into low badland terraces and shallow canyons where the rot has gotten in and been digested and healed over. There are no stones. There is almost no mineral anywhere. The Bole is a strangler that germinated on a stone continent eons ago, dropped a billion roots, fused with its own kin through grafting, and quietly *mined* the old world to death — root acids and tannins and the patient chemistry of decay dissolved the bedrock and pulled it down into the sap. The mineral past survives only as "bone stone": silicified seeds, erratic pebbles, ribs of dark flint that surface in rot-pockets like fossils surfacing in a sea floor. People kill for a good piece of bone stone. It is the only true stone in the world.

The great features of the terrain are the crowns — emergent trunk systems, hollow and buttressed, standing half a day's walk apart from one another across the whole interior. From anywhere on the steppe you can see three or four of them on the horizon, blue with distance, each the size of a hill and shaped like an opened hand. Between them: openness. Emptiness is the texture of this place. The crowns are so far apart that walking from one to the next is a small expedition, and the sky between them is enormous, bright, hard-edged, alive with heat shimmer by late morning.

The Bole is not growing and not shrinking. It has finished. The rim advances a hair each century; the core sinks a hair; the two exactly cancel. Erosion here is rot, and rot is an industry, and both are balanced against growth the way weather is balanced against itself. This is a landform in its own final state, achieved and held.

**Water, which is held rather than flowing**

There are no rivers. There is no rain to speak of. Water here behaves like something caught in the act of escaping.

Every evening the lowland furnace — the steaming country below the rim cliffs — sends its humid breath up the slopes, and the breath cools as it climbs, and around midnight the fog comes *up the low road*: a slow white flood rolling inland from the rim, moving at a walking pace, taking hours to reach the deep interior. Where it passes, the dew-fur gets soaked. Dew-fur is a velvet-textured growth, grey-green, thick on every windward slope, and at dawn it drips. People harvest it with shell scrapers, squeezing the wet into gutters of wax and fiber that drain into dew-sheds. A band's wealth is measured in nights of fog and yards of fur.

The other water sits in the tanks. Scattered across the steppe, one every few miles, are cistern crowns — trees whose central rosettes have cupped into basins the size of ponds, holding ten thousand gallons of tannin-dark, green-tasting water. Each tank is a world: red larvae rowing in the margins, glass-shrimp clouding the depths, a single potbellied frog that sings like a struck kettle and owns the acoustics of the whole basin. Tank rights are old, specific, and ferociously kept. The paths between tanks are older than any language that names them.

**The sound, which is the weather**

Now the thing that must be said plainly: it is deafening here, almost all the time, and the emptiness is not silence. It is the opposite. This land is *saturated with sound and nearly vacant of presences*, and the way to understand that is to understand that life here behaves like weather — it comes in fronts, in seasons, in floods, and between its arrivals the land lies open and almost bare.

The standing roar has three sources. First, the plants themselves. The leaf-flutes: leaves with tuned perforations that the wind plays, whole crowns of them, each spire holding a different note. The pod-trees, which on hot afternoons crack like rifle volleys, one horizon igniting the next, throwing seed ten meters at a time, a rolling barrage that lasts an hour. The boom-roots: buttress chambers that catch distant thunder and answer it back, delayed, flattened, like artillery remembering itself.

Second, the animals — of which more below.

Third, the Tide. Twice a day the entire continental organism shifts its sap. The xylem pressure swings with the heat, and the ground — the *ground* — rises a thumb's width and settles, a slow sub-bass roll crossing a thousand miles from rim to rim, felt in the chest and the jaw before it is heard. Animals key their lives to it. People tell time by it: the first swell, the letting-down. It is the heartbeat of a being so large that it has a body climate.

And the fog plays the instrument. When the nightly fog-front passes through a stand of flutes, the humidity tightens their membranes and the notes rise, audibly, in sequence. Locals know tomorrow's weather as a melody: *the fog came up the low road singing the far set of notes — rain by noon, sleep late.* Weather forecasting here is ear training.

**The Chorus**

Every thirteenth year — and no one knows how the wood counts, but it counts — the nymphs come up.

They have been down in the living wood for thirteen years, whole generations of them, sipping root sap in the dark, maturing in synchrony across the entire continent. Then, in one coordinated night, they emerge everywhere at once, by the trillion, and the world is coated in them: on the bark plates, in the dew-fur, knee-deep in the tank basins, crunching underfoot everywhere for weeks. They molt into glasswinged adults and they *sing*.

The song is the sound parameter of this world in its purest form. It is layered, geographically patterned — each region with its own chord, the way storms have their own shapes — and sustained at a volume that does not abate for forty days. Standing in it is like standing inside a struck bell the size of the sky. People converse by touch-code and drum; speech is a waste of breath. They wear felted wax plugs from childhood and read each other's hands the way others read faces.

Then the singers die, all together, and the manna-fall begins. Bodies rain out of the crowns for days. Husk-drifts pile like dirty snow in the lee of every buttress. The nutrient pulse floods the loam-pockets; the soil web detonates; everything that eats gets fat; every predator on the continent times its own breeding to the Chorus years and fails, generation after generation, to synchronize with a prime-numbered prey. The people feast too — sun-dried chorister paste, a black protein that carries a band for years — and they tie the thirteenth tally-knot in the calendar cords, and start counting toward the next one.

Between Choruses, the fauna is overwhelming only when you look small: the decomposer multitudes in every rot-pocket, the shrimp-blooms when the pollen season ("the yellowing," weeks of sulfur haze and knee-deep drifts) washes into the tanks and turns the water thick and green. Look large, and the land is nearly vacant. The mowers — the bark-grazing megafauna, vast slow rinded beasts — are so few that each has a name, and a person's whole life might cross a given mower's path twice, and it would be remembered both times. At dusk the smoke-flocks come: swallows of a hundred thousand, moving like their own weather front. But walk the steppe at noon and you may walk all day and see one living thing larger than your thumb.

**The people**

Few, and far between, by design and temperament. Kin-bands of twenty or thirty, territory measured in days of walking. Their homes are graft-huts — branch-forks coaxed over decades into hollows, thatched with fiber, waxed against the fog — and every band keeps a dew-shed, and a fire in a clay cradle near the tank, tended like a dangerous god. Because the one law everyone agrees on, from rim to rim, is the fire law: the entire world is fuel. A fire that escapes is not a disaster; it is the end of the only world. Arson is the single crime spoken of with a hush of its own.

Their tools: knapped gall-flint (galls hard as glass, bone stone where it can be found), shell scrapers, bark-fiber cordage, fired humus-ceramics. No metal; the metal of the old stone continent went down with it, into the sap, into memory.

Their messages travel through the wood. A trunk-post is a hollow spire with a code-beaten inner wall; a good drummer can put a thump down the living wood for thirty miles, and the band at the other end feels it through the soles of their feet before they hear it. On the Bole you always know who's calling before you know what they're saying, because you feel it in the ground.

**The Hush**

Once a day, in the coldest hour before dawn, the singers go torpid. The flutes sag. The fog drips. The roar drops away to almost nothing — the one thin quiet this world permits — and people rise into it on purpose. Grief is expressed in the Hush. Marriages are made in it. Children are told, in that hour, the things that must be spoken softly.

Even then it isn't silence. The Tide groans somewhere in the deep wood. The bark ticks as it cools. Lay your palm flat on the ground at first light and you can feel the continent gathering itself: the fog starting up the low road, playing the far notes, the first chorister clearing its throat, the ground beginning to swell under your hand.

The Bole is an instrument that learned to be a world. Everything on it is either playing, or listening, or both.
