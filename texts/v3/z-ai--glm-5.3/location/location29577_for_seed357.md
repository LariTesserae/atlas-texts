You come up out of the fog with the sun already high, and the first wrong thing is the horizon: a straight edge, kilometers long, standing above the cloud layer like a sea-wall. Mountains tear. Coastlines fray. This edge is milled — flat-topped, crisp-cornered, and sweating, with water running down its face in threads you can see from a distance, each thread following a groove that looks chiseled because nothing has yet had time to soften it.

It is not rock. Nothing here is rock.

The plateau — call it forty kilometers across, though no one has paced it — is a single body, one plant that has converged on civil engineering the way ivy converges on walls. It grew here. The whole country is younger than a middle-aged person: it has geology the way a compost heap has geology, fast and hot. What it has built, under a bright ordinary sun, is a waterworks the size of a province, and it builds and unbolds continuously, which is why the place never looks finished, only current.
