# The Terraces of Falling Water

It begins with the sound before anything else — a roar so constant it has stopped being sound and become weather. You hear it from a day's walk away, a low, bone-deep thrum that you mistake first for your own pulse, then for a landslide, then for something the mountain itself is doing. It is neither. It is the city.

The settlement sits high — very high, on a plateau where the air thins enough that visitors count stairs in their own breathing and locals have stopped counting anything. Below the plateau, the world falls away in long weathered benches of rock, each one rounded and softened like furniture in a house where generations have sat in the same chairs. The erosion here is not active. Nothing is being actively worn down. This landscape arrived at its shape a long time ago and has since simply *rested* in it, the way a riverbed rests in its course. Gullies are smooth-lipped. The stone has a matte, handled quality, like a doorknob polished by three hundred years of the same hands.

The city was built — this is the local claim, and it is mostly true — because of the water. Not water on the ground. There is almost no water on the ground here, no rivers of consequence, no lakes. The water is *in the air*. Warm, moisture-heavy air from the lowlands climbs the plateau's flanks every afternoon, and when it reaches the altitude where cold can no longer be postponed, it lets go. Clouds form *on* the city. Not above it. On it, among it, through it. Fog moves down streets like a slow parade. Every surface sweats. Stone balustrades weep from hairline channels cut into them centuries ago. Iron railings carry a permanent film of beaded condensation, so that in the ordinary afternoon light the whole town appears to be slowly crying, and to find this unremarkable.

The founders understood this, and the city is essentially a machine for drinking the sky. Tier upon tier of terraces step down the plateau's southern face — hundreds of them, each one roofed in corrugated tin and slate, angled precisely so that condensed water gathers and runs. Every roof drains. Every wall has a throat. Every street is slightly, deliberately not flat, so that it carries a thin permanent sheet of water toward the gutters, which carry it to the cisterns, which are the true architecture of the place: vast, layered, church-dark underground reservoirs stacked beneath the town like a second city built upside down. The locals speak of cistern levels the way coastal people speak of tides. Wealth here is measured in liters.

The industry is old, steam-and-iron, and it is deafening — that's the roar. Plateau water plus plateau altitude means boiling happens at the wrong temperature and the engineers of four generations compensated with *size*. The engines are enormous, slow, and many. A row of beam engines works the great pump-houses near the cliff edge, each one a building-sized reciprocating heart, and their combined thunder — with the hiss of spent steam, with the endless sluicing of the collection channels, with the deep harmonic groan of cisterns filling — makes a continuous sound wall that reshapes everything about life here. Conversation is conducted in a flat near-shout, or with hands. Children grow up reading faces closely. The city has a full sign language, and its residents speak it as naturally as their own tongues, their hands flickering through the roar in the doorways and markets. There is a local saying, half-joke, half-theology: *God spoke and the city answered, and neither has stopped.*

Vegetation survives only where the machines' breath falls. Along the steam-vents, in the leak-shadow of the pump-houses, there are gardens — small, dense, almost painfully green against the grey, their lettuces and bean-vines thriving in the trapped heat and moisture like little riots of the lowlands refusing to accept the altitude. Between the gardens, almost nothing grows. A grey lichen on the north faces of stone. A single line of stunted, wind-cropped trees along one aqueduct, planted at intervals so regular they look drawn. Fauna is scarcer still: the city keeps pigeons (for messages, historically) and there are rats in the cisterns that the keepers have named and half-domesticated, and once a year a raptor passes over at enormous height, its shadow sliding silently across the terraces like a clock hand nobody reads. That is close to the whole census.

What makes the place strange — and it is strange only mildly, only at the edges, the way an old face is strange when you look at it too long — is the water's behavior. The condensation on the upper terraces forms patterns. Not frost, not mineral staining: patterns, laced and whorled, the same forms recurring on different surfaces in different years, too orderly for chance. The old cistern-keepers will tell you, in the flat near-shout the roar permits, that the water has been through the machines so many times that it has learned the shape of the city, and it redraws that shape wherever it rests. Nobody has proven this. Nobody has disproven it. The patterns are left alone. Roofs with a strong showing are considered lucky and fetch higher rents.

Change here is glacial. The same families work the same pump-houses their great-grandparents worked. The engines turn over at the same slow cadence, needing repair now and then, never replacement — the city long ago entered a maintenance rhythm so deep it resembles ritual, and there are days when the whole place seems less like a living settlement and more like a very large instrument that was wound up long ago and has not yet finished its piece. A fog cycle takes an afternoon. A cistern cycle takes a season. A roof's useful life takes a century. When you ask someone from the plateau what is happening in the city, they say, correctly: *it is filling.*
