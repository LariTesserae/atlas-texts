# Condenser Gallery Twelve

The mountain is a machine. The whole range is — the Stacks, they're called, though from the dry lowlands they look like any range: blue in the morning, black at dusk, wearing cloud around the shoulders. Up close the shoulders are riveted. There is no stone in these mountains that was not cast, faced, fitted, or poured; no summit that is not a louver; no scree that is not slag. Somewhere in the deep past — before the lowlands kept records, before the lowlands were lowlands — the Works was built to drink the sky: to rake the passing cloud, wring it out at altitude, and send the water down through kilometers of pipe to the reservoirs, the orchards, the thirst. It has been running ever since. Nobody tends it. That is not quite the strange part, though it is strange enough. The strange part is Gallery Twelve.

You reach it by climbing through the mountain's body for the better part of a day — up trunk ladders in warm dark, through ducts where the air already has a pull to it, past junction rooms whose indicator lamps have burned amber for longer than anyone can say. The Works is mostly hollow. Gallery after gallery stands dry, dim, and enormous, seeded with nothing, echoing around the one cylinder of your lantern. The emptiness is a pressure. You can walk for an hour between presences.

Then a hatch, a weather-box of cast zinc, and inside the box a ledger in pencil, with entries decades apart. The last one is years old: *Figs good this year. Fan slower, I think. — E.*

You go through the hatch, and the sound arrives before anything else.

---

It is not loud the way a factory is loud — clattering, eventful, demanding. It is loud the way the sea is loud: structural, permanent, the room's own voice. The mountain inhales through this gallery, around the clock, and the air moving over two hundred feet of condensation fins, funneling toward the great throat at the north end, makes a standing roar with a bass you feel in your boot-soles and a hiss you feel in your teeth. The fins are tuned, each row a hair's width off the last, so that the roar has *chords* in it — a vast steely organum that never resolves. You cannot speak here. You feel your own voice in your skull and give up on it. After a few hours the roar stops being sound and becomes a floor you stand on, and small things arrive through it as pressure: the swifts' clicks against your cheek, the bees' hum arriving up the handrail and into your palm.

The hall is a nave, plain and enormous, running warm-dry at the south end and cold-dripping at the north. The south face is all clerestories — tall louvered lights angled to admit sun but not rain — and between the louvers and the fin-ranks, filling the middle third of the gallery like a congregation that arrived by accident and stayed, is the garden.

Because there is a garden. That is what the ledger-keepers climb for, and it is lush past any sense: a banyan fig that seeded in a clogged gutter sometime in the last century and has since taken the middle terraces the way a strangler takes a host. Its roots pour over the walkway and down into the drip-channels; its canopy, pressed its whole life by the permanent airflow, streams north like a flag of green. Ferns fill the fins' sound-shadow. Moss maps every surface the drips touch. Orchids ride the dead ranks — the sections of rake abandoned before living memory, now trellis. In the banyan's trunk you can find, grown clean over, a cluster of brass gauges the tree swallowed whole; their needles still stand in the wood like fossils, still reading, faintly, the humidity of 1941.

Nothing in this garden grows in soil that was ever ground. The soil is an artifact — a century of screen-lint, moth-wing, fig-leaf, and insect husk, composted in the works of the machine itself. Even the dirt is manufactured, made by the filtration it grew in.

The water comes from the cold end. Cloud is drawn in through the north intakes and passes the warm flanks of the fins, and the fog simply *gives up* — beading, streaming, running down channel-stems into lead gutters worn green, gathering into downcomers that drop away through the mountain toward the lowlands. On the wet end you walk in mist with your hand on a rail polished bright not by hands but by generations of moths landing on the same curve, leaving their oil, evening after evening. The wet end smells of rain-on-hot-metal. The dry end smells of honey: there are bees, a whole colony in a junction box, working the figs and the orchids, making honey of rust and condensate.

And the fauna — sparse in any cubic yard, abundant in sum, every one of them shaped by the noise. The moths live their entire lives aloft in the warm column at the gallery's throat, mating in the air, dying into the fins and becoming soil. The swifts have learned the fan's slow beat and hunt on it, folding their wings on the whoomp. The geckos brood their eggs in the lagging wool of the hot downcomers. The frogs have all gathered in one cast-iron stairwell behind the banyan's trunk, the single acoustic shadow in the gallery, the only place in this cathedral where a voice can carry — so the whole amphibian nation of the mountain lives in a pocket of quiet the size of a confessional, and their chorus is a thing you feel grateful to hear. And there is one falcon, who comes each year through a failed louver, nests in a blanked-off vent, and never cries at all. Her calls were swallowed by the roar so many generations ago that the calling stopped. She hunts by shape alone, turning in the fan's throat like a knot in the wind.

The fan itself is the altar. Axial blades forty feet long, turning at three revolutions to the minute — each pass a slow whoomp you feel arrive through the floor a half-second after you see it. Its governor is clockwork the size of a font. Its bearings are fed by grease-lines kept topped, as far as anyone can tell, by nothing but the condensers' own overflow. The machine, somewhere in its centuries of running, crossed over from being maintained to being *metabolic*. The garden's roots hold the intake screens clear; the screen-lint builds the soil; the soil grows the figs; the figs transpire into the intake air and raise the harvest; the harvest tops the grease-cups.
