The country is made. All of it — every cliff, every pavement, every stone that is not a stone. There is no natural ground anywhere in this region; the "bedrock" is laminated stems pressed like the pages of a wet book that never dried, the ledges are courses of woven root set like brick by something that had no hands, the pillars are cemented growth the way a coral is cemented growth, except nothing here was ever an animal. Whatever built this country built with its body only: secreted, shed, woven, gummed, died in place, and became architecture. Nothing in all these miles was made by tools. Nothing was machined, fitted, or designed the way a device is designed. Everything was grown the way a throat is grown — and one of the things that was grown is a throat.

There is no water. Not a film, not a bead of condensation on any cold surface, though the surfaces are cold. The world was built dry and stayed dry; where a river would have run there ran instead a slow resin, amber and glassy, and it has long since stopped and set, and the riverbeds are now just one more material, among many, all of them made. The air smells of it — cedar-cold, pencil-shaving sharp, faintly sweet — a cellar smell, because the whole country is cellar-cool. The light is generous and pale and high and bright, and it carries no warmth with it at all. Brightness without heat is the local definition of daylight. It falls on made cliffs, on basins floored with packed hexagonal sockets, and on almost nothing living.

The vegetation is sparse. That is almost the saddest fact about the place, once you understand that everything here is the leftover work of a flora: a few species, ochre and bone-colored and faintly rose, none of them green, because green was never chosen here.
