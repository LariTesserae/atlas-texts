You come over the rim wall in the early morning and the whole basin flashes at you — a highland bowl two miles across, terraced down in shallow steps, and every single surface in it is wet, and every single surface in it is made.

There is no sky-weather here. The clouds come from below. The basin sits high enough that the cloud deck presses up against the plateau like a slow sea against a harbor wall, and every morning the mist climbs the terraces from the lowest edge, rising through the works in a gray tide. The rim wall harvests it first: a great curving bulwark of fitted stone, taller than it needs to be for holding anything back, because what it holds is fog. Above the mist the sun is ferocious and thin, the light of altitude, doubled by reflection off the cloud sea below. Everything pale throws light back upward. Shadows are hard-edged, blue-black, and move like drawn lines across the terraces. The cold is a damp cold, the kind that feels cleaner than it is; your breath shows faintly in the morning and vanishes by midmorning, though the stone stays clammy all day.

Notice, second, what the ground is. There is no ground. Nowhere in the basin is there soil, or native rock, or anything that was simply *here* — every foot of it has been laid, tamped, fired, or chiseled and set in place by hands. The plateau's "bedrock" is masonry. In places you can knock on the floor with a knuckle and the floor answers — a hollow wooden note under stone — because under the floor is water. The whole basin floats on its own holdings; the terraces are lids over cisterns, and beneath every stone grating you pass, at varying depths, the collected water sits in the dark, colder than the air, listening.

Third, notice how full it is. This is the thing that takes longest to understand: there is almost no empty space. The terraces are open — you can see a mile of them at a stretch, the whole machine visible at once from the rim — but at the scale of your feet the place is saturated. There is a gutter every few steps. Channels braid around pools, split, rejoin, pass under stairs, surface again on the far side as cascades. The benches are weirs. The staircases are waterfalls waiting for the afternoon. The few flat plazas are the roofs of reservoirs, and even they carry a shallow groove around their edge, because a flat thing here is only a flat thing until the fog comes, and then it is a collector. Nothing in this place is only itself.

The materials tell you who built it, or rather what their hands were like, because no one is here now. Dense pale stone, the color of unfired porcelain, dressed with chisel points in careful rows. Long tapered pipes of fired clay, and on every pipe joint a thumbprint, pressed into the wet clay to seal it and then burned there permanently. Lime mortar, cream-colored, cured hard. A sparing use of copper at the spouts — the only metal, and it weeps green streaks down the pale stone like old tears. Tamped clay floors, polished by water and by nothing else. There is no wood anywhere. No bone, no leather, no fiber. You feel the absence in your hands before you can name it: every handle is stone, every hinge is stone, every tool left lying — and there are a few, a chisel in a half-finished groove, its edge still keen — wears a grip carved for a hand exactly the size of yours. The cold kilns at the basin's upper end, where the clay was fired and the lime burned, have been repurposed; they hold water now, quietly, the fire's old work converted to the water's.

The day has a shape. Morning: the mist climbs, and every surface below the fog line sweats. The works are built for this. Tall thin fin walls stand across the terraces like sails, their tops cut into combs, and the fog is *combed* — the condensation beads on the ridges, gathers, runs down grooves angled a hair off plumb. The whole landscape is tilted, in fact, so slightly you feel it in your calves after an hour: there is no level ground in the basin, only level-*looking* ground, and every gentle slope is a decision. Each drop is led from fin to groove to channel to gutter, down the cascade stairs, through the gratings, into the dark under the floor. By afternoon the mist retreats down the terraces, the sun takes the works, and the surfaces drip themselves dry one by one, basin by basin, in a slow sequence from the top of the bowl to the bottom. This is the day's entire music.

It is very quiet. That needs saying plainly, because the place is full of water and you expect noise. There isn't any. The mist's condensation is felt more than heard, a hush like the inside of a held breath. A single drop falls into a deep cistern somewhere under your feet and says *tok*, and the echo takes a long time coming back. Gaps open between sounds that you could drive whole minutes through. There is one recurring voice: a ceramic cup on a stone fulcrum that fills, tips, knocks against its stop, drains, rights itself, and begins again — the only clockwork, the only percussion, patient as a heart.

And yet the place is not dead; it has an ecology, if you widen the word. Nothing here grows but stone. The water arrives carrying its cargo of dissolved mineral, and where it slows, it pays: the lips of the basins thicken year by year, growing rims like mouths. Under the drip-lips hang stone fingers, some a hand long, sharp as chisels. Where water runs fast it takes back instead — edges are undercut, hollows polished satin-smooth, and the water is always, quietly, choosing new paths. Deposit here, dissolve there. Every surface in the basin sits on the boundary between those two verbs.

This is also how the maintenance happens, and this is the part that takes a while to accept. There are no keepers. There are thumbprints in ten thousand pipe joints and no people. But the cracked channels are sealed, the mortar in the oldest joints is fresh, the hairline fractures are beautifully re-banded — because the water re-points its own house. It welds its leaks with mineral, grows new lips where overflow cut old ones. The machine repairs itself with its own exhaust. In the deepest, coldest cistern — you can see it through a grate, far down — there is an opalescent sheen on the black water that breaks apart when you shift your angle and refuses to say whether it is alive. It is the only candidate for life in the basin, and it isn't telling.

Walking out the rules as you go, they assemble themselves like this:

Nothing here is native; everything was placed. All water comes from below, as cloud; the sky above is empty of weather. Water only ever moves down, and the whole art of the place is delay — to slow it, spread it, keep it as long as possible before it goes. What water touches slowly, it builds; what it touches quickly, it unbuilds. Nothing rots, so nothing is urgent. And the drips are not random. That last one you learn on the third or fourth day: the intervals between drips lengthen and shorten in patterns that repeat, long sequences, patient as anything else here, and the works are measuring out something, and whatever it is, they are not done measuring it.

The place has no name; it has a function. Deposition wins over dissolution by a hair. Every year the channels are a thumbnail narrower, and in the works' dead corners there are already finished channels — abandoned, clogged, a smooth ribbon of deposited stone running across the terraces like a river embalmed mid-run.
