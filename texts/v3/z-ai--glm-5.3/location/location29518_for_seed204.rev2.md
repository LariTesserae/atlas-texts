**The Still**

The range is too young to have agreed on a name. Maps print three different ones, and the mountains, being new, don't answer to any of them. Climbing it, what you notice first is the sharpness: arêtes like undressed masonry, ledges that still hold their tooling marks, talus that hasn't yet learned to rest. Nothing here is rounded. The range came out of the ground geologically yesterday, and it hasn't had time to be embarrassed by its own angles.

You are high, and it tells you in the usual ways — air thin and businesslike, sun hard and cold, fog lying below you in fields like a second sea. You are above the plant line, and the plant line here is not a line: there is simply nothing green, at any height, in any direction. Stone, ice, sky. The mineral world, with the mineral sky's permission. Set a hand on the rock to rest and the rock rings faintly, like a struck bowl, and your breath leaves you as a small private weather.

Nothing in this landscape announces a city. The city's entire address is a seam — a shoulder of the peak where an overhang weeps and a dark crack, tall as a door and half as wide, exhales a thread of mist on cold mornings. A single cable leaves the shoulder and drops into the cloud fields; whatever is under the clouds is the valley's business. From below, the peak seems to breathe once at dawn and then keep its mouth shut all day.

The seam admits one person sideways. The temperature falls a degree with every body-length inward, as though the mountain is taking your heat as a toll. The first sound is water — single drips, countable, each one a complete sentence. Then the passage opens, and you are inside.

The Hall is a chamber the size of a cathedral's interior, terraced, four hundred meters long, high enough that its ceiling keeps its own climate. Daylight falls into it from directly overhead, through a shaft from the summit snowfield that delivers the sun and the melt through the same throat. The sun and the water arrive by the same door here; that fact organizes everything. The light is honest daylight, a moving column, ordinary, unromantic, warm-looking even on the day it isn't. Every city has a roof. This one's roof is three kilometers of mountain.

And the city under that roof is small the way a violin is small. You can cross it in twenty minutes at a stroll; it would take a week to hear it properly. It is a settlement, not a camp — districts, building codes, a plaza, an unfinished edge — but it holds perhaps forty structures, one per terrace, spaced at intervals the water dictates. Sightlines run long and empty between them. A footstep is an event with an echo.

The interior terrain is wet by design and cold by birth. The walls weep on schedule. Shadowed zones keep frost provinces of their own — feather ice on the cables, rime growing in ferns from every bolt. The melt arrives from above as a bright thread and is braided through channels that are also the streets; the floors are grating so that the water can pass beneath your feet and be audited on the way. The materials are basalt filament wound and sintered, glass that knits its own hairline cracks closed overnight, and ice grown to specification. There is no wood anywhere, because nothing in this country ever grew tall enough to become a chair. No paper, because nothing here was ever a plant. Where the seeps run down the walls there is a faint coin-smell — a varnish-thin film of life simpler than any plant, and it is the bottom of everything.

At the unfinished edge stand the printers: gantries that lay one beam a day of stone lattice, sintering as they go, their clatter carrying across the hall like a typewriter heard from three streets away. The youngest district is tomorrow's. Streets end in fresh rock and bone-colored scaffold. The city is young because the range is — it was founded when the mountains were, and the two are being raised together, siblings on different clocks.

The walls' temperature is set point by point — the machines that do it are called temperers — so that condensation is not an accident here but a timetable. Watch the ceiling at dusk: dew arrives in sequence, panel by panel, the way lights come on in a city with a plan. At first light the hall warms half a degree and everything lets go at once — ten minutes of indoor rain, every morning, the city's alarm. The residents call it the Weep. What the city borrows, it returns: an outflow leaves through the floor of the entrance seam as a rivulet, handed back to the mountain downstream, slightly wiser than it arrived.

The city's work is a single long task, and the task is keeping. In the middle terraces stand the vaults: panes of grown ice racked like library stacks, each pane taking one layer a day — the day's inflow frozen slowly so its dust settles in order and its isotopes sort themselves into a sentence. Where the keepers have something to say, they say it in bubbles, patterned into the lattice as it freezes. You cannot write faster than water freezes. Patience, here, is infrastructure.

White readers travel the racks on rails, pressing tuned probes to the panes. As they pass they hum, and the hum is partly playback: a stored day murmured back at a hundredth of its speed, like birdsong slowed until the birds are continents. Most mountains get their archives only after they've lost their childhood — records begin when someone notices the glaciers going. This range got a diary on its first morning. The first page is nearly clear: one day of good weather, and forty signatures in bubble-script.

The people number a dozen, the Readers, plus the machine staff, which outnumbers them politely. Their clothing pipes body heat to the intake, so that standing still is a small furnace in the service of the melt. Warmth is legal for one hour, in one room, after shift; the conversations there are short and good. The ledger-line over the intake — *nothing drips indoors without a ledger line* — is not a metaphor. Newcomers are given three laws. Water is scheduled; you may not drip freely. Heat is never wasted and never hoarded; every calorie is routed. And the city works at the speed of freezing — nothing water does may be hurried. A fourth law goes unspoken, being the Tithe.

The fauna census is short. Glacier fleas graze the varnish where the seeps run — dark grains that jump like pepper with opinions. A spider holds the corner by the Tithe outflow and has for generations, in the way of a dynasty of one unbroken appetite. Two choughs nest in the lip of the entrance and ride the dawn updraft. Nothing else has asked to live here, and nothing else has been asked.

The quiet is load-bearing. Drips keep the metronome; the temperers hold a chord just below hearing; wind plays the entrance like a flute whose key changes with the weather outside. The beam is the clock — noon is when the column stands vertical in the plaza, and everything else is negotiable. At night, one steady worklight per terrace, nothing theatrical.

So the day runs: the Weep at dawn, the beam crossing east to west with its slow sermon of light, the afternoon gush when the snowfield gives its alms, the temperers shuffling the dew like a dealer with nowhere to hurry. At nightfall comes the minute the city is named for — the Still Hour — when the last drips stop mid-fall and the whole hall holds, balanced exactly between weeping and freezing. The readers do their best work inside that stillness, running the young shelves all night at a murmur.

In winter the pages grow clearest — cold, slow, legible. The snowfield thickens, blizzards seal the mouth for days at a stretch, and the city turns inward like a held note.

And the range is still rising, a few millimeters a year. The city was built with joints that ride the uplift, and the strain gauges in the framing write the mountain's growth into its own diary, a page a year: *taller again*, in script no wider than a cold front. Outside, the talus is slowly learning how to settle; small rockfalls at the mouth are just the range's young clumsiness.

Stand in the plaza at the Still Hour. The hall is holding a million drops in suspense, and your breath hangs in front of you like something borrowed. At midnight exactly, one drop releases — a single tick, the city's whole note for the night. From the valley, all anyone sees is a peak that breathes a thread of mist on cold mornings and keeps its mouth shut. Inside, the diary of a mountain's childhood lies a few centimeters thick and growing a layer a day, written in the only ink this high country has ever trusted: water, taught to hold still.
