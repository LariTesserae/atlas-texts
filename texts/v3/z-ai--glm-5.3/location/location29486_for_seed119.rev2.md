The country is low, and the country is made. Fog comes over it all morning, and under the fog there is no ground — there is paving, terracing, culvert and drain, dry-stone channel and lime-bonded course, work laid down by hands so long finished that the work has become the terrain. Dig anywhere and water stands in the hole before your elbow gets wet. The low land's water rides just under its own manufacture. Moss here grows on plaster, not on rock. The only unbuilt things in sight are weather and birds.

You come to it as a walled pocket in that manufacture: a court twenty paces square, one tree, and in the center a rim of stone with a doorway going down. That is the whole of the place. You can stand at the wall and see all of it at once — boundary, tree, stair, and the low gray sky pressed down like a lid of weather. It is small the way a bell is small. Everything important happens inside.

The lintel over the descending door carries three pictographs, cut deep and re-inked with ochre more times than anyone has counted: water, falling, house. That is its name — it is called exactly what it does. Below the name runs a short row of smaller marks, which we will come to. They are the law.

The stair takes you down in one flight, each tread dished by feet and by water both, the hand-groove along the wall polished to a glassy line. Cold air comes up the stairwell like a draft from a cellar that is also the world's basement. Before you see anything, you hear it — a room that is obviously full of something, though from the top step, in the dimness, you see almost nothing.

Because most of this room is nothing. That is the first fact of it. A hollow of air some twenty paces across and that high again, held up under a corbelled dome of thin flat stones, with a black pane of water at the bottom. The life here is a skin a finger thick, laid onto the walls and the water's edge; the interior is absence. But the absence is load-bearing. It is the body of the instrument — nothing else in the room could hold the sound the way the emptiness does.

The materials: a dove-gray limestone set without metal, dowels of oak and bone swollen tight with wet, lime plaster over everything that water touches, cut into grooves and runnels like veins drawn on purpose. Chisel chatter stands frozen in every face, the little speeches of the makers' tools. There is nothing here that can rust. The house owns only stone, bone, wood, shell, clay, and water, and it has no interest in anything else.

And the water is the point. The pool at the bottom is not a pool exactly — it is a window someone left open onto the water table, and its level rises and falls with tides in the made land that no one has ever seen. That water is warm — warmed by the ground it comes from — while the air in the vault stays cool, and so the building sweats. This is the physics of the whole place: warm water breathing up through cold stone, condensing on every surface, and the builders, whoever they were, refused to let a single drop wander. Every weeping point on the walls is carved to a little knuckle, a boss, a stone tongue, so the drop leaves clean and falls true. From each knuckle a groove runs, joining others, joining runnels, feeding basins — a circulation system for sweat. The grooves are tuned. A drip's tempo is set by the size of its drop-point and the flow feeding it; a basin's pitch is set by its depth; and the basins are also bowls, and the bowls are also instruments. There are fast drips that tick like a sewing bird's feet, medium drips in walking rhythm, and the deep drip, once an hour, a bass note into a stone bowl that makes the whole vault shrug. Two near-identical drips phase against each other somewhere near the entrance, swelling and fading in a slow beat that takes a full breath to come around. A clay siphon gurgles on its own schedule to refill the drinking cup by the stair. A shadoof stands at the pool — dark beam, stone counterweight, gourd — for lifting water to the upper basins by hand. At the threshold, a comb of stone fins combs the fog itself as it enters, stripping it of wet.

The din is not accidental. The dome is a tuned instrument with three flue-holes in its crown; when wind moves outside, the house hums a low chord, and when weather deepens, the chord drops a step. Whisper on the stair and the stair hands your whisper back to you as a phrase. The frogs sing in the rests — whatever the drip ensemble leaves unmeasured, they fill, so that silence never quite occurs. At night the drips thin out with the cold, and the room plays its slow movement, sparse and deep, one note to a breath.

The light is rationed through pinholes between the dome's stones — small stitched points the builders left or cut, nail-heads of light scattered on every surface, moving all day like slow bright animals. One hole is bigger, two hands wide, a true oculus, and it works as a dark room works: the outside sky, inverted, plays faintly on the pool — cloud shadows, the swaying of the fig, any bird that crosses. The fish have learned to read the ceiling. When a shape slides across the projected sky, the water scatters before the thing itself has arrived, and this is the whole tactical situation of the place: images come before bodies, and everyone downstream of light knows it. Moths orbit the nail-heads at dusk. Spiders hang webs across the light-shafts, which look, lit, like harp strings under tension. Once a year, at midwinter noon, one pinhole gathers itself into a blade that walks a carved spiral on the far wall, hour by hour, like a finger tracing.

For a hollow place, it is thick with tenants. The base of everything is a green-gold film that grows on the wet plaster — a paint that eats light and fog and isn't quite a plant. Snails graze it in slow lawnmower lines, and their dried trails have lacquered the walls near every basin, ten thousand generations of mucus glaze sealing the plaster like varnish. Pale crabs work the runnels by day, dark ones by night, picking grit from the grooves, clearing clogs, maintaining the veins of the building as if the job description were carved into them; when a groove clogs beyond a crab's strength, the crab stacks pebbles at the side of it like a flag, and the groove runs again within a day of someone's chisel — someone, no one knows who. Crabs drink at the big condensation events by turning belly-up against the weeping walls, like leaves. The tuning basins flood at those events and hatch fairy shrimp from cysts that mature, breed, and lay down next year's cysts in exactly the number of days the basins take to dry — either the makers tuned the basins to the shrimp, or the shrimp have tuned themselves to the basins; the lintel does not say. In the pool, pale blind fish travel a single slow carousel, a gyre, and at the gyre's center lies an old black eel, and the carousel is the eel's design. Above, in the dome's chambers, swiftlets click and squeak until the resonant vault doubles them into weather; their guano falls on one lit shelf that supports the only true green explosion in the place — ferns, moss, liverwort, a wet hanging garden one arm-span wide — and the green runoff drips down a special channel to seed the pool's algae, and so the whole food web can be traced, link by link, to a single shaft of light and a roost of bats. A kingfisher threads the oculus at odd hours and dives the light-tube; the best-fed birds are the ones that best read their own images. A heron arrives at dawn at the top step, on the fourth beat of the long measure, and the crab day-shift has known its schedule for ten thousand mornings. Warblers come through the court for the fig's fruit, and their droppings seed nothing, because there is nothing here to seed. The fig is the only lawbreaker the house has ever tolerated.

The row of small marks under the name, translated as best as anyone can:

- A drop, then a line: *every drop is owed a path. No water wanders.*
- A seed, then a slash: *nothing unbuilt crosses the rim.* (The fig has roots inside a mortise. The house looks the other way.)
- A hole with light through it: *images arrive before bodies.*
- A spiral, pointing down: *time falls. Count it downward.*
- An open, empty hand beside a finished wall: *add nothing. Repair only.*
- A crab in a channel: *the runnels must run clear.*

The building is not old, exactly. It is finished. It was built slightly out of true, and the water corrected it; grooves were cut nearly right, and the water kept cutting until they were honest. Weather finished this house and then agreed to maintain it: the lime the drips carry away is matched by the snail-glaze laid down; the grooves the drops flatten are re-cut by an unseen hand each dry season — chisel dust on one tread, fresh ochre on the seed-slash, a swept stair, a gourd refilled and hung back on its thong. A keeper, or the memory of one. The house keeps its own counsel.

And so the days run. Dawn brings the fog front, when the comb at the door hisses and every surface in the vault begins to weep at once — the rehearsal, some old writing calls it: the only rain in a world of fog, performed indoors. Pressure shifts; the pool exhales a boom from some surge-cavity beneath it, a sound like a lung clearing its throat; the crabs turn belly-up; the frogs sing in the rests; the whole room rains and rings for half an hour and then subsides, drip by drip, down through the tempos, until the deep drip sounds alone. Midday is image-time on the water. Dusk is chorus and moth. Night is the slow movement.

If you stand through a rehearsal, you will notice the last thing last: that the room holds the sound afterward the way a bell holds it — that this small bounded hollow is, in every way that matters, a landscape, with its own weather, its own calendar, its own economy of hunger and music, all of it farmed from gray air by a machine that has been finished, and therefore never done.

You leave the way you came, upward, and the drips count you out, one by one, as you climb.
