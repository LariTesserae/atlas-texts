# The Cistern of Nine Hundred Voices

It is a valley the size of a large room, and it is indoors.

You reach it the way you reach most true places: by accident, or by inheritance. A door that does not look like a door, in the flank of a mountain that is not entirely a mountain — the rock, if you tap it, answers with the flat, dense knock of something with a substrate under it, something *made*, and the mountain is only its cladding, its skin, weathered and lichened for so long that the seam between geology and engineering has healed over like a scar.

Inside, down a throat of stairs worn glassy by ten thousand years of feet, the valley opens.

## The shape of it

It is long and narrow — you could shout across it, though you won't, because nothing here would hear you. The floor slopes gently toward a central channel. The walls rise on either side, curved, ribbed like the inside of a whale, vanishing upward into a warm fog that the ceiling exhales and re-absorbs. The ceiling itself is never seen. It is a rumor of darkness and drip. Somewhere up there, water is condensing continuously — the whole space is one great lung-slow breath of condensation, the walls sweating, the air thick enough to taste, warm as a pressed towel, warm the way the inside of your own sleeve is warm.

Everything is made. This is the first rule and it takes a while to really *feel* it. The soil is not soil; it is a felted substrate, spongy underfoot, grown or printed or both, brown-black and absorbent, shot through with capillary channels finer than hair that pull water down and away. The stones are stones the way a theater prop is a stone — hollow, warm, tuned. Kick one and it hums a low note that the wall answers. The watercourse is obviously, proudly artificial: a channel of dark ceramic alloy, hexagonal crenellations along its lip, shaped by hands (or by things that had hands) to make the water *sing* as it goes.

And it sings loud. That is the second thing you learn, and you learn it with your whole body.

## The sound

The valley's name is not a poet's invention. It is a functional count. Nine hundred distinct hydraulic voices, roughly — the engineers who built this place tuned every weir, lip, channel, and drop so that the water passing through it plays a chord, a continuous, rolling, enormous chord, built from falling-water percussion and pipe-resonance and the deep organ-drone of the main channel pulling its load downhill. It is deafening in a way that is not painful but total: it fills the bones of your jaw, it blurs your own thoughts into the mix, and within an hour you stop hearing it as sound and start hearing it as weather. Some visitors say the chord is a language. Some say it is a pump schedule. The old maintenance records, if you can find anyone who keeps them, suggest both are true.

Layered through the water-chord is the fauna, because the fauna here is *abundant*, and all of it is loud.

## What lives here

Vegetation is almost an absence — a green film on the wet walls, a few pale ribbons of something algal hanging from the rib-joints, translucent, more like condensation shaped into a plant than a plant proper. Nothing else needs to grow here, because everything here eats the machine, or eats what eats the machine.

The substrate-felt is grazing land. Across it move the **dew-oxen** — fist-sized, dome-shelled, glistening molluskan things that travel in herds of hundreds, moving in slow waves, rasping the biofilm off the made-ground and leaving clean black trails that refill with film within a day. They are warm to the touch. They click to each other, a rhythmic castanet chatter that somehow threads itself into the water-chord without dissonance, as if they were tuned too — or as if they've had nine hundred generations to fit their voices into the available acoustic niches. Probably both. The place is old enough that its animals may simply be features of it now.

Above them, in the fog, the **glasswings** — insects with wet, unreflective wings that fly in swarms so dense they behave like weather, splitting around obstacles, pooling in the warm updrafts that rise from the sweating walls. They are the pollinators of nothing and the food of everything. They land on your skin to drink the condensation and then lift off in a sound like a struck match.

And in the channel itself, in the hex-lipped watercourse, live the **croakers** — something between a salamander and a bell. Big as a forearm, slick, amber-eyed, absolutely unbothered by current. Each one sits in a tuned eddy — a hollow in the channel engineered to make a specific note — and *adds* to that note, drone-singing, the whole population together holding the water-chord in tune the way a choir supports an organ. Whether they're performing maintenance, worship, or simply keeping their territories warm is one of the place's standing, pleasant mysteries. When something startles them — a shadow crossing the ceiling-fog, a dew-oxen herd clicking too fast — they all dive at once, and for a heartbeat the chord loses its bottom half, and the whole valley feels suddenly cold, floor to fog, like a held breath.

Then they resurface one by one, and the bass comes back.

## The light

There is almost none, and none is needed. What illumination exists comes from the channel: the ceramic alloy glows faintly, a low ember-orange, brighter where the water moves fastest, so that the whole watercourse reads as a vein of banked fire running the length of the valley, and everything in the place is lit from below. It is theater lighting. It was probably always theater lighting. The dew-oxen shells catch it and shine like wet chestnuts. The fog holds a portion of it and lets it go, so that the air itself has a faint warmth of color, a dimness that is not emptiness but *saturation* — everything near everything, every surface touching something else's reflection, the density of the place expressed as light: nothing far, everything close.

## The rules of the world

- **Nothing here is natural, and nothing here is artificial anymore.** The distinction expired millennia ago. The machine and its ecology are one organism now, in balance the way a reef is in balance — which is to say constantly, violently, and invisibly.
- **The water is the clock.** The chord shifts as flow shifts; the daily condensation cycle (the ceiling exhales heavier in the artificial night, the weeping walls run harder) changes the music's key, and everything alive here reads the key like a calendar. Croakers spawn on the flood-chord. Dew-oxen migrate from wall to channel on the ebb.
- **The sound is a floor.** Stand still long enough and you can feel which direction the water is moving
