# The Chorus Hollow

There is a place where the plants learned to build, and building, forgot they were plants. Or perhaps it is the other way around — perhaps the settlement was always alive and only pretended, for a few centuries, to be architecture. Either way the distinction has dissolved, and what remains is the Hollow: a single grove-room of woven living mass, low in a river basin where the water comes and goes according to no calendar anyone trusts anymore.

## The Shape of It

You enter through a gap between two buttress roots each wider than a doorway, and the sound hits you first — before your eyes adjust, before your feet register the give of the floor, the sound, which I will return to because it cannot be left, because it is the true weather of this place.

The Hollow is small. A person could cross it in sixty unhurried steps, and most people take far longer because nothing here permits hurry. It is a room the size of a modest cathedral nave, if the nave had grown rather than been raised, if the vaulting had been braided rather than stone-cut, if every pillar still drank. The walls are living stem-tissue, layered like the pages of a book left out in rain — you can peel back a strip and find beneath it another surface, and beneath that another, strata of growth going down further than the eye can follow. Some layers are green. Some are amber-dark, dense as old hardwood. A few, deep down near the base, are translucent as membrane, and light comes *through* them from somewhere, though the basin's sky is overcast and ordinary.

The ceiling is a lattice of interlocking crowns, not one species but eleven, braided by wind and by something older than wind into a roof that admits a green-filtered half-light. Not gloom. Just green. Everything in the Hollow is seen through leaves, including the leaves themselves.

## Water That Cannot Decide

The moisture here is indecisive in a way that feels deliberate. The floor is not ground but a mat of root and fallen husk, spongy, dark, and always faintly damp — never wet, never dry, a persistent exhale of humidity that beads on every surface and runs in slow veins down the pillars. Twice a day, or perhaps at intervals governed by something other than days, the basin collects a rain that falls only *inside* the Hollow — a fine mist that descends from the braided ceiling with no clouds above to explain it, condensing out of the roof itself, which sweats the river's water back into the room like a gland. The water goes up through the roots as vapor, crosses the living wall, and falls as mist on the same floor it rose from. Nothing enters. Nothing leaves. The Hollow keeps its own water the way a body does.

At the room's lowest point, a single slow pool, black and tea-colored from tannin, no larger than a bathtub. It never overflows and never empties. Things drink from it. You will see.

## The Built and the Grown

There was construction here once, or something like construction, and the evidence is everywhere and nowhere. alcoves in the walls have been shaped — genuinely *shaped*, with edges too regular for accident — into shelves, benches, recessed niches at hand height. Stairs of living wood spiral up three pillars, their treads worn smooth and concave by feet that must have numbered in the tens of thousands, though the Hollow is empty now of anyone who could have worn them. A bridge of braided aerial root crosses above the pool, and the braid is a *knot*, a deliberate one, tied by hands or by something that had hands, and then grown over and healed and kept.

But there are no tools. No metal, no fired clay, no cut stone, nothing dead. Whatever built here built by persuading growth rather than opposing it — bent stems into arches that hardened into girders, trained vines into lashings that thickened into beams. The whole settlement might be one long negotiation, frozen mid-sentence. And the negotiation continues: doorways are a centimeter narrower this year than last. Some have already closed entirely, sealed by new wood, patient as a glacier. The Hollow is slowly eating its own rooms.

## The Sound — and Here I Must Be Honest

It is deafening here. This must be said plainly and not softened.

The Hollow roars. Not with wind, not with machinery — with *everything at once*. The eleven braided canopy species each shed seed pods on their own rhythm, and the pods split with reports like knuckles cracking, hundreds per minute, overlapping. The root-mat underfoot crackles continuously with the expansion of fibers drinking. The pillars creak in tones low enough to feel in the sternum. Over, under, and through all of it runs the fauna: birds that do not sing but *percuss*, hammering resonant hollow stems in interlocking patterns; amphibians in the black pool producing a sustained pulsing drone that never breaks for sleep; insects in the layered walls clicking in staggered millions. The sound has texture — you can hear depth in it, near sounds and far sounds, a vertical architecture of noise rising through the canopy.

And underneath, sometimes, if you hold still long enough to bruise your stillness against it: a voice. Or something shaped like one. The Hollow's resonance, gathered from ten thousand small sounds, occasionally *almost* resolves into a word. Almost. Never arriving. The residents, if there ever were residents, would have spoken loudly, all the time, or not at all.

## The Life That Fills It

Fauna is abundant past crowding. Every surface crawls, flutters, or grips. Flightless bright-scaled birds the size of fists run the braided stairs like commuters. A colony of pale, eyeless grazers moves along the wall-layers, eating the oldest strata and opening the translucent ones to the light, which is possibly the Hollow's own slow way of making windows. Over the pool hangs a constant shimmer of mating insects, their wing-noise another instrument in the roar. Predator and prey pass each other closely here, almost politely — there is nowhere to run in a room, so running was never evolved into anyone, and the hunting that happens is slow, mutual, and mostly off-stage, in the deeper wall-layers where the grazers disappear and do not always come back.

## Where It Breaks

And all of this — the braiding, the knotwork, the mist that falls from no cloud — sits on top of a place where reality has thinned. You notice it in the light first: the green half-light has sources it shouldn't. Stand near a translucent basal stratum and look *through* it and there is daylight on the far side, daylight and the suggestion of a landscape that is not the basin, not the overcast sky — a landscape that does not match the Hollow's dimensions, that has more distance in it than the Hollow has room for. The pool shows constellations at noon. The stairs, followed upward, arrive somewhere other than the roof they visibly connect to — you can see the connection, treads to lintel, whole and honest, and also watch a bird take the last step and vanish into a somewhere else, walking calmly, not falling.

The deeper wall-strata are the worst or best of it. Peel back enough layers — and the walls permit this, they seem to *want* it — and the amber-dark wood gives way to the membrane-layers, and the membrane-layers give way to a darkness that is not shadow but absence, and out of that absence comes sound that belongs to no creature present: footsteps, sometimes, unhurried, circling the room's outside, though the room has no outside that isn't river basin, and the basin is silent.

None of the animals mind. That is the detail that finally unsettles or finally calms, depending on the visitor. The grazers eat toward the transparency. The birds hammer their resonant stems harder when the footsteps come. The whole ecosystem has metabolized the brokenness, incorporated it, built its rhythms on it the way cities build on rivers. The thinning is not a wound here. It is a *feature*. It may be load-bearing.

## The Hollow at Rest

At what can only be called evening — the canopy-filters darken, the mist thickens — the roar does not diminish but *reorganizes*, the daytime percussion giving way to a slower, tidal swelling, drone and creak and pool-pulse, the whole room breathing at the length of hours. The pool reflects stars that are overhead, or were once, or will be. The stairs wait with their worn treads. The knot in the bridge holds whatever it was tied to hold.

Nothing needs anything here. That is the Hollow's final character, the thing under all the noise: a room that keeps itself, water cycling, walls eating their own doors, light leaking in from elsewhere, life stacked on life in every stratum — an ecosystem at balance, in its own state, settled into its own permanence, which happens to include a few holes in the world, treated as decoratively as anything else.

Sixty unhurried steps to cross it. Most visitors take much longer. Some, following the stairs up, or the grazers down, do not cross it at all — they simply arrive elsewhere, and the Hollow does not remark on it, because the Hollow, roaring with every surface it owns, has never once in its long life remarked on anything.
