You hear the Kettle before you see it.

The trail comes up the mountain's shoulder through standing fog, switchbacking on stone that the rain keeps black and shining, and for the last hour the sound of the place assembles itself ahead of you piece by piece. First water — a layered, unmistakable plumbing of water, drips on leaves over runs over seeps, dozens of small waters running at different pitches. Then birds, so many and so various that they stop being birds and become a texture, a fabric of noise hung in the air. Then, faint under the birds, the tap-tap-tap of wood on wood, and human whistles, long and articulate, the way other valleys use sentences.

The trail crests, and the fog thins, and there it is: a bowl. A green bowl about a thousand paces across, set high on the flank of the range like a thumbprint, brimming with mist and forest and noise.

The bowl is old in the way mountains are old. Somewhere far back, beyond anyone's memory or anyone's grandmother's grandmother's memory, a section of the slope let go and slid, and then the mountain stopped moving and started weathering instead. The scar has had centuries to settle into itself. It is terraced now, softened, gullied into shallow ravines, everything rounded and finished and in balance — the kind of landscape that has already become whatever it was going to become. Water is the one thing still at work here. It comes out of the terrace face in a spring line halfway up the far wall, where the rocks weep — genuinely weep, whole cliffs of black stone furred with green and streaked white, water beading on every surface, gathering, letting go. A dozen threads run down the face and braid through the bowl's floor, joining, splitting, finally leaving through a single notch at the low rim, where the combined stream drops away into the fog below and is gone.

Everything else in the bowl is plants. Not plants on the land — the plants are the land. The ground underfoot isn't soil so much as a mattress of living green, decades of moss and liverwort and creeping fern, springy and dark and waterlogged. The ravines are cane brakes. The slopes are bamboo, a native kind with slender canes that grow in leaning groves and creak in wind. Tree ferns rise out of the moss with their black fibrous trunks and their great feathered crowns, and every horizontal surface — branch, cane, stone, roof, gutter, walkway — carries its freight of epiphytes: bromeliads like gray-green fountains, orchids, more moss, small ferns growing out of the moss, moss growing on the ferns. Rain barely touches the ground here. It is intercepted, absorbed, held, released. The whole bowl is a sponge that happens to have a village in it.

The hedge that rings the settlement is the boundary of the world, as far as the Kettle's people are concerned. It's a pleached wall of hardwood and laurel, woven living branch into living branch, four centuries of tending gone into it, and it stands maybe eight feet thick and fifteen high. Beyond it, the forest is "unkept" — same plants, same moss, but gone feral and self-willed, which to a Kettle ear sounds different, the way an empty house sounds different. Inside the hedge, the plants are kept, and keeping is the whole of the local civilization.

Because that's the honest description of the Kettle: it is not built. It is kept.

The town has about eight hundred people, and you can walk across it in a quarter of an hour, but that quarter-hour is as dense as a city. There is no open ground. There are no walls in the ordinary sense — a house here is a woven form of living trees, pleached frames of hardwood with rattan and bamboo worked between, the gaps filled by living fern and moss until the wall is green all the way through. The roofs are stitched thatch, palm and grass sewn down with bark cord, and within a year every new roof sods itself over with moss until the roof is alive too. Eaves nearly touch across the lanes, and the lanes themselves are raised lattice of split cane laid over the mud, so the whole town can be walked dry-shod, under awnings, in the rain. Smoke rises from the roofs in thin threads and vanishes into the moss, which filters it; the moss stays dry inside, which is the trick, because dry moss is a house and wet moss is a ruin. Every hearth is stone-lined, every floor is cane over a bed of charcoal and crushed shell to drink the damp, and everyone's breath fogs at dawn.

At the center stands the fig. A strangler fig, centuries old, whose original host rotted out long ago and left behind a hollow lattice of fused aerial roots — a nave of living columns, a green cathedral the size of a good barn, into whose cage-shadows the whole town can fit. This is the law-house. Disputes are heard inside it, the voices flattened by the fig's own acoustics, and by custom every testimony is spoken twice, because in the Kettle nothing said once can be trusted to have been heard.

The tools are few and all hand-fitted: ground-stone adzes, obsidian flakes traded up from the lowlands in corked gourds, bone needles, shell scrapers, a little cold-hammered native copper for awls. There is no wheel-turning industry here; the industry is pruning. Building a house is a courtship. A couple plants the frame at betrothal — cuttings from the nursery yard, hardwood posts set in the spongy ground — and by the time the marriage is three or four years old, the posts have taken and the pleaching can begin, and the couple moves in. Pruning rights are what people actually own; nobody owns a tree, people own the right to a tree's behavior, marked with knots and cords and stripes of vegetable dye on the bark. A single hedge can carry five families' rights on its five faces. And the one law that means exile is simple: you may not cut a bearer-beam. Anything living that holds weight belongs to the future, not to you.

All of this would be a quiet, mossy paradise if not for two facts. The first is the water. Fog season wraps the bowl for weeks, and the fog condenses on every leaf and the town rains indoors — steady, ticking, inside-rain, the whole settlement drinking aloud. The gutters are split bamboo run and moss check-dams, and they never stop; you fall asleep inside the sound of the building's thirst.

The second fact is the animals, because the animals outnumber the people by some uncountable factor and behave accordingly. At first light the dawn chorus arrives like weather — a solid wall of sound, thrushes and wrens and tinamous and things no one has named, the leaf-shriek of cicadas cutting through, so dense that conversation is physically impossible until mid-morning. This is why the Kettle's language has a whistled register, a drum-tap register, a finger-code for market days; speech here evolved in self-defense. Hummingbirds work the flowering hedges by the hundred, hot little engines in the cold air. Moths come to the evening lamps in blizzards. The true rival of the town is not any beast but the leafcutter ants, the other nation, whose columns would strip a living house to its bones in a week; the people fight them with decoy mulch heaps of preferred leaves at the boundary, and with flocks of tamed little brown tickbirds that patrol the walls in chattering shifts, trading pest-control for crumbs and warm roosts. Every roof keeps its own snake, a slender resident of the thatch who is politely knocked to before entering, because it eats the mice and is therefore household. Frog ponds in the gutter steads sing all night at a volume that outdoes the stream — the smallest livestock with the largest voices. Cuy rustle in their woven pens. Ducks raid the settling pools where the little fish live. A hawk works the rooftops at noon like a toll-taker. And sometimes at the hedge's edge the herders find prints in the moss, big and round, and the whole town speaks a little more quietly for a day, out of respect for the Quiet One.

None of it is ever finished. That's the thing a visitor takes longest to understand. The Kettle is old — four hundred years of continuous keeping, the same lanes, the same plan — and yet nothing in it is older than a grandfather's memory, because every beam, wall, roof, and walkway is organic and gets replaced in its season while the plan persists. The town is like a standing argument: green trying to become forest, people trying to keep it a town, the argument churning daily, snick-snick of blades from before dawn till after dusk, retying crews out in every storm, the hedgerows flowering and shedding and being resharpened. Storm season brings afternoon thunder that turns the notch-stream brown and loud; fog season brings the mold and the lamps and the inside rain; bloom season brings the birds and the honey harvest; market day brings the herders down from the higher grasslands with wool and salt, and the traders up from below with obsidian and dried fish, and the bowl fills with shouting and smoke and meat and gourds and cuttings and tame birds until dusk empties it like a held breath let go.

Then the fog comes back into the bowl, brimming it like milk in a cup. Lamps bloom along the lanes, each with its private blizzard of moths. The whistles go out calling children home across the noise. Somewhere the fig hums with its wasps, water ticks and runs on a thousand surfaces at once, and the Kettle settles into its nightly work of being kept — not built, kept. This place is not a thing. It is an act, performed continuously by eight hundred people, one hedge, a snake in every roof, and a nation of ants at the door.
