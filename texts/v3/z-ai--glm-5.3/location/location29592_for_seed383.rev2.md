The stairs go down for the better part of a day, and the light comes up to meet you before anything else does. That is the first wrongness, and it is mild: you are deep underground, below the last roots of any surface world, and yet you descend into brightness. The passage opens without ceremony, and you step out onto a ledge above a buried valley — a league of terraced stone, streets and stairways and cut-fronted dwellings, a whole town's fabric — roofed by a ceiling two hundred strides overhead. The ceiling is the sky here, and the sky is the light. It glows. Not lamps, not fire: the high roof sweats, condensation gathering on its cold underside, and where the damp gathers, a film grows. It is not a plant. Nothing here is a plant. It is a living shine, thin as breath on a window, and it spreads wherever water spreads, and it gives off a pale green-gold light strong enough to throw your shadow down the stair.

Brightness, then, is weather. The damp film feeds on condensation, and condensation comes and goes in slow tides, so the sky waxes and wanes. For a day or two it will brighten — the old ones read this the way sailors read a falling glass — and then the roof sheds its water and it rains indoors. Brief, patchy, cold rain, falling a long way, landing in the cut gutters and runnels that stripe every pillar and terrace face, running down into cisterns at the street corners. Then the sky dims where it has wrung itself out, and the dry districts go to a faint ember-light, and the wet districts burn pale and steady. Wet wall is wealth here. Property lines follow driplines. A household is measured by the length of wall it can keep damp.

The stone under all this is white, and it is soft. You can mark it with a thumbnail; wet, it goes greasy; dry, it sheds a dust fine as flour. It cuts with bone — most tools are bone, licker-tooth chisels, scrapers hafted with hide — and the few blades of hard imported stone are heirlooms, handed down like names. This softness is the whole secret of the place. From any distance the city looks ancient beyond argument: every carved edge, every spire, every stair-riser has been rounded and softened and rubbed down, because soft stone cannot hold sharpness for long. The skyline is melted, the towers are stumps, the profiles are settled as river cobbles. But reach out and touch the nearest wall and it is newborn — fresh chisel-marks, a door cut this morning, a sill still damp from the casting. The place is ancient at a distance and in arm's reach brand new, and both of these are true every single day, because the city is never finished and never allowed to fall.

That is the turbulence, and it is everywhere, pocketed. Crews work the collapse sites in silent clusters, unmaking rooms that have gone rotten and re-cutting them a handspan further into the rock. Staircases migrate; a lane that ran straight for a decade develops a dog-leg around a soft seam and the map-makers, such as they are, shrug and redraw. In the repair market the blocks are traded — puddle-blocks, they call them: the white dust swept up from the floors, mixed with cistern water, cast in hide forms, and left to set, because this stone dissolves slightly in seep-water and re-solidifies again, so that dust becomes block becomes wall becomes dust in one long patient cycle. Water is their fire; what other places do with kilns and flame, this place does with wet. The market haggling is done with fingers, offer and counter-offer flickered by hand, because there is almost nothing else to haggle with.

For this is the second great fact of the valley: it is nearly silent. The stone is so porous that the air itself seems soft; sound goes into the walls and dies within a few strides. Shouting is a waste of breath and a mild obscenity. Instead the people speak through the rock — raps and taps given to a pillar, traveling the architecture as vibration, read through the soles of the feet and the fingertips, a long conversation conducted on a wall like a drum with the skin of the whole city. The day's loudest events are small ones: a set block clapped into place. A drip finding its cistern with a struck-bell note. When the slow draft through the rift turns, a whole face of borer-riddled stone breathes a faint chord through its thousand holes, so low at the edge of hearing that you feel it in the teeth. That is the storm of this place. Everything chaotic, everything churning, and all of it quiet as snowfall.

And it does churn, in every register. The food web runs on the shine. The film eats the stone itself — minerals, seep, the faint chemistry of dissolution — and the lickers eat the film: pale, many-legged, blind, soft-mouthed things that graze the walls and ceiling in slow herds. Watch a wall long enough and it moves. There are thousands of them; the wet districts ripple. The borers riddle the dying stone with galleries, softening it further, and the carvers use those same borings as scaffolding notches, climbing the animals' ruins. Above the herds stand the pale herons, long-legged and motionless for days at a time, mistaken again and again for statuary until one takes a licker with a strike that makes no sound at all. Nothing in this ecology has a voice. The people sit in it like one more quiet species: they farm the shine by watering walls, tending runnels, scraping bloom into bowls to eat — sweet as chalk, faintly sour — and penning lickers against the wet faces. The air is cellar-cool everywhere, the stone colder, and the work keeps you warm.

The city eats into its own past as it goes. Behind walls there are older walls; doorways get bricked and re-cut a stride over; whole stairways deliver you to rooms that were streets once. The lowest level is the oldest cut of all, the first terrace, primordial, its features nearly flattened under white silt, given over to the lickers and the dim bloom — you can walk it for an hour and find nothing but the smooth vagueness of something too old to have features left. And up through the middle strata the population is thin for the city's size: half an hour between lamps, whole avenues glowing and empty, the anthill energy gathered in pockets — a crew here, the market, a wet-wall farm — with silence and soft dust between.

The laws of the place, such as they are, hold everything together. Brightness means water; water means food; so light is read like weather and the economy follows the glow. Sound does not carry, so the stone carries it. Nothing sharp survives, so nothing is built to last, and everything is built again. The shape of the city never changes; the city itself never stops changing. What comes apart is never lost.

Stand on a high terrace at dusk — though dusk is only a dimming of the sky when it tires — and you may see the one thing that moves the whole sparse population at once. The ceiling has been brightening all day, and now, sector by sector, it begins to sweat. The rain comes. And from every quarter of the buried valley the people set down their chisels and move to the runnels and cisterns, thousands of quiet hands catching water in the light of a sky that is raining, and the sound of all of it together is less than a single street in any loud city of the surface. They do not call this place a city, as it happens. They call it the Work, because it has never once been a thing that exists — only a thing being done.
