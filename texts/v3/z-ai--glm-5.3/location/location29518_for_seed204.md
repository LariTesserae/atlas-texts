This is not a dead world; it is a world ahead of its life, mineral from summit to base, green nowhere. The rock is glassy where the melt streams braid across it, and the streams themselves are young too: they gather, run thirty meters, and vanish into fissures, so that the mountain swallows its own rivers and you hear them falling somewhere inside. From the summit, on cold mornings, the mountain smokes — fumarole steam standing off the peak in slow banners, though nothing burns.

Snow is the weather here the way sunlight is the weather elsewhere. It comes often, thin dry grains that hiss against the rock, and it is the city's bank: the year's water, deposited at altitude, drawn down through the accounts of fissure and fracture. Frost shattering works the outcrops day and night — now and then a sharp report cracks across a cirque, and a fresh facet of stone rotates loose and slides, and this is the mountain's most violent sound. The air is thin enough that visitors count stairs.

You would cross the whole flank and miss the entrance. The Slot is a vertical crease in a weeping wall, a hand's width of shadow between two faces that run with meltwater even when nothing is falling from the sky. Slot swallows nest at its lip — small gray birds, the only wild creatures that use the door. Stand near it and you feel the mountain breathe: cold air slides down your collar on the inbreath, warm damp air lifts your hair on the outbreath, and the flag the settlers keep at the mouth hangs or lifts accordingly. The city reads its own breath as forecast. When the Slot exhales hard for an hour, a storm is within hours.

Inside is a city.

This is the fact that takes the longest to accept: the whole of it — streets, cisterns, plazas, twelve inhabited terraces and thirty printed but empty — folded into a fault-widened pocket inside the mountain, a hollow you can walk across in a quarter of an hour, rim to floor, and never once be rained on. The surveyors call the massif the Kettle, because its snow went in the top and its steam came out the vents, and they call the city Wick, because a wick draws water up, and so does the city.
