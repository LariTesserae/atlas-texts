# The Reservoir Cistern

Beneath the city — beneath several cities, actually, layered atop one another like sediment — there is a room the size of a cathedral, and the room is full of noise.

Not echo. Noise. The deep, structured roar of water being *managed*.

## The Architecture

The cistern is old in the way only maintained things can be old. Nothing here is ruined; everything is worn. The columns — three hundred of them, or two thousand, depending on whether you count the ones that have been absorbed into newer infrastructure — are hexagonal, cast from something that isn't quite concrete. Their surfaces are slick with condensation and faintly warm, like the flank of a large animal. Run a hand along one and it hums at a frequency you feel in your teeth rather than hear.

The ceiling is lost in darkness. Not far up — you can tell from the way sound behaves, the tight acoustic ceiling of the roar — but no light reaches it. The columns simply rise and keep rising and then stop being relevant.

Water moves through this place constantly, but you rarely see it. It is *inside* things. It moves in channels under the floor grating, in the hollow cores of the columns, in pressurized veins that run exposed across the vaulted spaces like plumbed anatomy. Occasionally a joint weeps. The weeping is the only visible water: a slow bead-line down a column, a dark patch spreading on the floor, a place where the ceiling sweats and droplets fall into a basin that has been catching them for so long it has worn its own stone.

Every few hours, one of the veins *pulses* — a deep hydraulic thud that travels through the floor into your boots, followed by a surge in the roar, then a slow subsiding. Someone, or something, upstream is drawing water. The cistern knows the schedule. You can learn it too.

## The Sound

This is the defining fact of the place: it is loud here in a way that is almost biological. A constant multi-layered thrum:

- The bass drone of pressure in the main arteries
- The hiss of flow through regulator gates, changing pitch as demand shifts
- Irregular percussion — valve-closures, distant hammerings from the metro-structure above
- And beneath all of it, so low it might be imagined, a resonance that seems to come from the water table itself, the sound of an aquifer breathing

Conversation is impossible at a normal distance. The few beings who work here communicate by touch — a tap on the shoulder, a pressed palm, a vocabulary of grips.

## What Lives Here

Almost nothing, and the space makes that felt. The cistern is vast and hollow; your footsteps and your voice are the only small sounds, and they vanish into the machinery's noise immediately, making you feel briefly nonexistent.

But not quite nothing. Patches of pale biofilm grow where the weeping joints dampen the walls — thin, matte, faintly luminous in the way of things that have never needed to be seen. In the condensation gutters, small blind crustaceans navigate the wet edges, no larger than a fingernail, thriving on the warmth and the mineral seep. They are the entire visible fauna, and their lives are presumably very simple.

Something larger is occasionally heard. A splash, once, from a channel that should hold nothing but flow. The workers have a gesture for it — two fingers, then a shrug. Meaning: *acknowledged, not investigated.*

## The Tech

The cistern's systems are old enough to have become landscape, and sophisticated enough that no one currently living fully understands them. The water routes itself. Pressure anomalies are detected, compensated, and logged somewhere no one has found. Self-repairing seals extrude a rubbery resin at stress points, decades after their installation, without instruction.

The newest layers — and you can see the strata, in styles of pipe, in generations of weld — sit atop the ancient ones without replacing them. The old system was never turned off. It simply got buried in its own successors. It is all still running. Down at the bottom of that stack, there may be machinery nobody alive has ever looked at, performing its function with perfect patience, waiting for the day it's needed, which is what machines do.

## The Atmosphere

It is warm. Comfortably, cellar-warmly warm — the heat of a million liters and a hundred years of pumps. The air smells of wet mineral, hot dust, and the faint ozone of whatever the luminous biofilms exhale. Humidity sits at that level where your skin never dries and your clothes slowly give up.

Stand still long enough and the place reveals its rhythm: the pulse-cycles, the gate-shifts, the far-off shudder of trains in some higher stratum, the almost-subliminal aquifer-breath. It is not empty, exactly. It is *occupied* — by water, by pressure, by process. You are simply the only thing here that is optional.

And that, in the end, is the cistern's character: a cathedral built by no one and maintained by everything, full of a sound like a sleeping giant's circulation, dark and warm and vast, holding the city's thirst in its hollow bones.
