Stand on the lip of the Hollow and the wind of the Overhead pushes at your back — cold, thin, smelling of old snow — while below you the land simply stops. The plateau ends at a raw edge of stone, the Cutface, and drops away into a bowl six miles wide and twenty long, floored in green. There is no gorge yet, no gorge smoothness anywhere; the wall is young, angular, still shedding. The people up here call the whole thing the Below, and they say it quickly, the way you name a place you don't intend to go.

Within living memory this bowl was the Old Water — a deep cold lake held against the Cutface. Then, in the grandmothers' time, the outlet sill at the south end failed. The lake ran off through what is now called the Throat in a matter of days, and what it left behind is still deciding what it wants to be. From the rim you can see the whole argument at once: the flat grey-green floor, the braided river crossing it in a loose silver script, the black line of willow carr along the scarp's foot, the small strings of ponds, the pale notches of gravel bars, and — small as spilled beans on the far half of the floor — the town.

The path down is a zigzag on a ramp of talus, and the descent is measured in your ears. The plateau wind falls away behind you step by step. Ten minutes down, your own footfalls are the loudest thing about you. By the bottom, even those are gone, eaten by moss.

Because the first thing to understand about the Hollow is that the lake never left. It went under. A hundred feet of silt — centuries of fine grey sediment dropped through still water — still holds the Old Water the way a sponge holds a spill. This is the floor's governing rule: dig anywhere, anywhere at all, and the hole fills within minutes. Press your thumb into the ground and feel the ground press back, cold and wet, like dough. The whole valley is a lake wearing a coat of plants, and every process here — the seeps, the ponds, the mist, the marsh itself — is that lake still trying to find its level.

The Cutface weeps. All along its base, along miles of raw rock, the aquifer the lake left behind comes out through the fracture line in a continuous spring-row the town calls the Weeps — a thousand small clear trickles too numerous to count or name, each one laying down an orange fan of iron minerals on the scree, fans that widen year by year like slow spilled paint. In this season the Weeps freeze overnight into beards of curtain-ice, grey-white, bluntly beautiful, and calve off in the mid-morning thaw with a crackle that carries. It rained on the Overhead two days ago and the wall has been shedding that rain ever since; the whole scarp is in slow supply, dark-streaked, glistening, dripping on a delay.

The river is called the Braid because that is all it is — a young river still learning its bed, spread across the silts in a dozen shifting channels that migrate visibly week to week. Its gravel bars are being sorted and re-sorted; you could visit the same bar in March and again in autumn and find it a different shape, a different place. The Braid makes the only sustained sound in the Hollow, and it makes it softly, a hush rather than a roar, something between breathing and static, that you hear from everywhere and from nowhere in particular.

The cold here is the kind that makes lushness strange. Frost stands on the north side of every hummock all day. The shallow pools skin over each night, grow a rim of grey ice a finger thick, chirp as it tightens in the dark hours — a sound like small glass bells, small enough to fit the valley's volume — and then thin and vanish by noon. But under and between the frost, everything grows with an almost alarming willingness, because the drained lakebed is young silt, mineral-rich, virgin ground, and pioneer green runs across it like fire running up a fuse. Sedge meadows stand thigh-deep and thicker than sedge has any right to be. The willow carr is so dense that a lynx can cross a mile of it without touching the ground. Moss covers everything not actively moving — rock, log, gravel, the shoulders of the paths — and then more moss covers the moss. There are more surfaces here than there is ground for, so the plants have stacked.

And all of it is young. That is the second rule of the Hollow: nothing here is old. The oldest tree in the valley is younger than the oldest woman in the town, and she remembers when this floor was open water and the mud smoked as it dried. The willows are the first willows. The birches climbing the Cutface's talus terraces — old beach lines from the vanished lake, stacked like stairs, each bench a different age — are first-generation trees racing up the steps. On the raw faces of the Cutface itself, the lichen has barely begun: pale coins of it scattered across the dark rock, and the town reads them like a calendar. A patch the size of your thumbnail has been there since the drain. A patch the size of your hand was there before your mother was born. Nothing measures time in the Hollow except what grows.

Out on the open silts the marsh is beginning to make peat — sphagnum advancing over the bare silt shallows in a slow, visible front, hummock by hummock, each hummock gaining its tiny tenants: the red-veined sundews, the bog-cotton gone to loose white seed, the low mats of cranberry that the town's children pick after the first frosts, the one sweet thing this ground offers without being asked. The cranberries come up sugar-cold, frosted on one side. They taste like the water here, which tastes like the rock: clean, and faintly of iron.

The animal life is abundant and nearly silent, which is the Hollow's deepest paradox — a place packed to its seams and quiet as a held breath. The frogs are already down in the mud, sealed for the winter. The insects are overwintering as eggs threaded through the moss, all the summer hum banked and waiting — for in July the mosquito rises off the marsh in a wall and the valley has its one loud season, and the town lives in smoke-smudge and grim humor until it passes. Now, in the falling season, the ducks go out in high, almost soundless strings, a few birds leaving each dusk. A heron stands in the shallows of the Weep-ponds for hours, a grey fixture of the cold. Swans come over at dusk with nothing but a soft whirr of wing, like fabric tearing slowly, very far up. Char have come up through the Throat from the old downstream waters, and every year they have pushed their frontier further up the braids, a colonization in progress you could map if you walked it: this pool held fish for the first time three years ago, this one last spring. The valley is still being found by its own animals.

And the beaver. The beaver keep the waterworks. Their dams string the Weep-line ponds into staircased beads, hold the wet season back against the dry, and turn what would be a rushing, leaking system into a patient one, a system with basements. The town has an explicit rule about this, one of its few explicit rules: never unmake another's arrangement. They read the dams as larders and cisterns both, and they leave them standing.

The town itself sits on the Long Reach — a storm-beach of the Old Water, a raised spine of lake-washed cobbles running arrow-straight for two miles through the marsh, the only naturally dry footing on the whole floor. The cobble spine is why the town is what it is: dry ground is so scarce here that everyone has had to stand on it together, and the town packs along the Reach like a strung bead of its own — fifteen hundred people, maybe two, shoulder to shoulder for two miles, a linear city with no room to sprawl and no wish to. The houses are sod domes over dug pits, willow ribs woven and trained into hoops, walls of stacked turf, roofs thatched in sedge bundles and rimmed with stones carried up from the shore one at a time, each stone chosen already-round from the lake's old tumbling. The lanes between the domes are sunk below path-level, floored in laid bundles, and they flood shin-deep in the wettest weeks, which the town regards as normal, the way other places regard rain.

The covenant under everything, the third rule, the one the children can recite: nothing here is made; everything is arranged. The valley gives silt, reed, willow, turf, fish, and stone that is already shaped, and the people's craft is placement, training, weaving, and keeping. Their technology is entirely alive. Their fences root. Their weir stakes are willow wands driven into the Braid's narrows, which sprout and have to be pruned back every spring — living weirs, growing weirs, fish-traps that need tending like crops. The walkways out over the marsh to the weir-sites and the garden-islands are woven from living willow, roots gripping silt, a latticework you walk on and that walks with you, creaking faintly, dropping its yellow leaves into the water below. The bridges heal themselves. The boats are bundles of reed, lashed with twisted sedge cordage, blunt-ended and buoyant, hissing softly when hauled out — and in the weeks before the Locking they stand upright in rows along the shore, a fence of boats, drying for winter. The only edged things in town are stones the water edged first, found in the talus and carried home, kept in soft sheaths of hide, used for peeling reed and cutting sod and nothing else. No one has ever struck a spark off a rock to make something with an edge. They strike sparks for fire — every pit-house keeps a stone-basin hearth, clay-lined, tended low and constant, the one borrowed heat in a cold town — but fire here is kept, not made with, and the smoke rises thin and blue from a hundred smoke-holes and flattens by mid-morning under the light.

Inside a house the arrangement is layered in time. Each pit-house is dug where a house has always stood; floors are re-skinned with fresh sod each generation and the old floors become strata beneath, mixed with shell and bone and ash and lost beads, and so the town is rising — the whole Reach climbs a handspan per generation on the compacted evidence of its own living. They are building their own ground as they stand on it. A town's archaeology is forming underfoot in real time, warm side up.

The quiet is not emptiness; it is saturation. That is what you understand, standing on the Reach at dusk. The mist rebuilds every evening from the water outward — cold air settling onto water still warm enough to give — and by dark the bowl is half full of it again, and every voice in town carries exactly as far as the room it's spoken in. The moss eats footsteps. The peat eats echoes. The mist eats the distance. What remains is close, small, and precise: drip from a thatch overhang, ticking as the day's frost lets go; the wet hiss of a reed boat run up on cobbles; a baby's brief complaint, instantly answered; the creak of living willow under weight; a single beaver tail-slap somewhere out in the dark pools, flat and enormous and then gone, swallowed whole. When the Cutface sheds rock — as it does, after hard freeze-thaw, a few times a season — everyone in the town stops, wherever they are, and counts the seconds of the rush and clatter. Ten seconds is a big fall. The counting is silent, lips moving. Then the hush closes back over like water.

Light in the Hollow behaves like light in a bowl. This late in the year the sun only clears the Cutface's rim from mid-morning to mid-afternoon, a three-hour window when the whole floor gets its direct ration and every wet surface — sedge head, cobble, ice-skin, moss — lights up at once. But the valley is never dim before or after that window, because everything here reflects: pale silt, pale sky, pale reed, water everywhere standing or moving or hanging as mist, the whole floor a diffuse lamp the size of a town-landscape, light arriving from all sides and no particular one. On a frost morning it is twice-lit — lit from the pearl sky above and from the frost sheen below, so that walking out along the Reach you throw two soft shadows that don't disagree.

The old woman who remembers open water has a saying the town uses for everything: the lake is deciding. The Throat is still cutting down, so the floor is still draining, one wet finger at a time — but the peat is still rising and the beaver still damning and the mist still returning every night to give it all back, and which of these three processes wins, over centuries, is exactly the kind of question the valley is not yet old enough to answer. A lake could come back. A bog could close over. A forest could stand here in three hundred years on ground that remembers being the bottom of things. The town lives, comfortably and densely, inside that indecision, on the interim, and has built all its arrangements — rooting fences, breathing hearths, rising floors — on the principle that whatever the Hollow becomes, it will become it slowly, and it will become it wet.

The Locking is close now. The ponds' night-ice is lasting later each morning, and soon it will not let go at noon, and then the whole marsh will close all at once, and the Braid's hush will thin to a single channel and then to a thread under ice, and the town will walk out — quiet on the young frost, breath showing, sledging their bundled reeds across what was water — on top of the old lake at last.

Until then, the evenings end the same way: the mist filling up from the floor like the bowl exhaling, the smoke-holes glowing, the last swans going over with that far, slow, tearing-fabric whirr, and the packed silence settling — the hush of a place that is fully occupied, still moving in, and in no hurry to finish.
