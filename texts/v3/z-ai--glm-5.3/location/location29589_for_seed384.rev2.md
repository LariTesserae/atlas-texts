**The Lower Works — under Tarrel Water**

Come down the drowned valley the way the water did: past the stone walls that used to hold fields, along a road now paved with silt, always down, always colder. The lake is fifty meters deep at its floor — the height of a church tower lying flat — and at four degrees it has pressed the last of the warmth out of everything it touched. At the bottom, where the valley's slope finally gives up, the dark resolves into a boundary: an apron of dressed granite, long and narrow, the footprint of a hamlet. You could walk the whole of it, dry-shod, in the time it takes a kettle to boil. Everything inside.

This is the Lower Works of the Tarrel scheme: a pump house, a coal lock, a switch room, a canteen, a mission hall, and one short street of blue-brick terraces called Baker's Row, all packed shoulder to shoulder inside the same granite close. Built on the old valley floor to feed the reservoir above. Then came the Second Raise, in 1951, when the water was lifted twenty-two meters over everything, and the engineers ran the numbers and found that a new bulkhead cost less than demolition, and that the machines, they argued, could run wet. So they bolted the doors, wound the programmer, and let the lake in over eleven days. The station's slate log ends with a requisition for grease. It was cheaper to drown it running than to stop it.

That decision is the whole place. Nothing here has been decaying since. The flood found its level, the silt settled to its angle of rest, and the water — cold, still, starved of oxygen — stopped asking anything of the metal. What happens now is slower than damage: the catwalk undersides grow stalactites of ferrous carbonate, the lampholders string themselves with aragonite pearls, the brick skins over in a green-black that is its final color. The station is turning to stone and is in no hurry. It reached its own state decades ago and has kept it.

Density is its character. The houses share a wall with the engine hall; the tailrace runs under the canteen floor; pipes bundle overhead like the roots of a ceiling; catwalk crosses catwalk so that no cubic meter inside the apron is unclaimed. To cross the yard you pass through strata — under the transformer balcony, beneath the penstock's flank, through the old drying room, out the transept door onto the Row — and the Row's ten front doors stand in a line under their slate lintels, numbered, facing a street of black water.

In the engine hall, in a glass case lit by one amber pilot lamp, the cam programmer still turns. It executes the 1947 rota with the fidelity of a monk: sluices at 05:45, run-up at 06:00, the noon surge, shutdown at 18:00, and at 23:00 the long night of pumping, when the station lifts lake water up the rising main to the upper basin — which is only the same lake again, percolated back down through the fractured dam. Every drop of Tarrel Water passes through the intake about once every nine years. The station is the lake's heart; the lake is the station's body; neither remembers who brokered the arrangement. The lock opens every Tuesday at ten past nine for the coal barge *Perseverance*, which survives as a name punched into the programmer's drum. The gates part for it faithfully. It is not the lock's concern whether barges exist.

Dark, mostly, and dark in specific ways. The pilot lamps — red, amber, one stubborn green on the switchboard — are the brightest things for fifty meters in any direction. The wet insulators carry a violet whisper of corona. Down Baker's Row, the streetlamps still receive their current; water-filled, they cannot light, but electrolysis beads each filament with a lace of tiny bubbles, so the street glows in silver halos, lamps being slowly strung with pearls. Above the fern shelf in the hall's vault, the noon lamp switches on at six and off at six, the schedule's one act of tenderness. And once a year, when the lake above cools to four degrees all the way down and turns over, green daylight washes the whole apron for a single afternoon. The residents' term for it is simply the Clear Day — the one day the sun photographs the place.

Quiet is not the absence of the machines; it is what the machines sound like through water. The pumps' thrum is felt in the jaw before it is heard. The programmer ticks. The sluices sigh at 05:45. The mission bell, struck at six for a shift change of no one, arrives as a dull copper knock that brings the fish. The Tuesday lock cycle is the loudest event of the week — a long muffled grind, like something very large turning over in its sleep. A dropped wrench would be a gunshot. Nothing has dropped a wrench in seventy years. The station does exhale: bubbles slip from the vault, climb for three days, and surface in some far cove as a single burp the anglers call the Toll.

For the station kept one pocket of air. Under the hall's barrel vault, the compressors — originally meant for the governor hydraulics — have held a bell of old air all this time: a few feet deep, twelve degrees warm, smelling of machine oil, ozone, and wet green. It is the only warmth in the valley, and warmth is the only currency, so it is where the sparse life has banked itself. The fern shelf: hart's-tongue ferns run pale and long-stemmed, descendants of the canteen windowboxes, now feral under the noon lamp. A moss in the mortar. Below the waterline, gray-green algal wool coats the hot outfall pipe and — the one properly strange fact of the place — grows along the field gradients of the cabling, so the vegetation faithfully traces the wiring. The garden knows the schematic. On the mission hall wall, under silt to the half, painted letters read *…E KEEPETH…*.

Fauna, you meet one animal at a time. The white char — a landlocked char gone eyeless-pale in the dark — graze the bacterial fleece on the warm pipes and shelter in the tailrace. They have learned the mission bell: at six, they gather beneath it, because the knock unsettles the silt and the silt carries food. In the fern soil, fungus gnats breed in their thousands for an hour each afternoon, and the ones that fall into the water feed the char. That is the entire ecosystem, and it is complete: lamp to fern, fern to gnat, gnat to char, char to fleece, fleece to heat. Four links, all inside one hall, all moving at the pace of cold water.

The laws of the place are few and none of them are posted:

— The schedule is law. Everything that lives here reads the same clock.
— Warmth is wealth. Life maps the leaks.
— Nothing decays here anymore; it mineralizes. The place is not ending. It is petrifying at the speed of balance.
— The Bell shrinks a few millimeters a year as the compressors wear. The countdown is measured in ferns; the shelf retreats a leaf's width at a time; there are decades left, and the decades are being spent.
— The lock owes the *Perseverance* a passage, and services the debt weekly, without interest or complaint.

Tuesday, ten past nine. The gates of the coal lock part for black water, which enters in a slow rolled sheet. The lamps of Baker's Row bead their silver bubbles. In the amber glow of the transept doorway a single white char hangs, fins working, facing the open lock — holding position, punctual, in the exact place a barge would come.
