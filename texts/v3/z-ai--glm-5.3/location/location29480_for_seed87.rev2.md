You will not find this city. It is a few weeks old, it is the size of a large room, and it is lying on its side at the bottom of the sea.

Begin above it, in the water itself, because the water is the country here. There is no air anywhere in this territory, no sky, no ground that isn't underwater — only the sea, stacked in kilometers, each layer pressing on the next like pages in a closed book. By a hundred meters down the sun has already lost its argument; by two hundred it is a rumor; by the time you reach the plain where the city lies, light of any kind is a foreign substance, contraband from a country the town has never heard of. The cold is not weather here — it is the resting temperature of the world, two or three degrees above freezing, held the way stone holds a grudge. And the pressure: a hand that never unclenches. Every citizen is built mostly of the water around them, soft and incompressible, so the squeeze finds nothing to grip. Gas is forbidden at this depth. The pressure polices that law personally.

The terrain is a plain of gray silt so fine it behaves like talc, flat past the reach of any eye, unmarked except by the slow weather of the deep: snow. Not the snow of air — the snow of the sea, a permanent soft fall of the dead and the digested, marine snow, the sun's account settled at the surface and paid downward a half-gram at a time. It gathers on everything. It is the only season.

Follow the snow downcurrent and, eventually, you catch the town's smoke. Nothing here burns; the smoke is a plume of chemistry, the dissolved announcement of a large body dying, drifting through the dark like a chimney's signature. This is how the city advertises itself. You could pass within ten meters and see nothing. You could smell it from a kilometer.

Then the ground breaks: a shallow crater, a berm of thrown sediment, a furrow where one huge bone plowed the silt on the way down. The impact was the groundbreaking ceremony. In the crater's floor lies the founder — a whale, drowned weeks ago, fallen out of the sunlit world with tens of thousands of liters of blubber, muscle, and bone, and the entire civic future of the place sealed inside its ribs like a charter. The town is the corpse. Every district, every industry, every century to come is a way of reading that one body.

It is already under construction, which here means it is already under demolition, and the two are the same verb. The first citizens arrived within hours of the founding, homing down the plume: sleeper sharks, huge and unhurried, working the flanks like demolition crews; hagfish by the dozens and then the hundreds, packed into the body cavity like fireworks in a box; amphipods in a carpet so dense the founder's skin has disappeared beneath a living shag of them, near-touching, overlapping — population measured not in numbers but in contact. Rattail fish patrol the boundary like surveyors. An octopus keeps an office in the shade of the skull, arms folded, landlord of nothing yet. The tongue is gone. The first district was eaten to the gumline in days.

All roads are mouths. The streets are being excavated backward — bored into the fat by things eating as they go, tunnels through blubber wide enough for a hagfish to turn around in, opening onto plazas of exposed muscle, onto the flagstone of skin where the sharks have lifted whole blocks. Nothing was built. Everything is being eaten into being. The city has no tools because it has no hands, and no hands because it has no use for any; every wall is a by-product of appetite, every avenue a wound kept open by traffic.

And yet, read it as architecture, because the citizens do. The ribs stand as an arcade, a colonnade of pale arches with the flesh stripped between them so that the sea now flows through the building the way wind eventually flows through every city. The spine is a boulevard of discs, cobbles the size of dinner plates, still half-paved in cartilage. The skull is the cathedral — a dome of bone the size of a small boat, its windows eaten open, its interior walls hung with baleen, keratin shutters, a fence of horn combing the water for nothing now. Slime and mucus are the mortar and the plaster. Hagfish slime gathers in the low places like poured concrete that never sets.

The infrastructure, though, is the strangeness at the city's heart, and it is the skeleton itself. Whale bone is a treasury: up to half its weight is oil, banked over decades of feeding at the surface, and the town will live for a century on the interest. Deep in the bone and in the crater's anoxic mud, bacteria that never meet oxygen begin to cook the lipid down to hydrogen sulfide. The sulfide rises through the porous bone like water up a wick — the bones are pipes, the founder's skeleton is the plumbing — and it sweats out of the walls into the oxygenated water outside, where other bacteria stand ready to burn it. The city runs on two economies trading across the bone boundary: sulfur inside, oxygen out, a market junction the width of a bone's surface. The lawns are single-celled, white as frost — bacterial mats creeping outward over the enriched sediment, millimeter by millimeter, the only vegetation for miles and all of it alive in the wrong direction, eating chemistry instead of light. The sun has no jurisdiction here. Nothing in this town photosynthesizes. Everything either eats the founder or eats what has already eaten the founder.

The lamps are lit by rot. Luminous bacteria working in the fat give the working surfaces a faint blue-green sheen, so the town glows at exactly the volume of its own decay — streetlamps running on the founder's body, dim, municipal, honest. Patrolling rattails carry small running lights, boats on a night harbor. Once in a while a siphonophore drifts through the arcade like a chandelier rolling down a corridor on its own slow weather, and the whole district is briefly, fabulously lit, and then it is gone and the town returns to its ordinary darkness, which is the darkness of a church at night.

Listen, because the listening takes the longest. The deep sea is the quietest large place in the world, and the town keeps that silence like a civic ordinance — but silence here is storage, not absence. There is a dry granular ticking, faint as rain heard through a wall: ten thousand amphipod mandibles at work at once. There are wet little pops where hagfish knot themselves to tear leverage from the carcass and slam their own bodies like slip-knots. There is the settling of the structure itself, the founder's bones shifting as their bracing flesh is removed, so that the cathedral creaks like a ship at dock. The loudest sound for kilometers in any direction may be your own blood. And then, rarely, something enormous passes through: a whale calling somewhere far above, one of the founder's kin, a sound like a door opening in a stone house, felt in the sternum before it is heard, rolling through the town and out across the plain and away. The mandibles do not pause. The town does not know it is being mourned, or does not behave as if it does.

Now the strangest citizens, who have not arrived yet but whose district is already platted in oil. They are worms — the bone-devourers — and they will settle on the exposed bones as larvae, out of the dark, out of the water column, homing on the town's chemistry like everyone else. They have no mouths and no stomachs; each plants a root system into the bone and farms bacteria inside her own body, a garden that eats stone, red plumes waving above the bone like a whole flowerbed rooted in a wall. The males are the strangest thing here, and the strangeness states itself calmly: dozens to hundreds of them, the size of dust, folded inside the body of a single female, an entire bachelor city inside one citizen, requiring nothing, doing nothing, existing devotedly. This is a real arrangement, not a fable. The deep sea keeps its wonders in plain sight and counts on the dark to make them optional.

The town's history is written in advance, which is its own kind of strangeness. First the boomtown years: sharks, hagfish, the amphipod carpet, flesh converted to citizens at tens of kilograms a day, the demolition district consuming itself out of existence. Then the opportunist quarter: worms and snails and little crabs colonizing the stripped bones and the enriched sediment, the mats spreading their white lawns. Then the sulfophile century, the long tenure of the root-gardens and the gill-farmers, clams plowing the lawns with bacteria in their gills like greenhouses on their own chests — decades, generations, the plumbing still wicking, the treasury still paying out. And when the oil at last runs thin and the sulfide fails, the citizens go; the charter lapses; and the town enters its final stage as a reef — bare mineral bones, weathering slowly, tenements for anemones and hydroids, a hard address in a soft world. After that, the archive: the bones sink into stone with their boreholes intact, a trace fossil the size of a city plan, catalogued by no one.

*The laws of the fall:*
*Nothing here kills for a living. The dying was done elsewhere, at the surface, long before arrival; the sun's account was settled above and the town lives on the balance transferred down.*
*Everything is eaten — fat, flesh, oil, marrow, and finally the appetites themselves, which starve in their own success.*
*Nothing is built; everything is secreted, bored, settled, grown. The city's tools are its citizens' bodies, which are also its materials.*
*The borders are chemical, not geographic. Where the sulfide ends, the city ends; a child could pace it, if the town had children.*
*Gas is forbidden; the pressure enforces this without appeal.*
*Ground is not owned; ground is inhabited, roots in bone, tenancy measured in remaining fat.*
*Every citizen arrived from elsewhere. No one in the deep is born far from a dead thing.*
*Time is measured in decay, not days. The calendar is the treasury, and it only runs down.*

Pull back now, upcurrent, until the glow drops below the threshold of any eye. Within twenty meters the town is gone — hidden inside the hugeness of the sea the way a single lit room is hidden inside a country at night, one pocket of warmth and chemistry and near-silent chewing, a few meters long, older than a city block and younger than a month. The snow keeps falling on the plain. The plume keeps drifting downcurrent, calling the next wave of settlers. Somewhere overhead the founder's kin pass in their hundreds, uncounted, singing their long doors-opening sounds into the water; and far below, the loudest process in the city is patience, and it works without interruption.
