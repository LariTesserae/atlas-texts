You come down into it, because everything here is down. The way in is a collapsed sink in a lifeless plateau — a stone desert above, wind-polished, gray, silent — and for the last hour of the descent the disc of sky shrinks above you like a coin turning on its face, until it is a pale stitch, and then nothing. That is when the sound arrives. Not gradually. You walk into it the way you walk into surf.

It has no direction and no weather. It is simply the country's voice, already going on before you got here, and it will still be going when you leave: a roar with visible structure, layered like sediment, with a hiss under it like rain on a sand roof, a crackle above that like frying oil, a fine whining reed running through the middle of everything, and punctuating all of it, at long intervals, deep hollow booms that you feel arrive through the floor before you hear them.

The land itself first. It is limestone, old past arguing — a low basin of linked dolines and dome-collapses, a whole sunken country of hollows and galleries that water forgot slowly. Every surface carries the record: fluting like pulled taffy, scallop-marks from water that hasn't run in ten thousand years, breakdown blocks rounded and settled and mossless, sitting exactly where they fell and have sat since, in balance, gathering dust that is itself mostly older dust. The far walls are rumors. You never see the country whole. The ceiling of it is fog, a slow gray sea of fog hanging at some indefinite height, and here and there thin columns of paler gray stand all the way up — the Needles, where the collapse opened a shaft to the dead surface, and mist climbs them like slow smoke, escaping upward forever and being replaced forever.

Water is not abundant here. It is punctual. Nothing stays wet long; everything gets wet on schedule. The air is cellar-cool, and the fog above is the reservoir: when it lowers, when the country inhales, every wall sweats. Condensation beads on the flutes and runs in threads; ceilings rain indoors in thin metronome drips that you hear individually, absurdly, against the roar — because at the drip-lines there are queues. Dense files of blind, flattened grazers waiting mouth-up beneath the tips, drinking the clock one drop at a time. And there is a river, of a kind: it comes out of one wall with a low brown voice, runs three hundred meters across the dark in a groove it has cut itself, crowds of pale eel-things standing in its current with their faces upstream, and then it folds itself into a sink and is gone, and you can walk an hour in the dry and find it again, resurfacing further on, as if nothing had happened. It has been doing this for so long that its beds are polished like the insides of shells.

Where the real wealth is, though, is up from below — the seeps. Warm water rises through faults in the basin floor, sulfur-smelling, mineral-dark, and around each seep the country contracts into itself in a way you can feel through your boots. Sulfur-white bacterial mats lie across the warm stone like unrolled smoke, rippling where the thermals breathe. Lawns of soft pale tube-worms feather the flows, each lawn moving in slow unison, exhaling. Rust-orange smears stripe the cooler drips; manganese crusts the dry margins in blacks so deep they hold light. Green does not exist here. That is the first strange fact your eye accepts and then keeps tripping over — the absence of the color you were trained to expect at the base of any living landscape. The palette of this place is sulfur-white, rust, wet black, and the gray of old stone, and it is not a poor palette. It is simply a chemist's palette instead of a gardener's.

On that base, the numbers begin. A hand placed on any wall rests on a hundred tenants; the ceiling in most galleries is a shag, a living pelt of clingers that shift and click as your air moves them. The swarms above the warm seeps thicken the air until walking through it is like wading through fur, and your own body — warm, loud, huge — moves through them as a moving deaf-spot, a hole in their sound that they are all aware of and you are not. The floor near the seeps is grazers: herds of blind snails mowing the white mats down to the stone, their shells worn mirror-smooth not by water but by each other, by generations of crowding, and their trails — this is the detail that takes longest to believe — are worn into the limestone as much as an inch deep. Roads. Snail-roads, grooved over millennia by rasping and return, the landscape shaped by appetite instead of architecture. Where old seeps have died, the shells of every grazer that ever lived there lie banked and cemented into gravel shores, a breccia of everybody, and you walk on the dead of previous economies.

The sound is the deepest thing here, and it deserves to be taken apart. The floor of the roar is the rasping: millions of radulae on millions of mats, the country's static. Above that, the scrape-stridulation of wall-hoppers drawing hard ridges across file-ribs on their own bodies, whole cliff-faces playing themselves like the sides of instruments. Above that the whine of the swarms, which has grain to it — you can hear individual pitch bend when the swarm turns. And in the pools, the snappers: small shrimp-like things whose claws close so fast they make a cavitation bubble that kills at a distance, and every snap is also — this is true, and it is the most beautiful fact in the country — a spark. A fleck of cold blue light, too brief to look at, arriving as an after-image on your useless eyes. In the deep galleries, where the glow-walls are dark, the pools twinkle. Slow lightning. The only sky most of this country has ever had is made of snapping shrimp.

Some creatures here see. A few. There is one wall, in the country's northern reach, where a fungus grows in broad sheets and glows — the Ghost Flats, a cold green-white constellation lying on its side. Around that wall, and only around it, gather everything that kept its eyes: a congregation of the sighted, pressed toward the light like moorings of small boats, because for everything else in the world light is a rumor and this wall behaves like a moon.

Silence, meanwhile, is an animal here. Because the roar is constant, the roar is invisible, the way sunlight is invisible; what is visible is its interruption. A predator moving through a gallery announces itself as a border of quiet — the hush-front — and you feel it pass over you like a cold wind with no wind in it. Hundreds of stridulators stop in sequence; the crackle tightens; even the swarms go dim. The Listener is somewhere in that quiet: a long-limbed, dish-eared, wall-walking thing that has never once been seen moving by anything that lived to describe it, because the silence it travels inside is a country-sized held breath. When the front passes, the roar comes back in a ripple, gallery by gallery, like rain finding its roof again. The prey have answers of their own — fur that eats echoes, sound-jamming shrieks, pool-colonies that clench into a wall of crackle so total that a hunter's own voice is useless inside it. None of it is built. All of it is bodies. There are no tools here, no artifacts, no made things anywhere in this world; the strategies are anatomy and the weapons are behavior, and they have been honing each other for so long that the arms race has reached something like treaty.

The whole country keeps time by air. There is no day. There is the Breath: when pressure shifts above the plateau, the fog-sea lowers and pours through the galleries, the temperature lifts a degree or two, every surface beads at once, mat metabolism surges, and the chorus builds into something genuinely deafening — the wet chorus, at full tide, in which you cannot hear your own hands in front of your face. Then the pressure turns, the fog drains back down into the sumps, the air stills and cools, and the country goes quiescent for the ebb: quieter, hungrier, the hour of hush-fronts. Every creature's calendar is barometric. Nesting happens at the fog-line. The fungal orchards — pale decomposers on mat-detritus and carcass — spore into the flood, so that at high tide the fog itself is partly alive and you breathe the place's spores without ever seeing them. And under everything, felt in the sternum rather than heard, there is the Hum: an infrasonic floor rising from the deepest sump, where the water goes when it leaves, a note nobody has followed to its source, the metronome the whole ecology keeps time against.

In one dome-collapse — the Amphitheater, a great bowl of scalloped stone with natural acoustics that gather every voice in the region and set them ringing — there are knobs of resonant limestone polished to a high black shine. Not by water. By drummers: heavy knuckle-boomed things that strike the same singing stones generation after generation to claim them, ten thousand generations deep, the closest this country has ever come to building anything — and it built nothing. It only struck the same stones until they shone.

And there is one more presence, unconfirmed, which the place keeps like a held card: passages of silence too long for the Listener, a displacement of air like a door opening somewhere in the dark, faint footsteps arriving through the floor at intervals too slow to be walking. Everything else in the country resumes afterward, carefully, the way one resumes speaking after a funeral.

That is the character of the place, in the end: an old country, settled past the need for change, its forms finished, its populations in ancient balance, damp on a schedule, cool as a cellar, sunk at the floor of the land where everything drains. Nothing here was ever made. Everything here was worn into shape — by water, by hunger, by ten thousand generations of the same visits to the same stones. You will climb out after a day or two, because your ears ring and your cold has gone into your joints, and on the plateau above, in the wind and the daylight, the silence there will feel thin and provisional for a long time, like a room someone has just left.

The country has no name. Nothing here names anything. If it did, the name would have to be shouted, continuously, by everyone, forever — and in a sense it is.
