You hear the valley two ridgelines before you see it. The sound arrives like weather — a slow pressure front of vegetable noise, layered the way surf is layered if surf were the size of a town. Underneath, a drone like a cathedral organ left running. Over that, percussion. Over that, small crackling volleys, thousands per minute, the sound of green gunfire. By the time you crest the last knife-edged ridge, you have stopped trying to separate it into instruments. It is one instrument. It is the size of the valley.

The basin below was seabed, and recently. The evidence is everywhere and nowhere weathered: stratified silt terraces with shell hash still glittering in their faces, a thin braided stream cutting gullies with edges too sharp to be a week old, low grey ridges at the rim that have not yet been rounded by anything. Erosion has not had time here, and more to the point, the plants have not given it time. The green begins at the salt line of the stream and simply does not stop — canopy to the basin's rim in every direction — and the canopy is a city. Not a city hidden in a forest. A city that is the forest, in the way a skeleton is the animal.

From the ridge you can read the urban form. Streets are seams of darker green, avenues converging on pale clearings that must be plazas — hard-packed salt pans where nothing builds, the city's neutral ground. Towers erupt in clusters, buttressed like figs, latticed like banyans, sixty meters of hollow architecture. Lines of elevated structure run straight for kilometers: aqueducts. A wall crosses the valley's throat, and the wall is alive, and the wall is newer than the district it encloses. There is no straight line in this place that is not a contract.

You descend. The entrance arch is days old — you can tell because its joints are still pale, its knucklewood segments unflexed by any season. The street surface is a living root-mat with the slight give of a tongue, worn smooth in the center by nothing anyone can name, since nothing walks here. Gutter-figs roof the thoroughfare, their leaves overlapped like scales, each leaf's drip-tip aimed into the next leaf's cup, so the whole street is a quietly ticking machine for moving dew. The air underneath is sweltering, windless, green-filtered, dense with the smell of hot resin, cut grass, overripe fruit, and pollen like flour suspended in the light. Normal daylight, technically. It just arrives green, rationed by altitude — up here light is a resource with a waiting list, and every cubic meter of this city is leased to something photosynthetic. Ceilings of flatcrowns. Street story. Gutter story. Understory. Rhizosphere. Near-touching at every level, packed like mail in a post office.

The noise, inside it, turns out to have anatomy.

The drone comes from the bellstalks — hollow towers grown as pipes, mouths tuned by the wind, and the chords change hour to hour because the towers are still growing, lengthening by centimeters a day, so the city is perpetually re-tuning itself like an orchestra during the concert. The percussion comes from below: drumroots, room-sized xylem pumps in the substrate, forcing sap sixty meters skyward, and their beat lands in your sternum rather than your ears.
