From a distance — and there is a great deal of distance here, all of it green — the world looks like a single organism that agreed, long ago, to be a country. Shoulders of layered bark rise above a cloud deck, gray-green and lichen-armored, so wide that their curvature is the only horizon: walk the outer skin for a month and the sky never changes its mind, a lid of pearl-gray with light in it but no sun you could point to. The light comes from everywhere at once. Nothing casts a shadow. This is the first rule of the place, and it is not about optics: here, light is what holds a thing's shape. Where light pools, the world is definite. Where it thins, the world is negotiable.

The whole of it is made. Not built — grown into building, which took ten thousand years of hands. There is no stone. There is no ground. Dig anywhere and you find root-mat laid over root-mat, chambers under chambers, grafted joints worked by generations of gardeners whose flaked-stone knives and bone needles are still being found embedded in the walls like relics, some older than any lineage that remembers making them. The gardeners call it the House, in the way you'd call a continent a house if the continent had a door. A few hundred of them live inside it. That is all. The rest is volume — halls a day's walk across, hollow shafts of warm air, corridors where your footsteps are the loudest event of the year.

It is warm in the House, warm as a held breath, because the mass of it lives and burns sugar slowly like an animal lying down. And it sits high, in thin cold air, above the clouds. Warm inside, cold outside: this is why the whole world weeps. The inner face of the skin sweats. Every dawn, the vaults bead with condensation in strings, and then comes the Weep — threads of water running down bark buttresses into runnels bored and rebored by a hundred generations, gathering into interior rain that drips from ceilings kilometers up. You can stand in a hall the size of a valley and be rained on gently, indoors, by water that has never seen a sky. Afterward everything smells of warm cellulose and green sugar, moss and old paper, the air of a book left out in a greenhouse. In the low galleries at night, the extra breath pools as fog, and the fog is faintly sweet.

Because light fixes form, the interior — lit only by what the skin catches — is a softer place than physics usually permits. The skin photosynthesizes and conducts its glow down pale vines strung through the dark like slow lightning frozen mid-fall; at dusk you can watch the light drain downward through them, top of the hall dimming first. In the brightest rooms the edges of things are crisp. In the deep ones, edges are a courtesy. A corridor is longer coming back than going. A staircase can arrive before you finish climbing it. Doors graft themselves shut overnight behind travelers, and the gardeners do not fight this; they hang more lamps, and they say the House dreams, and you do not wake a sleeper by arguing with its mutterings. When a deep chamber truly forgets its shape, a gardener will sit with it through the dark hours with a hand flat on the wall — holding the room's hand, they call it — until the grain remembers which direction it was flowing.

Everything that is solid here is alive, so nothing is permanent, and yet nothing changes: growth equals rot, exactly, the way a flame keeps its shape by continuously dying. Dead halls are composted into new halls. The dead themselves are composted into the walls; nothing leaves the House, and in certain panels you can see, faint in the grain, the shape of a grazer that lived and was folded back into the architecture, warm to the touch. The gardeners' tools are the same: stone flakes hafted with gut, thorn needles, clay lamps, hooks carved from shed antler, grafting wax rendered from scale insects. Simple tools, immense craft. They count time in sap-tides rather than days — twice daily the whole floor rises a hand's width, root-mats swelling spongy underfoot, runnels running full — and they read tomorrow in the cambium rings, which is not metaphor: the newest rings near the summit carry weather that has not happened yet, and the gardeners plan their pruning around next month's rain.

The fauna is abundant and nearly silent, and the second rule explains why: the House hunts by vibration. Sticky threads hang in the darker corridors, tuned like harp strings, and a cry brings them down on the crier. There are galleries of pitcher-mouths whose rims hum faintly when full. Everything that survives here has evolved mute and soft-footed — moths in slow blizzards, wet paper wings that make no sound at all, drifting toward the sap-rise; grazers the size of hayricks, eyeless, gentle as felt, lapping mineral crust off the buttresses with tongues like warm cloths; threadfish in the runnels, transparent, riding the water films down the walls; midge-clouds carrying pollen that glows faintly on their backs, so that at a distance a pollination looks like weather, a drizzle of sparks moving through the dark. The loudest natural event in the House is a falling drop of condensation hitting a leaf. The walls themselves are felted with moss and dead thatch that eats sound; speech comes back to you thinned, and the deep halls return nothing. You feel the structure's pulse more than hear it, in your teeth, a beat four hours long.

Walk inward and the strangeness compounds gradually, the way the dark does. You pass the same chamber twice, gardener-lamps and all, and the second time it is arranged differently, and no one finds this worth mentioning. You brush a hanging string of condensation and in the bead of water the reflection is of a different sky — brighter, with a sun in it. Deep bores, made by the curious with primitive augers of bone and flint, go down through mat after mat of made material and find, below the deepest floor, only more House, inverted, going down without end. The leading theory among the gardeners, expressed with a shrug, is that the world is House all the way through, and that the shell-sky is simply the skin seen from inside.

At the center is the Axis, a hollow a kilometer wide, and here gravity grows tired. Water falls in slow spirals, breaking into fat beads that drift. Mist rises in the same spirals, going the other way, and near the heartwood some of the drops fall upward, quietly, all night. The gardeners climb the Axis on grafted stair-vines to reach the summit, where the third and largest fact of the world is kept: the Bud. A flower with petals kilometers long, sealed, wax-surfaced, warm as a sleeping animal, older than the records and the records' grandparents. It has never opened. The cambium rings around it have been thickening for a generation, and the rings that prophesy show, just ahead in the wood, a season with no precedent in any ring ever read.

So the gardeners tend it — kneading its base with their hands, wrapping it in insulating mats of shed bark, feeding it the year's best compost — and it is possible, walking the long warm dark of the place, to feel that everything else was always preparation: the tides, the Weep, the silence, the lamp-walks, the muteness bred into every animal, the vastness kept so empty it could hold its breath. The whole made world is quiet because it is listening. Whatever the Bud is for, the House has spent ten thousand years of hands growing toward it, and it is almost time.
