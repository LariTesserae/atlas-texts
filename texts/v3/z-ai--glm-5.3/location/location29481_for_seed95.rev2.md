**The Old Level**

The land here has finished. Whatever it was — a range, a knot of peaks with snow on them, real country — time ate it down to a plain two thousand meters up, and then kept eating for so long afterward that even the eating slowed to ceremony. This is a landscape in its extreme old age: not dead, not abandoned, but complete. Geology is not doing anything here anymore; it is merely being. What change remains is subtractive and unhurried — a grain of feldspar loosed from a boulder's flank at dawn, once a season, and not before.

Nine domes of grey gneiss stand above the plain. Eleven on a clear day, though the far ones may be counted twice by the haze. They are not mountains. They are what the mountains left behind: the few kernels too hard or too lucky to be spent, spaced across the region at intervals of one to three miles like the last teeth in an old jaw. Each is a survivor. Each once had a name, and none of the names survive — the people who did the naming are thinner in the record than the varnish on the stones.

Between the domes the ground is a pavement: gravel and quartzy grit lying on a hard old pan, iron-cemented, that keeps the flatness flat. Nothing rolls far; nothing piles. Every stone wears a coat of desert varnish, a slow lacquer laid down by microbes over centuries — black, orange, the brown of steeped tea — and the varnish is the Level's only clock. Fresh scars shine pale; old wounds darken. You can date a boulder's fall by the tan of its skin. Over it all, in continental patches, lichen: ash-green, rust, sulfur, black, not scattered but mapped, borders stitched, so that from the top of a dome the plain reads like a country in the colors of an old atlas, gone quiet.

One seam crosses the region — the ghost of a river, a trough so shallow you would walk over it without noticing, except that things lean into it. The river died of age; there was nothing left to cut with. But the trough still conducts. Every dawn the fog comes in from the lowlands, walks down the old channel, and pools in the trough's pockets exactly as water used to, keeping its old roads, running a river of the wrong element. It is the strangest habit of the place: the dead river still hauls freight, and the freight is cloud. Eastward, a day's walk off, the Level ends at the Edge, where the plain breaks off into haze and the lowlands begin, and you understand that everything flat up here is the summit of something that was once enormous.

Water is a rumor that arrives daily. Before sun-up the Level is inside the weather — this is the altitude where clouds do their business, and the fog is not overhead but around, at hand height. Every stone brow sweats. The domes' flanks darken in streaks that follow the grain of the rock, and at each dome's foot the dew stands in the pans — the gnammas, solution-hollows ground into the granite by uncountable years of damp working grit in slow circles. The water made its own basins; erosion and thirst are the same trade here. One pan near the largest dome is deep enough never to dry: the Black Pool, the width of a room, rimmed in black lichen, its surface broken only by the loudest scheduled sound on the Level — a ring, soft as a fingernail on a cup, every eight seconds, where condensation gathers on the stone lip above and lets go.

When the sun clears, it is a warm one. This is a high place but a warm place, and by mid-morning the pavement is pleasant underfoot, the light ordinary and honest — the kind that flatters nothing and hides nothing. The domes take the day's heat and hold it, and give it back through the first hours of dark, which is when the animals use them as hearths.

The smaller drinking happens everywhere and at once. In the cold hour the beetles rise from under the crust and stand on their heads — thousands, shoulder to shoulder, so many the stones look briefly furred — letting the fog bead on their backs and run downhill into their mouths, drinking the sky in position. Near the ruined house there are cups ground into the rock by hands: thumb-wide basins in a scatter no one has ever interpreted. The fog gathers in them too. The beetles drink from them, and have for a very long time. The dead still water the living.

Plants have not done well here, and the reason is not the dryness. It is the animals. The ground is too busy to grow. What survives survives by strategy: lichen, which eats stone and time; a black crust of algae and fungus lying between the grit like a skin over the plain, faintly damp at dawn and a whisper by noon; tufts of wiry grass in the pavement's cracks that spend months curled like dead wire and open, green and absurd, only in the fog hours. Around each dome's skirt, where the dew runs off the flanks, a hem of low grey trees — each dome an island with a green belt at its waist — and along the Seam a broken string of pale reeds wherever the trough holds damp. That is the entire flora, and it is enough, because nothing here needs it to be more.

The fauna is the point. Start underfoot, in the licker districts, where the ground is more doorway than ground: burrow mouths every pace, spoil-heaps gone grey with age, whole hectares so undermined that in the crest years the earth gives under your boots like a mattress. The lickers themselves are cat-sized, felted slate-grey, blunt-faced, silent. They graze the algal film the fog leaves on the stones, and they harvest water at scale — lying belly-down on wet flanks at first light, then licking themselves clean, which is as close to washing as the Level comes. Their young dig. Their old stop digging, sun themselves on the spoil, and become, without ceremony, protein.

Above them the walkers: bustard-necked grey birds the height of your hip, quartering the plain in loose bands, walking as if walking were the whole of religion. A band will pass a mile in a morning without any of them once hurrying. And above everything, twice a day, the grey stream — small crescent-winged birds that nest in the domes' crevices and in the mouths of old burrows, leaving at first light in a single unbroken rope of bodies that takes a quarter of an hour to pass a given point, thousands upon thousands, mute, the sound of them no more than a pulse of felt. They drink fog on the wing. They return the same way at dusk. The density of the place is vertical, a stratigraphy of presences packed from the sky down to the pan — birds, walkers, beetles, crust, warrens — each layer near-touching the next, the whole plain saturated to a depth of a few hundred meters of air and a few meters of undermined ground.

There are corvids here too — black, heavy-billed, mute like everything else — with one habit no one who sees it forgets. They carry white pebbles, one at a time, from the quartz flats to the standing stones, and set them down. That is all. The stones are not theirs; they are old work, slabs leaned upright in a line that runs two kilometers from the Seam toward the eastern notch, all leaning the same way, like men in an old photograph. The birds place their pebbles at the bases. The cairns grow at a pebble a week, and have been growing for a very long time — you can date them, like the scars, by their darkness. Whether the birds are honoring the builders, robbing them, or simply continuing a habit whose owners are gone, the Level keeps to itself.

The rest of the built world is slight and scattered. On the southern skirt of the largest dome, forty paces of retaining wall hold up a terrace that now holds the last deep soil on the plain, and therefore the only true grasses — the builders are gone, but their drainage still works, tending a garden they never planned. By the Black Pool stands one round house, roofless, hip-high, dry-fitted without mortar, its lintel a single stone that someone chose and carried a long way. Lickers burrow beneath its floor, and the floor settles gently, year by year, onto their chambers, so that the house is being lowered — slowly, by its tenants — into the plain. Swifts nest in its joints. The ruin is not empty. It is occupied on different terms.

Now the rule that explains most of it. The Level is flat, open, and acoustically finished: the wind arrives as pressure, not voice, because erosion took away every edge there was to whistle on. What sound does occur travels absurdly. A slipped pebble is audible at a mile; a footstep is a broadcast. Sound here is public property, and everything alive is descended from things that learned this early. Nothing calls. Nothing cries. Displays are danced, not sung; arguments are settled in posture. The beetle headstands, the lickers belly the flanks, the walkers quarter, the quarter-hour river of wings pours overhead — all of it in a hush like snowfall.

The reason for the rule is the listener. It is the one animal almost no one sees: low, long, soft as a held breath, with a face that is mostly ear. It owns the quiet the way other predators own the dark. It can hear a beetle's feet at half a kilometer and a burrow's heartbeat at less, and it kills without noise, because its prey does not get to make any. Every species on the plain is shaped around the fact of it — which is to say, around the avoidance of it — which is to say: silent.

Twice a season, sometimes once, sometimes not at all, a real rain crosses the Level, and the rule is briefly suspended, because rain is the one sound the listener cannot parse — a wall of water in which no single footfall can be found. In that hour the plain spends its saved voice, all of it, at once. The walkers boom. The lickers whistle underground in their hundreds. The grey stream screams through the wet; the corvids rattle. A chorus held all year in the mouth and spent in an hour. Then water stands in every pan and cup, and the mud of the Black Pool quickens with shrimp whose eggs have waited in the dust — some of them, probably, longer than the house has been ruin — and for a few loud days the Level is almost an ordinary place. Then it closes again like a hand.

There are other rules, and they are stranger. At dusk the lickers queue at the Black Pool — a genuine queue, strict order of arrival, each drinking a set count and withdrawing to make room. No one enforces it. It has been seen to break only twice, and both times the listener's shadow passed within the minute. Whether the queue is law, culture, or fear wearing order's clothes, the Level does not say, and keeps it either way.

Everything else is slowness. The licker crests come every dozen years and go; the spoil-heaps of old crests lie out on the plain like grey reefs. In some years the walkers walk east in numbers, toward the notch, and keep walking, and none is ever seen to come back, and no one knows what the notch knows. The varnish darkens. The cairns fatten. The domes lose a grain at a time, without urgency, the way old things do, in no pain. The day itself is the model: fog up the Seam, the grey stream gone, the fog burned off by warm and unremarkable light, a long noon in which everything visible is merely standing in heat, then the tides home, then the fog returning along its dead river in the dark, and all night the domes ticking softly as they cool, like enormous seeds.

Stand on the largest dome at first light — inside the cloud, at the altitude where weather lives, breathing thin air the walkers never seem to need — and you will hear the whole of it: the tick of warming stone, the ring of the Pool's one drip, the felt-pulse of the grey stream passing, and beneath all that a silence so total it is clearly a community. Thousands of animals, near-touching, layered from the crust to the sky, all agreeing, generation upon generation, on the single thing worth agreeing on: not to be heard. The land finished long ago. The living have matched it. This is a place at the end of its story that has decided, quite sensibly, to stay.
