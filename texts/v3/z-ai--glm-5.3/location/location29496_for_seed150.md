**The rim.**

You come up out of wind. That's the first strange thing — not the fog, which you can see from a long way off, sitting in the basin like a lid on a pot, but the silence that arrives before you reach it. Three steps down the last slope and the wind is gone, cut off as if a door had closed behind you. The slope itself is raw boulders, glacier-plucked and glacier-polished, streaked with fine striae that still hold their sharpness because the ice left here only moments ago — decades, maybe, which for stone is nothing. The edges of things have not yet had time to round. Then the fog takes you, and you are inside.

The basin is small. You could cross it in ten minutes if you walked in a straight line, but nothing in here permits a straight line except the lanes, and the lanes are going to take a while to explain.

**The stone, and the city on it.**

The floor of the basin is young bedrock and younger gravel, unsorted, sharp-cornered, dumped in arcs at the rim like ramparts some giant raked together. Everything is fresh: the gravel bars are re-cut every melt day, the headwall at the back sheds a small rockfall most afternoons with a hiss like sand poured through a hand, and where the moss thins you can put your palm on stone so recently uncovered that it still feels startled. There is almost no soil. There doesn't need to be soil. The moss is the soil — a deep, black-green, waterlogged carpet stitched directly to rock, and growing out of it, in a density that makes you think first of a city and only second of a forest, a settlement built entirely of plants.

Which is the second strange thing. It *is* a settlement. The eye insists on the word. There are lanes. There are terraces. There are long colonnades with a rhythm to their columns, domed chambers with distinct walls, courtyards, thresholds, levels stacked on levels. And there is not one plank, one beam, one cut stone or scrap of cordage anywhere in it. Nothing here was ever a tool. Everything here is alive, was born in place, and is still growing.

**The rain that comes from the sides.**

It does not rain here, exactly. The fog is so dense at this elevation that water condenses out of the air onto every available surface — which is to say, onto everything, since every surface is claimed. Each stem wears a coat of beads. Each bead is a lens. The result, in the flat gray-green light, is that the air at canopy height seems hung with small cold lights, hundreds of thousands of them, trembling, letting go, re-forming. It rains upward here, sideways, constantly, in slow motion. By noon the whole place is ringing faintly with drip.

The water routes itself with an engineer's patience. Sphagnum terraces step down the slopes like amphitheater seating, each cushion holding its weight in water until it can hold no more, then weeping through a weep-hole in the rock face below, feeding the next terrace down. Under everything, a rhizome net — dense as rope-work, black, cold — ties the gravel together and carries seepage along lines it has chosen, or been given. The basin is a slow fountain: fog in at the top, one clear loud stream out at the bottom. At the outflow, if you wait, you may see the only dependable animal in the place — a dipper, dark and compact, walking *under* the water, flipping stones, climbing back out onto a rock to bow and blink. It makes the stream seem briefly like a street with one pedestrian on it.

**The streets, and who drew them.**

The lanes are the part that unsettles people. They run straight, and they run level, and their gravel is kept bare — not bare of life, exactly, but free of anything tall. The explanation is almost comfortingly mechanical: cold air drains downhill and pools, and every night the frost comes down the lanes like a wagon making its rounds, parking in the low spots, killing anything that grew above ankle height since yesterday. The streets are the cold's. They maintain themselves.

But straight? Cold air follows channels, and channels follow the joints and seeps in the bedrock, and life follows the water — so the lanes may simply be geology, plumbing, the stone's own cracks expressed in gravel and moss curbs. That is the sufficient explanation. It does not fully account for the regularity, the sense that the lanes connect *places* to *places* rather than merely downhill to further downhill. And nothing else accounts for it. There is no one here. If the plan had a planner, the planner left nothing behind, not even a bone, because one of the rules of this place is that the dead do not linger — anything that falls is roofed over with moss and absorbed within a season, so that the settlement contains no ruins of any kind, only its own growing substance, and the only ruins visible anywhere are the stone's.

If it ever had a name, it isn't telling.

**The colonnades and the chambers.**

The great columns are horsetails — the old kind, thick as a wrist and taller than a house, jointed every half-meter, ribbed with silica so they feel like scoured file-shanks under the hand. At each joint a whorl of branches, tier upon tier, so the groves read as architecture the way a colonnade reads as architecture: repetition, rhythm, a ceiling. The light under them is dim, shadowless, green-black, the light of the inside of a closed hand. In the morning, when the fog warms a single degree, the stems creak. The sound is slow, wooden, scattered, like a ship taking up tension — growth, or just expansion, or both; no one has ever settled which, since the growth here runs faster than growth should. Stone a few decades exposed; a city already standing on it. Either this flora grows at a pace the cold should forbid, or it has been waiting under the ice with its bags packed.

The chambers are warmer still. They are the domes of a kind of giant cabbage-arum, hooded structures with thick walls, and they cheat: their flowers burn a degree or two of heat, enough to hold their interiors above freezing while the night outside goes white. From the rim at dawn you can see them — the frost lies over the whole settlement like laid linen, and over each dome, a thaw-halo. Green circles in the white. The effect is exactly the effect of a dark town with its windows lit.

Inside a dome, the air is still, humid, and smells faintly, sweetly, of carrion — the arum's honest advertisement. This is where the crowds are: midges, and springtails hopping like sparks through the warm air, the only gatherings of animals the settlement ever assembles. Away from the domes, fauna is scarce to the point of secrecy. The moss is full of frogs you will almost never see — cold-hardy, sealed into the cushions, some of them frozen solid for months at a stretch, waiting for the few warm weeks when they will briefly, all together, click and chuckle in a dusk chorus that is the loudest the place ever gets. Old vole-runs under the carpet collapse and become channels; the animals here are plumbers, mostly posthumously.

**The rules of the place.**

Nothing here is ever dry. Nothing here stands alone — every structure is a substrate, every surface an address. The cold is the architect: frost draws the plan each night, growth executes by day, and the fog mediates between them, refusing the frost full victory and the growth full speed. Water moves downhill or becomes vapor; nothing else happens to it. Everything that dies is eaten on the spot and built into a wall. The settlement ends where its breath ends — beyond the fog lid, the wind is a butcher, and the alpine turf outside is clipped to the bone; within the lid, the frost is merely an editor. Both edges of the place are hard edges. You always know when you're inside.

The day runs like this: night, frost-silence, the crackle of ice letting go of stems. Dawn, the thaw-halos bloom. Morning, the creaking, the fiddleheads shoving up through the carpet with an audible papery push. Midday, the fog at its thickest, condensation at maximum, every surface beaded and glittering, the lanes dark and wet and empty. Afternoon, a rockfall hiss, the arums warming, the midges assembling. Dusk, a dipper, or no dipper. Night, the frost comes down the streets on its rounds.

If you sit still long enough — an hour, two — the dimness starts to seem less like absence of light and more like a substance the light has been dissolved into. The green of the moss goes nearly black. The beads hold what little light there is, and hoard it. It is a cathedral, but a small one: everything within a ten-minute walk, everything packed shoulder to shoulder, every cubic centimeter of air occupied by stem, frond, fog, drip, or the held breath of frogs.

Then you step back over the moraine, and the wind takes you like a debt collector, and behind you the lid stays down on the pot, and the little city goes on with its day — creaking, weeping, growing, lit at its windows — needing, so far as anyone can prove, no one at all.
