You hear it before you see it. The stair down is new — thirty-odd meters of it, spiral, the stone cut so recently it still holds a chill in the mornings and smells like the inside of a quarry — and as you descend, the sound rises to meet you. A bass hum first, felt in the sternum more than heard, and over it a hiss, and over that a constant ticking spray of water landing on water. By the tenth turn of the stair the air has changed too: warm, thick, green-smelling, wet enough that your skin starts to shine. You are going down into a held breath.

The chamber itself is small — a cathedral's footprint, a hamlet's population of walls — and almost nothing is in it. That's the first strange gift of the place: the volume is all air. A vault some fifty meters across and eighteen high, terraced in concentric rings like an amphitheater turned inward, and the rings are *streets*. Not metaphorically. Each terrace edge is a kerb. Each kerb holds a gutter. Each gutter carries water, and the water is the traffic. There are blocks, in the sense that there are rectangular planting basins arranged with the logic of city lots; there is a plaza, in the sense that the lowest floor is a shallow basin where all the runnels converge; there is even signage, in the sense that cold thin lines of light are set into grooves that label each tier with a designation in a script of short strokes. The builders stamped a survey mark by the stair: a spiral inside a square, and beneath it the numeral 1. Whatever this place is, it is the first of its kind, and the architecture is the architecture of a town — compressed, folded under the city above, and left empty.

Empty, and young. Nothing here has been weathered. The edges of the steps are sharp enough to catch a thumbnail; the rims of the basins are crisp as cut paper; there is no wear on the stair treads, no stain on the gutters, no lichen, no smoothness where a thousand shoulders would have leaned. The material is a grown ceramic — you can see how it was made, because it kept the record: faint tide-lines running through the walls, one line per day, marking the height the fabrication reached before it paused and resumed. The lower walls are three hundred lines tall. On the fourth ring someone's handprints are fossilized in a panel, pressed flat while the ceramic was still soft, the only portrait of anyone in the whole place. The structure is still curing. Every few minutes it makes a small internal crack — knuckle-pop, knuckle-crack — as the lattice settles into itself, and each pop goes off through the vault like a struck match.

And the walls weep. That was a decision, not a failure. The chamber sits just beneath the skin of the water table, and the builders did not fight the wet; they gave it lanes. Every wall is threaded with capillaries finer than hair, millions of them, so that the seepage comes out evenly, in ordered lines, gathering into runnels, sliding down the terrace fronts in braided threads that the ferns drink straight from the air of. Where the water has run longest there is the faintest first tea-color of mineral stain — the only mark of age in the chamber, and it is perhaps three weeks old.

Because everything here is perhaps three weeks old. This is the strangest thing to stand inside: a whole young ecosystem where nothing has died yet. There is no rot, no compost-sweetness, no leaf-lie under the swards. The moss terraces are bright and even; the vine-lattices on the terrace fronts are glossy and unblemished; the ferns stand in their colonies like the idea of ferns, and they grow at a pace you can *watch*. Sit on a kerb for ten minutes and a fiddlehead will visibly uncurl, a green comma becoming a sentence. The plants are engineered for it — warm, wet, low light, fast metabolism — and they are the light. There is almost no illumination in the chamber that isn't leaf-borne. The swards hold a pale green-white glow, the vines carry light along their veins like milk in glass, and the brightness follows the water: brightest where the runnels pass, dimming upslope, so that the whole chamber reads as a city seen at night from a hill, with the lit streets — the wet ones — winking.

At the center of the plaza basin stands the first tree. A sapling, maybe two meters, its bark still smooth and pale as new pottery, and it is visibly the point of everything: its roots have already found the mains, its crown is the tallest thing in the vault, and the survey spiral stamped by the stair is, you slowly realize, its root system drawn in section. This chamber is the sapling's infrastructure. Or the sapling is the chamber's heart. The designation 1 suggests the distinction won't matter long.

There are no animals. Not few — none. No buzz, no click, no scuttle, no birdstart. The register of sound that every other green place on earth fills automatically is simply absent, and the absence rings. Pollination, where it's needed, is handled by the water and by the motes: a sparse handful of machines smaller than gnats that drift the terraces on slow rounds, checking leaf-veins, coaxing gutters. They are the closest thing to fauna, and there are fewer than a dozen in the entire vault, so the emptiness stays empty. You hear your own footsteps too clearly in here. You hear the room instead of life.

And the room is *loud*. It is the loudness of rain on a tin roof with the rain one room away — enveloping, constant, somehow domestic. The hum underneath everything is the structure itself: the pumps, the curing lattice, the thermal engines running on the temperature difference between the chamber's warm air and the cold ground beyond the walls. Above the hum sits the water-register — the ordered weeping, the runnels' murmur, the bright ticking of drips into the plaza basin, which has the regularity of a clock made of falling water. And every forty-odd minutes, the ceiling rains.

The ceiling is where the place confesses what it is. It is a smooth vault, and it sweats — the warm air hits it and gives up its water — but the condensate does not gather randomly. It beads and runs along grooves, fine wet lines that cross and branch and knot, and if you stand in the plaza and look up long enough, the pattern resolves: it is a street map. It is the map of the city overhead, drawn overhead *in* the chamber, street for street, the water above tracing the plan of the city below. When the condensate film thickens past a threshold, the whole grid lets go at once, and for three minutes it rains straight down in thousands of fine filaments — each filament standing on a street — and the ferns all tilt, and the sapling's leaves turn over like palms, and the hum shifts under the noise and resolves, briefly, into a chord. A held major third, low and round. The building singing while it drinks.

In the far wall, low on the seventh terrace, there is an archway that is only half-formed. Its edges are soft, mid-fabrication, and beyond it is a darkness with the same tide-line texture beginning on its walls — a second chamber, budding, a smaller copy of this one already being grown. The numeral 1 stamped by the stair implies a long row of numerals to come. This is not a garden that was built. This is a garden that *reproduces*.

The rules of the place, as far as the place has declared them:

— Water obeys lanes. It goes nowhere it was not given a road.
— Growth runs on schedule. The ferns keep the clock better than any instrument.
— The city above is drawn in the ceiling, in water, always current, always accurate.
— Nothing dies here yet. There is no room in the design for death; it will have to be invented when it arrives.
— The sound never stops, because the systems never sleep, and the structure is still becoming itself.
— Every forty minutes, rain. Every rain, the chord. Every ninth day, a new terrace line appears in the walls.

The emptiness, you come to understand, is not absence. It is preparation — the emptiness of a set table, of a nursery before the child, of a first draft written in ceramic and moss and light. Whoever pressed their hands into that soft panel on the fourth ring built a town's whole plumbing and streets and plaza and tree, and then went back up the stair and left it running, warm and wet and loud and lit from within, waiting for people who have not come yet.

You stand in the plaza while the ceiling gathers. The map brightens, street by street. Then the grid lets go, the filaments come down, the chord rises out of the hum, and the first tree drinks the city's rain in the city's cellar, one ring at a time.
