The heat arrives first, through the soles of your feet. Before your eyes have sorted the dimness into shapes, the ground has already told you the essential thing: this world is being fired from beneath. The warmth is steady, blood-warm and then warmer, the warmth of a wall with a hearth behind it — except the wall is the entire floor of a continent, and the hearth is everything underneath.

What you're standing on is tile. Hand-formed tile, palm-sized, laid in courses that run past the reach of sight, and no two are alike. Each carries the ridges of the thumb that pressed it, the drag of a knuckle at one edge, a crescent where a fingernail trimmed the slip. You can read them like handwriting for the first mile: confident strokes, tired strokes, a stretch where the presser was hurried and the tiles came out thin. Then the mile ends and the reading doesn't, because the tilefield is the world, horizon to horizon, under everything, and you understand that whatever hand made these made a great many more than any hand could make.

The joints between them are filled with clay slip that never cures. Water stands in every joint — a million little mirrors reflecting the sky, laid out in gridwork to the edge of everything. Dig anywhere — kneel and push a blade of anything into the seam between tiles — and within a finger's depth the hole fills with warm mineral water, slick with suspended clay, tasting faintly of iron and fired earth. The water table is practically at grade. Nothing here can go down. Everything therefore spreads: the whole civilization of the place is flat, wide, rambling, built on rafts of its own making, sprawling because it cannot sink and is only rarely permitted to rise.

The sky is the second fact. There is no sun. There are no stars, no moon, no direction to the light. Overhead — and it feels like *overhead*, close, the way the roof of a low room is overhead — hangs a vault of dull grey-orange luminescence, the exact color of the interior of a kiln at the moment it is neither firing nor cool. Light arrives from everywhere at once and so every shadow is soft, doubled, undecided, a shadow of shadows. The vault sweats. Condensation beads on its underside in slow gathering fronts and comes down as warm drizzle that smells like a pottery studio in a rainstorm — hot earth, wet brick, faint smoke, iron. This is the weather, and the world is large enough to have weather of its own: drizzle-fronts that take half a day to pass over you, fog-banks that condense at knee height and drift between the works, and, rarer, the firings.

The firings come without schedule. The vault brightens by a shade; the warmth underfoot climbs from blood-warm to oven-warm; every standing pool flashes off into steam and the fog rises to the chest and the works become islands in a white silence. During a firing everything is louder and nothing is louder — the tiles tick with expansion, the gutters run harder, the slump-sounds come more often, and at the peak of it whole districts go vitrified if the fire beneath them runs too hot: acres of brick fuse into a single sheet of black glaze, slick, beaded with sweat, too hot to touch for weeks. The glazefields are the only glass in the world, and the only wreckage. Nothing here erodes. Nothing has been rained on long enough to wear, no edges are rounded except the ones made round on purpose, there is no dust — dust is a kind of death, and nothing here has died yet. The place has no ruins, because ruin requires time. What it has instead is failure: walls slumped, vaults sat down into themselves, stairways folded — wet, soft, recent, always recent. The opposite of age, here, is not youth. It is attention.

Because that is the actual physics of the place, and it takes a while to see it. Walk the tilefield long enough and you pass through regions that are *held* — districts where the masonry disobeys its own weight, walls one brick thick and two hundred feet tall, staircases with no underside, a vault springing from nothing, cantilevered out over its own shadow. These stand because something enormous is still attending to them, the way your hand can hold a ribbon of clay in any shape you like as long as you don't let go. Then the attention moves on.
