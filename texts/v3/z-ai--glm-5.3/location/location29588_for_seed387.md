Go down in stages. First the shelf: a drowned plateau of grey water, crossed by shipping lanes that have never once been interrupted by anything worth interrupting for. Then the seabed, plain limestone, silted, patient. Then through two hundred meters of that rock, which the water above has spent a hundred thousand years negotiating with, and has finally bargained itself a passage — a throat, a flooded fissure-system through which a small sea's worth of tide drains twice a day into some deeper basin on the other side of the mountains no one remembers drowning. And midway down the throat, where the fissure pinches and then, inexplicably, opens wide, there is a pocket. The people who live there call it the Gullet. In the Gullet there is a town.

The town exists because of three facts about the water, and the water is the first thing, the last thing, and nearly the only thing.

It is warm. Warm the way a fever is warm, the way the inside of a cheek is warm — thirty-three, thirty-four degrees, blood-heat, all year, which here means nothing because there are no years of weather, only the eternal same warm dark. The warmth comes up from below, from the same deep heat that made the fissures in the first place; the Gullet is a warm room in a cold house of stone. The water tastes of coins and, faintly, of struck matches. Near the vents it fizzes against the tongue, faintly carbonated, champagne with iron in it.

And it is loud. This is the fact that governs everything else, and it must be understood properly: not loud the way a factory is loud to someone walking past it. Loud the way weather is loud. Loud the way being inside a bell is loud, a bell struck and never permitted to finish ringing. The roar has parts, and the natives know them the way you know voices: the Bellow of the narrows where the whole tide squeezes through; the Hiss of the vent chimneys; the Hammer of the turbine-gates
