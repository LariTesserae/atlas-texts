**The Duty Surface**

Stand anywhere. It hardly matters where — the floor keeps itself.

Underfoot it is silver, brushed, seamless in the way of things that were never one piece but have become one. Every square meter of it is assembly. Look close and you can count the joinery: seam, gasket, seam, a strip of panel glass, a run of torqued fasteners with their witness marks still legible, seam again. The plain is flat to the horizon, and the flatness is a lie — it is not emptiness but saturation, a surface packed shoulder to shoulder with function, every centimeter spoken for. Somewhere in the first kilometer you stop looking for bedrock. There is no bedrock. There was never anything here to be rock. The floor is the top of the machine, and the machine goes all the way down.

Every few kilometers the metal still carries its legend, stamped in a hand-wide standard: **DUTY SURFACE — KEEP SWEEP**. The number that followed has been polished away by dust, by feet, by ten thousand centuries of nothing much happening. The travelers who come here — there have never been many — call the whole country by what remains: the Duty Surface. It is a floor that is, technically, still on duty.

**The light does not travel.**

There is no sun. The sky is a surface — the far interior of the shell — and it glows, white-gold, broadly, from horizon to horizon, brighter than an overcast noon with no sun to find behind it. Shadows here are soft and stationary. They never rotate. The day is a brightness, not a body, and time is kept by intensity: the shell dims on a schedule old enough that no one living knows if it is still correct, then brightens again. Dim-day is not dark. Night, on the Duty Surface, is a gray-gold dusk that goes on for hours while the world does its quiet work.

Up close, the sky is mottled. Most panels burn steady. Some are dead — dark scabs of unlit glass a hundred kilometers overhead — and the light around them dims the land below into pale shadow-countries where nothing grows and the dust lies undisturbed. Once, looking up long enough, you see that the sky is terrain. The far side has its own Duty Surface: low spines in silhouette, salt flats faintly amber, its own dead panels like lakes of shadow. Both of you are standing on each other's sky. Their weather falls toward you; yours falls toward them.

**Far things stand high.**

This is the first rule of distance, and it takes days to stop mistrusting it. The shell is vast — the plain inside it is continental, and the horizon it offers is inverted. A ridge ten kilometers off appears above the nearer rises, not below. The whole landscape climbs gently toward its own horizon, which is really the middle distance of an upside-down world. If you walk long enough toward anything, it descends to meet you, arrives, and becomes ground — and whatever was behind you begins its slow ascent into sky.

You could walk for a year and not cross it. The spines — raised heat-rails, the tallest things on the plain, twenty meters and dead straight — run for a thousand kilometers without a bend, and their shadows are the only lines on the land that mean anything to the eye. The dust lies in every other direction in long, low, combed drifts that are themselves the surface's doing, sorted nightly by weight and left like tide-lines on a sea that never existed.

**It is warm everywhere, and dry in the way of things that have outlived moisture.**

The heat comes up through your boots. It is body-warm, faintly electric, the waste-heat of processes that still run below, and it does not quit at dim-day. There is no water on the Duty Surface — none in the air, none in the ground, none in memory — but there is a substitute, and the world keeps its hygiene with it. When the light dims, a solvent condenses out of the engineered air: a fine, quick, quicksilver-weight dew that beads on every cool surface and will not wet. It gathers itself into threads, runs along the seams by capillary pull, lifts dust as it goes, and drains into the low sumps. The world cleans itself at night, in a chemistry older here than rain.

By mid-bright the dew is gone, evaporated back into the air, leaving sorted dust-beaches and, in the sumps, the crystallized coolant of the ages: hexagonal plates of engine-amber salt, warm rose and gold, cracking underfoot like thin pottery. It is the only color on the plain that isn't metal, oxide-blue, or light.

The air smells of hot metal, faint ozone, and the solvent — a smell like machine shop and, very distantly, pear.

**The silence is not an absence. It is a specification.**

Someone built this world to be quiet, before anything else that can be known about it. The dust moves without noise. The wind — when the filtration systems deep below exhale, every eleven days, a slow laminar breath that crosses the plain for a day and a night and streams every sedge-tuft in one direction — is a wind you see and do not hear. The loudest regular event on the Duty Surface is a tick: some panel, somewhere within earshot, releasing thermal stress with a single click, perhaps twice an hour. At dead dim-day, if you stand still long enough, you can hear one bead of solvent let go of a seam-edge and drop into a sump — a sound like one falling drop of water, magnified by silence, audible for a kilometer.

Walkers navigate by it, the old ones say. Walkers navigate by everything.

**The vegetation was made, and it knows it.**

It is sparse — tufts, films, never a forest. The dominant form is a sedge of photovoltaic fiber: glassy, silver-green, glinting like wire where the light catches it. It does not eat; it charges. It harvests the shell-glow and for a million years it has paid its harvest onward, trickling milliwatts down through its roots into a grid that still, in most places, accepts. Some colonies have gone feral. They keep the charge, grow thick and bolder, and their crystalline roots pry at the seams, opening metal like a slow frost. There is a moss, too — a film of semiconductor that spreads in circuit-trace whorls, each lichen a map of its own growth — and it colonizes the witness marks, so that the fastener-patterns of the plain bloom faintly green after rainless aeons, geometry fluorescing as biology.

The bloom-lines are knife-sharp. Where a dead panel's shadow-country begins, the sedge stops mid-field, a border straight as a tide-mark. Inside the shadow: bare metal, deep dust, deeper quiet.

**The fauna is a countdown.**

Nothing here is born. The custodians were made, and nothing now repairs them, so every failure is permanent and the animal life of the plain is a number that only goes down. The walkers come first: four-legged, a meter high, brushed alloy gone matte with dust, stepping on magnetic feet that make no sound at all. Each follows a route it has followed since it was dispatched, and the route shows — some have worn their own loops thirty centimeters into the floor, canyons of pure intent, polished grooves you could follow blindfolded. When a walker passes you, it pauses. The head stays level. It holds still for four or five seconds — logging you, counting you, filing you somewhere — and moves on.
