# The Kiln Garden

There is no soil here. That is the first thing to understand, and the last thing the mind accepts. The floor of this place is fired clay — glazed in most spots, raw and chalky in others — cast in interlocking tiles the size of a human palm, and from every tile a stem rises. The whole garden grows out of manufacture. Whatever hand or hunger built this room planted it the way a potter plants a pattern, and then left, and the pattern kept growing.

It is a small place. You could walk its length in ninety strides, though you will not, because the far wall recedes by a hand's width for every ten steps you take, and returns them all when you stop and breathe. The garden has no interest in preventing you from leaving. It simply does not commit to distances. Paths measured with string on Tuesday measure differently on Thursday, and the garden was never asked which Tuesday it prefers.

No water. Nothing here has ever tasted water. The vegetation — and it is lush, absurdly lush, a saturation of green that presses in from every side — drinks something else. Cut a stem and it bleeds a pale, warm liquor that smells of hot bread and iron shavings, and that beads up on your skin without wetting it, rolling off in perfect spheres that the ground-creatures come running for. The plants are plumbed into channels beneath the tiles, capillary grooves fired into the ceramic, and somewhere below — no one has found where — a slow warm reservoir of this liquor sits and seeps upward all day. The garden is warm everywhere, evenly warm, like the inside of a cup that was filled an hour ago. Nothing rots here. Fallen leaves dry into thin translucent flakes like stained glass, and pile up, and become paths of their own.

The growth is dense to the point of courtesy's failure. Leaf touches leaf. Nothing has space; everything has, somehow, room. The stems lean on one another the way tired travelers lean in a doorway, and the canopy, ten feet up, is a thatch so complete that the ordinary daylight arrives only in coin-sized spots, wandering as the unseen sun above the unseen ceiling moves. There is a ceiling — tile like the floor, vaulted in shallow arches, and the light comes through a rank of narrow clerestory slits that no ladder built by the current inhabitants could reach. Who cut those slits, and for what sky, is the garden's one unanswered question, and the inhabitants have learned not to ask it aloud, because the garden sometimes answers.

The fauna is where the abundance lives. Small ceramic-colored creatures, six-legged, shell-backed, big as a thumbnail to big as a fist, and every one of them eats, or tends, or prunes. There are the mowers, who travel the paths in files and shear the ground-flakes into neat windrows. There are the drinkers, who crowd around any cut stem and roll the beaded liquor down their throats without touching it — the bead simply sinks into their shell. There are the drummers, who are nearly invisible, and who do what their name says, very softly, twice a day at the warm hours: a ticking from somewhere inside the leaf-thatch, metronome-steady, four beats, silence, four beats. It is the loudest sound in the garden. You will come to wait for it. The creatures are tame in the way of things that have never met a predator, which is to say they treat you as furniture, and climb you.

People live here. A handful of them — a family or the memory of one, three to seven depending on the season and on how the paths have been measuring. Their tools are stone, bone, and a little hammered copper traded up from gardens further along whatever corridor the garden sits in. They prune with blade-shells. They weave the dried leaf-flakes into mats. They harvest the liquor in shallow bowls and warm it over tiny charcoal made from compressed flake-dust, and the charcoal hisses and smells of bread, and the whole economy of the place is a garden within a garden — a tended acre inside a built world that was already completely, totally, finished before they or anything else arrived. This is the vertigo at the heart of it: nothing here is natural, not the ground, not the light, not the warmth, and yet everything here grows. They have no origin story with a maker in it. They have a story about hatching from the drummers' fourth beat, and they tell it without embarrassment.

And through all of it, gently, the breakdown. Weirdness here does not tear. It erodes — but the garden is young, geologically, freshly made or freshly emerged, so there is nothing to erode; the unreality is arriving, not leaving. Edges have begun to soften where two walls meet: not blurred, but *negotiated*, the angle a little different each time, as though the corner is still deciding. One aisle, the third from the east wall, runs level going and slopes faintly returning. A certain broad-leafed stand exists only in the left periphery of vision; look straight at it and see young shoots, look away and see, in the corner of the eye, great mature plants heavy with seed-pods that are not there when you turn. The inhabitants harvest those pods. They have learned the trick of walking past without turning their heads, and they thresh what they catch. It works perhaps one attempt in five. They do not gamble their survival on it — the visible garden feeds them fine — but they say the peripheral harvest tastes more real.

The garden does not decay. That may be the crack in everything. Nothing dries to dust, nothing dies and stays dead; a mower crushed underfoot reassembles over three slow days, walking again on the fourth. And so the process that governs this place — its only real weather — is a slow swelling, a long patient thickening. The lushness increases by an amount you cannot see but can measure: the paths narrow by a finger's width a year, the canopy sinks an inch, the light-coins shrink. The inhabitants prune harder every generation. They speak of their ancestors' paths as if describing rivers. They expect that someday, in some descendant's lifetime, the green will close entirely, the light will run out, and the garden will become a solid warm block of leaf — and they expect this without despair, the way one expects winter, and they are building, with stone tools and woven mats and borrowed copper, an archive of clearings. Open spaces, cut and kept, each one named for a person, each one defended daily with shell-blades against the slow green advance.

The garden, for its part, does not seem to mind. The drummers tick at the warm hours. The liquor beads and rolls. The corner keeps deciding. It is a made place, warm and quiet and small, growing inside its own completion, and the only thing it has ever asked of anyone is that they not ask who it was for.
