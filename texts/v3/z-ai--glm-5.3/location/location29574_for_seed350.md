You hear it long before you see it — a weather of noise standing over the low country like heat stands over a road. The valley drowned recently, and it has not stopped being loud since.

From the rim, the water does not look possible. It brims the basin exactly to the hilltops, a green-black table of flood with no spill, no outlet, no explanation — a valley filled to the lip like a bowl carried carefully by someone you cannot see. The edges are young, that's the thing you notice second. Fresh gullies cut into the slopes like knife marks. The high-water line sits crisp as a shelf. Nothing has been softened yet; the bricks underwater still have their corners, the broken glass still has its edges, the mudbanks stand at angles water should have already argued down. The flood is new. Everything it touched, it touched sharply.

Only one thing breaks the surface: the foundry smokestack, breathing steam into a low permanent storm ceiling, marking the drowned town the way a mast marks a wreck.

Down through the surface is to go through a garden. The whole flood is capped by the Bloom — raft-gardens of vegetation woven tight enough to walk on if you were foolish, white flowers the size of dinner plates, the texture of boiled starch. Rain falls out of the storm all day and all night, a hard drumming that comes down through the water as continuous applause, and yet the flood never rises, because the Below-Rain falls upward out of the valley floor at the same rate, mud and silver bubbles ascending past you on the way down. The water recycles itself. The storm is a lid on a pot that is cooking.

The water is warm — bathwater, broth — and not evenly. You descend through thermoclines like passing through doors, some rooms of the flood hotter than others, and the hottest rooms are always downstream of the machines.

Because the machines are still running.

The town below is mostly gone — that must be said first. The flood took the roofs and most of the walls, and what it didn't take, the gardens buried. The streets survive as ridges of mossy cobble running through the green like the bones of a hand under a sheet. Scattered, partial, half-erased: a gable here, a doorway standing free of its house, a bathtub colonized by ferns. But three structures hold their shape. The Water Tower, crowned with a garden that blooms dry above the flood line. The chapel, of which more later. And the Powerhouse, humming.

The hum arrives in your chest before your ears. The turbines under the Weir turn because the flood turns them; the cables between the drowned pylons still carry current, and where the insulation split, the water holds arcing light — small auroras, green-white, the brightest thing this valley produces. Streetlamps burn underwater in their hundreds, each bulb haloed in algae, so that descending through the districts feels like sinking through a town at dusk with every lamp just switched on and no one home. The Stamping Hall still stamps. Every few seconds a press delivers its blow somewhere in the murk, and the sound travels through the water and through you, absolute — sound here is not something you hear, it is something the valley does to you.

Here is the loop, which is the closest thing this place has to a metabolism: the flood turns the turbines; the turbines power the presses, the pumps, the bellows that breathe water now instead of air; the machines leak their heat into the flood, which is why the water is warm; the warmth grows the plants at a speed that has to be seen to be disbelieved; and the plants — cane, seed-oil, the crushed sweetness of fermenting fruit — feed the boilers and choke the intakes and foul the bearings until a machine stalls, and the stall is the only silence this valley ever produces, and it never lasts. The whole basin is an engine mid-stroke. It inhales water, exhales garden.

And the ecology is engine-fed. There are no animals — none. No fish in a valley of water, no birds under a permanent storm, no insects, no faces anywhere, nothing that flees you, nothing that watches. Every niche an animal would fill is filled by a performance instead. The presses shake the pollen loose with every blow; the plants are pollinated by percussion. Seeds ride the conveyor belts that still crawl along the drowned tram-lines, are sorted, spilled, planted by machinery that was built for coal and iron and now, without instruction, farms. Fruits burst in the warm water into gas, and bladder-weeds harvest the bubbles and stand up into the gloom on them. The Dye House still bleeds its stock — plumes of magenta and chrome-yellow unfurling through the streets decades ahead of schedule — and the gardens grow in those colors, orchards of wrong-colored apples, lawns of vivid unnatural green.

The density is the real vertigo. This is not a landscape you look *across*; it is a landscape you look *into*. Surface Bloom. Cane forest thick as a market crowd at mid-depth. Roofline gardens where every surviving ridge sprouts its own thicket. Street-level meadow on the cobble bones. Benthic lawn. Root-cathedrals under the paving, arches of living wood holding up streets that no one walks. Every layer near-touching the next, saturation all the way down, and in the growth zones — warm pockets near the boiler outfalls — time pools and thickens, and a sapling becomes a giant between one shift-bell and the next.

And the valley does its own animal impressions, which is worse, somehow, than animals. Kelp that schools. Reed-stands that stalk the shallows with the patience of herons. The old loading crane that lifts and sets its feet like a wading bird. And once a shift, when the drowned tannoy crackles its slurred half-hour, the flood itself plays back the last morning: a dawn chorus, faint and flattened, a dog barking at the shift-change, bicycle bells. The water remembers the animals. It keeps their sounds the way silt keeps footprints, and it plays them on schedule. The only birds left here are archived in the flood.

The rules, as the valley keeps them: the flood is loyal — it will not leave, it may deepen. Dryness is not a condition of physics but a property certain rooms refuse to surrender; the chapel stands open to the water, doors wide, and the water stops at the sills, and inside it is dry, candle-dim, planted with land-flowers, the one quiet room, where the roar of the whole valley arrives reduced to a thought. Depth does not accumulate honestly here — swim to the floor of the basin and feel no honest pressure, only the Below-Rain falling against your face. Look down past the mud sometimes and you will see the sky-under: clouds, lit from beneath, drifting below the valley floor. The tower on Hill Street has a staircase that climbs out of the flood entirely and delivers you, dry, into a different evening, at a second surface no one has ever mapped, and it is a long way back down.

At the head of the valley stands the Weir, and over the Weir the storm is rooted — a slow rotating eye in the ceiling, turning like the turbine it feeds, or is fed by. The water is born there. Or the machines are. The two are so long married now that causality has flooded too; asking which came first is like asking which of a river's ends is upstream. Near it, underwater, the old bell tower still stands to its waist, and a rope of kelp swings in the current and rings the bell, once per shift. The valley counting itself.

Do not map this place; that is the last rule, and it enforces itself. It is turbulent the way a storm is turbulent, the way a piston is — describe a street and by the end of your sentence it has gone under the Bloom; describe a machine and by your last word a root has cracked its housing and it has shuddered back to life anyway. Everything here is either hours old or aging by the hour, and the valley has not yet decided which it is. The noise, if you let it, will read to you as grief: the loudest possible way to fill the silence the animals left. But it is not grief. Listen to the presses keeping the pollen moving, the bellows breathing, the bell tolling the shift. It is a birth, conducted industrially, at volume, in warm water — and you have arrived very early.
