**The Leak**

You come down to it, because there is no other way in. A stair is cut into the rock of the shaft, three hundred steps of them, worn hollow in the middle by feet, and the sound climbs to meet you long before the light does. At first it is only a pressure in the soles of your boots, a tremor in the handrail. By step one hundred it is a river in your ears. By step two hundred you notice something that has no business being true this far underground: the walls are lit. Not the gray half-light of a lamp behind you, but a warm gold coming up from below, as if dawn were a place rather than a time, and you were walking down into it.

The stair opens into one room. That is the whole country of this place — a single chamber, cathedral-sized, bounded on every side, with a ceiling high enough to lose itself in mist. Cavers call it the Leak, and the name is accurate and entirely insufficient.

Through the ceiling, where the rock has been cut back and sealed and cut open again, a trunk line descends into the room. It is a light-main, thick as a thigh, armored in a dull ceramic weave, one filament of a network laid generations ago to carry the traffic of somewhere else. It was never meant to end here. Somewhere in the dark above, long ago, it was severed, and the chamber has spent the years since learning what to do with a wound like that.

The trunk tried to heal itself, the way its makers built it to. Self-repair material bled from the cut face and hardened into a droplet of glassy regrowth around the break — but the bead set crooked, and cracked, and stopped. What it left behind is an accidental lens. The severed end hangs free below it, and everything the trunk still carries pours out through that bead in a cone of raw luminance, day-bright, thick enough to lean against. The end is under tension and under heat, and it never stops moving. It whips. Slowly, mostly, in a long patient arc, and sometimes all at once, cracking the air like a shot. The floor beneath its sweep is scoured pale and smooth, a kill zone decades old. A maintenance gantry once stood there; it lies where it was cut down, in pieces, the metal sheared clean as if the light had opinions about it.

And the light is not blank. It carries whatever the network above still moves — you can see the load in it. The beam surges and dims, flickers through greens and hard whites and sodium ambers, and in the mist you catch frames of what it has been: a coastline for a moment, a wheat field, a face mid-word, calibration grids, text in no living script. A broadcast with no receiver. When the world above is busy, the chamber is broad daylight; when the surface sleeps, the room dims to ember. The garden below keeps the hours of a sun it has never seen.

The water is what makes the room loud. A river comes in through a siphon at the eastern wall and drops as a falls into a churn, whitewater boiling over itself, before it runs the length of the floor and dives down another dark throat to continue whatever journey it was on. The spray never settles. Mist hangs in the beam in visible shafts, sweeps the room when the whip-end swings, and rises until it touches the cold stone of the high ceiling — where it condenses and falls back as a fine drizzle. The room rains on itself, softly, constantly. The walls weep long black streaks where the seepage finds its paths, paths that have not moved in a hundred years.

It is warm. Deep stone everywhere else is a cold that finds your bones; here, sleeves come off within minutes. The beam heats the dark rock and the water it touches, the trunk sheds its losses along its run, and the mist carries it all around. The pool that sits inside the cone glows from within, lit green-gold to its depth, and is bathtub-warm.

Against that warmth, life has taken its claim — sparsely, precisely, in the places where the light's margin overlaps standing water. A moss, gold-green and quick, carpets perhaps a tenth of the floor in a ragged crescent at the edge of the sweep. Ferns lean out of the wall-seeps like question marks. One vine with small white flowers climbs an old anchor bolt toward the bead, flower by flower, as if it had an appointment. And on the bare stone beyond the garden you can read the chamber's memory: rings of dead dry moss, ghost gardens from decades past, marking where the cone reached before the cable's mood shifted. Centuries of sweeping have bleached a comet-track of lighter stone across the floor, a orbit drawn by light instead of erosion.

The fauna is one species, and it arrived by accident, the way everything here did. White moths, small, their eggs most likely rode down in somebody's coat seam generations back. Their larvae live in the moss; the adults drink at the pools and visit the vine's flowers; at "noon," when the network above is busiest, they orbit the heal-bead in slow spirals, a private planetary system of a few hundred wings. When the light storms, they vanish into the crevices. In the siphon pool, sometimes, a pale shape turns once in the black — nobody has ever caught more than that flicker, and the moths pretend not to know about it.

What people have left is faint and scattered, spaced wide across the floor the way everything here is spaced wide: the failed vault door hanging open above; the chalk arrows and a tally of days counted to four hundred and something; three small cairns facing the light; a cup fused to its ledge by lime, left as an offering or forgotten, no way to know. And one artifact that is not a ruin — a maintenance drone, long dead, parked on a flat stone with its lensed face aimed at the break, moss grown up over its back like a blanket.
