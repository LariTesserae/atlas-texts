## The Pan — Thermal Station Four, seventy metres under the city

**The descent.** The cage drops through seventy metres of strata in stages — shale, marl, then a pale limestone that gets warmer as it goes, until the steel walls of the cage sheen over with the first condensation and you understand that the wetness below is not weather, it is a condition. Bats use this shaft too; they commute along it at dusk, and by ordinance the hoist stands down from 20:40 to 21:10 so the out-flight goes unbroken. At the bottom, a steel door, a sign-in board, and then the Pan: a single irregular vault, a cathedral's worth of hollow that nobody carved on purpose and nobody has finished carving yet.

The heat is not an event down here. It is the medium. Thirty-nine degrees at the working floor, warmer near the rising seams, and the air carries it the way surface air carries light. The Pan taxes time in exchange: forty-five minutes in the warm, fifteen in the air-conditioned office container, salt tablets on the shelf beside the kettle. Sweat doesn't dry. It is wrung out.

**Terrain.** The chamber is young — geologically speaking, an infant. It's a hypogenic void, born from below: warm, sulfide-bearing water still rises through joints in the floor and roof, and the rock is dissolving faster than it can decorate itself. There are no stalactite columns, no draped flowstone. There are a few soda straws, single hollow threads, the first earnest attempts — the oldest is shorter than a finger and, by drip count, younger than the shaft. The walls are raw, cream-and-grey, scalloped in palm-sized hollows that record the current direction of water which is still, faintly, moving. Where sulfide seams bleed, the rock rusts in orange tears. Hanging from the warmest cracks are soft, mucus-like sleeves of bacterial film, warm to the touch, that drip — and those drips are faintly acid, already pitting the new concrete plinths by about a millimetre a year, pits the survey book dates like birthdays.

The space is small by the standards of cities and vast by the standards of rooms: ninety-some paces across at its longest, a domed ceiling lost in the dark, a low end where the floor becomes the sump — a black, warm, softly moving pond whose surface sits a metre and a half below the lip. That margin is the whole project. A red line is painted on the rock beside it, the water's patience, marked.

**The works.** What makes it urban rather than merely a cave is the organization. The site has a perimeter, a floor plan, a chainage. The first pump loop is live: two pumps on concrete plinths, a surge pipe, a transformer humming in its cage, cable trays descending the shaft in black bundles like roots feeding a tree of light. The second loop is half-born — flanges, crates, scaffold, a rail track for muck cars whose sleepers are slowly going soft in the warm mud. Shotcrete patches grey the bad rock. Survey bolts wear red paint. Arrows stencilled on the wall: TO PUMPS / TO SUMP / TO SHAFT. A muster point sign, a laminated map, mugs, boots drying on a warm pipe.

Everything built sweats. The ceiling condenses; the ceiling rains. It has never not been raining somewhere in the Pan. So the switchgear lives under tarpaulin roofs, drip loops hang off every cabinet like untied shoelaces, and metal rusts downward from above — the corrosion line runs inverted, everything pitted and scabbed at the top and bright near the floor, as if the whole chamber were upside-down weather. When it rains in the city overhead, the roof begins to weep six hours later, joint by joint, and the crews can read tomorrow's weather in tonight's ceiling. The trains pass three streets over and a world away: every few minutes the puddles shiver, felt more than heard.

And the light: dim, deliberate, an archipelago. One string of site lamps down the walkway, half of them shrouded grey with moth bodies, their fixtures swept clean each shift and clouded again by the next. Indicator LEDs on the pump panel make a small constellation of green and amber. Away from the islands, ninety paces of open dark separate the pump hall from the container, and you cross it by headlamp through air so full of wings the beam looks grainy, like old film. The only green in the entire Pan lives in the lamps' penumbra — a moss-film on the wet rock that the crews scrub monthly and which returns in three weeks, obedient as a lawn.

**Sound.** The Pan is loud the way a river is loud: constantly, and almost without change. The live pumps lay down a bass drone at mains frequency with its harmonics stacked above it; a pressure-reducing valve hisses like a long exhale. Over that runs the drip-percussion off the ceiling, each drop with a cathedral's worth of reverb — a dropped wrench rings for ten seconds. And threaded through everything, a fine continuous insect static, thousands of small bodies stridulating and ticking at once, a noise with no edges. The soundscape today is the soundscape of last month, and of the month before. Nothing in the Pan hurries except the pumps.

Which is why silence is the fire alarm. When the drone stops, everyone stands still and listens to what is underneath it — the water, actually moving, actually rising. The rule is absolute: silence means get to the cage.

**The animals.** There are no plants to speak of, and the fauna is not diverse — it is multitudinous. The base of everything is warmth and chemistry. Rising sulfide water feeds the bacterial films: pale mats slicking the wet rock, and those hanging sleeves in the warm cracks, dripping. The bats and the moths import the rest — guano cones bank up below the old natural rift where the colony hangs, and tineid moth larvae work through them like a slow fire.

On that subsidy, abundance. The walls at the lamp margins look furred: thousands of pale isopods and amphipods grazing the film in drifts, moving like a tide line when your light sweeps them. Springtiles hop across the muck like spilled pepper. Cave crickets hang overhead, antennae longer than their bodies, hunting the ceiling's condensation. The moths come to the lamps in soft grey clouds and die there; wings collect beneath the fixtures in small snowdrifts that get swept out with the shift and weighed — the sweepings are tracked seasonally, like tide tables, and the moth bloom follows the warm discharge a season behind.
