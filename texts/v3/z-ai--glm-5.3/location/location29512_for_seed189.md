The way in is a crack that wasn't there last spring. It runs up the flank of a hill only three summers old — a hill the locals don't have a settled story about yet — and if you put your palm against its edges you'll find they haven't had time to round off. Chisel-fresh. A thumbnail flakes them. The stone still ticks faintly as it settles into being stone, and warm air rises out of the slot like breath from a mouth that has been sleeping. Someone has hung a curtain of pale felt across the entrance. Grown, not woven — it roots at both sides of the crack and closes itself behind you.

Then down. A short descent, thirty feet, half-turned and hand over hand, in light that gets stronger the deeper you go, which is the first thing your mind objects to and won't stop objecting to.

The room at the bottom is the size of a barn from outside and no fixed size from inside. That sounds like a thing people say. Here it is a thing that is true. The ceiling is a root crown — the underside of a giant young tree whose whole mass sits in the hill above you — and the crown glows the way stained glass glows, sap-bright, warm-gold, ordinary daylight bright, bright enough to read by, bright enough to see the greens of all these greens. It is the light that behaves wrongly. Light comes down here the way water should: it condenses on the cool ceiling in beads, gathers, drips. It runs downhill along the floor's low channels and stands in shallow puddles in the hollows of the leaves. It evaporates upward at dusk in a dim blue steam. Where the floor-pools shine, shadows climb the root columns and hang from them like smoke going the wrong way.
