# The Vented Dish

You hear it a kilometer before you see it.

The plain gives no warning. It has been finished for longer than anything has been alive — a peneplain, the world's final draft, everything soft ground off and carried away eons ago until only the hardpan remains, a gray-gold crust of laminated siltstone laid down in some ocean so old its water is now a mineral rumor. Lag gravel chatters underfoot. The horizon is a straight line in every direction, unbroken except for one far chimney, the height of a man, standing alone like a fence post for a fence that has otherwise been resolved into dust. This is a landscape at the end of its own story. Wind did its work, then gave up. There is nothing left here to flatten.

Except the sound. A low exhalation, continuous, like a kettle the size of a hill that has been coming to a boil for ten thousand years. Under it, a ticking — dense, metallic, arrhythmic, like a million clocks that never agreed. The ground carries it up through your boot soles before the air does.

The dish appears as nothing more than a change in the color of the gravel, then a step down, and then you are standing on the lip of the last interesting hole in the world: a shallow basin, four hundred meters across, maybe twenty deep, holding — cradled in it like a heart in a chest — the works.

---

## The Works

They are industrial in the way a whale fall is meat: unmistakably built, unmistakably dead, unmistakably sustaining everything around them. A processing plant, if that word still means anything — fractionating columns furred with rust and then with life, pipe racks running in ranks like the ribs of something that lay down here and was never buried, a compressor hall with its roof fallen in at one end so the light comes through in long dusty slats. Finned heat exchangers stick up between the ranks like the dorsal spines of fish in a dried lake. The light through the broken roof is ordinary, gray-gold, cool; motes hang in it, slow. Nothing here glows. Nothing here is strange to look at. The strangeness is all in the inventory of what is missing, which is water, and plants, and silence.

Because one bore still lives. Somewhere beneath the hall, driven two kilometers into the planet by hands nobody remembers, a tap into deep reduced gas still flows under its own ancient pressure. It feeds up through manifolds that no longer answer to any control but heat and habit, and every forty minutes or so — the period drifts with the day, with the deep tides of whatever is underneath — the hall exhales. A column of warm gas lifts from the vent heads, shimmers, and falls back.

That breath is the sun of this place.

## The Reef

Around every vent head, every warm pipe, every place where the machinery still leaks its cold fire into the cool air, life has grown in layers — actual, physical, countable layers, the way coral builds on a reef, except there is no ocean here and never was one near enough to matter. It is a reef hung in dry air.

The base of everything is a film: iridescent sheets of something too simple to call plant or animal, which creep over warm metal at centimeters per hour and brighten, visibly, when the vents bloom. It looks like oil on water, which is a simile this world cannot check. Grazers work it in thousands per square meter — limpet-shaped, chiton-shaped, domed shells of iron sulfide so like the pipes they eat from that a pipe and its population read as one object. Their radulae tick against the metal, and this is most of the ticking: the sound of an ecosystem feeding, amplified by the dish's acoustics into a continuous dense rattle, like rain on a tin roof in a world that has never once rained.

They shingle three and four deep. The bottom layer is always dead, cemented under the living, so the reef grows by dying in place — a sediment being laid down in real time. Cut a face into it and you can read the years: shell strata, band by band, thick bands where the vents ran hot, thin desperate bands where they faltered, and at the very bottom, flat things with right angles. A bolt. A strap. A plate stamped with markings no living thing here has the organs to care about.

## The Crowded Air

At a bloom, the air stops being air in any comfortable sense. Plume-flies — though they are not flies; nothing here is what it is called, the names are just the nearest fit — lift off the warm surfaces in a body and spiral up the exhalate column, wings of mica-dusted membrane, mouth-fans spread, harvesting molecules straight from the stream. They never leave the updrafts. In the still hours they stack on the pipe mouths in dense rosettes like barnacles on a fumarole, and when the breath comes, the entire column rises and resettles, and the sound of their wings in the metal halls is the hum of a substation, a sound with voltage in it.

Between the shells live the stridulators, small drummers that ride the hollow dead ones and beat on them — courtship, territorial claim, warning, no one here is sure the distinctions matter. Their chorus moves across the reef floor in fronts, like wind over a field, a wave of ticking that rises and collapses and rises.

The walls of the hall, where light falls through the broken roof, are barnacled with all of it, layered overgrowth on overgrowth, near-touching, dense past saturation — tubes of heatworms following the warm lines of dead pipe like roots following water, loops of them crossing the open floor like dropped cable, and always the mites, visible clouds of mites around the big bodies, thick enough to breathe.

## The Rules of the Place

The rules are simple and total. Heat is light: in a cool world, the warmth of machinery is the only harvestable energy, and everything hoards it the way a forest hoards sun. Gas is rain: the vents deliver, on a schedule, the carbon and the sulfur and the reduced plenty, and the whole calendar of the reef — spawning, molting, warring, resting — is keyed to the forty-minute breath. Iron is soil. And water — water is a poison nobody here has ever met. The old coolant lines, dry for geological ages, still run naked and bare through a reef that has grown over everything else, because whatever faint chemistry lingers in them, the fauna shuns it as a creature shuns a burn. Water is the one absence the ecosystem keeps a memory of.

The fauna are also the erosion. Understand this and the dish explains itself: in a world where wind and water finished their work long ago, the last agent of flattening is appetite. Generations of rasplers have eaten the basin's rock grain by grain, bio-karst in dry siltstone, the dish deepening a finger's width per millennium. The reef is not growing on the works. It is eating the works. Rust is a crop here, the sweetest one, and when the last column is digested the reef will turn, as reefs do, and eat itself, and then the plain will truly be finished, and quiet, and this will have been the world's last loud room.

Predation runs on the calendar like everything else. The sweepers — long, low, leggy things that move through the grazer carpets like a finger dragged through grain, front legs raked, mouthparts working like bolt-cutters — hunt in the still hours between blooms, when the plume-flies are grounded and slow. Where a sweeper passes, a wake of overturned shells, and the flies dive on the wreck of it, the reef's gulls. And in the deepest warm corner of the hall, against the bore head, stands the grand grazer — a thing the size of a shed, shelled in decades of accumulated epibionts, itself a landmark, itself substrate, breathing like slow bellows. It has not moved more than twenty meters in living memory. It does not need to. The warmth comes to it. Its cloud of mites is a small weather system.

## The Answerer

One more presence, and it is the reason the works feel occupied rather than merely inhabited. In the pipe ranks — the deep ones, the ones still warm — something lives that makes machine sounds. When the dead compressor housing ticks as it cools, something ticks back, a half-second late. When a valve hammer knocks, it knocks. The stridulators ignore it; it is not their grammar. Whether it is a mimic, a parasite of rhythm, something that courts by percussion in the idiom of the builders, or simply something that has heard the machines longer than machines have been dead — no observer has earned the answer, because the answerer only speaks to the works. Visitors who tap a rail twice report two taps in return, slightly wrong, slightly alive, and an hour of trying to make it happen again.

## The Coldest Hour

Near dawn, when the cool air pools in the dish and even the film pauses, the chorus drops away to almost nothing, and what is left is the base layer of sound: the vent hiss, low and eternal, and the tick of cooling metal — a sound that is not made by anything living, a sound that is the works' own slow heartbeat, counting down in the dark. Then the breath comes, the column lifts, the plume-flies rise in their thousands, the ticking of ten million radulae resumes like rain remembered, and the reef grows another layer over the last loud thing in a finished world.
