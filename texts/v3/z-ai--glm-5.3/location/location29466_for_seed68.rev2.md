**The Rises**

The road comes down into the valley through a cutting, and the cutting is young. That is the first thing you understand about this place: the rock faces on either side of you are still the color of the inside of the mountain. No weather has argued with them yet. You can see the drill marks, twin ranks of sockets where the charges sat, and the scar-tongues of blast all the way up. On cold mornings the whole cutting smokes softly, the stone breathing out dampness it has held for geological patience — the older keepers say the rock will go on exhaling for years, and you believe them, because it looks like a row of sleeping animals.

The valley below is low, wide, cool, and bright — a big silver sky leaning on soft green hills. A river used to wander through it, braided and shallow, drying to a thread in the rainless weeks, then throwing a fit in autumn. The engineers taught it manners. They dug it a straight, patient bed and spent the valley's whole nine metres of fall in two hundred metres of ground: a staircase of six lock chambers, water climbing and descending in steps, each gate a tread. On the map it is written *The Six Rises*. Everyone who works here just calls it the Stair.

Everything here is first-year. The brick of the lock walls is still orange-red, unstained, almost loud against the grey rock. The copings are blue engineering brick, sharp-cornered. The gate timbers were raw pine and have silvered only on their southern faces; the north sides are still honey-colored, still smelling of resin and of the tar they were dipped in. The iron — the railings, the sluice windlasses, the crane, the gear — is painted thick with black bitumen, and rust has not yet found its footing anywhere. Decay arrives here in a strict order, and the old hands can date any surface by it: first the tar goes dull, then the pine silvers all round, then the first coin-sized squares of lichen appear on the coping stones, then moss takes the shaded joints. The Stair keeps its own calendar, and the calendar has barely begun.

The settlement is strung out along the top pound like a sentence spoken slowly, with big silences between the words. Twelve cottages, a hundred meters apart in no particular hurry. A tally office. A coal store, the one genuinely gloomy building in the valley. A sawmill and a forge for the gate-repairs that already happen, because the young embankment settles a hand's width in every wet month and the gang tops it up and the gates go out of true. A chapel with a bell that speaks the lock signals in code. A crane standing in a wide gravel yard with nothing much to lift today, and one cart crossing a space the size of a market square, making it look, for a moment, like a town at festival — except the emptiness is ordinary here. The company built the place for a flood of trade that hasn't come yet. There is a warehouse with room in it for ten times its contents. There is a second row of cottages whose foundations were dug and stopped, raw trenches grassing over, a town-sized plan folded up and waiting inside a hamlet. You feel the future the way you feel a draft under a door.

And yet the valley is loud. That is the second thing you understand.

The signature sound is a fill. When the keepers wind up the sluices, the water comes through the culverts inside the walls with a roar like a train in a tunnel, and the pound climbs with a sound of wild breathing, and nobody talks during a fill — you shout into each other's ears or you wait. It goes on for minutes. Then it ends all at once, and the sudden quiet is a physical relief, like stepping out of a crowd. Between fills there is the bywash, where a turbine turns day and night under the surplus water, driving a dynamo in a shed that whines one thin high note, and the note is felt in the teeth and the boot-soles more than heard. A chain capstan clanks through its duty hauling barges off the stair. The bell rings its codes. In the tall pines planted as a windbreak along the spoil banks, rooks argue from first light at a volume that suggests an injury has occurred. And at dawn, the chorus — about which, more below — is bounced and doubled off the cutting walls, an acoustic bowl, deafening in the friendliest possible way.

The dampness here is a particular kind. Rain comes in short drumming bands on the tin roofs, runs off fast through the coarse young gravel, and is gone; the ground between the buildings is dusty by afternoon. The water lives in its pockets instead: in the pounds, in the millpond, in the bywash channel — and, every morning, on every metal surface in the valley. Dawn condensation sheathes the railings and the crane and the wire stays, and at first light there is a sound of a thousand small ticks, the whole ironworks dripping. One of the keepers claims the drips fall in the rhythm of the last lock-fill, whatever tempo the fill was worked at. There is no reason for this to be true. He has charts.

The lushness lives along the water and, most of all, around the warm places. Because the third strange thing about the Stair is the little summer. The engine house sheds heat, and the turbine outfall runs warm, and around that outfall there is a strip of valley where frost has never stood: hart's-tongue fern in the culvert shade, comfrey and fat nettles head-high, rushes, a fig ripening against the engine-house wall at an absurd latitude, and green grass going on through January while the rest of the valley goes grey-brown and waits. The spoil banks, mineral-rich and unclaimed, have been colonized at speed — willowherb turning the cut slopes rose-purple in August, birch scrub racing across the heaps, the greening working downward from the crests year by year. Balsam came in with barge ballast and behaves exactly like a species that arrived without enemies and knows it.

The fauna knows about the warm water too. In hard winters the fish gather at the outfall in a dark congregation, and so the herons have stopped leaving, one to each reach of bank, spaced out along the pounds like the cottages — sparse even in their abundance, each with its own address. A kingfisher owns the culvert mouth and its turbine-shed perch. Moorhen families wake the floating debris of the basin. Swallows nest in the lock gear itself, which has produced a rule: gates with nests are worked slow and gentle until fledging, and the company tolerates it, because the swallows take the midges, and the midges over the pounds at dusk rise in such columns that at night, when the lamps come on down the wire — one bulb per cottage, a moth-engine of a lamp at the basin — the air around them is audibly granular with moths, and the bats' echo-clicks stitch through it.

So the dawn chorus: birds funnel in from the whole cold valley to the strip of summer, and the first bell of the lock signals starts them, because they have learned the schedule, and the blackbird nearest the engine house sings into the rest-beats of the engine. That much is checkable. The keepers will also tell you the rock is still exhaling, and that the drips keep time, and they say all of it in the same flat tone, as information.

The tempo of the place is the last thing you learn, because it can't be hurried. A full ascent of the Stair takes the better part of a day, and a barge queue is measured in sunrises. In the dry months the river runs thin, and water is budgeted: the pound-keeper reads the millpond gauge every morning like scripture, and locks are worked only at set hours when the pond has refilled, and so the whole hamlet — the forge, the sawmill, the school run, the fig — lives to the timetable of a quantity of water. Hurry is not forbidden here. It is simply unavailable, and after a day or two it stops occurring to you.

Stand at the top lock at dusk after the last fill. The roar is done; the mist is rising off the pounds and off the warm shed, and under everything the turbine holds its one note like a chord the valley has decided to sustain indefinitely. The lamps come on along the wire, one by one, like someone counting. The rock faces smoke faintly in the cooling air. Everything you can see is still finding out what it will become — the bricks what color of stain, the banks what forest, the trenches what town — and the loud, bright, patient valley seems to know it, and to be in no rush about the answer.
