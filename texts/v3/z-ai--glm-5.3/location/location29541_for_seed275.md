# The Wick

There is no water anywhere in the Wick, and no one misses it. You learn this on the way down, in the shaft-cage, as the salt walls slide past in lamplight — dry as old paper, glittering here and there with veins of amber that are not minerals at all but sap, fossil and fresh, the same stuff. Then the cage passes through a last lip of rock and the world opens, and you understand you are standing inside a lamp.

A valley, roofed. Nine kilometers of dark country under a ceiling of stone furred with roots, and beneath that ceiling, strung from crown to crown, lines of incandescent light burn at full daylight strength. The light is amber and it is everywhere. The floor glitters. What you take at first for wet ground is not wet — it is wax-frost, hoar formed on the swept bedrock where the night-cycle's cold settles, catching the lamps so the whole basin floor shines like the surface of a lake that has never once existed.

Stand still and count what you hear: almost nothing. That is the second thing you learn. The Wick is a city of fourteen thousand people, dozens of engine-houses, presses, foundries of a sort, trams, warehouses, gasworks — and it is quieter than a night field. The loudest ordinary sound is the tick of cooling wax. Then the metronome drip of sap into catch-pails. Then the creak of a bough growing.
