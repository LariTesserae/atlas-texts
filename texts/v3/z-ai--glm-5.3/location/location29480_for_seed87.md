Begin with the absence. A plain of gray ooze so flat that its curvature is its only architecture, running a thousand miles in every direction, under a kilometer of water that holds itself at two degrees — the temperature of patience, of meat in a cellar. Nothing falls here except the snow: marine snow, the ocean's slow dandruff, a drift of dead plankton and fecal pellets and dust that has been settling through the dark column for weeks and will settle for weeks more. The plain is not ground. It is accumulated falling. At noon, if the water above happens to be very clear, a rumor of blue reaches this deep — less a color than a belief — and then it is withdrawn.

And then, faint as a voice through a wall, the rumor of something else. Not light. Not sound. A few molecules of fat in a million liters of water, a plume of sulfur and wax and marrow-breath unrolling across the current for kilometers in every direction. This is how the hidden city announces itself — chemically, the way a woodsmoke smell announces a village. Its border is not a line on the ooze but a thinning of scent. The city has no walls because its walls are wherever the flavor dies out.

Follow the plume downcurrent and the terrain arrives all at once, the way a mountain does when you are very small: a whale. Thirty meters long, lying on its side, half sunk in its own crater of sediment. It is the only elevation for a thousand miles and the youngest terrain in the ocean — not raised by uplift or fire but by subtraction, a landmark made of loss. It is also, right now, a construction site. The skull stands at one end like a domed capitol, its chambers full of dark rooms. Five hundred plates of baleen hang from the jaw in a curtain of horn, a fence of hanging combs still faintly combing the water for a food that stopped arriving two years ago. The spine is a boulevard of vertebrae, each knob a roundabout, each gap an address. The ribs arc up out of the ooze in a colonnade, a collapsed cathedral with the roof taken down and the congregation moved in. Cartilage still laces the joints — scaffolding left up from the demolition.

The demolition crews came first, and a few are still on site. Sleeper sharks, slow as icebergs, take long shifts along the flank. Hagfish — the original contractors — work the seams with no jaws and no hurry, tying their own bodies into knots for purchase, pressing with the knot to tear what the toothless rasping plate has begun. When a shark grabs one, the hagfish answers with the only structure it builds: slime, a gallon of it, exuded in an instant, water given the memory of solid form. The shark gags, gills fouled, and leaves with white cotton packed in its throat. The city's first law of defense was written in jelly.

But the demolition is nearly finished, and what makes this place a city rather than a carcass is who came after: the tenants. There is no surface unclaimed. Amphipods pave the skin like punctuation, thousands per handful, and where the amphipods graze, the bone shows through gold and porous, marbled with oil. Isopods patrol like slow taxis. Rattail fish hold station in the ribcage, their photophores carrying small blue lamps through the colonnade — the only streetlights, and they are worn, not posted. Scale worms bed down in the sediment apron around the body, the suburbs, each worm its own front stoop. And in the skull — the old town, the mansion — an octopus has folded herself into one of the dark rooms with her arms spread over a clutch of eggs, and will not leave them for years. She is the city's clock that runs on vigil.

There are no plants. Nothing green ever grew here and nothing green was imported. The parks are felt, not planted — bacterial mats spreading over the bones and ooze in soft paving of white and sulfur-orange, the streets laying themselves in living felt.
