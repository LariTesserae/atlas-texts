The sea is four kilometers above where a sea should be.

That is the first fact of the place, and it takes a while to believe. You stand at the shore of an ocean that is itself at altitude — a drowned highland, a plateau the size of a small continent, flooded rim to rim sometime so long ago that nothing here remembers dryness, and nothing here is waiting for it. The light comes down silver and even through a mist that never thickens and never lifts. The air is cold — not winter-cold, just permanently, structurally cool, the way a cellar is cool — and it is saturated. Every breath fogs. Dew beads on everything and never quite falls. You are slightly wet for as long as you are here, and the wetness is not weather. It is a property of the air, like its temperature, like the low hum you can feel in your sternum before you can hear it.

The true ground is not ground. It is the mat.

From shore to rim, the surface of the sea is occupied by a woven continent of vegetation — reed and rhizome, bladder and cable, grown layer over layer until it is kilometers thick in the deep basins, a living substrate with the water beneath it like a water table is beneath stone, except that here the stone floats. Walking on it is like walking on the deck of an impossibly vast ship that is also a bog: firm enough to bear you, saturated enough that every step exhales cold water through a thousand seams. Drowned mountains of the old plateau rise through it here and there, worn down to their final, unarguable shapes — rounded reefs of dark rock, furred to their waterlines, neither growing smaller nor holding still in any way you could detect, simply finished. Between them the mat runs flat and green-brown to every horizon, and this flatness is the emptiness of the place: it can be a full day's walk between one significant thing and the next.

The significant things are the stands.

They rise out of the weave at intervals of thirty, forty, fifty kilometers — single plant-edifices, trunks like distillation columns, half a kilometer of grey-green shaft and then a kilometer above that of branching, crowned at the top with wide condenser-leaves that exhale the mist you are standing inside. From a distance each stand looks like a tower, and up close it stops looking like anything you have a word for. The trunk is laminate — layers of woody aerenchyma, dense with vessels, the whole structure caulked and pressure-tight by living tissue, hot to the touch from the work going on inside it. Because work is going on. Each stand is a pump station. Its lift-lines haul water from the sea beneath the mat up the whole height of the column, tens of thousands of tons a day, and release it at the crown as vapor, which is the mist, which is the reason the sky is saturated, which is the reason the sea stays up here at all.

Follow that thought all the way and you get the shape of the world. The plateau leaks. At its rim — a continental escarpment, a single cliff that runs for thousands of kilometers around the whole drowned tableland — the sea spills over in permanent falls, water pouring off the edge of the world down into the warm low countries that no one here has ever seen. Left alone, a high ocean like this would drain off its own weir within an age. It does not drain, because the stands pump it back. The entire water cycle of the place is a single closed loop: over the rim at the edge, up the columns at the crown, down through the mist onto the mat, through the weave, into the sea, again. The vegetation is not growing on the world's water supply. It is holding it. It is the dam.

Everything here is like that, once you look. The deep basins between the drowned peaks are the lower works of the factory: cold black water where the mat's dead mass sinks continuously and is digested anaerobically, slowly, in the dark, its gas rising through the weave to inflate the buoyancy bladders that keep the whole continent afloat. The matted shallows are conveyor canals, channelled flows that the plants maintain by their own growth, moving nutrients on currents that have run the same course so long that their beds are as fixed as riverbeds. There is electricity in it — not lightning, nothing so occasional. The cold crowns of the stands stream a steady violet corona at night, and if you put your hand near a lifted cable-root you can feel the charge standing in it, a potential that has held its voltage the way a lake holds its level. At night, with the sun's diffuse silver gone, the mat shows its faint wired constellation: the stands glowing at their tips, thirty kilometers apart, all the way to the horizon.

And there is no artifact anywhere. Not a shard, not a plank, not a stake, not a cart path. That is the strangest thing to reconcile and finally the easiest, because there is nothing to reconcile: the industry here was never built. It grew. Every tower is an organism, every pipeline a xylem, every refinery a gut. It is industrial in every respect except origin and intent — scaled, mechanized, continuous, electrified — and its intent is nothing. It produces no product. Its output is its own continuance, fed back as its own input, forever. A factory with no factory-gate, no shipment, no inventory, no owner.

You can navigate the mat by sound, and you must, because the fog eats distance. The world is loud. Not loud the way weather is loud — loud the way a held note is loud. The rim-falls put out a broadband roar that arrives everywhere as a floor, the same volume at every point on the plateau, a sound you stop hearing and never stop hearing. Under it, the stands pulse: each one lifting water on a beat of once every four seconds, a bass thud felt in the chest before the ear, and each stand is tuned slightly differently — trunk density, column height — so that the plateau is laid out in a geography of pitches. Travelers' lore would call them pitch-roads, if there were travelers. Strike out from a stand heading east and the beat behind you drops through slow phases against a new one rising ahead, and when the two resolve into a chord, you are midway. It never drifts. You could set an instrument by it. You could set a life by it.

The chord is why the place is strange in the particular way it is strange: nothing in it modulates. The rain falls in fixed columns, at fixed coordinates, charted like landmarks — walk through one and it is always raining there, has always been raining there, a grey bead-curtain a hundred meters across with the mat beaten into a permanent hammered lane beneath it. The swells are long and slow and periodic, damped by the weave into a breathing seiche that rises and falls once a day, and everything alive moves on that breath. Time passes here and nothing happens. There are no events. There is no silt accumulating, no delta extending, no channel silting shut, because growth and decay are balanced to the gram — the travertine lips of the rim-falls are secreted by mineralizing plants at exactly the rate the water wears them, so the waterfalls have stood at the same profile for longer than anything has stood anywhere. Erosion is not a process here. It is a completed work.

And yet it crawls with life. The fauna are abundant in the way parts of a running engine are abundant: the great grey mowers that crop the mat in slow herds, keeping the flow-channels open, a thousand animals in a line and the line a day's walk from the next line; the gutter-swimmers sliding in the nutrient canals like bearings in oil; the small clicking crews that live inside the stand-intakes, cleaning the valves on a rhythm so constant that the pumps never once in remembered time have missed a beat. Big slow pilgrims cross the deep basins between the drowned peaks on routes that have not varied in ten thousand generations. Their migration is a timetable. You could keep a calendar by the herds, but the calendar would be circular, and it would tell you what part of the forever you were in, never how long the forever had run — because nothing here marks duration. Nothing ages at a rate that can be measured against anything else. A stand that is ten thousand years old is indistinguishable from one that is ten million. The world defeats the question.

Descend, if you want the whole truth of it. Pull aside the weave — cold water sheets in over your wrists — and drop through into the sea beneath the sea: clear, green-dark, cold as a well, the drowned mountains hanging below you in silhouette, their summits furred with filter-growth, the light coming down through the mat overhead in a dim ceiling of seams like a sky of grey clouds seen from underneath. This is the water the whole world is standing on, and it is as still as everything else — the currents fixed in their channels, the gas rising in the same bead-trails, the deep digesters working at their constant throughput in the black below. The hum follows you down. You can hear it through your jaw. It is the sound of a continent holding itself, and its water, at altitude, against gravity, without pause and without progress, a machine running at rest-speed forever.

That is the Held Sea. A cold, silver, saturated world with weather like a monument and a single sustained chord for a voice. An engine that grew instead of being built, with no product but itself. Everywhere you look it is doing something enormous, and nothing you could point to has happened in the whole time you have been here — and when you leave, you will not be able to say how long you stayed. The place does not keep time. It keeps only its note.
