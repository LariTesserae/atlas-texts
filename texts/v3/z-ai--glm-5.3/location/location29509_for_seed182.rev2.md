You come to the Kettle by going down. The adit — a rail tunnel punched forty years ago for ore that never paid — walks you under the hill for the better part of an hour, past ventilation doors you have to lean your whole weight into, past sleepers furred with moss, past a pipe that ticks as it warms beside you. The surface leaves gradually: the drip at the tunnel mouth thins, the air thickens, and somewhere around the third door you realize your collar is wet, and you are not cold, and you have not been cold for some time.

Then the adit stops pretending to be a tunnel and becomes a place.

The Kettle is a chamber the size of a modest cathedral — you can walk its length in ten minutes and climb its stairs in a day — cut whole out of blue-black rock by machines that still sit rusting at one end of it. The walls have not aged yet. You can read every scoop of every cutting head, every ring of drill-holes, the saw-true lines where the benches were trimmed. Nothing is worn, nothing rounded. The rock has not had time to forget being cut, and its edges will take the sleeve off anyone who brushes past. Fresh stone also holds its grudge: now and then a wall sheds a slab with a crack like a rifle shot, which is why rope-nets hang over the streets and why the stair-towers settle, visibly, a finger's width a decade. In most of the world *young* is a word for mountains. Here it is a word for sharp.

The heat comes up through the floor. Across the lowest bench runs a crack the miners named the Seam: black, mineral-lipped, breathing a slow plume of steam that never quite rises so much as stands. The water behind it is scalding; the air above it trembles like the air over summer tar. Everything here is paid for by that crack — the weather, the town, the green. The air is broth. Sweat does not dry; it waits. Salt maps itself on your clothes overnight, handrails are wound in rope for grip and for cool, and work happens slowly or not at all. No one in the Kettle has felt weather in years. They know rain only as an indoor event.

And the green — the green is the answer to the Seam, or the Seam's answer; no one agrees which. Here is the first law and the first strangeness: the plants do not need the light. They are fed by the liquor of the crack, the hot mineral water rising out of the rock, and they grow fat in the dark the way harbor weeds grow fat at a vent. The green is the wrong green — deep, bluish, leaves waxy as if varnished, veins pale as milk. Vines climb by feel. Ferns stand in the gutters. Bracket fungi shingle the underside of every beam like roof tiles. Moss occupies everything else, and there is no *everything else*: to touch anything in the Kettle is to touch something alive. Every pipe has moss, every stair has a fern tenant, every rope has a vine braided into it, and people shear their doorsteps daily or lose the street. The town is greener where the people are — the green leans toward breath — so benches grow beards, doorsills are the greenest places in town, and the seats under the third stair are famous, and thick, and soft.

The town itself climbs the walls. Two hundred and forty people live shelf on shelf on shelf against the old mine benches — houses of cut stone and sweating corrugated iron, brick, salvaged cable, glass fogged permanently by the damp. You enter some homes across someone else's roof. Stairways pass through kitchens. Catwalks of the town's own woven fiber swing between terraces, and every one of them glistens. Water is the quiet industry overhead: cold pipes run the length of the roof, and the cavern's breath condenses against them and runs to gutters, so the town drinks its own sweat and the rock's, both tasting faintly of iron. At shift-turn, when the fans slow and the roof cools, drizzle falls indoors for a quarter hour — the loudest event of the day, and it is a whisper. Children stand under it with their mouths open.

The mining company left its machines, and the town runs them slow. The beam engine they call the Heart lifts condensate at one stroke every ten seconds, patient as a pulse. The big fans — the Lungs — turn slowly enough to watch a blade move. The Patience Line is a conveyor slower than walking; trays of cuttings ride it for days with grafters strolling alongside, pruning as they go. Kilns dry the harvest on seam-heat. The Loop carries low voltage through caged bulbs spaced too far apart, because light is rationed to people and the green has no use for it. In the deep corners the moss shines anyway — foxfire, a greenish gleam like drowned starlight, enough to walk by, not enough to see color by. People know each other by voice and gait.

Nothing here is loud enough to interrupt itself. Drips out of any rhythm. The tick of pipes cooling. The papery knock of seedpods. The Heart's slow thump arriving like a thought you already had. Voices die quickly in the wet air and the moss eats every echo, so the quiet is not empty — it is thick, upholstered, a hush with pressure behind it.

Almost nothing in the Kettle has a face. No birds, no dogs; fur sickens in this air. The animal census is short: midge-clouds over the compost bins, a few pale blind geckos that live above the lamps and are called the little ghosts, and grubs in sealed trays, farmed for their thread. Everything else that moves is a plant, because the green has taken the animals' jobs. Midges are caught by coiled ferns sticky as flypaper. Seeds are carried by the strollers — walking ferns that genuinely creep, a centimeter a week — and the town's geography is partly a matter of where the strollers currently stand. And there is a drum-pod vine that knocks its seed casings against the pipes in the rising heat. The first settlers, who were miners, heard it and would not go near it, having heard of knockers all their lives — the pit spirits that tap and tap. Then somebody followed the sound with a lamp and found a vine doing the work.

The strangeness runs deeper if you look. The moss follows toolmarks: it colonizes in lines, reading the old cuts, so the walls look written on, and posters fossilize into legible velvet. Some say the green copies letters it grows over, and in the Shearers' shed there is one board where this is permitted. The dead are planted — grave-trees with brass tags, leafing out pale in the north gallery. And at the sealed end of the chamber is the Old Plug: an early attempt to dam the Seam, now a blistered corridor too hot to enter, measured once a year with a thermometer on a long pole. It is twenty years old and everyone calls it ancient. In the Kettle, *ancient* means thirty.

The laws are few and spoken rather than written. Never plug the Seam. Never shear a fan's blades while the fan turns. Take only what the Shearers have marked, because the Shearers are the town's border guard, and the border is a living thing: without their daily scything, the streets would close within a season, and the Kettle would go back to being green wall to wall. Give the dead to the gardens. And do not hurry — the heat collects the fine.

Time here is botanical. The bloom-bell rings whenever the night-vine opens, metronomic as chemistry, roughly every eleven hours, and the shifts are set by it rather than by any surface sun. A season is measured by the strollers' crossing of Third Terrace, which takes them the better part of one and stalls traffic politely the whole way. Against all this slowness the green alone sprints — the only thing in the Kettle that hurries — so the whole life of the town is a slow race against fast growing, waged with scythes, at a walk.

Stand still long enough — a quarter hour is enough — and the green takes an interest in you: a film of damp settles, then a felt of something seedling and optimistic at your cuffs. The locals joke that the Kettle keeps people the way it keeps everything — green, packed, patient, dim. It is not entirely a joke. Deep in the quiet shift you can hear the place growing, a faint dry rustle under the drips and the Heart's ten-second thump, and that is the loudest sound there is.
