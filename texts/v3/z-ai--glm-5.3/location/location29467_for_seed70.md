# The New Sea Above the Mountains

Not so long ago — a geological eye-blink, a few tens of thousands of years — these peaks broke the surface of the world. And then, in the slow catastrophe that followed, the world rose to meet them. Now the entire highland is drowned: a warm sea suspended three thousand meters above the old lowland shorelines, held in a vast caldera of young basalt like water cupped in newly-formed hands.

The water is the first thing. It is warm — blood-warm, warmer with depth, which is wrong and everyone here who could notice would notice it. The warmth comes from below. The mountains are still cooling. Heat seeps up through the fresh black rock in slow plumes, so the sea above them has no proper winter, no turnover, no seasons it recognizes yet. It has a temperature the way a sleeping body has a temperature: even, interior, patient. If you drifted in it — and nothing does drift, not yet — you would feel the heat as a gentle updraft, a rising column that carries fine suspended mineral dust like snow falling in reverse.

The light comes down through it in long straight shafts. The water is young enough to be nearly sterile of murk; no rivers have yet learned to empty into it, no silts have found their long downhill ways. So the visibility is absurd — forty, sixty meters of glassy blue-green clarity, the kind of clarity that makes the terrain below read like a landscape seen through air. And it *is* terrain. The peaks kept their shapes when they drowned. Ridges run for kilometers in saw-tooth lines, sharp-shouldered and unworn, their edges still crisp as broken crockery. Nothing has yet sanded the volcanic grit smooth. Underwater, everything has edges.

Because the elevation is high and the caldera wide, the sea has almost no fetch. No big waves have ever crossed it. Its surface is a mirror that holds the sky perfectly — clouds, the slow wheel of light, the long gold of afternoons — and only breaks when the heat-vents below push up wide soft domes of water that spread to the shores as ripples the size of a held breath. The sea is still learning its own weather. Some days a fine warm rain falls straight down onto flat water and the two surfaces — falling and holding — merge so quietly that no sound is made at all.

Sound is the strangest absence. Water carries sound, but almost nothing here makes any. There are no crashing shores — the edges of the sea are the mountain walls, which slope down under the surface in steep screes of fresh clinker, absorbing wave-energy without complaint. There is no wind-driven surf, no fish-chorus, no crackle of reefs. What remains is a deep structural quiet with only two voices in it: the low continuous hum of the still-cooling rock, felt more than heard, like the purr of a settling house; and, rarely, a soft resonant *tink* as a boulder of cooling basalt fractures somewhere down a slope, letting one more drop of the mountain's interior heat into the sea. The steep valley walls return these sounds once, softly, and swallow them.

And yet the place is dense with life — plant life, vegetable life, in extravagant warm profusion. The volcanic water is saturated with dissolved minerals from rock that has never been leeched before, and the light is strong, and the heat is constant, and so the green things have gone feral with opportunity. The ridgelines wear kelp-forests that are not kelp: tall ribbon-things in deep bottle-green and iron-red, rooted directly in bare lava, growing in curtains down every sharp face. Between the ridge crests, the valley floors beneath the water are meadows — genuinely meadows, low creeping mats of a thousand grasslike sea-plants, drifting in the slow heat-currents like wind-combed grain. Some of the meadows are terraced on the natural stairsteps of the lava flows, so the green descends in shelf after shelf, each shelf a slightly different shade, each shelf photosynthesizing at its own preferred height of water and warmth.

The profusion is layered, near-touching, saturated. Where a young lava pillar stands up off a ridgeline — and there are thousands, the frozen spines of the last eruptions — it is wrapped completely: first a felt of microscopic green film, then a shag of tubular sponges that are also half-plant, then draping ribbons, then on the pillar's shoulders small gardens of things like sunflowers that have never seen the sun but turn anyway, tracking the light-shafts as the day moves. Everything grows toward everything else. In the thickest groves the plants have grown into one another so thoroughly that the ridges beneath them are less terrain than rhizome — you could walk on the woven canopy of a gully, fifteen meters above its floor, and every step would be springy with living green.

Two strange habits have already appeared among them, and they may become the rules of this world. The first is the breathing chorus. At dusk, when the day's heat begins to drain upward out of the water, the great ribbon-forests exhale their gas together — not everywhere, but in patches, grove by grove, so that along a ridgeline you can watch a wave of fine silver bubbles rise through the shafts of failing light, one grove then the next then the next, as if the mountain were sighing in sections. The second is the mineral scaffolding: many of the plants precipitate the dissolved stone out of the water around their own stems, so that they grow inside sheaths of fresh white mineral like candles in glass. When a plant dies, its hollow sheath remains, and the forests are filling with these pale fluted pipes — a bone-forest growing among the living one, a slow architecture built by no one, the sea's first and only buildings. Give it a million years and the ridges will be organ-pipes from crest to base, and the heat-vents will blow across their open ends, and the silent world will have invented music.

Fauna is almost absent — the sea is too new for animals to have found it, too high, too enclosed. What lives here fits in one hand, almost. There are small translucent grazers in the meadows, seed-sized things that move in patient swarms and make a sound like distant sand shifting. There is one species of hoverer, fist-length, flat-bodied, that lives only at the boundary where warm vent-water meets cooler sea, endlessly holding station in the shimmer, feeding on the boundary's richness — the only creature in the world that lives on an edge rather than in a place. Sometimes great travelers arrive: a lone sea-turtle, an old wanderer blown off distant oceans by storm or accident, riding a rain-flood up the caldera's lowest rim-gap. They do not stay. They circle the young sea once, slowly, soundlessly — as if reading the place, as if checking whether it is real — then find the gap again and are gone, leaving the water faintly disturbed for a day, and the meadows rippling in their wake, and nothing else.

The world's rules are simple and slow and all point one direction: *grow, settle, accumulate.* Nothing here is in a hurry because nothing here has competitors yet, or predators, or seasons. The sea warms by a fraction per century. The mountains cool by less. The mineral pipes thicken by a millimeter a decade. The meadows creep a hand-span a year up the black screes toward the light. Every surface in the world is being claimed, patiently, greenly, by the first tenants of a house still under construction — the quiet warm drowning of a young range, mid-story in a tale whose ending (the reef-cities? the animal-arrival? the eventual wearing-down of every sharp edge?) is set ten million years away and cannot, from in here, even be imagined.
