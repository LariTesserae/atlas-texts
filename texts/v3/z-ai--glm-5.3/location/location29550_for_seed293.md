# The Level

The sound arrives an hour before the walls do.

Coming down through the low country — marsh, then damp meadow, then the first enormous stools of the boundary trees, coppiced so long their stumps have become hills of themselves — you hear a din rise up out of the ground ahead. It is not birdsong and it is not water, though both are in it. It is layered like weather: a deep metabolic hum underneath, a middle register of drips keeping complex time, a high chatter of living voices, and threaded all through it, unmistakably, *words*. Fragments. Half a sentence in a language nobody has spoken in three hundred years. A market-cry. A lullaby, slightly wrong, as if the singer has forgotten how it ends.

You pass through the gate — a woven gap where two of the boundary stools lean apart, the gap itself alive, growing, kept — and the place opens: a flat. Green floor, wide pearl sky, warm air that beads on your arms within minutes. And almost nothing in it. That is the second shock after the sound. The basin is small; you could cross it in a brisk half hour. It holds perhaps sixty dwellings, a well-tree, a row of market canopies, a steaming marsh margin at the far edge — and between all of these, enormous, deliberate *space*. The emptiness is the luxury here. People pay for the distance between their house and the next, and the inhabitants will tell you, with some pride, that you can see the whole town standing anywhere in it and count every presence in it at once.

## The floor

The ground is not ground. It is the weave — a living root-mat, kilometers deep in rumor, older than any rock you could dig down to. This was a valley once, with hills. The hills were not worn away; they were *eaten*. In a world this warm and this wet, the great erosive force is not wind or ice but appetite, and the end-state of erosion is not bare stone but a level of life — the way water finds its level, life found its. The flat is finished. It will never change its shape again. It settled into its final form so long ago that the final form has become a kind of geology, and everything that happens here now happens *on* it, the way weather happens on a plain.

It is springy underfoot. It squelches. Water rides a hand's width beneath the surface everywhere, and where the weave wants stone — a threshold, a well-ring, the footing of an old wall — it has grown its own: mineral casts, porous, that weep continuously, sweating out the water the mat pushes through them. Every stone in the Level is wet. Some of them drip in rhythm.

## The town

The settlement is laid out on a grid so old it has become instinct. The paths are not cleared; they are *permitted* — the weave grows a smooth, foot-friendly skin where it consents to traffic, and occasionally it changes its mind. A household can wake to find its front path amputated and rerouted two houses over, and there is nothing to be done but walk the new way and adjust the address, because in the Level your address is not a number but a description: *third graft past the well-tree, on the year-ring of the loud winter.*

The houses are trumpet-yews, grown hollow, their mouths flaring upward like listening horns. You read their history in their strata — layer on layer of grafts, each addition a recorded year, a wall-thickness of biography. Some dwellings are four hundred grafts deep and still adding rooms. The aqueducts are living vines under pressure, sap-driven, running water to every mouth; the messages travel as tap-code through the weave itself, thumped at root-nodes and arriving, hours later, as knocks in the floor of the recipient's kitchen.

This is the engineering here, and it is past explaining: the inhabitants write instructions into seed. That is their whole technology — a grammar of grafting, a discipline of patience. The rhizome beneath the town does the rest. It computes. It remembers. What it does with pattern and pressure looks, from outside, like miracles arriving on a schedule.

## What the wood remembers

The center of the strangeness — the reason for all the sound — is this: the wood stores everything it hears.

Xylem takes sound the way it takes a drought-year, and holds it. A fence-post will spend a decade muttering fragments of an argument from the season it grew. A house recites, at unpredictable hours, the lullabies that were sung inside it. The trumpet-mouths of the dwellings were not shaped for beauty; they are speakers. Everything ever said aloud on this flat is still here, in the fibers, and the weave recites what it holds, ceaselessly, partially, in no order — which is the din you heard from an hour out. The Level is an archive that plays itself.

So the local laws follow from the acoustics, and the inhabitants recite them to visitors as etiquette rather than statute:

*What is said here is planted. Speak as if sowing.* — Nothing said on the flat is ever fully unsaid.
*Never cut a living aqueduct.* — The town drinks by treaty.
*Step where the floor invites.* — The paths are its decision, not yours.
*The night-grown things are the flat's notions. Use them gently; do not mourn them.*
*Silence is property. Do not steal quiet.*

That last one is literal. The inhabitants — there are keepers among them, pruners by trade — graft recitations *out*, and the removed sound is sealed into a knot of pure absence called a still. Stills are the most valuable goods at the evening Exchange, where the canopies go up and the bazaar energy of the place comes out: seeds, grafts, instruments, and above all silence, called out in price by auctioneers who have to shout over the town's own memory. The wealthy here keep rooms paneled in stills. To sit in one, after a day of the din, is a pleasure the visitors' tongue has no honest word for — it is not the absence of sound. It is the *removal* of particular sounds, quarrels and centuries of them, cleanly cut.

## The life

The fauna are abundant and punctual. Pollinator processions cross the flat at fixed hours like trains on the weave's own timetable. Channel-eels run inside the aqueduct vines; you see them as moving bulges in the green. Quoting birds nest in the trumpet-mouths and learn their songs from the recitation, so that all day long the air is full of birds singing dead people's sentences, sometimes to each other, sometimes in courtship. There are small sound-grazing mammals, mouse-colored and quick, that chew reciting stems for the concentrated noise and hum it back as mating calls — secondhand love-songs, all of them. The pathwarden, an ox-sized slow grazer, stands in the wide emptiness between houses for hours at a stretch, trampling where the weave wants paths discouraged, one of perhaps four large presences on the whole flat, and the inhabitants know each of them by name.

## The day

Dawn: mist off the marsh, the whole basin exhaling, and the three-note call — the flat's own name, which is not a word and cannot be written, only hummed; visitors learn it in the first week because the wood will teach it to them, patiently, from every direction at once. Morning: the working hum, the dripworks keeping tempo, the commuting of ten thousand small wings. Midday is the loudest hour, the full chorus, when the archive seems to be showing off. The afternoon rain comes warm and brief, and the weave goes quiet underneath it — the one reliable hush — because it is drinking. Evening is the Exchange. And at night, out in the open emptiness where the density is lowest, the flat *tries things*.
