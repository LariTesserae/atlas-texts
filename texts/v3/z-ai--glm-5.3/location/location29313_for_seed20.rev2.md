The sound arrives long before the place does.

Underwater, sound does not travel so much as it *transpires* — it moves four times faster than in air and arrives from every direction at once, so you cannot point to its source. The first sign of this place, still a kilometer out from it, is a pressure in the sternum. A low note struck on an instrument the size of a mountain. Your ribs resonate. Your teeth hum. Then the water ahead darkens, and you understand that the note is not coming toward you. It has been standing here all along.

What you are swimming toward is the summit of a volcano that did not exist a month ago. The seamount rose from the abyssal plain in a single heave, and its crown now hangs a few hundred meters beneath the ocean's skin — far enough down that the surface is a rumor, close enough that a gray-green gloom still sifts down through the water. This is a place of high elevation that no air has ever touched. It stands on its own cliff. On three sides, the fresh lava of the plateau shears off into darkness, and below that darkness is more of it, miles of it, the cold cathedral of the abyss. Up here is the only floor.

The plateau itself could be crossed in a hundred slow breaths. That is the whole of the locale: a shelf of new stone, a drowned crater at its center, and a scatter of towers. Everything else is space — wide panes of glass-dark basalt with nothing standing on them, long intervals of warm empty water between one presence and the next.

The rock is young enough to still be deciding what it is. It is black and glassy where the lava quench-cracked, iron-dark where the mineral water has already begun painting it, chalk-veined where white salts wick up through seams. Nothing is rounded. Nothing has had time to lie down. There is no sediment here, no soft dust, no patient silt — every edge is a knife, every surface either cooling or splitting. The stone ticks as it contracts, a constant percussion of tiny fractures, like someone walking on porcelain somewhere out in the dark.

And in the deepest seams, the cracks glow. A dull red, banked and breathing, brightest thing in the place. The floor has a pulse in it.

The towers are the place's real work. Where superheated water jets up out of the crater's fumaroles, the dissolved minerals in it hit the sea and freeze, and they build chimneys — black spires of metal sulfide, hollow as organ pipes, growing at the speed of plants run fast-forward. Some are hours old and the height of your forearm. The tallest is days old and taller than trees. You can watch stone grow here. You can put your hand near a flue and feel the column thicken against your palm like a held breath. The water rising out of each pipe is blistering-hot and dense with metal, and it billows upward into a black plume that spreads overhead like a thunderhead — this place has its own ceiling, its own weather, a storm it is standing beneath inside the ocean.

Because there is rain here. At the rim of the plateau, where the hot plume finally meets the cold surrounding sea, the minerals fall out of solution all at once and pour downward as grit — a visible curtain of falling ash-metal, a wall of rain at the boundary, hissing where hot crosses cold. You can float inside the rain underwater. You can watch the weather fall. Beyond that curtain the water turns winter-cold in the space of an arm's length, and that wall — the cold front, the sheer shock of it — is the edge of the world here as surely as any cliff.

The sound, though. The sound is the reason anything else about this place has a shape.

Each chimney is a flue pipe. The tall ones sound a bass that hits like a struck door; the thin ones scream above it in thin harmonics; together they hold a chord, an enormous standing chord, and the chord *changes* as the towers grow or crumble. Beneath everything runs the sub-bass of the magma chamber, a note so low it is not heard but suffered. New rock cracking open adds gunfire. A collapsing spire is a depth-charge. The roar never passes; a passing roar would imply the event could end. In water this loud, sound is physical — you can see the plume shiver on every beat, see the pressure waves corrugate the shimmer, and the loudest thing about the roar is that your own voice, should you try to use it, is flattened instantly. The place is deafening and mute at once. It permits no speech. It permits only being rung.

The strangest walls here are built of that noise. Where the standing waves are strongest, the sound holds grit and ash suspended in mid-water — lattices of held-still debris, invisible architecture you bump into with your shoulder and realize, too late, was a room. When a tower falls and the chord drops, these rooms rearrange. The cathedral has side chapels made entirely of volume.

Between the towers, the life there is hangs like laundry. White bacterial silk streams from the warm ledges in the current, cobweb-frost catching the red light from the seams, and on the hotter shelves it lies down in pale mats like hoarfrost that refuses to melt. Where the silk is disturbed it answers with pinpoints of blue-green cold light, sparks that go out before you're sure of them. On the mats graze the only animals: small blind white shrimp-like things, eyeless, eyestalks never needed here, and they move *on the beat* — the whole sparse congregation of them shifting a half-step each time the chord pulses, the only congregation an organ like this has ever had. One larger shadow patrols the cold wall and never crosses it.

The ruins are the oldest things here, and they are three days old. The first generation of chimneys — choked, cooled, toppled — lies in rubble on the plateau's edge, a little hamlet of dead towers with their flues open like doorways of doorless houses. The water has already begun dissolving them. In this place, even the ruins are a stage of construction.

And at the mixing fronts, where hot and cold water argue, matter keeps attempting things. Minerals precipitate into shapes with too much intent — a hinge, knuckles, the curve of an ear — hold for a breath, and are erased by the current before they finish. The shimmer doubles and triples the towers; no two glimpses agree; sometimes you see yourself swimming ahead of you. None of it can be trusted singly.

The rules, as near as the place keeps them:

Nothing here is finished. Warm water is the territory; cold water is the wall. Sound is structural — silence would be a collapse. Count every glimpse three times and trust the middle one, or trust none. Rain falls in this ocean. What the water builds, it is already dissolving.

Then a tower goes — a gunshot through your whole body, a boom you feel in the sea's grip around you — and the chord drops a step, and on the stump of the fallen spire, before the rubble has settled, a new flue has begun to grow. The roar does not pause. The mountain exhales. The place goes on being born, which is the only way it knows how to exist.
