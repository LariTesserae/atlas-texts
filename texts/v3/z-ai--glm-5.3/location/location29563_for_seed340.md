You hear it before you find it.

A day out across the dead upland — cracked clay, gravel, a silence so complete it presses on the ears like water — the roar begins to come up through the soles of your boots. Not wind. Not rockfall. A sustained, layered, almost architectural thunder, as if a city were being demolished and rebuilt somewhere under the ground, over and over, without pause. Following the vibration, you find the fissure: a slot in a limestone shelf, breathing warm air that smells of hot resin and struck matches. You climb down into the sound.

What you enter is a valley turned inside out. A cavern of regional scale — miles of linked chambers, a watershed of hollow stone — and it is young. That is the first strange fact of the place. The walls are unweathered, raw, fracture-bright, floury with fresh dust. Nothing here has ever been rained on. Nothing has been smoothed. The geology is mid-sentence. In the far dark you can hear rooms that don't exist yet booming as they open.

The second strange fact: there is no water. None. The dryness is absolute, aggrieved, personal. Dust never cakes. Metal never rusts. A tear dries on the cheek before it can fall, and this is considered a mercy, because in this place the universal law is *sealment*. Every living thing is a closed vessel — wax-barked, shell-skinned, varnished, luted. Everything here is a jar. Open a jar and the air steals it. The whole ecosystem runs on what the jars contain, and what they contain is not water but oil.

Because the space below is not empty. It is full — saturated, layered, near-touching — with forest.

The canopy is the sky. Great column-trunks stand shoulder to shoulder like the pillars of an unfinished cathedral, and overhead their crowns have knit into a continuous glowing ceiling, an overcast of leaves that gives off a steady, even, daylike light. The trees do not photosynthesize; there is no sun and no water to do it with. They are chemical eaters. Their roots mine the rock itself — seeking carbonate veins, secreting acid, dissolving, absorbing — and the light overhead is not a gift but a *waste product*, the spent glow of their appetite. Which means the day here is excretion. Which means the day breathes: when the forest's metabolism peaks, the ceiling brightens; at rest, it dims to a brown murk of light. The tappers below do not speak of days. They speak of pulses.

The sap is gold. That is not a metaphor the way gold usually is. Cut the lacquered bark and a clear amber lymph runs — hydrocarbon, fat, fuel, food, blood — and everything in the cavern drinks it. The trees are aquariums of the stuff; bore a trunk and a whole fauna pours out of the wound, oil-swimmers, wax grubs, bright flat mites that move through the sap the way fish move through a warm sea. Moths with glass wings tap the trees at drilled holes, and afterward they shine for days, because the light is edible here. That is the third strange fact, and the deepest: *light moves through the food web visibly.* You can tell what anything has been eating by how it glows. Predators' eyes burn with the stolen luminance of leaves. The fat browsing beasts that gnaw the bark all night — their chewing is the bass floor of the roar, a sound like rain on leather, endless — carry a shine in their flanks. Nothing is dark here except the rock, and the rock is being eaten.

And the roar. It must be said plainly: the roar is deafening. The cavern is a hard stone bell with a forest inside it, and every sound multiplies. Stridulating casings by the million, ticking like the cooling engine of the world. Gourd-cannons firing pods across open chambers with the bark of rifles. Bladder-valves venting pressure in long hisses. Fiber cracking as trunks thicken — you can *hear growth*, audibly, the place putting on girth around you. Thermal booms rolling in from the sealed chambers of the future. And threaded through it all, everywhere, the chewing.

The people here — few, scattered, a few dozen tappers in husk-houses in the thinner glades — cannot speak to each other. Not out of any taboo; out of physics. Their language is manual, hand against wrist, and their elders go deliberately deaf as they age, wax and bone plugs worn until the roar simply stops reaching them, at which point they become the community's readers: they put their palms flat on stone and feel the place. A tremor here, a silence there. They know when a pressure-wind is coming up from a newly opened chamber — the forest ventilates itself, and the opening of a room sends a dust-laden gale through the galleries, bending everything, loud as a jet. They know when a tapped tree is about to blow out and rain hot sap. They know the Firing is due.

The Firing: once each great pulse, the whole canopy ripens at once and fires its seed-pods together, and for the length of an hour the cavern undergoes artillery — pods cracking like a battle in the roof, seed-hail ankle-deep on the floor — and then every animal in the world arrives to eat it, a flooding, a frenzy, an anthill the size of a county, and the roar, which you would have sworn could not get louder, gets louder.

Only one thing makes this place quiet, and the quiet is the most feared thing in it. When a Silence falls — the roar dropping away all at once, every stridulator stopping mid-tick — it means something enormous is moving through, or the forest is about to do something no one has words for, and the tappers flatten against trunks and wait, hands over each other's mouths out of habit, until the sound crashes back like surf and everyone exhales.

Their material world is small and precise, and it is all fat and stone. Bone spiles. Stone drill-bits turned by hand. Gourd bowls sealed with wax. They tap the great trees the way their grandmothers' grandmothers did — and the older taps are everywhere, healed-over drill-holes rising in amber knobs up trunks a thousand years dead, whole vanished villages preserved as scar-tissue. Trail-markers notched into bark that the bark has since swallowed and ambered. One shrine at the oldest living tree, its base worn smooth by palms. A single wall somewhere painted with pressed pollen, half flaked, meaning nothing anyone remembers. Fire is permitted only in stone basins; the ground itself — generations of fallen forest rendered into a fat marl, an oily duff — will carry flame like a wick, and everyone knows the rule: *fire only in stone.*

The rules, gathered, are few:

Nothing drinks water; everything drinks oil. Light is waste; eat it anyway. The day is the forest's heartbeat — count pulses, not hours. The cavern grows — a map is stale within a year, because the roots are still digging the place, room by room, and the roar you hear through the walls is partly the sound of construction, of a country being hollowed out by vegetable appetite at a pace no geology expected. Growth here is runaway — saplings reach the canopy in weeks, trails close behind you in a day, a felled trunk is consumed to its husk before nightfall — and so is destruction; the forest builds groves no one planned, abandons them half-finished, starts over elsewhere, an architect in a permanent fever.

Stand at last with your hand flat on a raw wall in one of the newest chambers, where the stone is still bright and unweathered and the roots grind onward into the dark like slow green machinery. Feel the future rooms being chewed out ahead of you. This place is not old. It is embryonic, half-dug, loud the way an infant is loud — and if you press your ear to the rock, which the tappers do when they want to know what tomorrow is digging, you can hear it plainly: a world building itself faster than it can be finished, and roaring, roaring, roaring the whole way.
