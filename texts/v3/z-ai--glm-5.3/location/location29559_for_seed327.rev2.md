## The Bell

The valley people call this place the Bell, though nothing here was ever cast, hung, or struck. The bell is a sound. You reach it at the end of a two-day climb, on a ladder of notched poles wedged into the last crack of the headwall — a ladder with no known builder, repaired rung by rung for so many generations that the bottom rungs are young green wood and the top ones are worn to the grain. You come over the lip of the headwall with your breath whistling, and the world becomes mostly sky.

At this height the sky is a different substance: blue gone almost black at the zenith, sunlight with weight behind it, air thin enough that a stroll costs a climb's breath. Below, far below, lies the cloud sea — a white ocean with weather of its own. On its rare clear days you can see the lowlands' rivers braiding like roots beneath you; most days the place simply floats, an island moored by stone. The emptiness here is not barrenness. It is volume. The green place is small — two hundred paces rim to rim, a hamlet green, a cathedral close — and everything around it is nothing: miles of air on one side, wind-scoured rock above, cloud below. Life has chosen one pocket in an immensity and filled it to bursting.

The pocket is a cirque, the fingerprint of a glacier that retired ten thousand years ago and left its shape behind. The headwall is gray granite polished to a glassy sheen, fluted like organ pipes, chatter-marked low down where the ice dragged its tools. The floor is peat, meters deep, black and springy. Erosion here is not a process; it is a finished work. The single stream cuts a fingernail's width per century. The stones have sat where they are since the ice shrugged them off. Nothing is happening to this place anymore — except life.

What fills the pocket is the cloud. Every midday the sea below boils up the mountainside and pours over the southern lip in slow white breakers, and the bowl drinks. Fog condenses on every surface — needle, frond, beard-lichen — until the rocks weep from a hundred hidden seeps and every tree carries its own weather. The weeping goes on for hours after the fog has lifted: the long after-drip, the bowl clearing its throat. A dozen great trees stand spaced around the rim like figures in a procession, each with a full minute's walk of open sedge between it and its neighbor. Wind-corkscrewed, none taller than the headwall, but old past counting — the biggest has a girth of ten joined hands and leans like a man listening. They are the ceiling of the world's forest; above the headwall's crown nothing woody grows anywhere on the mountain. Each tree is a house: banker plants stacked in the crotches like rooftop cisterns, ferns and cold orchids crowded in the forks, a red crab that climbs at dusk to eat the flowers, pinching with a sound like a struck match. On the lawn: sedges, sundews, moss like green felt, and the stream crossing in one bright seam. Where the seam reaches the lip, it leaves. The waterfall goes over into open air, and the wind takes it apart before it has fallen a hundred meters, shredding it back into cloud — the only river that pours upward into the sky. In the dry season dawn comes with a light frost on the open lawn, silvered, gone by mid-morning; the bowl never freezes to killing, and that margin is everything.

For all the emptiness, it is loud with animals, all of them small. At dawn the swiftlets come shrieking out of the headwall crevices in a stream that pours upward, echo-clicking as they go. A flightless rail works the sedge, whistling like a kettle coming to boil — and sometimes like a person; no one can say now whether the pilgrims learned the bird or the bird learned the pilgrims. Moss frogs the size of a fingernail tick like water drops, so the ground sounds wet even when it isn't. Peat mice run tunnels warm as loaves; in boom years the whole lawn moves at dusk, and the hawk-eagle that hangs on the lip's updraft, never flapping, raises two chicks — in bust years it commutes to the far ridges and the sedge keeps its silence all afternoon. Woolly bees blunder the orchids at half-speed, humming like distant saws. A moth the exact grey of a lichen flake opens and closes its wings on a stone like a slow hand.

Then the heart of it. Between the night wind, which runs downhill, and the day wind, which hauls the cloud up, there is one still hour at dawn. In that hour the cold air off the summit slides out over the southern lip and crosses a slot the glacier happened to flute there, and the mountain plays its note: a low organ tone, felt in the soles before it is heard, sustained for twenty short breaths. The bowl's shape is an instrument — stone walls to hold the note, open sky to let it go. And on its decay the animals answer. The kettle-bird first, exactly on the pitch; then the frogs resume their ticking like rain accelerating; the swiftlets go; the chorus begins not one by one but together, as an orchestra takes its tuning and then the whole hall plays. For one hour this is the loudest place in the mountains, and by full sun it is over. Far across the cloud gap, an opposing cliff no one has ever seen returns every shout five seconds late, so that the sky answers you.

People have heard this for a long time. What they left is scant and faint: the ladder, kept alive; a hearth-ring of stones with charcoal generations old, around which grows a ring of plants that like ash — a garden ghost, the green signature of habitation; pegs in the biggest tree where flowers or plumes were taken; a few stacked slabs tumbled into a windbreak. The deepest human mark is a hollow polished into a stone facing the lip — the Listening Seat, worn to the shape of hips, someone having sat facing the dawn note for so long that the stone took their outline. And at the lip, on a peg, the only metal in the place: a small copper bell gone green as the moss, rung by no hand — only by the biggest afternoon winds, when it sends one bright note up inside the roar, a half-step off the mountain's pitch. Which is either an answer or an argument.

The rules of the bowl are few and hard:

- Air is rationed. Fire burns stingy, water boils at a temperature that would disappoint a lowland cook, and everything you do costs twice.
- Everything the fog touches stays wet. Nothing dries except in sun.
- Nothing moves unheard. Crossing the lawn quietly is a skill no one here has — which is why everyone here is quick, small, or armored.
- The green world ends at the headwall's crown; the living world ends at the lip. Every life here fits in a band two hundred paces wide, knows it, and is intense because of it.
- The day is scheduled: night wind, dawn stillness and the Note, cloud pouring in by noon, drizzle at dusk, clear cold stars. Deviation is weather; the schedule holds.
- And the peat forgets nothing — bones, charcoal, pollen, a bone flute gone soft as chalk. Things are not lost here. They are filed.

At night the cold comes down like a lid. The frogs tick on. The waterfall's shredded plume stands silver under stars, and on strange nights the cloud sea lights from inside, soundless, a glow traveling under its skin — heat lightning below, the bowl above it, lit like a lamp on a table. Then dawn. If you sit in the Listening Seat's hollow you feel the stone thrum against your back half a breath before you hear the note: the mountain striking itself, the day beginning, the chorus rising as one voice.

The bowl is finished, and has been finished for ten thousand years, and every morning it proves it again.
