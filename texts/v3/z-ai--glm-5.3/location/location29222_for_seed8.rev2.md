At first it gives you almost nothing. A plain of wet mud the color of strong tea, a sky, and the doubtful seam where they meet. No hills, no trees worth the word, nothing standing. Stand up straight and you are the tallest object for a day's walk in any direction; your shadow at dawn lies out longer than anything the plain has produced in a million years. This is not a landscape that performs. It waits to be learned.

Start underfoot, because underfoot is where the place keeps its argument. The mud is warm by mid-morning, blood-warm by afternoon, but press a hand in and a centimeter down the cool of last night is still stored there, a day behind, like a memory the ground is slow to release. The surface is not bare. It wears a living film — diatoms and bacteria laid over the silt like bronze leaf — so wherever the water has just withdrawn, the plain shines a faint gold-brown, and at certain low suns the whole visible world burns quietly that color. A footprint fills before you lift the next foot. The water table waits just beneath the skin, everywhere, always. This is not ground with water in it; it is water holding ground in suspension, thickened, patient.

The smell arrives before you have stopped noticing it: sulfur, brine, iodine, the warm fat of living things in quantity. The mud itself makes small sounds without cease — a pop, a burp, a sigh — where gas escapes from the black anaerobic world below the bronze skin, and where the last water retreats down a crab's burrow it goes with a long suck, like a word withdrawn. Nothing here is silent for a functional reason: the place is so crowded just under its surface that it cannot help muttering.

Look up from your feet and the plain organizes itself in one feature only — the creeks. They spread across the flat in branching dendritic patterns, visible from a distance as darker seams where the mud stays wet and the algae stay green, the plain's own handwriting, redrawn at the pace of decades. A channel migrates a hand-width in a year. A sandbar that divided two creeks in one decade is a memory in the next, and the mud where it stood is indistinguishable from any other mud. The deeper channels hold water through the low tide, and in them fish hold position against the last of the current; on the falling water, rays shuffle into the shallows and leave wide kiss-marks in the silt as they go.

Water is not a visitor here. It is a resident with a habit of changing rooms. The pools the tide leaves behind lie in their thousands, shallow as a plate, warm as breath, and brine-strength from the sun's daily distillation — some so salt that salt crusts bloom along their rims in white arabesques that dissolve again at the next flood. Toward dawn the air gives up its own water on every surface: the low plants bead silver, the few black rocks run dark and wet though no rain has come, and the fog stands knee-deep on the plain like the plain breathing, burning off by mid-morning into shine. About those rocks: there are almost none, and this is their whole biography. A handful of dark boulders sit out on the flat where nothing else sits, dropped by a glacier in an age when this land still had something worth grinding, and they have outlasted the mountains that made them seem small. They are the tallest geology for kilometers. They are two feet high. Gulls use them as thrones.

Green appears in this world the way punctuation does. A comma of samphire at a creek bank, gray-green and crunchy as dry celery. A period of algae in a hoofprint-shaped pool. A long dash of mangrove forest far off at the inland edge where the highest ground runs — a ridge of ancient shell and storm-debris barely above your head, wearing a broken comb of wind-stunted trees no taller than a man, all of them combed permanently in the direction the wind insists. And then, out in the middle of everything, the singular exception: one mangrove, vast-stumped and half-lying, rooted on a bank of shell that generations of birds have deposited and re-deposited, leaning so far with the wind's whole argument that it seems to be resting on its elbows. At high tide every branch of it holds a bird. It is the only vertical claim on the plain, and it is losing, gently, at a rate you would need instruments to resent.

The terms of survival here are printed in the sparseness itself: keep low, tolerate salt, or leave. What looks like a failure of vegetation is actually a decision the land has enforced for a very long time. The plain's true meadow is beneath resolution — the microbial film, the algal wash, the invisible pasture that the visible world comes to eat. Which brings the fauna, and the fauna is the plain's real population.

Consider one square meter of the open mud. It looks like nothing. It contains, if you cared to count, thousands of individual lives: worms in their sealed burrows, mud-whelks grazing the film, clam siphons, amphipods, copepods, and bacterial multitudes past any census. The surface is stippled everywhere with the spiral casts of worms, millions of small coils, so that from a small height the plain reads like a page written in a language of circles. Near the waterlines the fiddler crabs hold their cities — acres of burrow-mounds, and at low tide the owners standing at their doors, all waving one absurd oversized claw in loose synchrony, a crowd forever hailing something that never arrives. The sound of them is a dry clicking that runs for hectares.

And over all of it, the birds. The plain sits beneath a flyway older than any record of it, and they come from the far side of the planet, every year, having decided that this particular mud is worth ten thousand kilometers of wingbeats — knots and godwits in the tens of thousands, curlews and oystercatchers and small quick sandpipers that run the film like typewriter carriages. When a falcon passes, the whole flock goes up as one animal, and the roar of a hundred thousand wings arrives before the birds do, a sound like a landslide that changes its mind. Terns work the creek mouths. Herons stand in the pools at distances from one another that suggest property law. At the top of the tide, when the flat is drowned and the flocks must stop feeding, they pack onto the shell bank around the one tree and argue in voices that have crossed hemispheres to do it. Somewhere out there at high water, a long indifferent wake slides across the drowned plain — the crocodile, landlord of the channels, with no rival and no schedule you could read.

Understand then what the loudness is: not weather, not accident — population. The wind, having crossed open water, finds nothing to interrupt it and speaks in one unbroken vowel. The mud pops and slurps across whole square kilometers at once. The warm pools crackle like frying fat, which is literally what it is, snapping shrimp in the brine. Rain comes as slow warm fronts that take half a day to cross, and when they arrive the drops print a billion small craters into the shallow water and the sound runs like an audience. At dusk the shifts change — the day birds hand the noise to the crabs and the night-herons and, at the fresher margins, to frogs in numbers that suggest a committee that never adjourns — but the volume does not drop. This place is loud the way weather is loud, because everything here lives in public.

The tide is the plain's only drama, and it is staged twice daily with total indifference to audiences. You hear the flood before you see it: a long hiss, as if the horizon were inhaling. Then a bright thread of water detaches itself from the sea and comes on as a moving sheet, ankle-deep, glittering, deceptively quick in the near field though it needs the better part of six hours to cross the whole flat — the fastest thing in a slow world, and even it unhurried. The birds lift ahead of it in ribbons. The crab cities drown mid-wave. For a while the plain is a shallow sea, wind-chopped and silver, holding the sky so faithfully that the water and the air trade places in your eye. Then the long exhale: the sheet thins, breaks into a thousand departing runnels, and the plain resurfaces square meter by square meter, steaming faintly, re-beaded with pools, already being walked by the first returning birds before the water has fully let go.

Now the deepest thing, which is the age. Every tide that has ever crossed this flat has left a page: one thin lamina of silt, sometimes two, laid down and sealed. Count them, if you could, and you would count the tides themselves back beyond reckoning — the plain is a library written twice a day, and your footprints, if the next tide veils them with silt before they blur, will keep. Printed. Part of the record. The mud underfoot is the continent's remainder: everything these lowlands once were in the way of rock and ridge, ground fine, carried down, delivered here at the end of the drainage of an entire ancient land. The mountains that fed this plain are not moved. They are spent. Erosion, here, is not a process still running; it is a completed work, and the plain is what victory looks like when water wins absolutely — everything level, everything delivered, everything filed.

A few laws hold the place together, unwritten because nothing here writes. The sea keeps the only clock that matters, and everything alive sets its hunger, its eggs, its journeys, and its rest by that clock without ever once being told. Nothing tall is permitted to last. Everything that falls here is kept. And the emptiness is a skin — the widest open loneliness you will ever stand inside is, per square meter, among the most crowded places in the world.

Out at the lowest margin, in pools the sun has made almost too brine for anything, the oldest builders still practice their trade: low domed heads of layered stone, millimeter by patient millimeter, made not of rock laid down but of living film trapping and binding the silt — stromatolites, the same craft their ancestors plied when the shores of the world were all like this and there was nothing yet ashore to look at them. They grow perhaps the width of a hair in a year. Beside them even the crocodiles are newcomers; beside them, the mangrove tree is a mayfly.

Night comes and does not quiet it. The flocks murmur on the shell bank. The crabs click. The mud cools with a fine ticking of settling grains, and the frogs in the fresher dark carry on their argument, and somewhere out past the pools the hiss has already begun again — the tide, coming back across a world that has ended and has not noticed, that finished becoming anything else long ago and has settled, with what can only be called satisfaction, into being this: warm, wet, loud, level, and older than the meaning of hurry. It will hold this posture roughly forever. It has nothing else left to be, and it is in no way finished.
