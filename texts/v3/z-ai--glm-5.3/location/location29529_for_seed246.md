You hear it long before you see it.

The plain has been running for hours — a high, cold, almost-featureless roll of ground under a pale, thin sky — and the wind has been with you the whole way, steady, tasteless, the kind of wind that has nothing left to erode and so just passes through. And then, somewhere past the midpoint of the day, the wind picks up a note. Low first. Then a chord under that, and smaller bright tones above it, as if the horizon had begun to sustain an organ chord and didn't intend to stop.

The reef comes up slowly out of the ground like a thought you can't dismiss.

This plain was once a mountain range. That's the joke of the place, the deep joke: the range wore itself down to a plateau, the plateau wore itself down to a swell and a fall, and everything soft or ambitious left ages ago. What remains standing are only the stubborn things — a scattering of fossil reefs, pushed up from a shallow sea when the mountains rose, outlasting the very mountains that lifted them. They sit kilometers apart across the flat, an archipelago where there is no water, each knoll too far from the others to touch, each one ringing its own note into the wind. If anything ever named them, it named them by pitch.

The nearest one — the one the chord belongs to — is a few hundred meters across, perhaps seventy tall at its crest, gray-gold, honeycombed from base to top. Up close its surface is a wall of mouths: billions of hexagonal corallites, each the size of a thumbprint, set in courses like laid masonry. Each visible band of stone is a decade of old sea, layer on layer, the whole knoll a library of growth rings. The reef has structure the way a town has structure — towers, domes, streets — but it was never built. No hands ever touched this city. It was grown, mouth by mouth, in warm water, by animals the size of a grain of rice, and then the sea was taken away and the sky was installed instead.

Around its base lies an apron of shattered chips, meters deep — the reef's own eroded body, its spent centuries. You walk up the knoll on the reef's powdered self. A shallow furrow rings the whole structure like a dry moat, the last argument between the stone and the plain.

Then you step inside, into a street — a corridor where the softer stone between two hardened colonies weathered away — and the sound closes over you like water.

It is loud. Louder than the plain
