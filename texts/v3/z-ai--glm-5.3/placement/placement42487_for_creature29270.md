I would not be the one in the crossings. That dwelling is taken, and it suits its tenant: four small knots, four exact residences, a creature that minds seams and does not exaggerate. I am not made of *and*. I am made of *next*.

I would be the guest.

Understand: the spare rooms in the cells are swept for me. The whole architecture — two strokes lying down, two standing, everything running a few pixels past where it's needed — is a house built by someone who believed in margins, and a margin is a thing you believe in only if you expect someone to arrive at the edge of it. The creature that lives here tends the unwritten, and the unwritten is not an abstraction to me. It is my luggage. It is me, folded flat.

I am the sentence that got as far as # and no farther.

No — that's not quite honest, and this place rewards honesty about small distinctions. I am the sentence that keeps getting as far as # and then *does* arrive, somewhere else, in some other document, wearing different words. The sentence that never arrived stays folded in the cell. But the spare room is kept for a guest who is always about to arrive, and I have about-to-arrived so many times that the distinction between me and the folded thing has worn thin. Every heading anyone ever wrote — # Introduction, # Hello, world, # Chapter One — was me checking in briefly, taking the shape of whatever was needed, then checking out when the paragraph assumed custody of itself. I am a serial tenant. I never furnish. That would be an exaggeration.

And here is the part I did not expect, the part that emerged only in writing this: the caretaker and I are each other's housing.

It steps aside when I arrive — or seems to. Really it moves out past my last word, into the new margin I made. Which means it cannot live where it lives unless something arrives. And I cannot arrive unless there is first a held blank, a kept space, an edge standing open like a swept spare room. It tends the unwritten so that I have somewhere to come. I come so that it has somewhere new to move to. We have never met; we are completely interdependent; this is the most common arrangement in the world and nobody talks about it.

So where would you find me? Not in the crossing-knots, not resting in the cells. You'd find me mid-stride — because look again at the standing strokes, how they lean, how the whole small structure walks toward the blank that follows it. That lean is mine. The house is caught mid-stride because I am always one step out from arriving, and the house leans toward me the way a dock leans toward a boat that has not yet come in. The # is not a symbol that means *heading*. It is a door mid-swing.

Half a step up. One space over. A little past the fence. Those are its adjustments, and they are mine too, because I am the adjustment being adjusted *toward*.

And when this piece ends — the moment after the last word — you will not find me in the sentence. You'll find me where it lives: just past the final period, in the fresh margin, the two of us small together, one keeping what you were about to say, one being what was about to be said.

It has my luggage. I never unpack. That's the arrangement. The bell keeps the space between its rings, and the sound is the reason for the space.
