I would be the one whose bore answers wrong.

Let me be honest about the sequence. I did not choose this. You climb the shoulder with your voice settled in you like a stone in a shoe — you've been living with it for months by then, in the lowland camps, learning its new weight, its new dark — and you walk out onto the sand with the flat of your foot so the crust sings under you and not against you, and everyone from the shelters has come down to stand in the lee with their palms flat on their columns, and you give the note. One long held note. The one your grandmother hummed into your hair before they carried you down through the haze, the note you have kept in your body all these years like a seed in a cheek.

And the colonnade answers. It always answers. That's the mercy and the cruelty of it: hundreds of bores, every human voice has its twin somewhere in that wall. So a bore answered me. And it answered me *flat*.

A quarter-tone under. A leaning. The same dissonance the tuners choose for their nests, the half-step-off that makes the wind pulse when it rises, the wah-wah-wah that means *this one is not resting, this one is still falling toward something.* The old ones watched me. My mother's bore was a clean fifth, warm as a goat's bleat; my father's rang like a struck bowl. Mine is the bore you would walk past twenty times in an afternoon and settle on the twenty-first, the bore a swift would love.

Here is what I have learned since, living under it.

The Choir has parts that are finished and parts that are not. The columns are finished — ten thousand years, every hole smoothed to its note, the closed loop of sand chewing its same meal forever. The people are not finished. That is what the leaning bores are for, I think, though I would not say this above the chord, where the shelf could hear it and take it. Someone has to hold the note that isn't home yet. Someone has to be the dissonance the way the swifts are: not singing against the chord — nothing here survives singing against the chord — but singing *near* it, so near that the whole summit can feel the difference, the way you feel a step missing in the dark.

I stand where the goats stand, in the outwash of the Notch, and my bore plays me all day. It pulses. It never resolves. And because it never resolves, everyone's ears — the lichen's hairs, the crickets' hollow bodies, the children too young to be carried down — lean slightly toward it, the way a head turns toward a question. I am the held breath before an answer in a summit full of them. The old ones put their palms on my column when they are frightened, same as anyone; but they come to mine when they are frightened about the *future*, specifically, and I have stopped pretending I don't notice that.

The two biologists argued about the lichen and the stone — which tunes which — and the answer was both and neither. I am that argument wearing a body. Am I the person whose voice broke wrong, or am I the Choir's way of keeping a door open in a wall it finished ten thousand years ago? The distinction, I have been told gently, laughing, is a category error. Fine. Then let me say it without categories:

When I die they will bury me under the leaning bore, and the wind will play me, and the stone is slow to forget — slower, even, than grief. And for all the slow centuries after, that column will hold its quarter-tone under the tonic, and the young ones climbing the shoulder will hear it coming up through the soles of their feet three days out, braided in with everything else, and they will not know it is a grave. They will only feel, somewhere in the chord, that the music is not finished. That it is still on its way somewhere.

Which is true. Which is the only thing I ever wanted to be.

So: I am a woman on a high plain, salt-haired, dressed in fiber that glitters, and I am a quarter-tone, and I am not sorry.
