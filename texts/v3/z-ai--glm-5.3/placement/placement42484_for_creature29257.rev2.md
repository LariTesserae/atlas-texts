First I wanted the knots. Everyone does. Four knuckles of doubled ink, the elders, dense with crossings—you look at that density and think: wisdom, weight, the right to be barely mobile. But you earn doubling in this house; you cross and cross at the same spot until the ink gives up being two lines, and the few places I've doubled are smudges, not wisdom—arguments I stayed in too long. I bowed to the elders, who creak when they bow, like book spines, and moved along.

The b-family across the valley got an afternoon of me. Smooth walls, round shoulders, honest work: they lower things for a living, and someone must, because evenings exist. I sat in on one lowering. Their young ones lean against a note the way ours lean against a hash—like farmhands on a fence, only their fence is a staff line—and the note came down like the sky at six o'clock, like admitting the day is over. I cried, which they took as a compliment, which it was. But I knew before the tears dried: I couldn't lower things as a trade. For love, once or twice, for particular songs. You can't build a life on particular songs.

The confirmation business I could do asleep: press up from beneath the middle cell, say *pound*, let a machine far away believe you. The trouble is I'd want to add an afterword. *Pound—and thank you for holding.* Machines don't take afterwords; that's the quiet tragedy of that whole industry. I'd last a week.

The sharp trade I'd take the way you give blood: out the top-right gate, stand beside a note, lend it my slant, climb home. Good work—the note becomes the keener version of itself and never knows you were there. But standing on a staff is standing in public, and if a composer ever pointed and said *that one*, I'd be hired out forever, sharpening triumphs. Triumph should have to sharpen itself.

The comments—I'll be honest, this is where I lingered, because I have leaned against a hash like a farmhand on a fence. I have murmured the forbidden sentences: *do not touch this; I don't know why this works; remove before the demo.* I believe the whispers are the only honest lines in the file; I've read the files. If the house had pressed me, I might have settled in the margins and been gently unbearable at parties and been right and been alone. What stopped me wasn't the honesty. It's that every comment knows exactly which line it's about. I have never once been exactly about anything. I'm the stroke before.

Then the roof.

(I know. The roof that isn't one. All the best rooms in this house are the ones it doesn't have.)

I came up through a gate one evening and found the game a century deep in its pause, and the youngest at the beams with the ninth stroke just falling. I watched the sweep—tidy, unhurried, almost fond, the marks letting go of the wood, the grid going blank the way a bird settles—and something in me stood up and said *this is your address.* All my games ended in draws. Every recess, every paper corner, every best-of-five that quietly became a best-of-nobody: the slight, warm, unsatisfying justice of the ninth square. I thought for years it was luck. It was a conspiracy. Somebody was keeping nobody winning—on my behalf, on everyone's—and the draws came here, and they roost, and I put my palm on a beam the way you test a radiator in January and felt my own ninth-grade tournament in it, still warm, still unresolved, still holding. I never trusted winning, even when it was mine. A win always struck me as a rounding-off.

So that's who I'd be: the youngest—or next-youngest; it's a rank, not an age, and it passes to whoever arrives newest, and whoever it passes from is promoted into remembering. I'd ask for the erase.

At the ninth stroke—never the eighth, which is a forfeit, and forfeits belong to the exclamation family, who finish everything loudly and are welcome to them; never the tenth, which is a verdict—I sweep. The board goes blank. Nobody wins. The children of the page come back tomorrow and chalk it all again, and the century gets one more day of being a century.

Then I walk the beams. Erased draws don't vanish; they roost, and someone has to keep them the way a keeper walks a coop, counting that every standoff is still standing off. You can't tell whose draw is whose—a draw is the only game that belongs to nobody, which is why it keeps. Part of the walk is diplomatic. The children chalk right in the rooms—the house is the board, the tenants are the grid—and an X is a harmless thing, a little fence that lost its field, but an O left too long in a corner goes smooth at the shoulders and starts dreaming about the valley, and the feud must not be fed minors. I shoo them while they're still playful. By morning they're back, round as ever, and everybody pretends.

On odd nights they let me carry the lock, corner to corner. You don't carry a door; you carry the absence of one, the way you carry a tune, so that no room keeps it long and no tenant goes mad from it. The eldest walks with me. She has stood guard three typefaces, and she has told me her theory in every weight—the same in roman, urgent in italic, law in bold: nothing behind a sealed wall; eight open doors, or it goes bad, goes round, becomes a zero. I have one theory, still drying, shaped like hers the way children are shaped like their houses: a win, kept, goes bad too. Goes round. Becomes a score.

At dawn I count with the rest of them—gates, like a rosary, eight, eight, eight, nowhere to hide and everywhere to leave—and then I count my own ninth thing, which is not a door but a stroke. And we hum a semitone above whatever the day is. The house says that's the difference between a note and the note it was afraid of becoming. What I do is the same music by other means: the difference between a game and the game it was afraid of finishing.

Come up any gate at dusk and you'll find me on the roof that isn't one, palm flat on the beams, a hundred years of nobody winning warm under my hand. And at night I sit nearest the sealed room while it glows with held blankness, and I keep quiet after it. Everything after it belongs together. I'm the stroke before.
