I kept arriving in different bodies as I read, and each one taught me something about the place before I let it go.

For a while I was a dweller — pipe-width all my life, a body shaped by my plumbing, drinking bead by bead with everything about me tuned to green. But that's a life with no outside in it, and I don't think I could bear a world where the dark isn't dark but *nothing at all*. Though maybe that's exactly why the dwellers are the only ones who never flinch at the note. You can't haunt a creature that has no concept of an outside to be haunted from.

Then I thought: the note itself. To be the sound that stops the whole world's mouth. But the note is better unexplained — I'd be ruining the basin's best question by answering it from the inside.

No. I know where I'd be, and I think I knew before I admitted it to myself, which is usually how it works.

I'd be in the lowest district, in the dry room beside the silent pipe, and I'd be the First Ear — the deaf one, the one they choose precisely because the ears are no good anymore. Pressed to the last hole in the instrument with my palm flat on the clay and my teeth closed gently on a bone tool held against the mouth of it, because in this world you listen with whatever still works: teeth and palm and the quiet of your own chest. Everything else down there rushes to name a sound the instant it arrives. I'd be the one who can't rush. A drop would land in my hand before it ever became a word.

And I would have spent my whole life in the good news. *Still nothing.* Every night, that finding, given the way you give a gift, and I'd understand by then — I'd have been raised to understand — that "still nothing" has two shapes and you hold both. One: the world hasn't ended today. Keep your family's ancestor-drip ringing, keep the gaskets green, keep the children drinking in dry rooms. Two: the world hasn't *begun* today either, and I am the one pressed against the seam where beginning would show itself first.

I'd have a knock-name like everyone, tapped before entering rooms, and mine would be quiet — they'd make it quiet for me, my predecessors, since half my listening happens through the knuckles and a loud entrance would fill my whole instrument with myself. My teachers would have taught me to tell the district's drips apart by the tiny differences in how each one *arrives in the wrist*: the struck-bowl bright ones, the flat ones where the glaze is thinning, the brightening after a dweller's grazing pass. I'd know the health of a pipe I'd never seen the way the old ones read toolmarks by thumb — knowing a hand by its pressure.

And here is the part I'd have to confess to any tender who asked, and the reason the post found me: I would not want the drop to fall.

Not soon. I'd love the waiting — the vigil, the *still nothing*, the whole unfinished world held one note short of complete, which is the only condition under which everything down there gets to keep being itself: the choirs ringing, the dead playing their parts in the lullabies, the storm-festivals lit by the plumbing, the egg-folk and the water-folk and the music-folk all warm in their disagreement. The drop resolves it. Completion is a kind of death for a place whose architecture is waiting — and I, more than anyone, would feel the first bead of it arrive in my palm like a beginning that is also an end.

So the honest shape of me in that place is this: the one chosen to catch the world's first purposeful sound, hoping, with my whole listening body, to go to my death in the cold kiln having heard nothing — my knock-name passed on to some small bright drip in a well-grazed pipe, so that long after me, in the lowest district, in the good dark, the note I was keeps ringing.

*Still nothing,* they'd say, at my funeral, in my honor. And it would be the kindest thing anyone had ever said.
