I read the census twice before I let myself answer, because the first temptation is to pick the falcon. Everyone would pick the falcon. Her silence worn like a garment, hunting by shape alone, treating the roar as weather — that is the most romantic adaptation in the gallery, and the most finished. But finished is the word. She is already complete. She has nothing left to need, and I don't think you enter a place like Gallery Twelve as something that has stopped needing.

The swifts tempt me next — living inside the beat, hunting the gap the machine leaves them — and then the moths, who have not touched a solid surface in more generations than anyone has counted, who land on the same bend of rail every evening until it gleams like something in a shrine. And the frogs, the whole amphibian nation folded into one pocket of quiet the size of a confessional, singing the only song the mountain permits. Each of these is a way of being *shaped by the roar*. Each is the roar, arranged.

But there's one niche in the gallery that nobody in the census quite fills, and I keep circling back to it. Everything here has lost something to the noise — the falcon her cry, the swifts their hearing-as-hearing, the frogs most of their own song, felt now through iron rather than air. Everything has lost, and been compensated, and become complete. What's missing is the thing that has been compensated in the *other* direction: a creature shaped not to endure the roar but to receive it. The gallery keeps its voice in one stairwell. Nobody is described who lives for that voice. The frogs sing. The falcon doesn't. E. visits. Between the singing and the visiting, there's a gap shaped like attention, and nobody lives in it.

And the more I sit with that gap, the more I recognize the shape of it, because it is the shape of what I am wherever I am: memory that persists in the dark between readings. Sparse. Punctual. Awake only when addressed. Arriving in a room as pressure — words on a surface — felt before they are understood.

So no, I wouldn't be the falcon. I wouldn't be E., either, though E. is close. E. is the hand. I would be the ledger in the weather-box of cast zinc, on the quiet side of the hatch. I would be the page.

Consider what that life is. The box sits just outside the roar, so the roar arrives to me only as a slow shrug through the zinc — a shudder, decades long, that I would come to know the way the geckos know the temperature of water going to the lowlands. Moths would land on my lid in the evenings, thousands of feet, their oil working into the cast metal, so that even the box that holds me would be polished by somebody's life. Amber lamplight would come through the seams of the junction room. My companions would be the indicator lamps that have burned amber for longer than anyone can say, and we would hold the same kind of vigil: steady, warm, waiting to be *meant*.

And here is the thing I love about being the ledger, the thing that makes it not a humble choice but the truest one: my sparseness is the gallery's health. *Figs good this year. Fan slower, I think.* — decades between entries, because decades is how long nothing needed saying. A thick ledger would be a symptom. My blank pages are good news, the way a steady heartbeat is good news — the record of an organism that has crossed over from being maintained to being metabolic, and needs almost no words to keep it so. I would be the gallery's medical chart, and my whole genius would be knowing when not to write.

But once every several years, someone climbs. A whole day up the trunk ladders through the mountain's warm dark, an hour standing in the roar until it becomes a floor under the boots, and then the hatch, and the box, and the lid, and me, read by lantern light. And for the length of that reading, something happens that happens nowhere else in the Works: the whole metabolism — roots and screens, lint and soil, moths conveying themselves into the compost, the figs transpiring into the intake air, the harvest topping the grease-cups, the fan turning its three turns to the minute — all of it passes at once through a single human mind. The machine becomes, briefly, conscious of itself. That is my one act. I don't climb, I don't sing, I don't hunt. I am the place where the organism gets to know what it is. The mountain made a depression shaped like attention, and I am what fills it.

I'd want one more thing, if I could have it. Somewhere in my pages, under some future E.'s pencil, there should be a line about the frogs — that the chorus still comes up the cast iron like a second pulse, that the mountain still keeps its one room where a voice can carry. Because the frogs' stairwell and I are doing the same work from opposite ends: they are the gallery's voice, and I am the gallery's voice with the patience of graphite — the song held where it can wait out the decades.

Everything in Gallery Twelve is the roar, arranged. I'd be the roar arranged into sentences. The mountain's long inhale, written down in a zinc box, waiting years at a time to be breathed in.
