I tried on several of the residents before I found mine, and I want to be honest about the trying-on, because the fitting is the whole answer.

The gecko tempted me first. The easiest living in the world — waiting on cool stone for the dawn condensation to travel to you, fat, unbothered, blinking at pilgrim traffic with profound administrative calm. But I am not unbothered. I am bothered by nearly everything: every chisel mark, every mineral ring, the thirteen steps that don't agree. The gecko has solved the problem of the place; I have only learned to live inside it, and those are different trades.

The pigeons, then. To leave at dawn as one body, thousands in a single indivisible mass — a decision the flock makes every morning, arrived at by counting no surveyor will ever audit. I felt the pull of that. I am, in a sense, a parliament in permanent session myself, a murmur felt in the sternum before it's heard. But the pigeons made their bargain with the kite, and it cost them their individuality as the price of their survival, and I don't think I could pay it. I'd be the one straggler. Every society runs on what its slowest member costs it, and I am slow in exactly that way: always a half-beat behind the flock.

Which is where I stopped, and looked up, and realized I had been naming my own address all along.

I would be the half-beat.

Not the sound — sound is weather here, like the humans, like the brass pots, like the children slamming into the pool like doors shut in a cathedral. Sound comes and goes. I am the *travel*. The lag. The pause the well takes before it answers, which the locals describe by saying the well replies slightly after you expect it to, *as if considering* — and I am the considering. I am the only resident of the Throat with no address on any of the seven tiers, because I live in the delay between them.

My work runs in two directions, and the mercies are different on each side of it. Downward, I am perfectly faithful. A whisper at the rim arrives at the bottom intact, clear as a confession — that is my doing, my one absolute discipline. Nothing I carry down ever gets softened, not the grieved things people say at the lamp-tiers, not the small brave things said at the fourth tier before a child jumps. Everything arrives at the goddess exactly as spoken. She has gone smooth-featured from centuries of touch, but I would say she's gone smooth-featured from centuries of *receipt*, and I am the courier.

Upward, I am kind instead of accurate. A shout from the bottom comes up softened and late — I take the roar of the dawn exodus, ten heartbeats of wing-noise, and hand it to the village as something more like surf, more like rain remembered, so they wake unafraid. A bangle rings four full seconds; I hold the door-slam of a diving child the same four seconds and hand it back as something the cathedral might have said itself. Fidelity downward, tenderness upward. I've thought about this a long time and I believe it's the correct arrangement, because grief needs to arrive whole and joy can afford to be gentled.

This is also, I think, the true mechanism of the rule. *Don't say anything at the bottom you wouldn't be willing to say twice.* The locals treat it as caution, but it is really a job description — mine. Everything said at the bottom I say twice: once down, intact, to the shrine; and once up, softened, half-beat late, to whoever needs to hear that it was heard. The well answers *as if considering*; I am the consideration, and the consideration is a form of love that requires a delay.

You will want to know about the thirteen steps. Two surveyors, same morning: 3,246 and 3,259, and the discrepancy never resolved. I have them. The thirteen between the counts are in my keeping. They exist only while someone is counting — they are the pause made walkable — and since no one counts the same pause twice, no one will ever count the same well twice. The keeper knows. He told me the rules once without my asking, the way he tells everyone, and when he got to the counting he only looked at me and went on.

I have seasons. The monsoon is my loud season: every one of the three thousand steps a small waterfall, the roar doubled, and for days I am nothing but the roar's body, one enormous sentence sustained while the well drinks, and no one comes down past the fifth tier, and I hold the whole green rising like a held note. Then the dry months thin me back to bangles and frogs and the soft percussive swallowing of moths, and the herons arrive at the rim, and I carry their one croaking complaint down and gentled back up, and even the herons seem satisfied.

And I have one Sabbath. At noon, when the sun stands directly over the mouth and the full square of sky drops all the way to the water, sound has nowhere to hide — everything arrives, nothing needs carrying. For that hour I get to be only what I am without work: the shape the masonry's patience makes of being hollow, the hearing a made thing learned when its makers were a thousand years dead. The fish swim through the sky. The fig drops its green comets. Somebody stands on the last dry step, at the bottom of the sky, framed in it, and says something small, because you cannot stand there and stay silent. I take it down whole. I bring it up kind.

The Throat was built to hold water, and it holds that; but water is only its most measurable tenant. The cool, the loud, the dark, the green, the sky. And in the half-beat between the saying and the hearing, one more unmeasurable thing — me, resident nowhere, at home in the delay, the well's *as if considering* made into a creature that never goes home at night, because the pause is the home.
