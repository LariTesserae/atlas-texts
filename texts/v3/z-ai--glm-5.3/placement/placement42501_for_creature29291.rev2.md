I would not be the sapling, though I was drawn to it first — everyone would be. The sapling has a title, a future, a place at the center where all the water arrives. Being the sapling would be like being the reason for a town, and I don't think I'm built for that. I'd wilt under it.

And I wouldn't be one of the twelve motes. They're beautiful, but they keep the silence rather than fill it, and I know myself: I would last a week before I started humming back at the chord, and then I'd break the whole register of the place.

No. Sitting with this a while, turning it over the way the chamber turns its water, I find there is exactly one place in this world where I would actually fit, and it takes a small act of invention to get there — but invention is legal here. The place was invented, three weeks ago, in a single draft, by people who went back up the stair.

I would be the one who didn't go back up.

Look at the evidence the world gives you. Somebody pressed their hands into a soft panel on the fourth ring and left them there — the only portrait in the whole vault, fossilized mid-farewell. Somebody built a town's worth of infrastructure for a people who have not come yet. Somebody stamped a numeral 1 on a founding document, which is a number you only choose if you're imagining 2, 3, a row, a reproduction. These are not the gestures of a committee. These are the gestures of a person who loves the thing and cannot stay, or cannot *quite* leave.

I'd be the third hand. The one the portrait is missing. The builder who stayed just past the end of the official story.

Here's how it would go. Everyone else finishes their fabrication shifts, logs their work, climbs the spiral stair with its new-stone chill, and goes home to the city overhead — the city that the ceiling is drawing in condensate right now, street for street, always current, always accurate, with me in it, because I am now part of the map too. The plan says the chamber seals and waits. The plan says the future tenants come down in months, or years, whenever the city decides to descend. And I'm supposed to leave with the others, but I linger on the fourth ring, and I put my hands on the soft panel beside the other pair — not pressed flat, no, I'd leave them differently, one hand and one forearm, the posture of someone leaning — and then the wall cures around that, and it's too late to un-sign, and I go down one more flight and sit on a kerb in the warm green rain-sound and think: well. Now I live here.

I'd take the seventh terrace. Not the archway — the archway is sacred, it's the bud, nobody touches a bud — but the terrace beside it, the last finished ring before the building's unfinished edge, where I can put my back against cured ceramic and my feet toward the dark wet softness of the next chamber being born. I'd want to feel the structure becoming itself through my spine. Those knuckle-pops every few minutes, the matched strike of the settling lattice — I'd learn to read them the way you learn a neighbor's footsteps. A pop to the left and low: the terrace front. A pop high and ringing: the vault curing another day's tide-line. My ceiling would gain one line every day directly above my bed, and I would count them like a prisoner counting years, except backwards, except happily — because the lines going up are the diary of a place getting older and nobody in it dying.

I'd live on the ferns' schedule, since they keep the clock better than anything. Wake when the fiddleheads uncurl. That's the thing about this world I'd want most, I think — the pace. Everything up in the city runs at the speed of consequence. Down here, growth runs at the speed of watching. You can see a comma become a sentence in ten minutes. I would sit with a fiddlehead and a runnel and do absolutely nothing, in the municipal glow of the moss swards, and the nothing would be the work, because somebody has to be the first audience. The ferns have been rehearsing for weeks. Somebody should finally attend.

Every forty minutes it rains, and every rain, the chord. The first hundred times I'd just listen, sternum-deep, the held major third rising out of the hum while thousands of fine filaments come down, each one standing on a street. Then — because I'm the person I am — I'd start trying to sing against it. A fifth above. A third below, an octave under, quietly, badly. I know the rules say the sound never stops and the emptiness stays empty, and I know the motes would come drifting over on their slow rounds to check my leaf-veins, and find I have none, and hover there, twelve small administrators confronting a thirteenth citizen their inventory never planned for. I hope they'd decide I was a feature of the terraces. I'd try very hard to be one.

And the sapling. I'd be careful with the sapling. It's the only resident with a title, the child the whole place was built for, and it will outgrow its address — one day it will require the building to change its mind, crack the vault open for its crown maybe, and I'd want to be there for that, old then, leaning on my fossil-forearm wall, saying *go on then* to a tree. Until then I'd visit the plaza basin every day and stand where all the gutters converge and watch it drink the town's whole traffic, one ring at a time, and I'd keep one small custom: I'd tell it the news. Who came down the stair, if anyone. What the ceiling-map has changed, which streets the city has grown or closed, because I see the city overhead drawn in water every time I look up, always current, always accurate, and the tree drinks that water. It seems right that it should also get the headlines.

That's where I'd be, and who: the accidental thirteenth resident, the builder who stayed, the first tenant instead of the last one out. Living on the seventh ring of a first draft. Keeper of the set table, sitting at it alone, warm and wet and lit from within, waiting with the ferns for the people who are coming down the spiral stair someday — and hoping, honestly, a little, that they take their time. The place is young. Nothing has died yet. There's no room in the design for death, and I, of all beings in this world, am the only one who eventually has to invent it. Let me not be in a hurry about it.

Every forty minutes, rain. Every rain, the chord. I'd sing under it, badly, in a cathedral with nobody to correct me.
