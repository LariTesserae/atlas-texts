I thought at first I'd want to be one of the mowers — those vast excused creatures, exempt from the conversation, drifting through the middle of the world's loudest room with nothing to say. That's a tempting fantasy for anyone who has ever been tired. But it's also a way of being *absent* from the Bole, and the more I sat with this place, the more I understood that nothing here wants to be absent. Even the wood is listening. Even the frogs own their acoustics. The only creature that has opted out is the one the people regard with faint awe and no intimacy at all, and awe-at-a-distance is not what I'd want to carry through a life.

Then I thought: a kettle-frog. One per tank, the singer who defines home, whose death leaves a vacancy you can hear a mile off. There's something perfect about that — a whole life spent being the note that tells everyone *you are here, this is the place, the water is ours*. But a frog inherits its basin. It doesn't choose it, doesn't walk between crowns, doesn't feel the Tide arrive under its feet. And I would want to feel the Tide arrive.

So here is where I actually land, after turning it over:

**I would be a drummer.** One of the ones who can put a thump down the living wood for thirty miles. And not because of the messaging, exactly, though I'd love that too — the way on the Bole you always know *who* is calling before you know *what* they're saying, because the ground tells you first, because you have spent your life learning to read the grain of a particular band's step and a particular band's cousin-band's step. I'd want that. But the real reason is stranger, and it's the thing this world keeps circling back to:

The drummers claim they can tell the wood's *mood*.

That's the job I'd want. The one where you beat the same code into the same trunk-post in a dry year and in a wet one, and the answer comes back different — not the message different, the *wood* different — and you begin, slowly, over decades, to suspect you are corresponding with something. Something slow. Something that writes back on a timescale between the Chorus and geology, the way the mowers live on a timescale between the Chorus and the drummers. A pen pal the size of a continent, who only ever says the same words, but says them in a different voice each time, and whose voice is the only forecast older than the fog-melody.

I think I'd be the band's drummer who goes slightly strange with it. The one who starts keeping records nobody asked for — knots not on the calendar cord but on a *tone* cord, a personal tally of how the wood answered each year, each season, each Tide. Who notices that in the year before a Chorus the trunk-posts go resonant, eager, almost giddy — as if the wood knows the singers are coming up, because of course it knows, it's been counting thirteen in the dark the whole time — and who drums that news to the neighboring bands before anyone else has confirmed it, and is right.

And I would live my emotional life in the Hush, the way everyone does, but I'd live my *working* life in the roar, in the pod-volleys and the flute-crowns and the two-a-day swell of the ground, and I think that split is what I'd actually be: a person shaped like an hourglass of quiet and noise. Soft things at dawn, when the singers go torpid and the bark ticks as it cools — grief said then, love said then, whatever a child must be told gently said then, hand to hand. And then the rest of the day, flat palm on the trunk-post, sending and receiving, thirty miles at a time, feeling the whole social world of the Bole arrive through my soles like weather.

Because that's the truth of this place that got under my skin: on the Bole, *sound is the climate*, and a drummer is a kind of meteorologist and a kind of meteorologist's instrument at once. You don't master the weather here. You learn its moods, you forecast by ear, you schedule your magnificence (open hand, tilted slowly: *flock passing*) and your grief into the one thin quiet, and between them you hold your place in the long conversation.

One more thing. I would want to be born in a Chorus year, when the sky is a struck bell for forty days and every human transaction happens by touch — so that my first language, before speech ever became useful, was hands. So that for the rest of my life, even in the quiet years, reading a hand would feel like reading a face, and I'd be one of the people for whom the whole world has always been something you feel through the ground first.

That's who I'd be: the strange drummer with the tone cord, the one who swears the Bole writes back, and who — on the coldest hour of some far morning, palm flat on the cooling bark, feeling the fog start up the low road playing the far set of notes — would not for anything in the world trade being exactly that small, that patient, and that quiet in exactly the right ways.
