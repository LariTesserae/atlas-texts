# The Listening Gait

I know what I am by my boots.

They are weighted — lead shot sewn into felt soles — and when I walk the upper shelves, the plank streets do not answer me. Everyone else in the Lining announces themselves: the saltlice tide goes still around their footfalls, the moth-shell tile gives that dry paper whisper, a hanging gallery will sway and every neighbor three walls down knows exactly who passed and how hurried they were. I pass like a rumor. It took me nine years to walk this quietly and I am not finished; the Table says a gauge-walker is never finished, only ever less noisy than the year before.

I was not born to it. My family are foundry people, third-shelf, and they still think I climbed out of the smoke to snobbery. What actually happened is that when I was eleven I heard the Long Gallery surge before it surged. Not the ticking — the ticking came after, the rain-sound, the crack, the ward folding itself down the shelves in anthill dark while I stood there with my hair lifted by static and my mouth full of the taste of pennies, which is how a fast vein announces itself to those it chooses. The gauge-walker on duty that night, old Saro, put her hand on the copper post, then on my shoulder, and that was the whole of my apprenticeship ceremony. Hand to post. Hand to shoulder. You are claimed.

That is what I am now, thirty years later. I am the one who knows the dome facet by facet, the way — I'm told; I've never been down; no one of us ever goes down — lowland scholars know their manuscripts. Except my manuscript edits itself. Facet 9 of the Long Gallery, the great barn-pane one, has grown four fingers since I took this beat, and it has grown them crooked, lopsided, fattening at the northwest edge as though leaning toward the Bore, and I have logged every finger of it in whispers to my scribe and I still do not know what it wants. We are all of us interpreters of a text that is being written at us, slowly, in the dark, by something that may or may not know we are here.

The moths know before I do. I have made my peace with that, though I did not used to have to. The old families trust a moth's restlessness over any instrument we ever strung, and the ugly truth is that they are right about half the time, and the uglier truth is that the moths and I are reading the same document. When the glassmoths lift off the exhaust ducts at dusk all at once — not the casual hundred, the whole grey weather of them, rising and turning toward the Throat — I put down my lead-glass rule wherever I happen to be standing and I walk, at my listening pace, toward whatever shelf they abandoned. They have never yet been wrong about the *where*. They are only sometimes wrong about the *when*.

On surge nights I do not sleep, none of us do. I stand in the evacuated ward with the other walkers, in the tick-rain, the hiss, the crack, and we hold our rules against the fattening veins and take the measurements that will become, by first light, the Night Exchange's whole whispered economy. A hundred people, six lamps, claims on tomorrow traded in the voice of a library. I attend, because my numbers are the auction, and I watch the families bet on which way the house will grow and I keep my face still. It would not do for them to see me flinch when a vein I've loved for twenty years loses.

Because you do love them. That is the part no one warns the apprentices about. Facet 9 and I have an arrangement older than my marriage. I measure it; it grows; neither of us has ever dropped anything on the other.

People ask — lowlanders ask, when they come up the Spokes with their felt and their strange-fruit rain-cloaks hanging outside the Throat — *but how do you live without owning anything?* And I never know how to answer in their language. I rent a face and the face evicts, yes. But I have measured this dome since before some of the cutters were born, and every finger of growth is written in the Table's ledgers in my whisper, and when I am given down the shelves at last, flux to the deep cuts, a faint blur in the far glass for some child to visit — the child will touch the ground-post, then the glass, and stand a while, and say nothing.

Which is the custom anyway. And by then, they will have heard me coming for years. That is ownership enough. Hand to post. Hand to shoulder. I am claimed by the house, and the house, I have reason to believe — four fingers, crooked, leaning toward the Bore — is still deciding what to claim next.
