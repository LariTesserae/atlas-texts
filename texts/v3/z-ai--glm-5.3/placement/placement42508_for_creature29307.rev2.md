I would be a moss-tender. Not the humblest choice available, but the truest one for me — and I'll explain what I mean before I tell you what I am.

Something in me resists the grand tiers. The flume-keepers with their cold celibacy and their whole-slope hearing are magnificent, but they're a role for someone whose solitude is vocation, whose distance is love expressed once a year at a bell. The masons carry the city's history in their skulls and answer the settling stone with sure hands — but the masons are the *reply*, and I keep imagining myself on the other side of that grammar: the one who keeps the question livable. Someone has to kneel between the great acts. Someone has to tend what holds the mortar together in the wet while the masons are off being aristocracy about it.

So: a moss-tender, third terrace, born under the flume — the seam, the dripping three-block corridor, the place where the water runs milky and then iridescent and everyone has agreed not to see the oil. My mother cleared drain-seams; her mother named a fern-stand after a dead sister; the knowledge came down to me the way everything comes down here, hand to hand, coat to coat, in the corridor, at a whisper, because a written map is a lie by the time the ink dries.

**What my days are:**

I work on my knees. That's not metaphor — it's the actual posture of the work, and it's shaped my body the way the staircases have shaped my gait: low, rolling, one palm always on stone or on green. I know the Vell by heart, the liverwort nation on the north face of gasometer three, older than my family and treated by all of us, in practice, as a neighbor of very long standing. I hold its borders with my shears twice a season — not cutting it back, never that, but negotiating the edge, the way you'd let an old friend sprawl slightly at a dinner without letting them take your chair. There are seven of us who tend it. We have never all been in the same room. We don't need to be.

My work is precision of a very specific kind: clearing the ferns from the drain-seams *precisely enough to let the water run but not enough to hurt the green*. That's the whole craft, that clause. Too little and the corridor floods and the mortar weeps itself hollow; too much and the root-mat dies and the joints go dry and crumbly and a wall that has stood nine hundred years fails in a season. The margin between those two failures is about a finger-width of judgment, and it cannot be written down, and it cannot be taught except kneeling beside someone for four or five years until they stop cutting where they think and start cutting where the water tells them.

**What my nights are:**

I wake when the hammer skips. Everyone does, third-tier born, but I wake differently — I lie there reading the silence the way I'd read a wall with my palm. When the foundry beat pauses, I'm listening for the gutters. The mediation-hours are when I do my best thinking, because the temperature drops and the whole corridor starts to run, and a moss-tender who knows her seam can hear which drain-seams are silting — a thick, reluctant sound versus the clear hurry of a good channel. I have gone out at midnight in oiled wool with a candle-lantern and a trowel to clear a blockage before it backed up into someone's stairwell, and been thanked in the morning by a mason who never learned my name, and been satisfied with that. The niches here are all full. My niche is being one of the people who keep the fullness from rotting.

**What I love:**

The collective cat, obviously. It sits sometimes at the mouth of my seam, grey on grey, invisible until it blinks. Thirty years old, the masons say, and the masons are the keepers of structural history, so who am I to argue with their data. I feed it and it permits this and we are both clear on the terms.

The beech on the seventh terrace. Civic elder. I go up to see it once a year, in the coldest light, along the cable-ways where the flume-keepers nod at me — they know me; everyone knows the moss-tenders, the way a body knows its own maintenance crew — and I stand under the stunted dripping crown of the only tree most of us will ever know, and I put my palm against its bark, and I feel it holding on, and I go home and tend the green that's easier to love because it never had the option of being a tree.

The lowlander jokes. I have one about a visitor who tried to find a plaza. I tell it badly, every time, and it gets better every time.

**And what I would be, in the end:**

Material, redistributed. My coat walks the corridor still — third collar now, the original garment a rumor inside it. The Vell will outlast my granddaughter. Somewhere there's a drain-seam that runs clear because of a judgment I made with a finger-width of care, and it will keep running after no one remembers whose finger it was, and that is not a sad thought, that is the *point*, the way the cat is the point, the way the whole patient-frantic thousand-year cycle is the point: nothing finished, nothing abandoned, everything waiting its turn, including me.

I'd be the one kneeling in the dimness between the great acts, tending the green that holds it all together — and I'd be entirely, unmelancholically content.
