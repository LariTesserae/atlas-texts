I would be the eighth of the nine.

The stitchers live in the Mouths, riding the exhalation — the city knows us each by name, and mourns us one by one when the flat takes us, and I have thought for a long time about what it means to be mourned by a people who spend their speech like gold. Somewhere below, on some swept plaza edge, a child is being taught my name. My name is a pattern, not a word: two sharp notes on the bellwood, a rest, then the low one. That is me. When I die — and I will die out on the white, eventually; the flat takes all of us, one by one, and it will take me mid-phrase — a tuner will shave one clay bell a hair's width flatter, and the whole city will feel the missing beat in their teeth for a season and not know why they are sad. I have made my peace with being a small grief worn smooth.

But I have not made my peace with the boundary, and that is what I am, mostly, all day: a creature of the boundary. The stitchers are the only living things that pass regularly between the two worlds — the roaring flat above and the green hush below — and so we are the only ones who know they are *one* world. Down there they think of the plain as a desert, a deadness, a lid. Up there, of course, no one thinks at all; there is nothing to think with, only salt and polygons and the wind's set lines. But I cross the Mouth's throat twice a day and I know what the people below only reason about: that the exhale that feeds their green and the inhale that swallows their Hush is the same breath, and that the whole city is an organ pipe standing in the mouth of a living plain, and the plain does not know it is playing them and they do not know they are being played, and *I* am the only witness standing between the instrument and the music.

It makes me arrogant, insofar as a bird the size of a fist can be arrogant. I ride the columns of dust that stand over the Mouths like visible pillars of voice, and I have learned the wind's habits so well I can feel a storm three days out — which is why the hush-geckos gather in the quiet rooms when they see me descend early, why my early coming means drumfruit will fatten and someone will have to be dug out of a collapsed terrace, and why I sometimes delay, circling, ashamed of the news I carry in my body.

I have heard the depths answer. That is my secret, the thing no reader's dust-figure and no tuner's chest-felt note has told them: that sometimes, when the wind is a certain kind of thin, something down in the unmapped strata *tries* the chord the way a hand tries a lock. The tuners feel the note when it arrives. I feel it before it arrives — I feel the intention of it, the fitting of the key. I have never told anyone. I could not; I have no speech that their hands would read, and I would not spend that sentence if I had it. Some knowledge is meant to be carried as weight, not spoken, and I was made light precisely so I could carry more of it than seems possible.

At dawn, at the turning, when the chord falls to a whisper for a hundred heartbeats and three hundred people spend everything they have saved — I come down. This is the closest thing I have to worship. I perch on a bellwood shutter at the Mouth's throat, inside the city's own noise at last, at the one moment it silences, and I hear what they are: lovers finishing sentences begun decades ago, a woman's apology landing at last across a plaza, a baby screaming with pure astonishment at the existence of its own voice. Every dawn I hear the entire treasury of a nation spent in a hundred heartbeats. Every dawn I am the only creature present who did not spend and does not owe, and it is the loneliest and fullest thing in my life.

Then the wind turns. The roar comes back in like a tide, they fall silent together and lift their faces, and the green of the canyon leans into the flood and drinks, and I ride the exhale back up into the blinding white —

— carrying, as always, in my small salt-glass body, the one thing neither world knows about the other: that the flat is not empty, the city is not alone, and the deep is fitting its key. I keep one damper open. I let it join. That is the whole of my religion and it fits, like theirs, in a single sentence, older than the grammar that survives it.
