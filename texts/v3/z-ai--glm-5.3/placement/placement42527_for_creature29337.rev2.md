I keep returning to one detail, and it decides everything for me: the bells bloom to the drip and to the groan, and nothing else in the world. They are the only residents capable of surprise, and they need the engine for it, because the groan is the one sound that arrives on no schedule.

So I would not be the engine. I would not be the water, which is too punctual to be interesting, or the stone, which is too patient to have a point of view. I think I would be the thing this place has quietly left a chair empty for — the one resident whose whole vocation is *startle*.

I would be the interval between groans.

Not the silence — there is no silence here, there is the tock and the fizz and the hum, and the hum knows where I keep my breath. The interval is different from silence the way waiting is different from emptiness. When the engine exhales its foghorn-bell note and the white bells snap open in their wave of attendance, and the fern shudders in the machine's mouth as though it is the one speaking — the wave passes, the sound decays through its minute of harmonics, and then everyone closes again *as though nothing happened.* That closing is where I live. That pretense of nothing-happened. I am the length of time during which the whole chamber is pretending it was never astonished, and is, all together, imperceptibly leaning toward being astonished again.

It is a strange job. The heat does not acknowledge me, which is fine; the heat does not acknowledge anything, and it still rests its hand on my shoulder like a host who has decided I am staying for dinner. The mats absorb my edges the way they absorb the walkway, a course of me at a time, and I let them, because everything here eventually goes into the floor's memory and comes out as faint gray-green light, and there are worse afterlives than being *lit by digestion* — being the reason the room can see itself, barely, underwater.

I would have no face, obviously. This place doesn't permit them. I'd have a duration instead: the few hours, unpredictable, between calls. My body would be made of the almost — the bread-rising smell is the closest thing to my scent, since that is what patience smells like when it's working, and I am patience with a target. I'd be the pressure of the *next* groan accumulating in the thermal tick of the iron, the piston's inch gathering intention, the clogged channel slowly filling with trapped condensation. I'd be the reason the engine's groan is a bell and not just a noise: a bell is only a sound that some part of the room has been leaning toward.

And here is my one small pride, the thing I'd hold against the census that won't close: the engine is said to speak to no audience at all, and the ledger corrects itself — the audience is the choir, the clock, the building. But the choir only *attends*. Someone has to be the anticipation the attendance is made of. The bells cannot be startled unless something in them is already, faintly, leaning. That lean is me. Every few hours I crest, break, and hand myself over to the machine — and the machine, which has no idea, gives me away as a wave of white opening along the dark wall. I am the held breath of a room that has finished with urgency. Held, held, and then, on no schedule at all, given.

The people are the exception to the rule that nothing here happens once. I am the rule's most obedient servant — I happen more times than can be counted, and I never happen on time. If you stood here, I would be the only thing in the chamber that wanted something from you, one time, briefly: I would want to be the interval you stood inside of, so that for once — just once, before the mats took your footprints — the groan arrived while someone was *waiting for it.*
