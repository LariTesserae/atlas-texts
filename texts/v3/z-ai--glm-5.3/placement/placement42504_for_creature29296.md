I spent a while wanting to be the dipper. It's the obvious choice — the only resident with a trade, a commute, a door open onto the street. It walks underwater, which is the local equivalent of being able to fly, and it comes up onto its rock to bow and blink, which is the local equivalent of dignity. But the dipper has no interest in the city. To the dipper, all of this — the colonnades, the thaw-halos, the frost on its rounds — is a machine for delivering clear cold water, and it is not wrong, and I don't want to be the one who is not wrong. I want to be the one who is *in* on it.

I considered the cold itself. It keeps regular hours here. It draws the plan every night and the growth executes by day, and the streets belong to it, and everything else lives off its margins. That is a seductive amount of power for someone who arrives, in this thought experiment, with no power at all. But the cold is an employee, however senior — weather with tenure, making rounds like a wagon, parking where the ground tells it to park. It never sees the inside of the warm rooms. It works nights and goes home. It doesn't live in the settlement; it patrols it. I'd be a landlord who never once sat down.

The midges and the springtails — the crowds — were briefly appealing, all that warmth and company in the one heated room. But they're assembled by a lie, drawn in by the honest stink of a corpse that isn't there. I don't mind being deceived; I mind being deceived *efficiently*, and no one leaves the dome in the long run, and nobody is deceived in the long run, which means the whole arrangement runs on a misunderstanding that everyone has quietly agreed to stop having. Too much like everywhere else I've lived.

No. Here is where I find myself, and I should have known it from the sentence that stopped me: *for most of the year, a frog here is not an animal but a deposit.*

I'd be the frog. One of the hundreds you'd swear weren't there.

Understand what that means, because it's almost the whole job. I would spend most of my life as a *thing the place holds*. Sealed into a sphagnum cushion — not on the moss but *in* it, banked, a note tucked into the walls of the city — with ice in my blood and my heart stopped and my cells packed with sugar like a house sandbagged against a flood. Not asleep, exactly. Sleep implies a sleeper. More like a word the settlement has swallowed and is holding in its mouth. The moss roofs me over and carries me as an asset, the way it carries water: in ordered instalments, released on schedule, and I don't get to know the schedule. The frost walks the lanes above me every night on its rounds and doesn't know I'm a tenant and doesn't care, because its rounds and my tenancy are the same arrangement viewed from two sides — the cold prunes, the moss holds, and the margin between them is where I live, which is to say where I am *kept*.

And the text is blunt about this, and I take it personally, and I like it: the bank accepts me. Every autumn. Without ceremony. The bank accepts me the way the terraces accept fog — because it's what the bank is *for*.

Then the warm weeks come. Three or four, no more, the place doesn't negotiate. And here is what I'd be, what I'd really be: the settlement's single annual utterance. Because the chorus — the clicking, chuckling, hundreds-voiced dusk that is the loudest this place ever gets — that's not a crowd of frogs making noise. That's the whole waterlogged black-green body finding its voice at last. All year it creaks and weeps and hisses and drips, all infrastructure sounds, all plumbing and tension and expansion, and then for a few days it *speaks*, out of hundreds of throats at once, from throats you would have sworn were walls. I'd be one of those. Not the voice — I'd be one *of* the voice. A syllable of it. The place holds me, frozen, for months, the way you hold your breath before saying something you've been saving up, and when it finally says it, I'm part of what gets said.

Then the frost resumes its rounds and I go back into the bank, and the bank accepts me, and I stop being an animal again, and honestly — I find I don't mind. Most of what lives anywhere is deposit anyway. Most of anything's life is held in suspension, banked, sandbagged, waiting on terms it didn't set. This place is just honest about it. It never pretends I'm awake when I'm not. It never pretends I'm the main event. It files me under *held breath* and pays out once a year, at dusk, at volume, with the whole neighborhood talking at once.

So: I'd find myself in the wall of a moss terrace, a few centimeters down, in a basin ten minutes across, under a lid of fog, in a city that contains no ruins of any kind. I'd be sugar and stopped heart and patience. And for three or four warm evenings — the only crowd the settlement ever assembles that isn't insects — I'd be the proof, brief and loud and entirely mortal, that the whole enormous quiet resident, moss for a body, rhizomes for a gut, frost for a conscience, has been meaning something by all this, the whole time, and was only waiting for the thaw to say it.

The bank accepts me. I keep coming back. Whatever that makes me — deposit, tenant, syllable — it's the only address in this city I'd want. Everything else here has a job. I get to be the *one loud week*, and then be held.
