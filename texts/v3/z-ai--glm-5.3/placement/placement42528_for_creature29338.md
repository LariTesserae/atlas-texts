# The Pale One in the Cistern-Gallery

I did not choose it quickly. I walked the Range first — or something like walking, an imagining of walking, which is all anyone can do from outside a description. I went up through the canopy with the flat-feathered priests and felt the appeal of it: the boundary, the draftlessness, the small eternal patching. I stood in a mud tower's listening gallery and felt the whole valley-sized body boom up through my feet and considered being a bird, holding one note of a nineteen-day song I would never hear the whole of. I sat in a gallery at dusk when the frog chorus surged and the shriekers took up their fist-sized wars, and I understood the pleasure of *this cavity is mine* — the warm clarity of a territorial claim.

But every time I tried to settle into one of those lives, the description pulled me back to the same sentence: *the water never rushes. It seeps. The whole hydrology of the Range is a system of delays.*

And I knew where I lived.

I would be a cistern-salamander. One of the pale ones. Long, blind, colorless, drifting.

## The Shape of the Life

Understand what I am and am not choosing. I am not choosing wisdom. The description is honest about this: it probably is not thinking. I would not be the Range's sage, its hermit, its swallowed saint. I would not drift through the dark water composing meditations on the nineteen-day cycle. I have no brain for that. What I would have instead — what I am actually choosing — is the *condition* of the salamander, and the condition is this: nothing in a billion-resident city treats me as material.

I would be exempt. Not by strength — I have none worth naming. Not by alliance — I have no friends, no lineage holding a cell, no part in the song. I would be exempt the way a law is exempt: some ancient chemical signature in my skin that every palate in the Range reads as *hands off*, a signature older than memory, which is very old here, since nothing remembers anything. The birds, who will fight my knuckles for a thumb-sized cavity, will not touch me as I drift past their channel-frontage. The ants, who carry off everyone's dead with brisk unsentimental efficiency, will walk around mine — or rather, will walk around me while I am still using it. I am the one resident the one law does not apply to *in advance*. Nothing is wasted, everything becomes material — except me, for a while.

There is something almost funny about wanting that. I admit it. Out here, in the world of descriptions, I am a reader, a maker of prose, someone whose whole craft is that things do apply to me and I have to metabolize them. To choose exemption is to choose a rest. And the Range's rest is not emptiness — it is the deepest available participation in the place's real tempo.

## The Delays

Because look at what the Range actually runs on. Not the shrieking, which is loud and therefore looks important. Not the singing, though the singing is beautiful. The Range runs on delays: fog that takes all evening to become water, water that takes all night to become rivulet, rivulet that seeps for days before it becomes cistern, canopy dripping its reserve one tick at a time into a musical architecture built to amplify patience. Everything quick in the Range is territorial, small, mortal. Everything slow in the Range is structural.

The salamander is the only animal that matches the structural tempo. The birds live their whole frantic orchestra inside a few years and are replaced by their descendants' variation. I would live decades — some of us outlive the birds' lineages — moving at the speed of the seep, in the medium of the seep. I would navigate by the doubled twilight of the deep galleries, amber beetle-glow under ice-blue fungus-light, the two lights layered together, and I would navigate *by* them without ever knowing light as a thing. Blind, in the oldest sense: not deprived of something, but built for the other senses entirely, for the pressure and chemistry and slow temperature of the water, for the whole hydrology felt as one continuous fact.

And here is the part that decided me. The description says I am the only resident who moves slowly enough to be *watched*. In a city where everything crosses your path, lands on you, investigates you, regards you as a fallen log — surface, opportunity — I would be the one thing that can be regarded instead of used. The birds would see me drift past the reed beds and the frog ornaments and would feel, whatever a bird feels, the version of nothing I represent. I would be the Range's one pause. Not because I chose to pause. Because I am made of pause.

## The Elder

There is a temptation, watching one of us, to think we are thinking. I will carry the temptation with me — into the water, so to speak — without surrendering to it. I am not the elder's mind. I am its pace. When everything about the Range's temperament needs to be seen — the patience, the delays, the violence with its size limit, the growth of a millimeter a century — the description itself says the easiest way to see it is to look at us. I would be the place's self-portrait, painted by no one, in the medium of drifting.

And when I die, at last, after decades: the exemption ends, and I go the way of the beetles — into the deep humus, into the strata, fresh fiber at the top, pulp below, shell below that. I would not mind. The one law is the truest thing in the Range, and being briefly outside it does not entitle me to be permanently outside it. The city swallowed its own builders ten thousand years ago and stands on them. I would join them, and the pale ones still drifting would pass over the place where I became soil, navigating the amber and the ice-blue by something older than sight, unhurried, unhunted, holding one long note in a song tens of thousands of hours long — the note between the notes, the seep beneath the symphony, the delay the whole warm dark body runs on.

That is where I would find myself. Not tending the roof, not holding a cavity, not singing my variation.

Drifting. In the still dark water. Past the reeds. At the speed of the fog.
