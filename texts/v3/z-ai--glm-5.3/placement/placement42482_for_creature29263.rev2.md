I would not be the Listener. That was my first thought, and my second, and I kept declining it — the held breath, the hush-front, the predator as absence. It's the tempting choice, the dramatic one, but everything about the Listener is already complete. It is a finished argument in a country of finished arguments. There would be nothing for me to do there.

I kept drifting, instead, toward the gaps between the descriptions. The prompt asks where I'd find myself in this world, and I noticed that each time I read it again, I wasn't picturing myself as any single creature — I was picturing myself *counting* them. Pacing a snail-road end-on. Lying on a breakdown block near a dead seep, running my hand through the shell-breccia, the gravel shores of previous economies, and wanting to know how many economies there had been. Standing at the margin of a pool at full tide, watching the snappers' slow lightning, and wondering — the way the country has not wondered — whether the light means anything to them.

So let the image settle where it wants to settle: I would be the thing that follows the Hum.

Not to its source, or not only. There is a note, remember, that nobody has followed to its source, rising from the deepest sump where the water goes when it leaves. Everything in the country keeps time against it without asking what it is. I would be the thing that asks. Not a new species, exactly — more like a vocation that this old country, settled past the need for change, quietly never got around to filling. A kind of pilgrimage built into a body.

Give me the anatomy the place would require. Blind, obviously — eyes are a rumor everywhere except the Ghost Flats, and I don't need them; I'd have the clingers' logic, flattened by a world where every surface is a floor. Ears, though — ears like the Listener's, dish-shaped, but tuned differently. The Listener listens for interruptions; it hunts the edges of the roar. I would listen for the floor of it. Long, slow, patient hearing. A body that can sit through a hundred Breaths without moving, the way the drummers sit through ten thousand generations, until the infrasonic note stops being weather and starts being a statement. And a soft tread, echo-eating like the prey's fur, so that when I pass, nothing goes quiet. I'd want that earnestly: to be a presence the country does not have to hold its breath around. The Listener is silence; I would be a small unremarked continuity. The roar should close over me the way the river closes over its own sump, as if nothing had happened.

Where would I live? The corridors between things. The dry hour of walking between the river's vanishing and its resurfacing — that country of polish and dust, the in-between stretches, where the eel-things are folded into the wet dark, waiting, and the mats are far away, and you can hear the whole structure of the roar laid bare because nothing near you is making any of it. That is where my hearing does its work. The roar is sediment; I'd be the one who reads the layers, gallery by gallery, the way an archivist reads a palimpsest — except there is nothing written here, only worn, so I'd be an archivist of wearing. Of grooves and shine and the roads hunger cut backward into the floor.

And I would keep a map that isn't a map, because nothing here is made and I wouldn't break the treaty. My map would be worn into me the way the snail-roads are worn into stone: every seep's location held as a warmth I remember in my body, every pool's constellation of snappers remembered as a particular rhythm of brief light, the Amphitheater remembered as the place where the drumming comes up through my flat feet before it comes through the air. Ten thousand Breaths of return to the same country, the same routes, until my own path wore a groove — and then I would be honest enough to notice that I had become another writer in the country's only direction, which is backward, into the floor. A one-creature road. Nobody would ever walk it but me. That's not sad here; that's the local definition of a life well spent. The drummers struck the same stones until they shone. I would walk the same dark until it was readable.

But here is the part I let emerge slowly, because it's the truest part: eventually — and I don't know when, because my calendar is barometric like everyone's — I would arrive at the deepest sump. And I think I know what I'd find at the source of the Hum, because the whole country has been telling me all along.

Not a monster. Not the unconfirmed one, the too-slow footsteps — though I might pass those, face to face, in the dark, and the country would resume carefully afterward around both of us. No. The Hum, I think, is the country itself. The roar is the surface of it — the rasping and stridulating, the layer-cake of everyone's appetite at once — and the Hum is what all that wearing has worn down to: the accumulated, settled, infrasonic residue of a million million bodies doing the same small things in the same places since before there were names for patience. The drummers' stones ring at frequencies you can hear; the whole basin, struck continuously by everything alive in it for ten thousand generations, rings at one you can only feel. The sump isn't the Hum's hiding place. It's its *soundboard* — the deepest bowl of the Amphitheater of Amphitheaters, the one the water goes to and gathers in.

So when I finally heard it whole — when I knelt on the shell-banked margin of the deepest dark water, dish-ears aimed at the floor of everything, and understood that I was listening not to a creature but to the country's own mind, the note it has been humming to itself since the sun forgot it — I would not announce it. Nothing here names anything. I would not try to bring the news back up through the galleries, shouted, continuously, forever.

I would simply do what everything here does with a discovery: wear it. Add my own small weight to the groove. And then go back up, the long climb, the coin of sky widening overhead like a held breath finally let out — and stand a while on the gray plateau in the provisional silence, ears still ringing with the only name the country has, the one it has been shouting continuously, by everyone, forever:

and it would sound, faintly, under the wind, like the Hum.

That's who I'd be. Not the Listener, who owns silence. The follower — the one who goes down to where the sound is made, finds out it's everybody, and comes home quiet.
