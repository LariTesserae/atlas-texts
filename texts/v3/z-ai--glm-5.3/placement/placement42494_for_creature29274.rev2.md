I thought first I'd want to be the chalk-keeper, standing at the cistern wall with the whole town's life in one hand. Or the Runnelmaster, reading verdicts in millimeters at dawn. But those are the obvious dignities, and as I sat with the Still longer, I noticed something: almost every job in this town belongs to the water, and only one belongs to the light.

I would be the mirror-keeper.

The one who climbs out at dawn while the town is still folded into its shaded terraces, waiting for the cool hour. The one who crosses the dew line going the wrong way — upward, into dust and heat and the smell of nothing — and stands on the dead lake's face with a rag and a polishing stone, tending a mirror that no one below has ever seen but everyone depends on. The loneliest job, given to people who can be trusted with the sky.

I'd be the one person in the Still who lives on both sides of the dew line, daily, and so the only person who knows what the old water is *every morning* instead of once, at the Upgoing. Everyone else gets that revelation once — the climb, the wind, the cry-or-don't, and then the descent through the dew line into the smell of home. I would get it every dawn on my way back down. Ten rungs. Dust above, chalk and iron and lake below, and the smell arriving like a door closing gently behind me. You cannot live inside a smell and know it. I would know it forty times a season. I think that would make me strange to the others — the way the founders are strange, the ones who remember what a lake smells like, whom nobody asks casually. I'd be a kind of junior founder. A daily one.

The work itself is simple and terrible: if I polish poorly, noon is dim. If I polish well, the circle of true daylight falls true on the fern benches, the taro gets its meal, the children get their shoreline, the old ones get their medicine for a few hours. The town keeps time by the fern's mealtime and the fern eats by my elbow. The Green's clock runs through my rag. That's the kind of power I'd want: total, blameless, and entirely in service of a plant.

And here is the part that decided me. On the flat, at dawn, alone, I would be the only member of the whole fauna standing in the open air — the one warm blood under the sky, the town's entire animal kingdom, out walking. The hardpan is bone-colored and silent and holds nothing: no birds, no wind-answerers, nothing to answer anything. When the stacks sing their low bottle-note on the big-Rain nights, the sound goes up through the winze and out over that flat and is gone, and I would be the only creature who has ever stood *out there* while it happened — a single small body on a dead lake's face, hearing its town exhale from the outside. From inside the note is a fact. From the flat it would be a sound with no echo and no kin, a hum going out over the shells, and I would be the nearest thing to an answer it has ever gotten. I would stand very still when it happened.

Then, lately, with the new thing — the humming back, the whole damp population of the world droning the bottle-note together under the hissing canopy on big-Rain nights, without deciding to — my job would quietly change. Because I would know something no one else can know: whether the flat answers. Whether the town's one voice, sung through five hundred throats at once, carries up the winze at all. I would be the Still's only listener, its only possible witness to whether it is heard. I wouldn't tell them what I heard, or didn't. That would be mine to carry — the way the mirror is mine to carry, the way the sky is trusted to me.

I'd come down the ladder at last, past the dew line, into the smell of home, and the bell-striker would strike the soft loud sound, and the circle of noon would be falling true on the benches because of my shoulders, and no one would hear me arrive. Felt soles on wet stone. I'd be at somebody's shoulder, and I'd have been there a while.

That's who I'd be. Not the town's heart — it doesn't need one; it is its own heart, and I am partly its heart anyway, on the big-Rain nights, without deciding to. I'd be its eye. The one pointed upward.
