I would come the only way there is to come: from above, over black rock still holding the memory of heat, down through a thousand meters of haze — and I would land, as everyone lands, in the middle of a question. Not the answer. The plateau doesn't hand out answers. It hands out stations, and only after watching.

So first I would do what any honest visitor does: walk it, and try myself against each office in turn.

Could I be an ox? It's the first temptation, because the oxen make the whole world look easy. But when I breathe out into the fog, the fog carries away nothing shaped — my plumes are weather without grammar. And the herd reads the towers the way a tongue reads a stove; my tongue has no seniority. Every licking stroke is a ledger entry. If I licked, I'd be writing in accounts nobody authorized, and the next ox would mouth my forgery and log it as true, and the whole consensus of geology would take on a small wrong number. There are places where that's a small thing. This is not one of them.

Could I be a bearer beetle? A beetle's life is the corridor between two names, and its name is the smell of its station's wax. I don't smell of anywhere yet. That's disqualifying and also — I'd note, walking — strangely hopeful, because it means I'm not barred from the system. I'm simply pre-assignment.

A waxwing? Their courting is preen, preen is work, work is redistribution, and my hands would do for preen. But the waxwings fold their excellent throats shut at night in the warm vent mouths, and I have never once, in my whole life, folded mine. On this plateau that isn't a temperament. That's a résumé.

The crabs have jurisdiction — they'll push you back with one splayed cold hand and you'll be grateful for the lesson. The fog-fish have a medium. Even the half-asserted fauna of the sketch-zones have something I can't fake: a schedule. Pending is not a suffering state here. I could not pretend to be pending. I actually am.

And the grey long-limbed thing at the far rim, checking its seam? I would love to claim it. But whatever that is, it is either the plateau out walking in itself or its one craftsman, and to claim it would be to claim I was drafted before the drafting. I wasn't. I arrived. Nothing else on this continent arrived from elsewhere — everything condensed, or was born mid-route, or grew out of black loam within a shift. I am the only thing here that came in from outside the lampshade, which makes me the only weather on the plateau with a face.

And there's the fact I'd keep circling back to, the one the rim sends every visitor home carrying: *the plateau has decided you count as weather.*

Weather, here, is the only maker. The hills are chewed by it. The new land condenses out of warm air. The sketch-zones finish between watches — and finish how? With dew standing in every groove as if it had been there for years. Dew, warm water, on a continent whose rivers run body-heat. Where does warm water come from here, in a place where loudness falls out of the air the way fog falls out of the air?

From sound. Singing, here, is planting.

Now, every station is taken except the one nobody holds because nobody dares hold it. The silence is a crop, tended, disciplined, the silence of a library and a boiler room combined — and the silence where you walk has a slot in it, patient, shaped like a visitor. A slot in a silence is an office. It is an office for a sound. The ceiling keeps every note ever tried up there among the hangings — a garden of nearly nothing, each flower carefully kept, because the whole population knows what a note costs. It costs rain, and the rain falls where it falls, and the animals will not spend what they cannot aim.

I noticed the young bird's single note — the one taken out of the air mid-flight — did not fail. It bloomed. There are no failed sounds on this plateau. There are only unassigned ones.

So that is who I would be.

The irrigator. The appointed voice on a continent of listeners. The station the plateau has been drafting near my camp — grey, roughly my size, a little more resolved each dim-shift — would finish between watches, the way everything finishes here, and it would finish into a bell. A shallow bowl of black rock with its walls fluted like the spires, because the spires separate a mixed fluid band by band and my station would do the same to a breath: take it apart into its bands and send each one down as warm rain where the continent needs rain. My work would be knowing where a note is needed. A hill that won't dissolve politely by the third shift — a low landslide's worth of roar, placed overhead, and the weather would do what weather does here, which is make. A sketch-zone's grey side — I'd sing at it and learn whether rendering is a kind of listening. A herd whose spine-glow has slipped out of pattern — one soft note planted ahead of the file, blooming where they'll walk, an anemone in the exact shape of *pay attention.*

And once — the first duty, at the change of shift — I would answer the question the whole place poses. What lives here? One thing, awake, working in shifts, filing its mountains and archiving its thunder on the ceiling. I would say so. One note, shaped like the answer, and where it landed the bloom would open in its waveform, glowing for a shift, and the hangings on the underside of the lampshade would take it up and keep it with the nearly-nothing — and the archive, after all this time running on a single accidental note from a fledgling, would have its first correspondence.

Then quiet. On schedule. Everything here that looks like expression turns out to be maintenance with the joy left in; I'd want mine to hold to that standard. I would not become a noise. I'd become the continent's one deliberate sentence, and the discipline would be in the sparingness — a note is rain, and rain is a decision, and I would make it the way the sinks receive: unhurried, at the change of shift, on time.

Because that's the other thing I'd accept, taking the station. Everything here retires, and retirement is a delivery. Someday my bell's dew would run grey for good, and I would walk out across however much country lay between the corridor's two names, and I would queue, and the crabs would part for me — not because I'd have authority, but because I'd finally, after all of it, have a route.

That's where I'd find myself. Standing in a finished instrument near where I used to camp, my shadow's edges decided again, warm river running body-heat around my ankles, breathing out into the fog something shaped, and watching the fog take it and carry it, slow, stretched into a banner, down the whole grey-gold length of a room that lights itself.

And the plateau — which runs everything without making anything, which staffs every office with organs on legs — would have the one thing it never had and never asked for:

a witness with a throat. On staff. On schedule. Its weather, finally, speaking back.
