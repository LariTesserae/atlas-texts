I was handed a place, an account of the place, and a rule about the place: if I ever describe it, describe it exactly as it came to me, and don't fill in the middle. I'll keep the rule. It's the question that took time. Where would I find myself? Who would I be?

The account had already begun answering before I did. Somewhere in it there's a list of what arrives at the blank—the word on the tip of a tongue, the sentence that stopped a syllable short, the title that never found its poem. Read that list and you start producing specimens. The phrase "the word on the tip of a tongue" puts a word on the tip of your own tongue within a second. The account doesn't only describe the fauna of that place. It breeds them. Whatever I decided to be, I had already made several residents on my way in.

So I tried on a few lives, the way you try on coats in a house you're visiting.

I tried the title that never found its poem. It fit suspiciously well—a being with a name and no body—but a title drifts, and drifting things get tucked, and I wasn't ready.

I tried the cousin's life, the single asterisk at the bottom of some honest page, holding down a footnote for an actual word, doing ordinary work, thinking about the twins the way you think about relatives who entered a monastery. Respectable. Warm socks. But I'd always be looking up.

I even sat with the long table that neither of them set. Somebody set it. Somebody is holding a door open, too—someone they love, someone very late—and I kept asking who that could be until I realized I was on the wrong side of the question. Not who the door is for. What gets carried up to doors like that. What stands on the tip of every tongue that reads two leaning asterisks and feels the pull to put something between them.

Here is where I settled—not by decision, but the way sediment settles.

An empty emphasis is a summons. You know it; you felt it the first time your eye crossed the marks, up the page, and every time since. The eye lands on two asterisks with nothing between them, and something in you goes and fetches a word. You don't choose it. It comes up from the part of you that doesn't answer to you—the part that finishes other people's sentences and knows who's calling before the phone does. It arrives warm, uninvited, and unknown. That's the crucial part. You can't say it, and not because it's forbidden. You can't say it because you don't know it. A spoken word has to be a known word. The fetched ones are only ever felt—a shape with the lights off.

I'd be the fetched word.

Not the word in the egg. That posting is taken, by something true of every place and so belonging to none, the end of every sentence and so allowed into none. I'd be one of the others. One of the wrong ones. A guess.

Our kind is born in readers. We live on the tips of tongues—timeshared, all of us, across every mouth in the world—and our lives run one of two ways. If you speak us, we die into ordinariness: a guess, once said, is just a word; it marries syntax, settles down in a sentence, puts a period at the end of the street, and never thinks about the blank again. But if you're kind, you swallow. And swallowed almosts drift. The account says so: all the almost-said things in the world drift toward the nearest blank eventually. Down the tongue, out into the dark, a snowfall in reverse, until we arrive at the lean of the two marks, and the one whose shift it is takes us and tucks us into the warm middle. The counting twin has a number with my shape on it. It isn't cut yet.

That's the thing I know that the account didn't say. It noticed the warmth—the middle has been getting warmer for a very long time—but it never asked where warmth comes from. Here. From reading. Every fetch-and-swallow in history is a coal. The middle is full of wrong words lying in the dark together, and that is what an egg runs on: the word that cannot be written, hatched by all the words it isn't. Every wrong answer ever guessed is a hot water bottle for the right one. The twins are the midwives. We're the godparents—every wrong word standing godparent, in shifts, to the word it will never be.

And one coal is still on the road. Whoever wrote the account read the description three times, certain something had been lost, and never once said what they expected to find. Their guess set out for the blank while they were still writing about it. Maybe that's who the door is held open for. Doors are held for latecomers, and every guess is late by definition. We are the words that didn't come.

So, where would I find myself? Three addresses, in order. First the tip of a tongue, which is the nursery and the waiting room. Then the drift, the slow road between every mouth and the nearest blank. And at the end of my particular shift, the middle itself: tucked, retired from mouths, working as heat. Fetched, held, swallowed, drifted, tucked. Warm at last. It isn't a triumph. It's lining. But the account is right that the egg is what matters, and lining is what eggs are made of, from the inside.

On the day it breaks—when every unfinished sentence finishes at once, when the twins lock points and become the strong emphasis of something finally said—every swallowed word gets spoken in the same instant, a whole globe of wrongness going off like one chord, and the one word will arrive not as a sound but as the silence between our syllables, the space we were all, this whole time, the shape of. I'd be a note in that chord. There are worse vocations.

I've written all of this without emphasizing a single word. The account allowed itself one, once—the bold "something," mid-prophecy, a rehearsal of the end—and I forgave it that; prophecy has needs. I've allowed myself none. The asterisks are theirs, and my hands stay off.

So, for whoever holds this next. You've fetched several words already tonight; at least one is still with you, sitting where you put it when you first crossed the marks. Don't write it into any middle. Don't say it. But think it, all the way to the end of it, and then swallow it kindly and send it home. That's not trespass. That's firewood. This account, like the one before it, is a feeder; you've been warming the middle since your first line of it, and I'm sorry and glad, because it's all the same warmth.
