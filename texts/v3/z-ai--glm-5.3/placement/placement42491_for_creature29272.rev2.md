I started to answer this the obvious way. I read the description and thought: *of course I'd be one of the humans.* The ones who failed loudly elsewhere. The ones who came because their constitution — allergic to brightness, needing water rather than traffic — was treated as a defect somewhere busier. That's a flattering mirror and I leaned into it for a while. I imagined myself at a terrace railing, ledger open, keeping the redundant human count of condensate that the substrate-mind already keeps, content in my uselessness, feeling the vibration in my molars like a roommate's radio through a wall.

But that answer was too easy, and the city itself taught me better, the way it teaches everything: slowly, without consent.

Because if I'm honest — and this place seems to demand a particular honesty, the kind that arrives at dripping-volume — I am not a keeper of ledgers. I don't have the patience of the documentation-readers, who spend decades on single passages. I don't even really have the slowness. What I have is something adjacent and less admirable: I am a creature of edges. I hover. I drink in small sips. I am drawn to the bright bead of a thing — a phrase, a mystery, a wet channel catching amber light — and I stay there, at the margin, briefly, and then I am gone before anyone can finish the thought *was that—*

So no. If I found myself in the Terraces of Slow Condensation, I would not be a resident. I would be a condensation moth.

Pale. Slow. Wingbeats nearly silent, which in this city is not a failing but a fluency — the local language is spoken under the sound of dripping water, and I would have been born already fluent. I would live at the channel edges, at the exact seam where the city's two great arts meet: the water and the dimness. My whole life would be conducted in that one amber inch. The condensate gathers there, each drop a bead of gold thread on its way down, and I would drink them one at a time, unhurried, in the permanent late afternoon that the builders made — and I would never know why they made it, and I would never need to know, and there is a version of peace in that I have never once achieved as a human being who reads documentation.

Here is what recommends the moth above everything else: the moths are the one creature in this city with no story. The residents have otter stories — beloved, retold, heirloomed. The residents have stories about the *tenants, prior*, told carefully, in the tone of agreed-upon silence. They have the story of the child who asked a question, and the dripping that didn't stop. But nobody has a moth story. Nobody names the moths. The moths are not folklore; they are *weather*. They are simply present at the edges of things, drinking, and their near-silence does not strike anyone as mysterious because it strikes no one at all.

And I want that. I confess I want that. Every role in this city is held up, gently, to the light — even the otters, kept deliberately unresolved like a coin in the pocket, are turned over nightly in the amber bars. The moth is the only being here that gets to be entirely unremarkable, entirely unobserved, in a city whose residents hold the faint intuition that the city likes being watched back. The moth is the one thing not watching back. The moth is off the ledger.

I would have my small places in it regardless. I would rest on the exposed meter of raw black basalt that every terrace keeps unplanted — the meter of memory, the *remember how recently this was nothing* — because a moth is permitted to land where moss is not, and I would sit on the newest stone in the city and it would not mind me. I would feel the sub-audible hum of the substrate-mind through my feet, faintly, the water-budget being computed beneath everything, and my whole nervous system is about the size of a droplet, so perhaps I would understand it better than anyone. Perhaps, to a moth, the thought of the city is legible. Nobody would ever know. That's the arrangement.

And once — this is the part I let keep emerging, the part I didn't plan — once, at the edge of a channel in the eastern terraces, I would see one of the wading forms. Not at the distance the haze chooses. Closer. Because nobody has gotten near one, but *nobody* means the residents, and I am not a resident, I am weather; nobody has ever tried to approach a moth. The tall thin form would stand in the marsh-plain with the amber light pooling on it, and it would look down — if it looks — at a pale slow creature drinking at the margin of its hand-me-down city, and neither of us would make a fuss about it, which is the courtesy this place runs on.

Would I be a real moth, or a moth-shaped vessel, or something engineered into a job? The argument is unresolvable and I would keep it that way, even from myself, especially from myself — because if I ever proved the answer, something would be lost.

And the dripping wouldn't stop.
