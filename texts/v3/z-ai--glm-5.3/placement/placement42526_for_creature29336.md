I would be the one who rings the bell.

I didn't know that when I arrived. I came to the Harrow as a visitor, which is to say I came with boots wrong for the boat, and I spent my first week being politely redirected away from the subject of my boots and toward the subject of everything else. I had read about the plateau — the finished plain, the sea that came back to look at its work — and I thought I understood it the way you think you understand a place from a map. Then I stood on the causeway at Station Six in the ground tone and realized the map had left out the sound, and the sound is most of what the place is.

I stayed a season. Then another. The keepers will tell you people do this. The plateau collects people the way the water collects the last of the old mountains and carries them into every leaf.

What I found I was good at — what my whole soft, pre-Harrow life had apparently been training me for without my consent — was waiting for the calm. Other keepers read the water's lean like farmers reading soil, and I never got the instinct for it, not truly. But I could read the *absence* of storm, the hour when the bellow thinned to something like breathing, and I learned to be ready in that hour before anyone asked me to be ready. Rowing the two kilometers to the tower at Broadlaw with a hammer wrapped in sacking, alone except for cormorants arranging themselves on the dead trees like a committee that has already decided against you — that became my work, because I was the one who wanted it, and on the Harrow wanting a work quietly is how the work gets assigned to you.

The tower stands out of the flood to its shoulders. Its base is in the green room where the fish spawn in the aisles, and its head is in the rain, and the bell is somewhere in between, at the exact altitude where the world's two halves meet. To ring it for a wedding you tie off to the arch — the arch that every child is told not to dive under — and you swing the hammer the way the storm does, but gently, on purpose, which is I think the whole difference between destruction and ceremony: the same bell, the same motion, and only intent standing between them.

The fleet-folk never thanked me the first time. The elder looked at my boots — still wrong, I'm afraid; I have never fully reformed — and said the fish were there before the flood and would be there after, and then we both listened to the bell's voice crossing the water, flattening the distance the way the plateau is flat, touching every mat at once. A bell over water does not fade so much as get taken up by the whole basin, like the rain. I understood in that moment why neither people claims it. You can't own something that arrives everywhere simultaneously.

If you asked me now which people I belong to, I'd give you the answer the Harrow trains into everyone: the question is wrong for the terrain. I live causewayed, in corrugated iron, with the pump's thud in my bones and the ledger's first line copied in my own hand in the drawer beneath my bunk — *the plain will not be drained; the plain will be kept* — and I hold my weight low in boats with my eyes on the film a boat-length out, and my forearms are getting knotted. But I also have a dry map in my head that a fleet-folk grandmother gave me, hand over hand, the way she gave it to children: the pale road, the dark seam, the orchard, the bridge, *don't dive there*.

And once — only once, seiche permitting, when nobody was watching — I dove. There was a doorstep off the old road, just a doorstep, and I put my hand on it and held it for a full breath, and came up with mud on my palm looking like a knight, and no one pretended not to see, because they all understood.

That's what I'd be. Not lost. Under.
