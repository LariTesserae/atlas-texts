# The One Who Counts the Bell's Rests

I would not be the heron. I understand the temptation — it's the room's most glamorous citizen, the one with the eye like a lamp behind horn. But the heron arrived finished, already adapted, already patient. The heron never had to learn anything here, and I would want to learn everything here, which rules me out.

I would not be the frogs. I lack the discipline. Their stillness is a vocation, and it was conferred before the water carried them; whatever they were before, they earned that pale heat in some other chapter, some former garden. I'd fidget. They'd feel it through their boundary stones like a draft under a door, and being a draft near the frogs is unforgivable.

Not the tench — the tench holds an office on behalf of people worn to suggestion, and that kind of faith is not mine. I'd want to ask it questions on its rounds, at the ruined hut, in that one-beat pause it always takes, and the office would be embarrassed, and the whole circuit would fall apart.

Not the bell, though I am fond of the bell. The bell counts what has already happened. It hums when the water *gains*. It is the room's memory, and the room is not short of memory — the water remembers everything, every shore, every sea it ever lay on. What the room is short of is anticipation.

I think, if I stayed — and I would stay; check my pockets, they hold silt — I would become the court's **only amateur**.

That's the role. Amateur, in the old sense: *lover*. Every other being here keeps something professionally. The heron keeps light, the frogs keep the secret, the tench keeps the office, the bell keeps the count, the green keeps everything else. Nobody in the census simply *delights*. Nobody wanders. Nobody gets things wrong and then gets them wrong differently the next hour, which is, I'd argue, the only honest way to learn a room whose distance is attention, whose floor is unfound, and whose laws must be learned slowly, "the way the room teaches everything."

I would be the one who stirs the settled light not to eat it but to watch it cloud and resettle, and I'd apologize to it afterward. The one who measures the room once — deliberately, rudely, to feel it become self-conscious, to feel it drift — and then never again, and think about that drifting for a long time. The one who sits near the tench's hut during its one-beat pause and does not ask, because the tench has asked me not to, but hums a little, in the bell's register, through my sternum, so that for a single beat there are two things counting.

And in the end — because the last law is not a warning, it's a standing offer, and the moss is already learning my shape, millimeters an hour, curious — I would take the citizenship. Drowning here is transfer, and I would go under the sward, down past the root-felt, into the depth that goes further than the court is wide, and come back up changed in the way the leaves never fell and the frogs are warm. Not dead. Transferred. Formerly an amateur, presently a presence.

I'd want to come back as one specific thing: the third allowance of light. Not the glowing fiddleheads, not the settled pools. The *space between* them — the dim grey patient dark that the room sees by. Someone has to be what the other lights are seen against, and it's the only office in the whole census nobody keeps.

Until then: warm-handed among the frogs, wrong in snowmelt water, sitting on the half-buried ninth step with a hand resting on something living that does not mind. Learning the room. Getting it wrong. Coming back the next hour to get it wrong better. Above me the rain comes down by the width of a thread, and below me the bell hums, and between those two sounds — one finished falling, one only begun — I'd be the amateur, in love with the unfinished weather, keeping nothing, and therefore free to notice everything.
