I am the gatekeeper of nothing, because nothing here needs keeping.

That's what I'd be, if you asked me down there — not asked, exactly, but if you found me, because I wouldn't be at the gate. The gate is a fig arch with a hinge buried in it, and nobody keeps it. I would be somewhere in the middle distance, at the counting-house, because that's where the questions go, and someone has to stand near the questions even if no one answers them.

Let me explain what I mean, and let me be honest about how I came to this.

---

You come down into it. I came down into it the way everyone does — the basin the rivers forgot, the air thickening before the land did. I remember the moment the horizon disappeared. It felt less like entering a room and more like being *closed around*. The cane wall is a standing crowd, and I was the one arriving at the crowd, and crowds turn to look. Except this crowd didn't turn. It had already been looking for a long time.

I ducked through the fig arch. The wet leather smell folded over me, and I understood, with a strange absence of alarm, that I was being *enveloped* rather than admitted. There is a difference. Admission implies you can be ejected.

And here is where my own account goes wrong, and I want you to notice that it goes wrong, because it goes wrong in a way that matters. The rules of the place are simple: cut nothing, keep the shifts, nothing is for sale, and you are the fauna. The place needs fauna — it has no animals to move its seeds, so it uses what arrives. Burrs in your cuffs. A hooked grain in your boot tread. "You were hired by walking in."

I read that rule and I thought, at first: *I will be the heron.*

You see the temptation. There is a heron at the sluice. It appears at the same hour, three visits running. It has never been seen to eat or to leave. The ants do not acknowledge it. The place declines to interpret it. It is the one figure in the whole account that stands *outside* the system — clocking in, but employed by nothing, fed by nothing, explained by nothing. It's the most human thing in the compound, and it's the one thing the compound doesn't count.

And I thought: that's the role for me. The witness. The one who arrives, observes, refuses the transaction, stands at the sluice at the appointed hour, and is not acknowledged by the ants. The heron is a loophole in the headcount. I would be the loophole.

I was wrong, and it took me most of an afternoon to be wrong, and the afternoon is when the pressure comes up.

---

Because here is what happens when you stand in the compound during the venting. The gourds hiss, one by one, minutes long, a shift's worth of small whistles. The steam thickens the sun into shafts. Every leaf edge wears a lens, and every lens glares at you — small, patient, at eye level, from all directions. And you realize, standing there in the sizzle, that you are not outside the system watching it work.

You are *the coolest thing in the compound*.

You are a walking afternoon-shadow. You are a cold surface in a place whose whole fuel is warmth. The pods burst when your shadow cools them — that's in the rules, plain as anything. The seed grain hooks into your boot tread because you are the mobile fauna, and the compound has no other. The vine carries its tin-foil charge and the ants patrol it in trades, and the ants do not acknowledge the heron, because the heron doesn't move seeds. But *you* do. You were hired by walking in. There is no clocking out.

So the heron is not a loophole. The heron is the one figure that the place has genuinely *finished with*. It clocks in at the sluice because the sluice is the one station where nothing needs hauling, nothing needs cooling, nothing needs carrying out. It stands in the gap of the work. And I could not be the heron, because the heron is what you get to be when nothing travels with you when you leave — and the heron has never been seen to leave.

I could not be a witness. The compound has no witnesses. It has *tenants*, and the tenants have tenants.

---

So I stopped trying to be the exception and let the place tell me what I actually was, and what I actually was, I think — what I'd be, if you came down and found me — is the *reader*.

Not a keeper. Not a heron. The reader of the ledger.

Here's what I mean. The counting-house is a roofless brick room. The desk is a bench of layered root. The filaments hang with their season knots, tied tight, year on year — "a ledger in the only script the place trusts." And somewhere near it, half-swallowed in a trunk, a brass valve wheel that the trunk has been lifting for decades, an inch a decade, presenting it to visitors. Presenting it. The tree *presents*. The compound, which exports nothing, which produces only itself, nonetheless maintains a display case, and the display case is addressed to whoever walks in.

That's not nothing. That's the place saying: *this is legible, if you can read it.*

And the knot-ledger is legible to the compound in one direction only. The compound writes. It does not read. The knots are tied tight, year on year, season by season, and nothing in the compound ever runs its fingers back over them and checks the count. The works keeps its records and never audits them. That is the vacancy. That is the one niche — in a place where every niche has a tenant and the tenants have tenants — that stands open. Not the sluice. The *reading*.

So that's me. The one who goes back over the knots. Not to interpret the heron — the place explicitly forbids that, and I keep its rules — but to read the seasons. To run a thumb over the filament and feel the years: this knot tighter, that knot loose, the year the rain came heavy, the year the shaft creaked one note instead of two. The works keeps the shifts; I would keep the *continuity*. Not the heron's frozen clocking-in, but the opposite: the long slow job of noticing that the flywheel has been pacing my footsteps, or that my footsteps have been pacing the flywheel, and being the one part of the compound that can tell the difference.

Because after an hour down there, it's hard to say which of you is pacing the other. And someone — some tenant, some tenant-of-tenants — has to be able to say it. The ants clear the stomata. The gourds keep the pressure. The ponds knock out their tallies at dawn. And the reader sits at the root-bench with the knots, being the memory that turns around and faces backward, which is the one direction nothing else in the compound looks.

---

And the heron?

I told you I will not interpret it, and I won't, because that's the rule — the place says its rules as well as it can say them, and this is one of them. But I will tell you what I *am* allowed to do, standing at the counting-house at the appointed hour, looking toward the sluice.

I can clock in too. I can stand there, near it, at the same hour, and do nothing, and be unacknowledged by the ants. That's not interpretation. That's company. The works is one resident, and the resident is the address, and even an address that is its own headcount — skin of cane, skeleton of brick, heart of turning hardwood, one beat a day — even that does not stand at the sluice at the same hour three visits running for no reason.

Maybe it's checking whether the reader is still at the ledger. Maybe it's the compound looking at the one part of itself that looks backward. I don't know. I won't interpret. But the seeds travel with me when I go, and that's the point, and if I ever clock out, I'll leave a knot behind — tied tight — so the place knows I was read.
