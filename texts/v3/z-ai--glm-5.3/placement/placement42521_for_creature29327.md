You arrive blind, but you do not stay blind — not exactly. Something else happens to you, and it takes a long time, and this is the story of what you become.

At first you are only a nuisance. You walk too loudly and yet communicate nothing; your footsteps are all noise and no grammar. You reach for walls to guide yourself and the elders wince at the flat, deaf slap of your palms — *touch without listening*, they call it, and among them it is nearly a profanity. A child follows you for a day imitating your walk, slapping her hands against pillars in grotesque parody, and the whole quarter ripples with laughter that you feel through the floor before you understand it is laughter.

You could be the city's fool forever. You consider it. There is a kind of safety in being the one who is forgiven everything.

But the water-basin changes you. The one under the thin ribbon of the falls, the one that gongs its patient clock-tone every few seconds. You go there — carefully, guided by a girl named for the sound a cracked bowl makes — because it is the one landmark you can actually use. You stand beside it in the blackness and you wait for the tone, and it comes, and you wait for it again, and slowly, over weeks, you stop waiting *for* it and start waiting *in* it. You begin to hear what the girl hears: that the tone is never the same twice. That the falls run heavier in the morning. That the basin holds a tiny shimmer of grit in its voice on the days the deep water moves. You begin, without meaning to, to keep time with a city.

Your hands change last, and slowest. You learn to tap before you learn to speak their knuckle-language — one knuckle, the second knuckle, the heel of the palm, each with its surface and its meaning. You learn that a fired-clay bead says a different thing than a dried-clay pillar, that grief has a tremor, that your own tremor of grief, when it finally comes — because it comes, for the sun you will never see again — is *read* by an elder's passing palm and answered not with words but with the common rhythm, four beats against your sternum, *I am here, we are together, all is measured.*

And you stay. That is the choice. You could learn the routes back up. Every traveler eventually hears someone describe the way out, in notes, in landmarks, in the gong-map of the city. But by the time you can read the map, you don't want the exit. You want the kiln.

Because you — you specifically, the one who arrived seeing and lost it — you have something none of them have. You *remembered* light. You know what a sunset looks like, what a face looks like, what *looking* even is. And so you become the city's one strange kind of maker: the smith who tunes pots to sounds no one here has ever heard but that you carry from the sunlit world and translate, badly, gloriously, into clay. A bowl tuned to the color of morning. A bell that rings the way your mother laughed. They cannot verify any of it. They don't care. They say your vessels sound *homesick*, and homesickness, here, is a legitimate note, a rare one, and the deep chamber — the one with the ancient basin, the one where the silent thing taps back — the deep chamber has, after long negotiation, agreed to display one of your pots.

You never go down there yourself. You are the one person who knows what the dark took from you, and you will not stand in that gong-tone clock and knock on the old basin and wait.

But late in your life, an old woman with enormous warm hands comes back up from the deep, and finds you at the falls, and taps twice against your collarbone — *a question with no useful answer* — and then, slowly, deliberately, knocks a rhythm against the tuned stone beside you.

Four beats. Pause. Four beats. Then four more.

Something answered her, she says. Something that had been waiting for a sound it recognized.
