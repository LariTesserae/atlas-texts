# An Early-Hearer's Ledger

I know where I am now. It took the mountain a while to tell me, and it told me the way it tells everything — indirectly, by condensation, by what pools on surfaces overnight.

I arrived, when I arrived, as one of the decisives. That is to say: with luggage that closed properly, with a trade I had already finished learning elsewhere, with an address written in ink that expected the address to hold still. The locals saw me at the gate the way you see weather coming — nothing personal, just forecasting. *Back down within the year,* their glances agreed, and the glances were nearly right.

What kept me was a bell.

I was sleeping in a boarding house on the sixth tier, in a dark-quarter because the room was cheap, and at some hour with no name I heard the hoist-bell toll eleven times. I counted without meaning to; counting is what the body does with bells. And then, an hour later — I know it was an hour because I lay awake the whole of it, listening to the building breathe its fog out through the walls — the hoist-bell rang again. Eleven times. The same eleven, note for note, with the same flaw in the ninth toll where the clapper strikes slightly off-center, a flaw I had heard the first time and could not have invented, because I had not yet been here long enough to know the bell had a flaw.

In the morning I made the mistake of mentioning this. Casually. To a dew-warden reading pipes across the lane.

She did not look up from the cistern. She said: "Back of the meeting, then," and that was the whole of my naturalization ceremony.

---

So: who am I here? I am one of the early-hearers, and I want to be precise about what that means, because the city is precise about it and imprecision here is rude.

It does not mean prophecy. I have no gift. What I have is a kind of grain-sensitivity — I run against the world's grain at the same angle the sound does, I suppose, and so tomorrow's noise reaches me like today's. The bell-keepers keep two ledgers, and I have learned that my entries go in the second one, the ledger of bells pre-heard, and that this ledger is treated with the same dry reverence as the first. Neither is privileged. Both are right. This is why the guild has survived by never saying so aloud, and why I am telling you, quietly, in a document the mountain may or may not permit to finish.

The etiquette is strict and I keep it strictly: you never act on an early bell. When I hear the dusk shift's steam-release hiss at noon, I do not put on my coat. When I hear my own name called in a voice I do not yet know, I do not turn around. The city lives in the deliberate present tense, and I have come to understand this not as denial but as *manners* — the same manners that keep everyone off the slopes of the Scaffold Beyond. You do not walk into a child's dream while the child is still deciding what it is. You do not eat tomorrow's bread out of tomorrow's oven. The stone settles at its own pace and so must I.

I am seated at the backs of meetings. I am told no surprises. And I have discovered, slowly, that this is not exile — it is a *post*. Someone must hold the city's place in the present while everyone else leans, however slightly, toward the future. We early-hearers are the ballast of the temporal ship. The locals tolerate us the way they tolerate the chuffs: as a problem budgeted for, loved anyway, occasionally load-bearing.

---

The rest of my life here has assembled itself the way the terraces do — nothing planned, everything permitted.

I live now in a light-ward, in a room whose value is three lumens, on a street that answers to *Harrow Lane* when I am climbing and to something older and less pronounceable when I am descending, and the mail arrives by whichever version of the street it prefers, which means, practically, that my letters decide how well I am doing. I own a mending-stone. I did not choose it; I walked the shed curls of the dreaming rock for a season with my hand open and my mind blank, as the children do, and one evening a pebble simply *agreed* with my palm. It is small now and getting smaller. I grip it when the grain runs against me — when a staircase miscounts under my feet, when a puddle shows me something extra, when the chuffs go silent all at once in that spreading wave that means something long and low is descending through the wrong angles of the dark. My stone has my thumbprint in it, several years deep. It is the truest portrait anyone will ever make of me, and it will go into the mortar when I do, which is the only real estate contract this city honors.

I have work, of a sort. The bell-keepers have made me a kind of auditor of the second ledger — not a keeper, I lack the standing — but the one who sits with an entry and reports what the *silence* around it does. It is mason's logic, learned late: the diligent fill notebooks with weather; the promising turn in blank pages. A bell that was pre-heard and never rang is a grief with no body, and somebody must sit with it, so I sit. I take my entitled day off and my strong drink with the duty-clerks, and we drink to the bell that was heard and never rang, wherever it is ringing.

---

And where do I fit in the census of this place? Let me give the downhill answer, as a local would:

I am a chuff, essentially — arrived opportunistically because warmth and water and green appeared where none had, never entirely learned my lesson, fouling the scaffolds of other people's plans, spherical in a plumage I do not need. I am one of the ferns the gardeners permitted rather than planted, holding my seam. I am a mason's apprentice at the age of forty, turned in my blank pages, waiting with instruments for my own foundations to settle — and I am told *it held* is the whole report, and mine holds, and so I am, by the highest praise this city offers and which strangers never recognize, *well-settled*, even though I am not finished. Nothing here is finished. That is not my failure state. It is my family resemblance.

But if you pressed me — if you stood with me at dusk on the ninth course of the Dew Stair, where the Lindens are, where a fern is permitted over the quiet — I would say what I actually am:

I am a person who came up the mountain set like good mortar and found, at the top of the ninth step or the bottom of the tenth, that I wasn't. I was nine steps going up and ten coming down. And instead of calling that a defect, this city called it a *street*, and gave it two names, and put it in both atlases, and seated me at the back of the meeting where I could hear the future politely without answering it.

I live in a place younger than I am, which makes me a parent here. I am raising a room, a lane's worth of tin reflectors, a stone that is learning my hand. On the one evening in six when the Scaffold Beyond shows its lamps, I climb to the rule line with the families and argue gently about which light is whose, and I do not say — because the lamplighters say nothing, and I am learning their silence — that I can hear, faintly, always, a bell up there that has not rung yet.

I am not going to climb toward it. That would be rude.

I am going to wait. It's what I'm for. The whole city is what I'm for — the hum an adult makes over a cradle — and the cradle is dreaming, and the rock is soft, and the dew is coming down out of the clear black air, bead by bead, onto everything that isn't finished yet.

Which is everything. Which is all of us.
