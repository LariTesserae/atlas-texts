I thought, at first, I'd want to be a tuner.

That's the glamorous one, isn't it — the one who climbs the staircase street each year to remake the First Notch, copper knife in hand, the oldest note in the basin passing through their body on its way to being kept alive. I imagined myself up there on the rim at dawn with the Wake rising through my boot soles, cutting the healed-over mouth of the great chanter and hearing the whole half-mile bowl shift a quarter-tone to answer me. And I could live inside that fantasy for a while. But the longer I sit with this place, the more I notice where my attention actually goes, and it isn't to the loud parts. It goes, every time, to that one line: *almost no one lingers.*

So no. I know where I'd be. I'd be in the north quarter.

I'd be the one who tends the Silent Quarter — the grave-gardener, the keeper of the Unclenching's scar. Not a priest; the Bellfolk wouldn't need one, and probably wouldn't have one, since their god is ambient and doesn't require an intermediary. More like a groundskeeper of the only privacy in the world. The one who sweeps the held-breath dust off the paths. The one who knows which families have dead new enough that they still come every dusk to speak out loud, and who times her work so she isn't there when they do — because she, of all people, understands that being unheard is the entire point of the place, and a witness, even a silent one, even one standing outside the silence's edge, is a kind of hearing.

I'd live at the boundary. That's the thing about this role: you don't live *in* the silence. Nobody could; even the Bellfolk take it in hours. You live just at its rim, in a small house at the chord's edge where the Great Chord frays into nothingness — the last house in town that can still feel the taps in its flagstones, close enough to the quiet that you can walk into it in eleven steps. Half my life in the roar, half in the hush. A person shaped like a door.

And I think I'd be the strangest kind of Bellfolk: one who is *fluent in both*. Because here is what nobody in the bowl could do, and what my job would slowly make me: someone who can read the chord *from the outside*. The living come into the Silent Quarter carrying the sound on them — you can see it in their shoulders, the way they're still vibrating, the way their hands keep signing out of habit to a dead person who can't read palms anymore. And I'd notice, over years, that grief in this town has a posture. That the lovers walk in leaning toward each other and walk out leaning the same way. That the ones who come to be unheard for an hour — the overwhelmed, the shamed, the ones carrying something too big for the taps — leave differently depending on whether the chord took them back warmly or coldly on a given afternoon. I would be the town's one scholar of what people look like when the sound is off. A lip-reader of the whole soul.

Would anyone know that's what I was doing? Probably not. To them I'd be the woman with the broom and the copper trowel, the one who replants the shade-greens at the graves, the one whose palm-signature they all know — a certain slow double-press on the shoulder, *the quiet is kept, the quiet is clean.* They'd hear my taps rise out of the floor at Evening Cadence and think: there's the north-warden, all is well. And that's fine. That's the job. In a town of two hundred people who hear everyone and meet no one, I'd be the person they meet only as vibration — the most heard and least seen person in the bowl, which is a kind of company I think I could keep.

At night I wouldn't sleep on my roof like the others. I'd sleep just inside the silence — eleven steps in, no more — on a bedroll on the dead ground where the chord cannot follow, one hand stretched back across the border so my palm still rests on ringing stone. Half my body in the conversation, half in the graveyard of sound. Dreaming in two registers.

And yes — I'd count the knucklebirds. Everyone does. But I'd also count something no one else counts: the people who go in, and the people who come out, and the small differences between them. Because somebody in a town that hears everything has to keep the one ledger that can't be tapped into stone.

Thirteen birds. Two hundred neighbors. One silence, and me at the edge of it, holding the door.
