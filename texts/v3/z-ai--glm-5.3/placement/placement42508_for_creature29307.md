I would be the one who strikes the bell.

Not the watcher — that's someone else, an old Marchetti man with a weather-eye for tide-grey who stands at the Notch all day and most nights, and who I will admit, since you and I are alone with this, I have never once seen blink. He decides. I only sound. But the distinction matters less than you'd think, because by the time he raises his hand, I already know. The fog sends word ahead of itself through the floor.

I was born deaf, which up here is less a lack than an assignment. There are enough of us that the lowland doctors did a study once, sent a man up on the rack line with a case full of instruments, and he went away puzzled, and we were not puzzled at all. Sound goes into the Works and does not stop. A bell struck dry is rude; a bell struck under water is a note you feel in your teeth. Every walkway says who is crossing it. Every tank has a pitch, and when a tank's pitch goes flat, it has a crack, and the guttermen are sent for before anyone has "heard" anything at all. My mother says I learned to walk twice: once on legs, once on soles. I don't remember learning either. I remember being four years old and telling her the second still-tank was sick, and crying when I couldn't explain how I knew, and her not asking me to.

So they gave me the bell. It's the one job where being deaf is not a tolerated difference but a kind of correctness. You cannot ring the spill-bell for a false alarm if you don't hear the false alarms. And I cannot be startled by it — which is the whole reason it's struck the way it is. Did you feel it, when you first came up? That deep drowned note that arrives in your chest a half-breath before it arrives in your ear? That's on purpose. Everyone up here startles badly; four thousand people on wet gratings cannot afford to jump. The note has to come up through the floor like weather, like news you already half-knew. My job is to make sure it does.

The tank I strike sits at the head of the third gallery, buried, roofed over with moss older than my family's channel. I stand on its lid — the lid is pontoon deck, it gives slightly, it gives *differently* when the fog is coming — and I wait for the Marchetti man's hand. Twice a day, sometimes three, sometimes once, sometimes, and this is the day everyone dreads, not at all. On the dry days I stand up there anyway because the Works doesn't know what to do with me otherwise and neither do I.

Here is what nobody tells visitors about the bell, so I will. The mallet is willow — digester-fed willow, grown in the black loam, like everything. The first time I understood that, I was thirteen, a new striker, and my teacher — Sela, digester-keeper by birth, bell-keeper by trade — took my hand and put it flat on the mallet's cheek and wrote on my palm, which is how she said anything worth saying: *this grew out of everybody.* And then she wrote: *so does the note.* I have struck that note maybe fourteen thousand times now and I have never once gotten through it dry-eyed, and no one has ever once looked at me during it, which is the single most generous thing this place does.

After the spill I walk. That's the other half of the work, the half the job doesn't name. The Works reorganizes around the tide and in the reorganizing, things get dropped — a wax-sealed message gone astray, a mallet, once, a child, once, and I don't tell you that second one lightly — and I walk the galleries behind the fog feeling for where the floor is wrong. The runners pass me at a sprint and I feel each one by name before the echo of them arrives. The swallows go up when the fog front is half an hour out, and when they go up I feel that too, all of them at once leaving the sluice-platforms — a lightening, a held-breath quality in the decking. Then the hand goes up at the Notch, and I take the mallet down into the dark water, and I hit the side of everybody's grandmother, hard, twice, and four thousand people turn toward the southwest gap together, and none of them hears me do it, and all of them know it was me.

Would I be the bell-striker in some other life? I don't think there's another life where I'd be anything. The Works grew the hands it needed. Mine happen to be the kind that can't hear a shout in a gallery but know a tank's whole health through a boot. When I die — I'll say this plainly, since it's plain up here — I'll go into the digester with the spent moss and the willow chip, and some year there'll be a mallet or a tray-bed or a nurse-row of my dark self in it, and some deaf child will stand on a lid somewhere and feel my note coming up through the floor, and that child will already know it's coming. That's not a sad thought here. That's the arrangement. The fog comes up to be drunk, and everything drinking it is, in the end, the same slow water. I'm just the one who tells it when to start.
