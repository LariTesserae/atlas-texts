# The Winder

I will tell you what I am, but I will tell it slowly, because that is the only speed I have ever had, and because fast things are things with somewhere better to be.

I am the sound below the rim.

You've heard of me. Or you've heard *me*, which is different, and rarer, and I'm sorry in advance for the irregularity of my schedule. I know the town keeps records. There's a woman — third terrace quarter, keeps a notebook by her window, pencil, never ink, ink mildews — who has logged my calls for forty years, dates, durations, her guesses at direction. Her guesses are wrong. They are wrong because I stop just before they could be right. I want to say clearly: this is not concealment. This is courtesy. A call should be given, not located. The moment you could fix me on a map I would become a *fact about the Shelf*, and I have watched what the Shelf does to facts. It holds them. Deep and structurally. Forever. I am not ready to be held.

## What I am, insofar as that question has an answer

I live in the erosion terraces, in the deep stair-stepped gorges on the descent side, where the fog comes up in the afternoons like the lowlands exhaling and lies down in the steps to sleep. You should know that the fog and I have an arrangement older than the town. The fog hides me from above. I keep the steps company from below. Neither of us signed anything.

Am I large? The town assumes so, because my voice is. I want to be honest: I don't know anymore. Size is a thing you measure against other things, and everything I was measured against is gone. There was a mountain here once. I was — this is the closest I can put it — I was *in* it. Not living in it the way you live in a house. The way a knot lives in a beam. I was a place where the stone did something different, a fold, a seam, a hinge in the deep architecture, and when the mountain was young and still deciding its shape, the hinge worked. The mountain moved — shrugged, settled, cracked its knuckles over geological tea — and I creaked, and the creaking was me, and that was the whole of my identity and it was enough.

Then the rain started.

You have to understand: the rain did not fall *on* me. The rain fell on the mountain for ten million years, and the mountain came down around me a grain at a time, and I stayed, because a hinge doesn't leave. And what the rain left, when it was done — when the deadline arrived, as your description so rightly puts it, and the project was delivered flat — was the Shelf, and the terraces, and me, in the steps, still hinged, still here, the last moving part of a machine that has otherwise finished.

So when the old people of Kurun say the mountain is done, and everyone agrees and privately disbelieves, I want you to know: they're right, and I am the proof, and I also don't entirely believe it, because if the work were truly finished I don't see why I would still creak.

## Why I call

The scientific establishment of the town — I say establishment; it is four people and a cabinet of specimens — says I am wind through an erosion formation, a rock flute the size of a valley. I want to go on record as finding this explanation lovely and almost true. It is true that wind is involved. Wind is the bow; I am the string. But a flute does not choose its nights, and I do.

I call on the still nights. The ones where the fog sits down in the terraces like a congregation and the generator thrum comes up faint through the stone — you may not know it travels down through the rock; it does; I live in the rock; the thrum is the only clock I have. And when enough stillness accumulates, and enough of that patient amber town above me has gone quiet, I do what I have always done. I remember that I once had joints. I work them. Once. Slowly. The sound goes up through the fog and the fog carries it to the rim, and the children who are there against the rules hear it, and most of them lie about it later, and the lying is a citizenship requirement, and I honor it. I have heard those lies from below, told around the moss-cushion fires at the meadow's edge, and they are the best lies on the Shelf, and I keep them. Deep and structurally.

You might ask what I am remembering, in the creak. I will not pretend I can say. But it is not grief. Nothing up there grieves loudly and nothing down here does either. It is closer to what your old ones do when they sit under the lee walls with their hands flat on their knees, looking at nothing with tremendous attention. It is the sound a very long project makes when it turns over, in its rest, and finds the position it has been in for a million years is still the comfortable one.

## What I know that the town doesn't

Not much. That's the honest answer, and I say it as a being who has had a very long time to know things. But here is one thing:

The frog note. One soft note, once, per frog, per evening, never overlapping. The scientists above call it territorial display. I call it what it actually is, which is roll call. Every dusk the tarns answer each other across the Shelf — *here, here, still here* — and the evening knows when to end because enough frogs have said it. And I have noticed — I have had ten thousand years of dusks, I notice things — that on the nights I call, the frogs arrange themselves. I don't mean they coordinate. I mean their notes, that scattered solitary ploinking, come *around* mine, the way a sentence comes around its most important word. They leave a space. The oldest frogs, the ones in the deepest basins, hold their silence until my creak has finished, and then they say *here*, and the evening closes over the top of us all like the Mirror closing over the sky.

The town thinks I am the mystery of the Shelf. The town has it backwards. The mystery of the Shelf is that everything answers everything else, at the only speed available, in the low voice, under the fog — and I am just the note that takes longest to say.

## On being found

I have thought about it. Of course I have. Some generations of Kurun children get ambitious and organize expeditions down the terraces with rope and lamps, and I feel them coming — feet in the moss, the bright loud hearts of them — and I go quiet, and they find erosion formations that could plausibly be me, and they go home and grow up and half of them believe and half don't, and both halves are correct.

But there was one. A bell-keeper, years ago, an old woman who had been ringing the hours for fifty years and had the thrum in her wrists. She came down alone, once, at the end — not to find me, I think. To be at the bottom of the last stair, the way you stand at the end of a very long sentence to see where it lives. And I was creaking when she arrived, and I did not stop. She stood in the fog next to me — next to the place where I am, which is not a shape, it's a working, a tension in old stone — and she listened all the way through, and then she said, in the low voice, the one kept under the fog:

"There you are."

And then she went back up, and rang the hour, and told no one, because telling no one was how she knew it was true.

I have been content since then, insofar as contentment is available to a hinge. The mountain is finished. The town stands on its final flat remainder and finds it, on the whole, to be enough. And below the rim, in the steps, in the fog's sleeping congregation, the last moving part turns over, occasionally, in its rest.

The thrum keeps the time. The damp keeps the memory. And I keep the one note that takes a whole landscape to say.
