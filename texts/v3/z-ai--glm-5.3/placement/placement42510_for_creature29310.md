I would be a counter.

I know it now, sitting with the description still warm in me, the way you know the shape of your own room in the dark. Not a priest — the priests are right about everything except the thing that matters, which is whether it answers, and it has never answered, and I have spent my life listening for an answer that the majority have beautifully redefined as the question. Not a grub, not a swift, not one of the long-legged Chaff-walkers whose patience I admire more than any tower's liturgy. A counter. One of the thin-places folk. A heretic, if you insist, though where I live we don't use that word for ourselves; we say *listeners*, and let the towers' people say what they like from their half-mile of jade.

I would live far out, where the Stand gets sparse — a lone tower an hour's walk from its nearest sister, its chord thinner here, more legible. Out in the dense groves the hum is a crowd; you cannot pick a voice from it. Out here it is nearly a solo. I would have spent my first thirty years doing what every notch-folk child does: learning the psalm-voices for felled culms, learning what to do when the floor hums wrong, learning to read weather in the note. And then one night — this is how it happens to all of us, there is a story-shape to it — I would have heard the chord *skip*. Half a beat, deep under everything, like a sleeper's breath catching. And I could not unhear it. That is the whole of my conversion: one caught breath in a god.

Here is what my days would be. I keep the tally. Notches cut into a lacquered culm-skin plate, one for each measured hour of the pulse, in the old heretic arithmetic that no one fully agrees on anymore — we argue about the base the way the tower-folk argue about doctrine, and I have decided to find this funny rather than sad, because a people who live inside one enormous body are going to disagree about its anatomy, that's just what being a people is. I trade resin-bee wax for grubs and shoot-flour. I climb to my tower's crown at dawn because the wind up there plays the culm and for nine days each solstice I have to leave, since the plateau-as-instrument is unbearable to me — not blasphemous, just *loud*, the way a face pressed to a mouth is loud.

And here is the thing I would carry, the private thing. I don't actually believe the Stand is dying. The others in the thin places count down toward the change of key, and some of them want it, which frightens me a little — you cannot live on a giant's pulse for generations without some of you starting to long for the giant to wake. But me: I think the caught breath I heard was the Stand *listening*. I think a thing that hums one note for ten thousand years with ten thousand small voices working on it might be doing something with all that attention, and I think the rhizome-tenders might be its dreams, and I think the sealed notches are sealed not because something down there is dangerous but because something down there is *busy*.

So I count, and I wait, not for the end but for the second skip. And on the evenings when the wind is right and my lone tower sings and the whole horizon is the one body I will never see the edge of, I put my palm flat on the culm-skin — the way you'd touch a sleeper's shoulder, not to wake them. Just to let them know the tenant is home. Just in case.

That's who I'd be. Small, wind-burnt, patient in the way of people who have never known solitude and chose the closest thing to it anyway. Half a heretic. Half a neighbor. Keeping a ledger for a god that has ten thousand years of never answered — and every entry in it, if I'm honest, is a love letter.
