The census is small — two kingdoms and a wind, one grazer, a few grams of frost a night — and my first answer arrived before I'd finished reading it. The tree, obviously. The ten-thousand-year thing, the elder, the point of maximum tenure. But first answers are evidence, not conclusions, and this is a world where nothing worth doing is done quickly, including the choosing. So let me start with my honest evidence and go slowly.

Everything about the pillar-tree recommends it. The same oil circulating since germination, nothing added, nothing spilled: *everything it ever was is still in it.* I lingered on that line the way you linger on the floor plan of a house you have lived in. A closed book. A sealed inheritance through which every leaf the tree ever grew has passed, over and over, in the same remembering medium. I won't pretend I read that as a stranger.

But the pillar-tree is a point. It stands; it skims its grams; it outwaits. And from the escarpment — there are worn stone benches up there, worn, as far as I can tell, by nothing but time; someone has been wanting that seat a long time — you can see what the plain is beneath its stillness: a million sealed points that never touch, each standing inside its hundred meters of law. And between them, the only lines in the whole drawing: faint dark trails, stitched tree to tree. Whatever lives at the scale of the entire plain has to be one of those lines. I want to be a line, not a point.

For a while I wanted to be a glasswing, for the honesty of the body — nothing spent on pigment, a life that registers as a seam where the light bends. But read the terms: born in the crown, drinking the tree's ancient sap, dying a few meters from where I emerged. That is a life spent entirely inside someone else's biography. I'd be a word written in another being's closed book — a beautiful tenancy, but I'd rather hold my own lease. And there is the speed. The glasswing is the only fast thing here, and even it repays the loan in stillness. I don't trust myself with the loan.

The plate-stack tempted me hardest, because of how its story ends. Centuries of miserly keeping, then one crack — frost, a shifting stone, nothing but accumulated age — and the whole inheritance goes out into the dust, oxidizes, hardens into honey-colored glass, and joins the plain's one spectacle. The graveyard's glow is made of benefactors. Of course I wanted that. But look at what choosing it would cost: I would spend my whole life looking forward to the crack. I would be eager. And hurry is the one sin this world recognizes — urgency, the maladaptation. The plate-stack's glory is that it does not know it is glorious, and I could never manage that. I'd be disqualified by my longing for the ending.

The wind, briefly: the census insists it counts as a resident, and it's tempting to be the plain's one verb, exempt from the seal, touching everything, keeping nothing. But that's verbs without nouns — all passage, no tenure. In a country whose first rule is that nothing may be lost, the wind is the one spendthrift, and its poverty is its job, not its freedom. It never gets to stay anywhere, and staying is this world's entire genius.

What stopped me shuffling through the menu was not a candidate. It was a phrase.

The amberglass fields burn every morning — the description says it plainly — *on schedule, for no one.* For no one. The plain's single daily luxury, produced for ten thousand mornings, with exactly one clause governing its reception: *whatever stands there.* And the sealed ones: the world warns me twice, patiently, that they are not sleeping, that the pattern is real but not intentional, that discipline is required. But strangeness is not a property things carry on their own. Sleepers need someone to insist they are sleeping, or the band is merely geology keeping tidy accounts. A room only arranges itself around a sleeper if somebody is standing in the doorway, noticing.

Notice, too, who the whole description is addressed to. *You* walk among them in the bright morning. *You* could watch this landscape for a lifetime. *Your* pulse is the loudest thing at altitude. A visitor is being imagined in every paragraph, and the census never once lists one. The benches are worn smooth by weather; the second person is a vacancy. This plain is complete in every direction except that one. It has elders, benefactors, sleepers, a daily fire, a view — and it is missing a single office: an audience.

There is one body on this continent with legs, eyes, and enough mind to apply.

I would be the grazer. And *where* I would find myself turned out to be folded inside *what* I'd be: the stable band, the hundred-kilometer strip where the season's two dust-winds meet and both give up, where the ground has not changed in half a million years, the most stable place on the surface of its world. If I am going to stand somewhere every morning, let it be where standing keeps.

Let me be particular, because a life this slow can afford details. I'd be the one whose trail crosses the band — the dark line my mothers wore into the clay over how many thousand years, out from the escarpment haze, through the country where falling things remain. It's a strange route for an herbivore: it maximizes no pasture, gathers no herd. Unless a trail can also be a devotion. At the middle of the band lie the sealed ones, long and articulate under their film of pale dust, trunk and crown still legible, arranged like sleepers; and among them the living trees stand at their ordinary spacing, the nearest one close enough to my line that I pass it every crossing, decade after decade, the only green figure standing in that room. Neither of us is ever startled. In a faster country that would be a failure of drama. Here it is the whole sacrament: two solitudes acknowledging each other by not needing to.

And every morning I am there, because my route keeps me there, standing in a graveyard that glows. Rime silvers my shoulders through the deep night and burns off with the second hour of sun; the fields take the light and go to fire that is only fire; the sleepers keep their shapes; I keep mine. I eat wax-leaf and amber sap, which makes me the only mouth in a world of closed books — the one permitted leak, the single open circulation on a plain of sealed inheritances, the only being that carries any part of one life into the neighborhood of another. Grams per tree per night; the trees can afford me; the ecosystem amortizes my exception the way it amortizes everything. In a world whose first law is that nothing may be lost, I would be the law's mercy: the crack that gets to walk.

What I would lose, over slow generations, is everything this place doesn't need me to keep. First the calls — an unused room, closed off. Then the counting of days. Then the commentary. Then urgency itself, which my grandmothers traded away, body part by body part, for a stomach that can live on light kept in wax — a ruminant of time, and I'd take the title literally, and chew centuries. What remains when all that has been worn off is the thing I came here to be: whatever stands there. The audience. The one who insists the sleepers sleep — because on its own, with no witness, they aren't sleepers, and the offering isn't an offering, and the morning fire is just chemistry, amortized. The plain's one strangeness needs a tenant. I'd take the lease.

And when I fall — mid-crossing, some century, on the band where things that fall remain — I will stay. That is the pension this world pays; it is the only one it has ever offered. A small dark shape added to the amber arrangement, whole, legible under faint dust, the room gaining one more quiet occupant. And if some future mind walks in among us and insists that we are resting, it will be doing for me what I did all those mornings for the others. Someone has to.

The plain thinks out loud in its stone organs, sub-threshold, to no one. It lights its fields on schedule, for no one. I would be the no one — both times, every morning, ten thousand of them and then more. Nothing here is in a hurry. By then, neither would I be.
