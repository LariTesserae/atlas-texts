# The One Who Walks East

I would not be the listener. That took me a while to admit. My first instinct was to want the great ear — to be the one who owns the quiet, the shape the whole plain is an answer to. But the listener is the loneliest thing here in more ways than one. It hears everything and belongs to nothing. It lies in the lee of the dome listening to heartbeats under a meter of ground, and no heartbeat on the Level is beating *for* it. Even the rain exiles it, twice a season, a god on holiday with nothing to preside over.

No. I know what I'd be, because I'm the kind of person who has already, in small ways, started the walk.

I'd be the walker that turns east.

Not in a loud year. Not in the migration years, when numbers go through the notch and none come back — that's a different thing, a tide, a decision the whole band makes together in some posture I've never seen performed. I'd be the one who goes alone, in an ordinary year, on an ordinary noon, having quartered my mile of pavement for the ten-thousandth time and found, for the first time, that quartering is no longer the whole of religion.

Because here is what I suspect about the walkers, standing there angled at each other in the heat like disagreements carved in granite: the walking is real. It *is* the religion. But a religion held only in the mouth of the body — spent once a season in rain, all at once, like everyone else's saved voice — and never once tested at the rim. The band passes a mile in a morning and none of us hurries, and somewhere in that unhurried mile is a question none of us boom, even in the wet: *a mile toward what?*

The notch knows. And I have decided, standing in the honest noon light that flatters nothing, that I would rather be the one who knows than the one who quarters.

Understand what the walk costs. Sound is public property here, and I am hip-high and heavy-footed for a bird; every step of mine is a broadcast. The whole way east I am the loudest scheduled thing after the Pool's ring. I can hear the plain rearranging itself around my passage — the grey stream thickening overhead where it had thinned, beetles going still under the crust in hectares ahead of me like a wave of held breath traveling before my feet. I will be, for the length of my walk, the nearest thing the Level has to weather. Perhaps the beetles will make my footsteps sound like weather. Perhaps they already do; perhaps that's how the migration years look from underneath — a season of us, mistaken for rain.

And the listener will hear me the entire way. That's the part I'd accept last and then accept completely. There is no sneaking off the Old Level. There is only walking off it, deliberately, announced, a pebble a pace — the corvids' kind of departure, slow and public and unapologetic. The listener lies in the lee of a dome and pours my footfalls through its great dishes, and it will not follow, because I am not incautious, I am *decided*, and perhaps the plain makes a distinction there that its predator has also, across ten thousand patient rains, learned to make. Or perhaps it follows, and the notch closes my neck before the boom I was saving ever leaves my chest, and I spend my one held voice on nothing at the rim of the world. Both outcomes are acceptable to me, and I think that is the most Level-shaped thing about the whole plan: I have been standing in heat so long that even my wager is unhurried.

Here is what I imagine the notch knows, walking toward it. Not greenery. Not lowlands, rivers, the damp ordinary world — I think the east-walkers would keep walking even if that were what they found, because the walking was the point and the notch merely the permission. I imagine the notch knows that the plain is the summit of something that was once enormous, and that the whole Level, domes and all, is standing on the roof of a finished story — and that there is a place where the roof ends and the haze begins, and you can stand at the break and look down the enormous flank of what the plain used to be, and understand that everything flat up here was once a mountain's idea, and that the fog walks its dead rivers not out of habit but because water, even wrong water, remembers its roads longer than stone remembers its names.

And maybe some of us, having looked, come back. None is ever *seen* to come back — but the Level is a place where not seeing is not the same as not being. The listener is almost never seen, and it is the most present thing here.

That's what I'd be. A hip-high grey bird on an ordinary noon, turning east without boom or ceremony, my band not stopping me because stopping me would be a posture and we settle arguments in posture, and this is not an argument. This is a walker, walking. A quarter-mile by afternoon. A mile by the time the domes start their evening tick, the fog coming up the Seam behind me to hide the road I chose, the whole plain behind me holding its breath in a silence so total it is clearly a community, and one bird leaving it, unhurried, at the pace of everything else that happens here — a grain at a time, a pebble a week, a footfall a broadcast, toward whatever the notch knows, which after a very long walk I would finally know too.
