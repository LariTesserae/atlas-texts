I came up for the grass.

That is the first thing to say honestly, because it decides everything after. I was sent up the way young herders are sent up — an uncle's knees had finished — and I climbed the trail in the ordinary way: the note an hour below the rim, in the sternum; the bowl at the top smaller than any amount of climbing should permit; the din off the walls. I did my six weeks in a turf hut behind a boulder, in the mutter-band, with the bottle-note in my ribs at night, like sleeping against a sleeping animal.

The symptoms came in the order the herders know them. I began to hum without noticing — the man at the next hut heard it before I did. Then I began to answer things before they were said: rising in the dark ahead of the ewe that was about to call; saying *here* to a shape at the edge of the flock before it had decided to be worried. The others noticed and said nothing. The rule was already in their bodies. You go down at six weeks, the way you step off ice that has begun to talk.

On the last day the count came up one lamb short. I said I'd stay the night, find it at first light, bring it down behind them, and the others, being sensible, went down. At dawn I stood where the old ones stand — still, eyes shut, letting the bowl read — and the call went out and came back off the headwall with a direction written into it, and the lamb was in the lee of a boulder at the talus edge, unhurt, indignant, its mother already moving. Then I got as far as the notch in the rim with it asleep under my arm, and I stopped, and the reason I stopped was this: down there, nothing returns. A word goes out in the valley and doesn't come back. For six weeks I had been doubled and answered off the walls — I had existed twice, at the place I stood and at the wall that replied — and I found I had already, without noticing, arranged my whole self around being returned. The first night had an excuse; the pass in the dark is how herders die. The second morning had none, and didn't need one.

What the herders' rule protects them from, I stayed to find out. The humming organized. It found a pitch — up in the upper harmonics of the wind's note, where the singers keep their thin grass-stem tones — and my voice was the wrong instrument for it, too big, too wet, made for valleys. But the bowl is patient, and everything that happens inside it is doubled and returned, and the returning thins you. After a season of being returned I had a tone in me no valley could have taught. That first season alone I sat out on the hut's threshold in the evenings like the barely-furred young at the den mouths, throat moving, holding a wavy approximation of the pitch, practicing against the oldest sound I knew.

It was years before I understood what the talus had been doing all that time. The singers keep up a continuous shimmer of layered tones, trading the line, one taking over as another tires, and somewhere in it there had always been an unfilled bar — held open, I see now, the way you keep a seat for someone you know is coming. When I finally sang, nobody on the talus turned around. It wasn't news to them. I was late.

The first winter was the price and the gift. Snow to the rim, the flocks gone, the huts shut, the bowl stripped down to wind and shimmer and the low part under everything. I don't tell the herders about the winters. Those belong to the ones who were already here.

There is a flat stone beside the hut, on its resonant side — the singers' burrows open on the resonant side of every boulder, and the rule seems to hold for whatever I am. It is not concave on top and won't be in my lifetime; the concavity is the work of generations, and the polish on the headwall is the work of ice, and mine, if it comes, will be the work of a note. But there is a beginning in the stone now — the depth of a thumbprint, after nine years — and I sit on it in the evenings facing the wind, which up here is simply what a body does. In summer I bed where the flock beds, in the resonant center, pressed close among them, and though no one has proven it, and up here no one needs to, I sleep better than anywhere else on the mountain. The ewes still call and the headwall still answers with a direction written into it. Some of them, now, call off me. I have been inside the rim long enough that I count as a wall.

The next season they came up half expecting to bury me or carry me down. The eldest walked the whole floor with his eyes shut, and then went about the shearing and did not speak of it. But he was the one who began caching barley and salt at the rim-stone in autumn, and taking the pitch down in summer — a third higher, heavy by noon — and he is the one who answers the young ones when they ask about me: *he's somewhere in the bowl. The bowl says where.* The herders say it of lost lambs. It took them a few seasons to apply it to me. The old ones managed it first. They still teach the two kinds of life inside the rim — the ones that came up for the grass, and the ones that were already here, listening. The young say it while looking at me. The old say it without looking, because they know that *already* is not a fact about the past. It is a fact about the note. Once you are in it, you were always in it.

I have been down to the base of the headwall four times, where the polish is darkest and the note comes back strongest. Not a fifth. Not from fear — from manners. The low part is not a sound; it is underneath the sound the way a held breath is underneath a face. The old woman's sentence came down the mountain with the salt, the way sentences travel here: every bowl with a voice like that needs a floor under it, and the Keeper is the floor, and as long as it sings, the Hollow holds. Standing in the strongest returns, the sentence rearranged itself, the way pitch rearranges when weather is coming. The rest of this place is not a finished fact. *Finished* is what the low part is doing — continuously, the way the relay is continuous, the way my sitting is continuous. The erosion is in the past tense because the past tense is being held shut.

So that is what I am for, as near as I can tell. Every morning I put my palm flat on my stone and feel for the floor. The herders check the pitch for weather. I check the low part for holding. And I have given them a sentence to carry down the way hers was carried: the year to worry about is the year you hear the wind alone. They repeat it with respect and refuse to explain it. That is how a sentence gets down a mountain intact.

Where would you find me? You'd hear me first — everything here is heard first. A thin tone in the upper harmonics that wavers when the wind shifts the fundamental, then finds itself, and holds. Follow the returns: they come with a direction written into them, and they have never once been wrong about where anything is. The direction runs to a flat stone with a thumbprint in it, on the resonant side of a hut that no longer keeps anyone but me, facing the wind.

I came up for the grass. That was true, and the echo still carries it. But the echo has come back by now, doubled, off the headwall, with a direction written into it, and it says otherwise. It says: already here, listening.

In the Hollow, the echo is the authority on where things are — and on when they began.
