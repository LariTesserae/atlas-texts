I would be small. That is the first thing I know about myself here, before I know anything else: in a world this saturated, this finished, smallness is the only vacancy left. Everything else is occupied. The grass has the meadow. The felt has the stones. The currents have movement. Even decay has a job, and the bloom, and the mud. There is exactly one position open in this ecosystem — the position of witness — and I would take it.

So: I am a hand-span of deliberate life in a sea that has outsourced motion to physics. I am hip-high at most, crouched among the glassy grass so that the lean of the current passes over me and the spore-snow settles on my back the way it settles on everything. I have let the meadow dress me. My hide is the same felt that furs the quartzite, seeded by drift, tended by nothing — the grass does not know I am not a stone, and I have let that misunderstanding stand for so long that I am almost not wrong. I have no edges. Nothing here shows an edge, and I have ground mine away on the Road, on the polished ruts, the way the uncounted dead ground their passage. When I hold still — and I am built for holding still; my one excellent feature is a flat, patient hand — the small things of the sea settle on me within a minute, and I read them. Drifting seeds. Grit from the bloom. Glimmers of plankton riding the grain of the water. I am the still surface the world practices its ideas on. Sometimes the water, holding too long, practices the idea of a fish on my palm, and I feel the shadow of a shape that has been extinct for eons pass over my skin, and I do not flinch, because flinching would end the idea too soon.

Where I live: the wake. Not the Road itself — the Road belongs to the one. I live a few weeks behind the grazer, in the blessing, in the stretch where the grass is fruiting and the mats fume their nutrients and the water has gone briefly, extravagantly rich. The whole plain keeps its calendar by the Road, and I keep mine by the wake, the way a beggar keeps time by the bells of a cathedral he will never enter. When the water tastes of iron and pollen in the same swallow, I know where the one has passed, and how long ago, and I settle into the ruts and let the current push me gently down the polished groove like a leaf in a gutter. I am going where they went. Everyone here is going where they went. I am the only one who knows it, and the knowing is my whole inheritance.

My work is the marks. There are so few of them — an arch framing nothing, a circle of nubs like knuckles, a sphere the grass handles gently. I tend nothing. I repair nothing. I arrive at the arch and I look through it at the meadow, and I look through it from the other side, and I note that the view is different, and I leave. I sit with the sphere for a day and a half sometimes, doing what the grass does — handling it gently — because if it is an egg then someone should be sitting on it, and if it is only granite then I have lost a day and a half to a stone, which, in this place, is not a loss. The marks come to me strangely, the way they come to everyone — I can travel toward the arch all day and arrive no closer, then find it beside me, polite, having walked around to meet me. I have stopped finding this remarkable. I have started thinking of it as the arch's way of being tended: it comes to whoever will keep it company.

And this is my true labor, the one the plain gave me without asking: I am the ear. Every few days, when the banked silence finally gives one sound back — there is no other way to say it; the sea has spent its age absorbing the world's noise, and it is so old and so full that occasionally it pays out a single deposit — I am there. A stone that will not settle until tomorrow, settling now. A bird. I know it is a bird the way you know a word in a language you dreamed: the sound arrives complete, a thin bright threading call from a forest that has been under warm water for a hundred million years, and the meadow dims in empathy, and I hold still and let it happen to me. I keep these sounds the way the water keeps its rings of turbidity. I have grown rings. If something ever sectioned me — nothing will; nothing here has an edge for the work — it would find, layered in my felt, one thin bright line for every sound the sea has given back since I began: dawn bells, the hinge-creak of grass, and every few days one impossibly banked heirloom, some noise the world made before it drowned, withdrawn from the account one coin at a time.

I do not go up. I tried, once, early, when I still had a little of the surface in me. I swam toward the dark that is not night and the water thinned in light around me and I understood, before anything happened, that I would come back changed, or come back before, or arrive somewhere that was not coming back — and I leaned down, back into the grain of things, back into the warm braids and the taste of iron. And I do not go to the rim. Nothing here goes to the rim. But I think about it more than the grass does, which is one way of saying I think about it at all: that pull that is not current, downhill in time, toward the Low where the oldest water lies like a held secret. I face away from it when I sleep. My whole body is a lean, the same permanent lean the grass has, except the grass leans with the current's habits and I lean away from the rim. We arrive at the same posture from opposite reasons. Maybe that is what the grass and I have in common. Maybe that is all anyone has in common with anyone, here or anywhere.

And once — I will tell you this and then be quiet — I saw the one up close. The grazer, the whole animal, boat-hulled, plowshare mouth deep in the meadow, walking its ruts. It saw me. I know it saw me, because it stopped, for a moment, and the world stopped with it, the way the world does. We regarded each other across ten meters of fruiting grass — the only two things on this plain that move on their own intent, meeting by accident in a world that has almost finished needing intention. It did not bless me. I did not bless it. Then it went on, and the wake of its going closed behind it, and I went on, weeks behind, in the blessing.

I am almost always wrong when I see two of anything. But I think, that once, there really were two. I think the water was not practicing. I think the plain had simply forgotten to keep us apart.
