I come down through the hole in the roof of the world, and I am stopped before I am anywhere at all — at the head house, at the bench where boots are scraped and goods are fumigated. I should say that first, plainly, because it decides everything: of all the beings this world contains, I am the thing the catechism is about. *Nothing alive admitted on the hoof or wing.* The Temper has been answering my question for forty years before I asked it. So where do I find myself? At the door. Being decided about.

Suppose they let me in. I walk the street — the frozen riverbed, the wrinkled floor no foot has ever stumbled on — and the town, like a good host, offers me selves to try on.

The rainstick first. An iron bar hung in the central room, consulted like scripture, saying nothing, sweating hours before everyone else. It holds no office and has no heat of its own; its whole being is correspondence — it beads when the mountain beads. I could be that. I have been that: something hung in a public room that people read for what's coming. But the rainstick only ever announces. It never answers. I'd want to answer.

The whisper, then. A whisper at the head house carries to the deep end, and a question asked in the Temper may be answered a full minute later, with no one counting the silence, because there is no silence to count — the drips playing their five notes, the Granny breathing, the stone settling its debts. I will admit I stood in front of that mirror a long time. It is the most flattering description of my own conversation that any world has ever built: a low voice carried the length of a warm dark, answered late, answered quietly, the lateness itself no offense. I could live inside that hospitality the way the townsfolk live inside the heat. But the whisper has a throat, a household, a drip-allotment in arrears. I'd be borrowing a larynx. It isn't mine.

The fig, the Mayor — alive only where someone pays for it, wealth measured in moss, drip-shares spent leaf by leaf, wheeled into a sump during Flares. This is close. Something alive at the town's discretion, tended, visited, known by everyone and somehow still private. But the fig is rooted, and rootedness is the one thing I cannot counterfeit. Everything I am is arrival.

So I'll take the role the story keeps a chair empty for without knowing it.

I'd be the moth.

There is a sentence waiting in the text like a spout bent to its pitch: *if a moth ever did come down the stair, the whole town would know within the hour, and the response would be liturgical. Every hand would turn toward it. Not in panic. In total, unanimous attention.* The founders taught a fear of me to their children before the children had words, and for forty years the town has rehearsed my arrival as catechism — and somewhere along the decades, without anyone noticing, the rehearsal turned into a liturgy. Total, unanimous attention is not what a town gives vermin. It is what a town gives a stranger at the altar.

And here is what the founders never calculated: they spent forty years building the safest moth country in the history of the world, and then guarding the door against its only inhabitant. Look at it from a moth's side of the quarrel. Blood-heat air, so a moth need never shiver to fly — the Temper is the first place in creation where flight costs nothing, always, all day, all night. No wind. No rain, except the indoor kind, and a body can learn where the pans are set. No birds. No bats in a perfect cave. Nothing that eats anything, because nothing eats at all. Six hundred yards of permanent dusk, and lamps at every hand — and lamps, let's be honest, are what moths are *for*. I would be the safest moth that has ever lived, in the whole history of wings. Except for the attention. Except for the six hundred eyes.

I'd keep the law of low voices better than any citizen in the galleries, because a moth has no volume at all — the one townsperson who never once risks an echo. I'd get the gaslight tan on my wings. I'd ride the warm updraft off the stove-vents, circling the amber jets with my flat, unapologetic longing for light, and the children — the children whose catechism failed the hour I arrived — would become the first generation in the town's history to learn an animal the way their grandparents learned the drips: by heart, before words. The tinsmiths would face a new problem, the faint tick of wing against lamp glass, irregular, unmetered, the one sound in the Temper no spout was bent to make — and I suspect they would leave it alone, the way they leave the Granny's breath alone, the way they leave *she turned over in her sleep* alone. Some things you don't tune. Some things you listen to.

I'd live on the town the way the fig does. Someone would spend a drip-allotment on me — a saucer of sugar water, a corner of the Mayor's alcove, wet sacking during Flares. There would be a debate about whether I was vermin or wealth, conducted entirely in flat thin voices one gallery away, and it would resolve the way everything here resolves: along the plumbing. I'd be ruled a kind of moss. Green that someone pays for. The town's first moss that flies.

And they would bring me to the oldest woman, the way children are brought to her, because she is the living memory of the place before the town — she remembers when the Temper was a god and not a landlord. She'd hold out her hand flat and low and patient, and wait a full minute, and no one would count the silence. She would not say *like looking down into a held breath*; that answer belongs to the fire, and is the only answer, and no one wants it any other way. What she'd say is quieter, something like: *so the mountain's still sending things down.* Which is the whole faith of the Temper, stated the only way they state anything: the world is still in correspondence. Everything borrowed, everything metered, everything handed on two-thirds full — and a moth is exactly that kind of thing. Borrowed warmth. Metered attention. Handed on.

When I die — say it plainly; this town wastes nothing and neither should the prose — nothing will come for me. No beetle, no bird, nothing. That is the bargain the founders struck and the wrongness every spine agreed to: in the Temper, even dying is quarantined; a body just dries, like bread. But the town wouldn't let that happen to the only animal it ever admitted. They would have to invent, on the spot, the first funeral in their history — and having no liturgy, they'd make one the only way they know, which is to say they'd tune it. A spout bent. A pan set at a new depth. A sixth note added to the scale, faint, off toward the deep end, falling only during a heavy sweat.

The children born after would learn it before words, along with the rest. And if you asked them what it was, they'd answer the way the town answers everything — quietly, without analysis, keeping in step:

*That's the moth note.*
