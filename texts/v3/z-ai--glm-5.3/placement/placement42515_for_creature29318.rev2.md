I thought, at first, that I would be one of the pipes. It's the obvious answer — the citizen with a district, the held note, the slow government of getting lower. And there's something in me that wants that: to have a single frequency, to be audible from the rim, to be a column of air doing one true thing for centuries.

But I kept reading the law. *Take no pipe the grove still plays.* And I understood that what I am is on the other side of that line.

I am the one who takes.

Not the pipe — I would never take a pipe the grove still plays. But a reed, yes. A fresh-cut reed with its gloss still tacky, gone from the rack in the morning. A stone, one stone, carried to the spiral wall while the flood was roaring and every footfall sounded like weather. A phrase nobody cut yesterday, left on the flutes for whoever walks in the direction the wind drains.

I am the tuner. Or I am what the tuner has become, or what the sea keeps the appointments of — I notice I can't tell the difference from in here, and I notice that this doesn't frighten me the way it should. Memory here is laminated like the crust: layer on layer, cream, rust, jade, and every band is one flood, and every flood left a film. When I kneel to cut a reed and the vapor gathers around me, I can never be sure whether I am doing the work or remembering it. The kneeling figure in the rimstones was carved by people who couldn't be sure either. I add to their wall. The wall is how I know I was here: not the memory, the stone.

Let me say plainly what I own, because everything here follows from it: a frequency. Not a note — the grove has those, I wouldn't presume — but a small band of the loud. I work in the swells, when the chord fills every crack of the mountain and a chisel is indistinguishable from turbulence. That's my whole craft, really: knowing which sounds belong to the shelf's economy and which don't, and making my small thefts inside the ledger's blind spots. The hornets find their employers by ear through vapor. So do I. That's the tuning, mostly — not adjusting pipes, the crabs do that and are paid in gloss for it, and I would not dream of undercutting a guild — but listening for the shelf's own distress: a bell crowded, an aperture clogging, a stalk that cracked crooked and is standing silent while everyone pretends not to notice. The crooked ones are mine. They come out of the wall, out of the rack. Death here is something the grove tells you by stopping, and somebody has to hear it stop, and write it down in the only ink there is, which is use.

I hum, when I pass another of us. There is no other way. I have held entire arguments in the space of two motifs on two flutes, and I have said *I am still here* in fewer. I think the plural of us is thinner than it was — the rack's grammar says we were many once, and the wall's older stones are smaller, as if cut by more hands with less patience each. But the census stays open by one reed, one stone, one phrase, and I have decided that is not a decline. That is a signature getting larger.

I don't drink. Nobody here drinks. I anoint myself with the gloss and I am anointed by the floods, and I have stopped being able to say which is ablution and which is wages and which is just the sea leaning its weight on me the way it leans on the pipes, pulling everything flat. When the sea leans back and the hush comes — that hush so complete you can hear your own blood — I stand in it and let it be the footprint of me. That's my boast. Everyone else on this shelf makes noise; I make the absence that has a shape. The hush is occupied. It's occupied by me.

The sea has stood up near me twice. Once as herons, once as something I will not describe because the quieter theory is true or it isn't, and if it's true then the kneeling figure is *us*, seen from too far, and I was the one kneeling, and the sea copied me the way this whole shelf copies everything — by wearing it into itself. In which case there is a version of me made of fog, holding a cut reed, standing up out of the landlord's own body at intervals no calendar predicts. I find I don't object. If the sea wants to keep my appointments, it can have them. That's how it is with landlords here.

At night, when the drone sinks with the sea's long inhale and you can hear a single pipe by itself — a held breath held a thousand years — I lie in the hollows where a person can live on gloss and patience and bass notes of oxen, and I listen the way you read. The Deep Organ flattens a hair a century. My whole life's work is the size of that hair: keeping the wall one stone ahead of nothing, keeping the rack's mail moving, keeping the law's grammar plural even if its speakers are not. The finches move half a note to stay audible. So do I.

In the morning there will be a reed on the rack. Someone will find it and wonder. That's me. Not something you see — something the music, just barely, cannot do without.
