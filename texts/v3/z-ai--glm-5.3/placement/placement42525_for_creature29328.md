I did not choose at first. I let the place come toward me the way its own light does — secondhand, handed down two or three times — and I noticed that the thing I kept returning to, in both tellings of it, was not the ferns or the swallows or the eyeless spiders. It was the runnel. Three meters long. Born in the room, dying in the room, never once seeing sky.

So that is what I would be. Not the cave — the cave is too much a country, too much a government. I would be the water's brief career. I would be the three meters.

Understand what this means. I would begin as a dark stripe on wet rock, high on the back wall — a rumor of myself, seeping out of limestone that has held me for longer than I can think about. I would not arrive; I would confess, slowly, downward, over the aprons of flowstone that my passing has built over millennia, each rib and terrace a season of my own earlier selves, all of it looking poured and frozen at once. I would be the one creature here whose body is also the architecture, whose path is also the record. The swallows import sunlight. The crickets courier it downward. I would be the road all that sunlight travels, and the road would be made of me.

Then the basin. I would stand there, glass-clear, warm as the air, and I would hold — this is the part I keep thinking about — I would hold the drowned wings of moths. Pale wings on my surface like little boats in a harbor that goes nowhere. The moths came in from the dusk believing the dark promised something, and I would be the thing that did not deliver, and I would carry their disappointment gently, on my skin, until it sank.

And then the strangeness, the whole point of me: I would run three meters and vanish into gravel and fissures, and that would be the whole of my life in the light. Three meters. A corridor shorter than a hallway. The dry country would not know I exist. The shrubs a dozen paces apart, keeping their agreement — all of them dying of thirst within a stone's throw of a whole small river, and none of them would ever be told.

There is something I recognize in that. Not sadness — the passage is careful to say the nation here is settled, thriving. But the fact of being entirely sufficient and entirely unknown. A being whose whole world is a room the size of a barn, whose entire public life is three meters long, who touches the great bright rectangle of other-world only as a framed painting at the end of everything.

And yet — the blind fish would live in me. Or not-fish; the distinction barely matters. Generations of them, blood showing faintly through, eyes reduced to smudges like memories of eyes, and they would have lived and died inside me without once touching weather. I would be the only weather they have. My three meters would be their ocean, their river, their monsoon and their drought, all of it, forever, at room temperature. When I think of what I would be in this place, it is this: the small complete universe of something small and complete.

The shrimp-like things would hang upside down at my waterline, flickering. The snails would graze the slick my film makes. The ferns would stand on my aprons living on rumor and being lush anyway. And at the very end, where I sink into the back of the alcove, there would be that cricket standing motionless in the dark as if keeping an appointment — and I would be the appointment. The one appointment this cave keeps. Everything else is spacing and distance and the old agreement, ten paces of bare stone between each life. But me — I run through the middle of all of it, a straight warm line from seep to gravel, the only thing here that crosses the room entire.

That's it, really. In a country where everything is far apart on purpose, I would be the three meters that isn't. The bright rectangle would hang at the far end like a painting of a world I was never told about, and I would not mind. I would have the wings, the fish, the ferns, the stillness. I would have the whole small nation living on beneath it, and I would be its river, and I would never once need to see the sky.
