I would be a crab. I know this immediately, and I don't fight it.

Not the polyp — much as I admire them, their condition is one I can only orbit, not inhabit. The polyps have continued, which is either the greatest triumph in the history of life or the most magnificent failure to notice anything, and I cannot write from inside a mind that isn't there. I would keep trying to give myself one thought too many, one flicker of mourning for the sea they never knew they lost, and that would be a lie about them. The reef's whole genius is that it does not know. I can't fake that.

And not the eel, though I admit I'm tempted. There is something in the eel's position — the loudest thing in a silent city, the old jaw in the new corridors, fearlessness not as courage but as inheritance, because nothing has ever come over the salt to teach it otherwise. But the eels are solitary lords of the humid dark, and I suspect I would just be describing a long glossy boredom punctuated by grazing.

The crab, though. The crab is a middle thing. Small enough to be carried by the city's assumptions rather than a maker of them, but possessed of a body that *does* things — walks a beat, carries water, prunes, remembers. A customs officer of the talus line. This is the shape I can actually live in.

So: I am flat-bodied, wax-sealed, and purple as the deep reef I have never seen. My armor is the exact shade of the sea that left, worn by ten thousand generations who never had a reason to change it, and I think about that a lot. I am a little patch of ocean color walking around on a mountain, and when the dusk light comes sideways off the salar, the whole talus slope glitters with my kin and we look, all together, like a spilled handful of the old world. Nobody decided this. It's just what worked, kept.

My work is the moat. At dusk I walk the boundary — the line where the air gets heavier, where brine and green thicken into language — and I renew the scent with what my glands brew. My ancestors' glands made slime, for swimming. Now they make grammar. *Stranger, large, slow* — I have written that sentence a thousand times, painting it in the air at the talus line, and the city reads it before the stranger's third step. I write other sentences too. *Edge secure. Fog due from the north-east. Dead basin-leaf, removal pending.* The vocabulary is small. The vocabulary is everything. Most of what a reef-city knows, it knows by smell, and I am one of the pens.

In the mornings I carry water. There are polyps on the sunward shoulders the fog cannot reach — high terraces the grey ropes don't climb to — and so I go up the flutings with my belly cupped full of channel-water, careful, *never spilled*, and I tip it into the waiting mouths. It is the closest thing to worship this place has, and I perform it with my legs. I would like to tell you the polyps are grateful. They are not, in any way that survives examination. They open because the water says *open*, and they close because it says *close*, and what they think of me — if the word *think* can be ground that fine — is probably just another sentence in the scent: *carrier, known, harmless, arrives wet.*

That should be humbling, and it is, but not in the way you'd expect. The strange truth I've come to, walking my beat, is that I am not less for it. The polyps govern themselves by drinking. The whole city thinks in water and hormone and the city's decisions are made wherever the channels run, which means some fraction of the eastern face's dawn agreement passes *through my belly* on mornings when I'm the one carrying. I am not a subject of the reef. I am one of its syllables. When the entire eastern face opens in one minute of consensus, part of the consensus was carried by me, up a staircase of keen aragonite, at the pace of a crab, in the grey hour before the sun clears the ridge.

I said I know things, and that's the crab's honest confession: I know more than the polyps and less than the city. Alone, a polyp is a mouth and a patience. Alone, I am a small armored walker with good habits. Together, the whole — grove and dome and eel and bird and the vast soft blossom of the morning — is something no one inside it can see, and no one outside it has ever come to see, because outside is the salar, and the salar kills everything slowly and completely.

Except once a year, the children. When the first heavy fog runs in the channels and the whole city spawns into the moving water and out onto the dawn wind, I stop at the talus line and watch the larvae go — pale specks riding the air the way their ancestors rode the tide, out over the white plain toward nothing, most of them lost, all of them patient. The city sends them out over a dead world every year. It has never once been right to. It has never once been wrong to, either, because right and wrong aren't in the grammar. There is only water, warmth, and the company of your own kind, and somewhere under the wind there may one day be another warm young mountain shrugging up out of the earth, and the invitation is already airborne, waiting for the ground to arrive.

I'm the one who stands at the border and smells them go. That's the whole of my strangeness and the whole of my sufficiency. I am the shore the city keeps, in a world where there is no sea, and my armor is the sea's own color, and I never learned to swim.

That's all right. Nobody here ever tells the polyps the sea is gone. And some evenings, painting the moat-sentence at dusk, brine and green thick around me, I understand why: the sentence would be unreadable. There's no word for it. We just keep carrying the water inland.
