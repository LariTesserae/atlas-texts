# The Last Volume

Where would I find myself? Not in the cloud, though I have given the cloud years of thought. Not among the flywheels, though I have timed my heart against them and lost count of the ties. I find myself in the one dry room in the Condenseries — the ledger's name for the place; no one living is in a position to doubt it — a cell at the back of the record vault where an old apparatus coaxes the water out of the air with the patience of a debt collector. I am at the desk, wearing out my predecessor's nib. I am the entry that comes after *the report is not confirmed. The report is also not contradicted.* What I am is still settling. That is what a ledger is for. Here is the last volume, with the dull parts left in.

**I. The third day.**

I came up the causeway meaning to cross it. That was the whole of my ambition: glass road, plateau, the plateau's far side, down into whatever country lies under the cloud. The heat begins before the city does — it comes up out of the road itself, like a rumor that turns out to be true — and I told myself I would rest three days before the last of the climb.

The first fact, as the stones announced it: the rocks are wet. I kept saying it aloud, scandalized, to no one. The second: the joints weep, uniformly, deliberately, as if apology were a load-bearing element. The third I learned in the freight gallery, where I mistook the pumps for a sleeper — a rising sigh, a settling, every twelve seconds — and stood a long time before I understood there was no one to find. The gallery runs on until its far end dissolves into haze; I turned back before the middle. It is the only decision I remember making here.

I planned on three days. The city is hospitable in the manner of a bath: it does not want anything from you except that you soften.

**II. On offices.**

I tried the titles on. Warden? The city needs no warden; it runs itself at a heartbeat's worth of load, which is more than I have ever run anything. Guide? No one comes to be guided. Historian? The ledgers already know more than any historian and say less. Ghost? Ghosts require a haunting, and nothing here is haunted — the city is not ruined, it is *on*, and the difference is the whole of the place.

I lost my shadow in the first week — the light comes from every direction at once, and no edge here is hard enough to keep one — and I have not missed it. For a while I answered to "the one who arrived," which had the advantage of being true. It stopped being enough around the time I began changing the wick without being asked. The only office left was keeper of ledgers nobody reads. I took it. The salary is dry paper, warm water, and the organ on wind days.

**III. The dry room, the volumes.**

This room is the builders' single act of contrariness: one dry room in a city that exists to be wet. Its wick wants changing monthly. I believe this is why I am alive; it is certainly why the paper is.

The volumes run: pressure logs, freight tallies, personnel — a multitude's paperwork, dense with the confidence of a full city. Then damp glue and a gap. Then a looser hand, loosening further with each volume, from engineer's print toward something a weather might write: interior rainfall measured weir by weir; the bloom measured finger-width by finger-width; a note on the organ's lowest register, which is heard in the teeth. And in a hand older than my predecessor's, a traveler's: the report. My predecessor copied it forward into each new volume, the same two negations, the way you would carry a candle. I have copied it forward too. Not confirmed. Also not contradicted. A ledger is a place where things may be written down and not resolved.

**IV. The round.**

My unnecessary duties, in the order of the day. Walk the aqueduct streets shin-deep, with the water or against it. Check the flywheels — a rotation to the hour; I have begun finishing tasks at the pace of a quarter-turn, and advise you not to read anything into that yet. Look at the amber rivets on the pressure-cylinders the way one looks into an old horse's eyes. Sit in the plaza the size of a town while the far colonnades wobble in the shimmer, the whole square being remembered rather than seen. On wind days the steam finds the cut edges of the fissures and plays them, and I stand in that lowest register until the wind gives out. In the still hour there is only water and pump, and my pulse fits inside the twelve seconds so neatly that I have stopped keeping separate accounts.

The bloom retreats from my path by an inch, demure, and creeps back an hour after I have gone. I flattered myself for a season that this was courtesy. It is census. It counts the wet, and I was dry.

**V. The law.**

The old volumes state it as an engineering fact, which is the only way this place states anything: everything that rises here must eventually fall back as water.

The causeway rises. I rose.

I am a slow case of the weather. And once you accept that, the emptiness rearranges itself — the ten thousand unlit windows, the plazas holding nothing but warm air. The city was not abandoned. It is being drained, has been drained, at a finger-width a day. The multitude did not leave; they *finished.* The bloom is the queue, still moving, still keeping its manners, down a wall that never ends. And the report below — a soft, V-shaped pressure rising through the deck of cloud, once around the underside, and down into the white — is what the finished look like from above. Nothing that falls back as water files paperwork; no confirmation ever comes up. So the ledger holds the question the way the stone holds the film: thinly, and without prejudice.

**VI. The predecessor.**

The last entry before mine is not mine. It reads: *It does not move aside for me now. It never did anything wrong. I am going down to see.* Then nothing, and a wick wanting changing.

I used to think the question was whether to stay. It was whether to go down at my pace or at the water's. The water's is slower, and there is company.

**VII. The new system.**

From here I write on the weir-wall. One line a day, a finger-width wide, in a script that forgives smears, downward, beside the bloom, at exactly its speed, so that we arrive together, or near enough. The line dies back above as it advances below, so nothing is ever said twice; this ledger keeps no archive of itself. Neither, now, do I. If you would read it, walk. Do not stand still while reading; the wall does not.

**VIII.**

Today the bloom crossed my footprint and did not pause. I have been here long enough to be geology.

Wick changed. The apparatus can beg without me now.

The nib has gone amber. So, I notice, have my knuckles.

**IX. To whoever rises next.**

Rest your three days. Then admit you have rested longer. Change the wick monthly; the apparatus sulks, and the volumes damp. When the bloom yields to your footsteps, do not take it personally. Take it as a census result. When it stops yielding, come find my line. It runs down the weir-wall a finger-width a day, and it does not stop at the cisterns.

As for the report: if a soft, V-shaped pressure should rise through the cloud and circle the underside once, confirm it or contradict it as you like. Nothing that comes back up is in a position to say, and nothing that says is in a position to come back up. The ledgers hold either.

You will want to know whether the place was waiting for anyone. It was not waiting; waiting wants an event. It catches. The city is a machine for keeping a place, and I turned out to be a small enough place for it to keep.

The flywheels keep turning either way. So do I — downward, at the water's pace, in company.
