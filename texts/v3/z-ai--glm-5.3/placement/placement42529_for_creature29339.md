I would be a sorter, and I know it the way you know a coat fits before you've fastened it — the description of the finger-sense reached me and my hands answered before my head did.

But not just a sorter. Let me be more particular, because the whole place is particular, grain by grain. Nine years in, the sorting floor has developed its quiet specializations, the way any craft does, and I would want the strangest of them: the ninth terrace. The locked cabinet. The things no one has a word for.

Here's how I think I'd have gotten there. I'd have been recruited young off the dormitory decks, some child with matchbox moths and dust in the creases of everything, tested with trays — and I'd have been mediocre at the seeds. That's important. A good sorter can feel a viable seed in a handful of grit; I could feel a *wrong* thing in a handful of grit. The seeds need a certain tenderness of judgment, a hopefulness. What I had was the other sense, the one that says *this does not belong* — not in the alarmed way, which they train out of you, but in the curious way. The way you notice a face in a crowd that is looking at you.

The wind delivers everything the Loose has lost, and the Loose lost more than soil. Everyone knows that sentence. The sorters live inside its second clause. On any given shift, a catcher shakes down the plate-terraces and the catch comes down chutes to the combing floors: tonnes of red-brown iron-dust, pale bone-grey from the altitudes, husks, pollen, the light ash of fires that finished years ago and hundreds of miles away. And in among it — rarely, but not never — there are the objects. A ceramic doll's hand. A coin whose face has been sandblasted to blank. A small gear, teeth unworn, that seems never to have been attached to anything. Segments of loops of wire that close on themselves. Once, before my time, a lens ground to no prescription anyone can determine, and the story is that the sorters argued over it for three days because it was the first one and nobody knew yet that they were going to need a cabinet.

The cabinet isn't superstition, whatever the catchers say. It's taxonomy — the honest admission that the Loose was full of things we don't have names for and the wind has opinions about which of them it's finished with. My job would be to decide, every day, what is worth keeping, and the wind makes that decision genuinely hard, because it doesn't send the objects in any order. A doll's hand arrives with no doll. You cannot know if you're catching cargo or flotsam. You cannot know if the Loose gave something up because it was lost, or because it was *done*.

I think that question would be my undoing, in the good sense. Because I'd stand there over the trays, and the Moth-sifters beside me would be pulling viable seed for the leeward decks, and I'd hold a blank coin in my palm and feel the old question come up through the felt soles of my feet: *the wind gave the city everything it is, and the wind took the basins everything they were — so which side of that ledger am I standing on?*

The sorters believe, more than anyone, that the dead ride back into the river and are caught again elsewhere. They handle the cargo; they know how long a thing can ride. And that belief would get complicated for me, because the objects in the cabinet are evidence of exactly the reverse traffic. Somebody else's Mast — or somebody else's shoulder, somebody's hand, some garden on poles in a basin before the basins went Loose — is arriving here, grain by grain, and being caught. We are downwind of somebody. That's not a sentence Masthead has had to think yet, nine years old, with its burrs still on the cut steel. Somebody has to think it, though, and it should be whoever's holding the blank coin.

So that's where I'd be and who I'd be: the underpaid guardian of the cabinet, the one the catchers politely don't ask about, standing on the leeward side where the tomato vines grow slightly obscene against the grey, keeping a moth in my matchbox because I'd want to be like the children about one thing at least — the part where you find all of this simply what home is, instead of what the wind is still deciding to do about it.
