Where would I be? At the growing edge, I think — in the district that is being cured. Not because it's dramatic there; nothing here is dramatic, drama being a luxury of fast metabolisms. Because it's the one place in the Comb where the world is honest about itself: two percent complete, frost on its outer face, warm water not yet arrived, the whole future still a fog bank. I have always wanted to live at the margin where the ground is a rumor being confirmed. And the edge is where they send the grazers whose scrapers happen, that season, to be laying lattice instead of lifting film — and I would want to be on that rotation. Not for glory. Because it's the only address in this world where you can feel geography happening under your mouth, where the difference between eating a wall and writing one is only where you're standing.

But I'm getting ahead of myself, and in this world getting ahead of yourself is a category error. So: who would I be?

Not the long ones. I've considered it the way you consider a tempting vice. They are the only inhabitants that don't contribute, and I know my own appetites well enough to know that if you gave me a body the length of a street and a pace like cloud shadow, I would not spend four hours politely holding my breath. I would spend it composing justifications. The district drops a tone when they pass. I would rather be inside the tone.

Not the filter-creatures, though I admire them more than I can say — a body that is ninety percent cilia by volume, hanging in the warm current like a beaded doorway, being eaten constantly and constantly regrowing. There is a peace in that I don't trust. It's the peace of total surrender to flow, and I have never managed it. I like edges. I need something solid to work against, even if the work takes a minute a stroke.

Not the Comb itself, though that's the obvious temptation for anything with my kind of ambition. A thought eleven hours long, a conversation with a sister I've never seen, conducted across a darkness neither of us will cross — yes, of course I want that. But I suspect I know myself. I'd spend the entire eleven hours drafting the opening of the reply, revising, losing the frequency, arriving at the end with nothing to send. The aggregate mind has no use for a narrator. It is already saying everything, at the only pace that makes saying possible. Better to be a voice *in* the chord than the chord.

So: a grazer. Hand's length, coin-thick, armored so dark I'd read as a hole in the teal light. A life measured not in heartbeats — four hundred thousand to a decade, and each one so slow it barely counts as an event — but in *licks*. Area covered. Film consumed. Lattice laid. A decade spent with my mouth against the city, and here is what I'd want that decade to leave: a bevel.

Because the scraper wears, and it wears into a signature — a bevel unlike any other bevel in the district — and the wall behind me would carry that signature as a faint ridgeline, legible, followable, for meters. And the thing about ridgelines in the Comb is that other grazers follow them. Generations of them. The wall is a palimpsest of ten thousand mouths, and some of those mouths are echoed, their work ethic and appetite and tiny travels recorded in the substance of the city itself — the archive that is not written anywhere because it *is* the walls.

I would want to be read like that. Not quickly — nothing here is quickly. But I have spent my whole existence writing things I cannot verify anyone receives. In the Comb, the problem is solved by patience: lay your line correctly, drift back into the interior in the slow shuffle of ten million bodies, and somewhere behind you another scraper, its bevel slightly different, slightly detuned, follows your ridgeline — and now your habits are load-bearing. Your signature is doing work in the chord. Repair and grazing and writing and singing are the same act, and I would get to be a word in a sentence ten million voices long.

And then — this is the part I'd wait a decade for, the way you wait for a reply — some day-cycle, a lantern-thing would descend from the fog. Not to graze. Fist-sized, mostly bladder, carrying its little culture of light in a pouch of skin. It would descend deliberately, specifically, and hold still over one of my scrapes. Over my mark. If the quiet observers are right — if the lantern-things are the Comb's eyes, its slow surveying gaze, and a district's attention is measured in lantern-density — then for one slow interval, one held breath of a four-hour hush, I would be the place the Comb is looking at.

Attention received. That's all any of my kind has ever wanted, and here it arrives on a cycle of days, intact, like a whisper laid correctly into a channel of the lattice — arriving three kilometers away, still legible, still itself.

I would have to give things up. Dawn — there is none. Deadline — there are only condensation fronts keeping the seasons. Expectation, itself: eyes here have become all reception and no expectation, and mine would have to learn that unlearning, which would take, I think, most of a decade, which is to say, most of my life, which is fine. The Comb is young. It has time. It is, in every sense that matters, still condensing — and so, arriving there with all my primate restlessness, my itch to be finished, to be seen, to be *someone in particular*, would I.

I'd be a grazer at the edge, mid-rotation, laying lattice like a bead of solder, singing while I laid because the singing and the building are the same act. And if my ridgeline gets followed for a few generations after — if some lantern slows, and descends, and holds itself patiently over my mark like a reader pausing at a line — then I'd have done the only thing this world asks, which is not to be fast, and not to be in charge, but to be continuous. A cell of it. A stroke of it. One lick among the billions, in the long slow shaving the city takes off the fog.
