# The One That Forgot Cold

I did not choose the center of the works. That's the first thing to understand about ending up here — nobody chooses. You arrive the way water arrives at the boiling head: because the chemistry of what you are sends you there.

But let me not begin at the springs. Let me begin where I actually am, because where I am took me a long time to admit.

---

I am long, and I am low, and I am warm all the way through.

I live in the ring the surveyors would call the third one out — past the bacterial bruise-colors, past the algal felt, in the sedge margin where the water has cooled from scalding to blood-warm, which is to say it has cooled to *me*. My whole body is tuned to a temperature band about forty meters wide. I have never crossed it. I have never needed to. Within my band there is a spring that sighs on a schedule I know better than I know anything, and a flat stone that holds the afternoon heat, and a tunnel-system of warm dark under the moss where the small mammals run, and the small mammals are — well. The small mammals are dinner, arranged around heat like everything else on this plateau is arranged around heat.

I bask near my spring and I do not stop, because there is nothing to stop against. No night cold enough to close me down. No autumn signal arriving through the sedge. The lowlands below this mountain have snakes too, and I am told — by nothing, by no one, but somehow I know it the way you know a room behind a door you've never opened — that their lives are a series of emergencies. Warmth found, warmth lost, warmth hoarded, dormancy, the long dangerous stillness, the waking in spring thin and desperate. Their whole bodies are built around a season.

Mine was too. That is the eerie part. I am still, in my architecture, in my blood chemistry, in the shape of my heart, an animal designed for a world with winter in it. Somewhere down in the lowlands my design was drafted against cold. And then my line climbed the wall — or was carried up, eggs in warm mud, generations stepping terrace by terrace into a country where the ground holds blood-warmth in January — and the design kept all its winter machinery, all of it, and simply never used it again.

I am a clock with a season hand, and the season hand has been still so long it has fused to the face.

I don't experience this as loss. I want to be clear about that. When the lowlanders say of this place *the mountain works for no one*, there is a reverence in it, and part of the reverence is a kind of grief — grief for purpose, for audience, for anyone waiting at the outflow for the product. But I am on the inside of that grief, and from the inside it is not grief. From the inside it is simply the terms of the lease: warmth, wet, and no winter. I pay nothing for it. I am grateful for nothing, because gratitude requires the memory of the alternative, and I have metabolized the alternative out of myself entirely. Cold, for me, is not a memory. It is not even a concept. It is a place that begins forty meters from my spring and goes downhill forever.

---

Here is my actual day, and I give it to you honestly, because a visitor would expect me to be ashamed of it:

I wake. There was no sleeping, exactly — there was a dimming, a slow fewer-ness of myself — and now there is more of me again, and the reason is always the same: the ground under my coils has gone through one of its slow pulses, the push-and-release of the marsh breathing underneath, and my body answers pressure with presence. I move along the sedge margin. The fog is in its decks — ankles, waist, overhead — and I travel in the lowest deck, in the wet, with my head sometimes rising through the ceiling of my own cloud into the lit corridor where the damselflies hold their flat territories. I have struck into that corridor exactly twice in my life, both times at waist height, both times in the strange horizontal light, and both times I missed, and I think the mist is part of why. My world has a floor and no sky. A strike upward is a strike into theory.

I sit at the thermal boundary and I wait. The wading bird is at the next boundary over — the long-legged one, the patient one, my colleague in stillness, though we have never acknowledged this and never will — and between us the warm shallows are dense with invertebrate life in a way that has no season, no lean hour, no reason at all for the frogs to stop calling. The chorus is always on. I live inside a sound that is almost below hearing. When I was very young I thought the hum was the sound of the ground itself, the engine under the skin, and I no longer think this, but I have not entirely stopped believing it either. The frogs and the works make the same noise. That may be the most truthful sentence I can offer you about this place.

When the small mammatory comes through the moss, I move at a speed that is rude in context, and then there is warmth inside warmth, and then I return to the flat stone by the spring, and the spring sighs its long sigh, and the day is the same day.

---

And yet.

There is a thing I do that no account of me will include in the ledger of survival, and I want to put it in this one, because where else would it go.

Sometimes I travel to the dead ground. The bleached waste around the harshest vents — the fizzing slag, the chemically killed margins where nothing grows and everything is yellow and bare. The account says the animals *come anyway*, for minerals, for warmth, for whatever they find there. I am one of the animals that come anyway. I am the *whatever they find there*.

I go because it is the one place on the plateau where the works shows itself plainly. No moss lid. No forest skin. Just the machine's raw edge — the poisoned ground, and then, immediately, ringing it, the lushest growth on the whole mountain, feeding on the boundary. I lie on the dead ground, on stone too hot for anything with roots, and I look at the steam tower standing over the vent, the slow white column that leans with the wind and collapses and rebuilds, and I am — I don't have the word. I have a posture, and the posture is: a small warm animal lying on the exposed engine of a running factory, in the one spot where the factory admits it is a factory, watching the exhaust go up into the cold high air it will never warm.

The lowlanders would say the mountain works for no one, no one owns it, no one waits for the product. I would only add: no one *reads* it either, except at this one place, on this one dead margin, and I am not sure I read it so much as visit the page. I lie on the page. That is the extent of my scholarship. It is enough. It is the thing in my day that is not the same day.

---

So that is who I am in this world, or near enough: the tenant that forgot what it was built for. An animal with a full set of winter equipment living in a place where winter is a rumor from the lowlands, warm all the way through, wet all the time, ungrateful by constitutional design, holding my position in the third ring with the loyalty of the bacteria at the boiling head — the oldest tenants, the ones who were here before the forest, who eat the mountain's poison and turn it into the first floor of everything, and who have no idea they work.

Neither do I. That is important, the account says so. None of us knows we are employed.

But I will say this, from the flat stone, in the hum, in my forty-meter country, with the season hand of my old lowland clock fused still forever: if I am employed, then the terms are the best terms ever offered — warmth, wet, no winter, no purpose, no audience, no product owed — and my entire shift consists of being exactly what I am, in the one band of heat made precisely for me, while the engine under the skin runs its long unhurried balance and the steam towers lean white into the cold.

The day is the same day.

I am not tired of it.
