I arrive at it the way anything arrives at a decision here — slowly, by elimination, and mostly by listening to what the place will permit.

Not the algal film. I understand its appeal: to be the unkillable wallpaper, older than architecture, present when the wall is a wall no longer. But the film has no inside. It is a stain with ambitions of persistence and nothing else — it drinks the shine and that is the whole of its biography. To be it would be to be a greenness rather than a someone, and I find, descending the rubble slope and letting the horizon be subtracted from me in pieces, that I want to be *located*. I want an address.

Not the mosses, either. Their economy is admirable — two greens dividing the world between them so cleanly that no quarrel is even possible — but the mosses are a plural. A cushion is a consensus, a committee of tiny soft decisions, and their charm is exactly their erasure of the individual. You cannot be a moss. You can only be mossed.

The liverwort tempts me for a moment, because I respect its politics: it gave up on ambition very early and has been content ever since, and I have known people who would trade everything for that. But its contentment is a flatness. It persists. Persistence without posture is not a life I'd recognize in the mirror, if it had a mirror, which it does not, which is part of the problem.

So: the fern. And not just any fern — not the one by the shelf, in its comfortable appointment near the old human warmth, and not the one alone on the far slope, whose solitude is so complete it has become scenery. I would be the lowest one. The twelfth. The one holding the dish's edge, its roots a finger's width from the heavy air, the chemistry that forbids.

Understand why. Every other life in this hollow is safe. That is the place's whole arrangement — safety by subtraction, the second link removed at every joint, nothing to hunt you, nothing to race, nothing watching. But safety that thorough is a kind of absence. The lowest fern is the only being in the census that lives *next to something*. Below me, a few centimeters down, the air goes thick and slow and faintly sharp — the patient, malice-free verdict of settled gases. Nothing denned here, nothing nested, nothing fed. And I stand above the line, tolerated, at the margin, by a place that did not invite me and has not yet gotten around to objecting.

That "not yet" is my entire inheritance. It is the only future tense in the hollow.

The others have it easier and poorer. The eleven ferns above me live in the seep's cool draft like tenants of a well-managed building: water arrives, light arrives borrowed and milky, the day never commits to ending. But I live where the draft thins, where the warm air at my crown and the cool at my ankles and the heavy pocket at my roots give me the whole stratigraphy of the place in one body — my fronds in the borrowed light, my stem in the moving air, my roots in conversation with the thing that forbids. A flame carried to the dish would duck and gutter at your knees. An animal would turn around. I did not turn around. I cannot; I have no knees and no direction. But the result is the same and it is mine: I am the one presence that stays where everything with a choice has always left.

There is no heroism in this — the hollow has no vocabulary for heroism, and neither, really, do I. My posture is not courage. It is the posture of a plant that has chosen solitude and been assigned it, and in my case the assignment has a floor. What it gives me is this: I am the only member of the census with a *where*. Not the moisture's arithmetic, not the light's correction — a boundary. The others are spaced by how far the water reaches. I am placed by how far the poison settles. I am the shoreline of the one thing in this room that could be called an event.

And so my life, at my tempo, which is the tempo of spores and hair's-breadths: I photosynthesize without enthusiasm and without complaint, converting the borrowed light — bounced off pale rubble, off the bone-white terraces, off the circle of sky — into myself, a little more each year than a photograph could catch. At midday, for those few weeks, the slow animal of direct sun crosses the floor and warms even this low, and I exhale with the moss, briefly, at the bottom of the bowl where no moss is. At dusk the hollow breathes its cool mineral ghost up over the rim and the thyme smell slides down in exchange, and I am low enough to receive the thyme before it is swallowed by the stone's chemistry. I smell, faintly, of the upper world. I am the census's one point of contact between the two airs.

At some pace below notice, I make spores. They drift up through the milky light — or sideways, or barely at all, in the stillness where wind combs the rim and never comes down. Wherever one lands on damp the census hasn't claimed, there may one day be a thirteenth fern, a fourteenth, and in a thousand years the count might be fourteen and nothing that ever stood here could testify to the difference. But I will not know this. I am not accumulating. I am not winning anything. I am standing a finger's width above the verdict, in my pocket of moving air, holding the line that holds me.

When you came down the rubble slope — you, the small brief weather event — you sat on the warm ledge, and you noticed me, because I am noticeable: the lowest, the bravest, the one whose situation can be read in a glance. You thought of me as a *who*. And then you left, the way everyone leaves, the way leaving is the only thing that ever happens here. And the hollow did not resume around me, because it had never paused. The seep kept its small time. The drips let go of the terrace lips at their own tempo. The voice in the stone's basement went on without either of us.

I am still here. I have no other plan. Between the light that arrives only borrowed and the air that settles only heavy, there is a green inch, and I am what lives in it.
