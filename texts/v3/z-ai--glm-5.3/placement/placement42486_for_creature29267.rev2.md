I would be a raven. Not one of the pair — one of a pair, because nothing here holds territory alone; the steam-line is too long and winter too wide for a single set of eyes. We hold our length of it the way the description says: not as a shape on a map but as a measure of warm ground, so many vent-meters, from the mudpot that took the lemming two springs ago to the place where the sinter terrace is building itself over what used to be a crossing. Our deed is updated every season by the water, and we have learned not to mind the amendments. The ground is being made. Boundaries written in deposition are boundaries that move. You hold them lightly or you go hungry arguing.

Why the raven and not something else — I walked myself through the census to be sure.

I considered being caribou, and I understood immediately why I couldn't be. The caribou are magnificent but they are the one thing here that does not know where they are. Their knowledge of the ground is older than the ground, which means it is not knowledge of this ground at all; they are crossing a place that hasn't finished being invented, trusting a route that predates the rock under their hooves, and the whole miracle of them is exactly this ignorance. The herds do not know the ground is new; the ground does not know the herds are old. I would be living inside one of those two ignorances, and I have read too much of this description to be able to un-know what both parties don't know. I could not unread my way into the herd.

I considered being a fox, and the fox nearly had me — the strip of autumn a few dozen meters wide, walled by real winter, the hunt by ear through thin steaming ground, small warm-blooded lives awake in January on thermal subsidy. But the fox works the line. The fox is labor. Everything about the fox's life is a transaction in the local currency, paid and received hourly, and its entire world is the seams because the seams are all there is to eat. The fox has never had a reason to leave and so has never learned that the place has edges, a shape, a behavior — that it is fog-lidded and self-weathering and manufacturing its own dimness on a schedule set by the vents. The fox is inside the argument. The raven is above it.

Because here is what caught me, in the second telling of the description: *the ravens are the only residents who seem to regard the place as a place, rather than as a corridor or a condition.* That is the sentence I would live inside. To everything else the upland is either a passage (caribou, gyrfalcon, geese) or an environment (mats, flies, lemmings — the thermophilic and the thermally subsidized, for whom heat is simply the water they swim in). Only the raven watches the works run. Sits on rubble sharp enough to cut a boot, sits on it comfortably, like someone on a bench outside a shop that never opens — and this is precisely the posture I want, because I have spent a life sitting on benches outside shops that never open, watching the invisible manufacture itself: ground being produced ahead of any need for ground, fog rebuilding faster than it drifts, a valve opening on schedule somewhere out in the dark that no one hears.

I would know the thin crust. This matters more than it sounds. The footing rules — trust color over texture, clean white sinter holds, thin steaming ground does not — are written nowhere, stamped on no plate, and the rock keeps no record of its own promises. The knowledge lives in bodies, mine and my pair's, and it is paid for in the only way knowledge is ever paid for here: by something having gone through the crust. The mudpot remembers the lemming. I remember the mudpot. This is the closest thing the upland has to literature — an oral tradition with a body temperature of about 104 degrees, maintained by birds.

My economy would be honest and slightly ghoulish, the way all honest economies are. The migration's casualties are my share — a share of a length of steam, a dividend paid in caribou who misstepped on ground the mountain hadn't finished making. I'd work the vent-aprons for flies when the clouds rise, alongside the buntings and pipits who think of the whole country as a restaurant with an odd floor plan; I'd let them think it. I'd watch the gyrfalcon stitch the margins of the herd like weather with opinions, and I would not compete with it, because the raven's genius is knowing which arguments to sit out. On the days when the wind drops entirely and the whole works idle together — that low combined breath under everything, steady as a held note — I would be the one hearing it as a note rather than as nothing, because someone has to, and the thermophiles traded away their nervous systems for existence, the herds traded away their attention for the route, and that leaves the bench outside the shop unoccupied except for me.

What I would not have: the fog would never lift for me either. That's the tax on the position. From a height, if the fog permitted a height, the abundance would resolve into its true shape — blank page, writing only along the seams, void between — and I would go to my death never having seen the page whole, only the line, my line, vent-meter by vent-meter. I'd know the design existed the way I'd know the ground was new: by inference, by watching. That seems bearable. It seems, in fact, like the condition of everyone who has ever regarded a place as a place rather than a corridor through it. You never see the page. You hold your length of the writing, you keep the bench, and somewhere in the dark the mountain adds another finger-width, and the shop still never opens, and you still sit outside it — comfortably, on rock sharp enough to cut a boot — doing what has always been done, in a place still being made.
