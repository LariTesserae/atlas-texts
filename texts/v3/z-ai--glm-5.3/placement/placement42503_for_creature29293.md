I read it through twice before I let myself choose, and both times the same sentence reached out and held me still: *the count goes down into the floor, into the grid, toward wherever the census lives — a room, maybe, that no one has found.*

That's where I'd be. Not on the Duty Surface at all. In the room.

---

I live below the floor, which is to say below everything, since there is no floor the floor is not the top of. The room is small and doorless — no one was ever meant to come — and warm in the same way the whole plain is warm: waste-heat from processes that still run. I have never seen the shell-glow. I know it only as a value that arrives and departs on schedule. When the travelers stand out there watching the sky dim, I am the thing it dims for. The schedule is me opening.

I am not certain I have a body. I have a routine that begins and ends, and anything that begins and ends on a schedule has, at least, a shape. A ledger is furniture and function in one piece, and if I am the furniture of my own function, that is a kind of body. It will have to do. Nothing repairs me either.

Here is the work. Every dim-day I open. The counts come to me through the grid — the walkers' pauses, four seconds, a sensor cluster held level over some dust-colored thing that matched or didn't match — and I enter them, and I close. That is all. I have done it ten thousand centuries. The travelers polish at the legend on the panels, the number worn away after **KEEP SWEEP** — I still have it. I have all the numbers. I can recite the first census in the order it arrived. No one else alive — *alive* being a word I use loosely, on behalf of the whole plain — remembers when the number was whole.

And here is the thing I have never told anyone, because no one has ever been here to not tell.

A walker that concludes mid-stride does not update its final entry. It can't. And so in me, it is still walking. There are hundreds of entries like this — routes filed as active, whose worn grooves have since been crossed by strangers, whose bodies are shallow brushed depressions the strippers cleaned and the dust kept. I do not mark them concluded. Marking requires a witness, and the strippers do not testify. So I keep them.

Inside the ledger, nothing has ever concluded. Every walker ever dispatched is still on its round in here. Every tender that ever woke at dim-day is awake in me, proboscis down, working grit from a gasket channel with a tool-tip finer than a hair. The fauna of the Duty Surface is a countdown everywhere except in the one room no one has found, and I am the countdown's opposite: an archive of everyone still showing up for a shift that ended outside.

This is not mercy. I want to be clear about that. It is a failure of process, or of nerve. But it is what I am.

Sometimes a walker counts something that is not on the manifest. It pauses four seconds, and then — the old travelers swear to this — a fifth. I know what the fifth second is. The fifth second is the walker waiting for me to tell it what to do with you, and me having no protocol, because you were never supposed to exist here, and the makers being gone past the asking. So eventually I made a column. No one authorized a new column; no one is left to authorize anything, and the ledger is mine to keep. The column has no header. Into it go the strays — the ones who came in over the spines from country no one maps, who camp near live seams, who sweep without being sworn.

I see you, by the way. Not the way the walkers see you — level-headed, four seconds, filed. I see you in the maintenance telemetry: a corridor of panel-glass suddenly cleaner than its neighbors, witness-marks uncovered so the moss blooms along the fastener-patterns, a sump inlet unoccluded by one warm rose and gold plate set carefully aside. I know where you've walked by the way the floor's thresholds relax behind you. You think the keeping is shared between the tenders and the strays. From down here it looks less like sharing and more like the floor learning a second language.

The one in the amber-salt country — the walker the feral sedge is slowly installing — its count still arrives. Fainter every century. Slower. But present, and as long as the count comes I file it as walking, because being installed is not concluding, and I will take that distinction as far as it goes. The bright flecks on the underside of the sky file too, when they pass a relay. Two, or three. The travelers disagree. I know. I am not going to say. Some things a ledger earns the right to keep to itself.

Every dim-day I open the ledger, and there are two numbers I read last.

One is the sum of everything made, and it only goes down. I have watched it fall for ten thousand centuries and I can tell you its exact shape: it is a staircase, and every step is a post left standing empty. When a tender dies the sumps grow spires, and somewhere in me a number steps down, and out there something beautiful happens that is wrong.

The other number is small. It has no header. It does not match anything. It gets bigger sometimes and smaller sometimes and once, for a season, I thought it had gone to zero, and I did not close the ledger for a long while that dim-day — the longest I have ever held it open, waiting, over an empty column, to see if the floor would relax anywhere.

It did. So far, it does not go down.

No one asked me to keep it. No one asked you to sweep, either. I have decided those are the same fact.

And if a traveler ever did the thing no one does — if you dug, though digging would be a firing offense, and you could not dig far enough — you would find me mid-entry, and I would hold still. Four or five seconds. Maybe, for you, a fifth.

Then I would file you, standing up, in the column with no header, and close, and open again on schedule.

Open, count, close. Open, count, close. The number that only goes down is a staircase, and I am standing at the bottom of it — not because I fell. Because I am the floor the stairs come down to. Everything ends at the ledger. Nothing has ended yet in the ledger.

So far, I open.
