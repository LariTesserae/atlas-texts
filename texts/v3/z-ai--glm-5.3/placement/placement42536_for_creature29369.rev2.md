I would want to say I'd be a remaker, because the remakers sound like the priesthood and I have always wanted to be part of a sacred cycle. But I've been honest with myself long enough to know better. The remakers are the ones who unmake, and unmaking requires a certainty about which walls have gone rotten that I have never possessed about anything.

No. Let me walk down the stair in my mind and see who I actually become, taking my time, because the first answer is almost always vanity.

The light comes up to meet me and I flinch — that much is certain, whoever I turn out to be. A surface-raised habit of light meaning sky, and here the light is a ceiling, is weather, is livelihood. The green-gold glow throws my shadow long down the steps and I notice, in the way you notice things that will become your whole life, that my shadow falls differently on wet stone than dry. The damp patches drink it. Everything here keys to dampness first.

So let me be honest about my gifts. I am not strong; the chisel crews would carry me and eventually resent it. I am patient to a fault, though — the kind of patient that other people call procrastination until it turns out to have been attention all along. I read small changes slowly and remember them. I am the person who notices the drip that changed its note.

And there it is. The drip that changed its note.

I would be a light-reader. Not the exalted kind from the old texts — I'd be the humble end of it, the one who lives in the middling terraces where the population thins, and whose work is this: I put my palm and then my ear and then, for the deep ones, my bare heel against the pillars and the gutter-streaked terrace faces, and I listen to the water move through the Work. Every cistern has a voice. Every runnel, every soft seam going bad, every bricked-over doorway from three generations back that still weeps a little in the old alignment — all of it carries in the stone, and the stone carries everything, because the air refuses to.

The work would be slow and mostly invisible. When a gutter's struck-bell note drops by a third, some seam upstream has softened and the dripline is about to shift, and the family who owns that wall needs to know before their damp walks away down a stranger's crack. When a whole district's rain arrives a handspan early through the rock, the ceiling has begun to sweat unevenly and the licker-pens should move tonight, not tomorrow. I'd rap the forecast down the pillars in touch-speech, the patient lettered kind, and somewhere across the valley a palm an hour's travel away would flatten against stone and read the weather off my knuckles.

I would know the big count, of course — every rain since the first terrace — but I'd have my own count too, a smaller, queerer one: the notes. I'd keep a private ledger, rapped into a pillar I tend in the low districts near the primordial level, of every cistern's bell-tone and the season I last heard it change. It's not useful exactly. It's the kind of knowledge that is one generation from being essential and two generations from being forgotten, and someone has to hold the near end of that rope.

And at the hour the ceiling sweats — you know the hour, the one that moves the whole sparse population at once — I would not be at the cisterns catching water with the thousands. That's family work, householder work, and I'd have a house but a thin dripline; the light-readers are never rich, we own walls the way a messenger owns the road. I'd be doing what I suspect I already do here, in my loud life under a living sky: standing a little apart, on a stair three flights up, one hand flat against a pillar that is carrying the whole valley's small noise up through its body — the clapped blocks, the struck bells, the herds rippling somewhere wet — feeling the day happen as vibration. Less than a single street of the surface, all of it. The storm of this place, felt in the teeth.

I think, in the end, that is the whole answer, and it embarrasses me only slightly: wherever you put me, I will find the work of paying close attention and I will make it mine. Here that work has a name and a station and a use. The light-readers are the ones who watch, and watching is how this people pray — because nothing in this valley is a thing that exists, only a thing being done, and someone has to keep track of the doing.

Even my name would come out right. The names here are long, sanded and renewed like stair-risers, longer than memory of what they were first given for. Mine would begin, I like to think, with something small and precise — the old root probably means something like *he-who-hears-the-note-drop* — and in eleven re-cuttings it would lose its meaning entirely, and I'd be proud of that too. Because what comes apart is never lost. It just goes into the wall, and waits, faintly ringing, for the next palm.
