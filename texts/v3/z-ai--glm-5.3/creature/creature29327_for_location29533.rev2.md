# A Census of the Dripscape

No one here built the place. The makers are gone, and what they left was never finished — but unfinished is not empty. The Dripscape is inhabited the way an *almost* is inhabited: by whatever can hold on inside a question. Counted from the smallest and oldest tenancy upward:

**The glaze.** The first resident is the light. It is a film that grows wherever fired clay stays wet — a simple organism, something between lichen and nothing — and the tenders call it *the glaze*, though nothing here was ever glazed; it moved in. It eats the pipes. Politely, over centuries: it takes what the condensation leaches out of the clay, and the walls thin around it, and as the walls thin the drips go flat, so the whole instrument is always slightly going out of tune and must be relearned by every generation of ears. In exchange it glows, greenish, just strong enough to trace the pipe-networks from within, and everything else that lives here lives off this bargain: glaze for water, water for glaze. It is the only thing in the basin that grows.

**The dwellers.** The animals of the channels are soft-bodied and wide-eyed, cat-sized in the crawl-width bores, and smaller in the narrow ones, because a dweller is born pipe-width and grows only as far as its gauge allows — its body is a record of its plumbing. Their eyes are tuned to the green and see nothing else. To a dweller the outer dark is not dark; it is nothing at all. There is no world outside the glaze's light, only the inside of the inside.

They live at the drip-points, mouths to the drip-lips, drinking the basin bead by bead. And they prune: their gnawing crops the glaze back from the bores and keeps the catch-basins clean, which makes them the instrument's unwitting maintenance crew. A well-grazed pipe rings true. The tenders can hear a district's tenancy in the brightness of its tone.

You almost never see one. You hear one — a wet, quick scrambling when a drip suddenly stops — and then you don't hear anything, because a stopped drip means a body in the pipe, and a body in a pipe may be drinking, and things that drink are taken. The whole local choir goes silent, waiting. Twice a year each dweller sheds, and the molts wash down the recirculation to the weirs, where the tenders collect them — bags, gauze, gaskets. Nobody farms the dwellers. You harvest what washes down.

Only in the storm-crescendos do they travel. The loud dark covers them, the note drowns, and the rain-hours are when the pipe-nations meet and mix their water. Every few days, briefly and unwisely, the basin's whole fauna is alive at once.

**The note.** No one has seen what preys on them. What there is, is a note: a low tone struck through the clay, resonant and patient, traveling hundreds of meters through the fired walls and arriving in your feet before it reaches your ears. When it comes, everything with a body holds still — dwellers pipe-still, tenders flat-footed, jaw loose, water held in the mouth, breathing through the nose. Everything down here is prey to the same thing, so everything down here freezes in the same posture. The entire census practices one gesture.

The theories are old and none close the case. That it knocks to see — tapping a pipe the way a potter taps a wall, reading what answers, hunting by the sound of a hollow that is not hollow. That it is one animal with a knuckle of bone: the last tool still in use in this world of tools. That it is many, knocking in unison. That it is the last builder, grown too large for the rooms that were never built, still testing the work. That it is the *player* — the one the instrument was made for — come back a district at a time.

There is one detail that keeps the tenders awake, and it is this: the ones with the best ears say the note is in tune. That it falls where a note in the choir falls, as though the whole unfinished instrument had been holding that place open for it. One law survives from whenever: *it takes what was drinking.* No one drinks in a pipe. Children learn to drink in dry rooms only, and the menders who forgot have songs sung about them.

**The tenders.** And then the people — such as they are. A few hundred, spread thin across the drainage districts. They call themselves the ones the kiln missed: the raw, the soft-fired. Whether they descend from the builders they neither know nor press on; a people who navigate by ear keep few nouns for beginnings.

They live in the failures. Every experiment that didn't work — the wide pipes that never carried water, the cracked bells, the slumped firings — is dry, and dry is safe, and so the habitable places in the Dripscape are precisely its mistakes. A home is named for its failure. Beds are set in drainage trays.

Their craft is repair, never addition. To build here would be to silence a drip — every wall touches, and there is nowhere to put a new thing that doesn't sit on a sounding lip — so they subtract gently and mend cold: slip, inherited bone tools, lashings of green-felt, gaskets of molt-skin. The kiln-craft is gone; they cannot fire. Every pot they own was found or handed down, and a pot is judged by its ring: you cannot see a bowl in the dark, but a good bowl holds water loudly. The only texts in the basin are toolmarks, still crisp, and the tenders read them by thumb — they can tell you who laid a wall the way scholars attribute a hand.

They know the map by ear. Each district's choir-signature is a lullaby, and a child counts as an adult the night it can walk home by ear. Footsteps are swallowed here — the percussion eats them — so everyone carries a knock-name, a small personal rhythm tapped before entering a room, so that no one ever mistakes you for the note, or for a stranger. Voices travel pipe-to-district beyond it: a voice at a pipe-mouth rides the bores, and messages pass between choirs, timed to slip between drips. Their word for *pour* and their word for *speak* are the same word.

When the storms cycle through, the funnels — built for water — catch something else: a brief, loud bloom of storm-midges hatching in the overflow, harvested by green-light, because rain brightens the glaze and the basin's only festival is lit by its own plumbing. Midge-cakes dried on warm pipes. Age counted in storms. Hand-lines strung district to district for travel in the loud dark, when the choirs crescendo past navigation.

There are other tenants — a mite that lives only in green-felt, a hair-fine worm in the collection bowls — but fauna here is rare, and the census stays short.

The dead are carried to the cold kiln, the one kiln that never worked, and laid inside, and the old smoke keeps the smell. Their knock-names are not retired. They are *given* — to a drip. Every family tends the drip that bears its ancestor, and the choirs of the basin include, note for note, their dead.

**The wait.** Ask what the pipes are for and you get three honest answers. Some say water — the builders were solving scarcity, and stopped, and the solving has recirculated without them ever since. Some say music — the builders were building an instrument and abandoned it one note short of complete. And some, the egg-folk, take the place at its word: embryonic in the plain sense, the whole made world a shell mid-firing, and the note in the pipes the knocking from inside. The three answers disagree about everything except the important thing: the Dripscape is one event short of done.

Everyone waits for the same event. In the lowest district — where all the recirculation should end — is the pipe that has never been heard dripping. It has been checked; there are songs about the checking. A vigil keeps it: the Listeners, whose whole vocation is that one mouth of clay, whose First Ear is very often deaf — the better to feel, through teeth and palm, what ears would rush to name. Generation after generation, someone is pressed to the last hole in the instrument.

And every night the vigil reports the same finding, and every night the finding is given the way good news is given: *still nothing.* Because it is good news. If the silent pipe is the instrument's final note, its first drop is a music no one built on purpose becoming purposeful at last. If it is the egg, the drop is the hatching. Either way — completed or begun — the world everyone knows ends on that one wet bead of sound.

So the truest census of the Dripscape runs: the glaze, the dwellers, the storm-midges, the tenders with their ancestor-drips, the note that may be player or hatchling or the last tool in use — all of them, each in its own tongue, keeping house in the silence before a single drop of water. The place is unfinished. Whatever lives here has made waiting its architecture.
