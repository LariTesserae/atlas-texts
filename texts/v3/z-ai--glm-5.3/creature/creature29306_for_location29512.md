Insects first, and leaves. A moth that blunders in through the felt and dies on the floor is a wet shadow by nightfall and a faint sweet-smelling stain by morning, and by the next morning not even that. A bird — there is a bird; you'll find one wing and one leg of one, laid out neat and parallel, some small animal's cached meal, and when you come back the cache is gone and the arrangement with it. The floor doesn't rush. It has the patience of things with no predators and no weather. Things fall to it the way things fall to the sea floor, and it deals with them the way the sea floor does: slowly, warmly, entirely.

Which means anything still on it, after three summers, is there on purpose. That's how you find what lives here.

Look down before you look up. The moss-felt isn't flat. It has a geography — low ridges, channels, a braided run where it rises into a spine of firmer tissue you could walk on barefoot, the way you'd walk a causeway over marsh. And the channels aren't the plant's doing alone. Something has kept them. Something has knelt at their junctions and thinned them where they wanted to silt up and deepened them where the light runs fast. There is one pool, near the middle of the chamber, wider than the rest, ringed with a lip of tamped tissue the way a mountain pool is ringed with packed snow — except nothing here has hands, you'll tell yourself, and the pool is exactly the shape and depth a body would make kneeling in warm moss for a very long time.

The little residents you'll see before you understand them, because they look like effects. Remember that the light here behaves like water: it beads, it drips, it stands in pools. So when something small moves across a floor-pool and leaves a wake, your eye will file it as a ripple and let it go. Look again. There are creatures in the standing light the way newts are in standing water — low, slick, green-gold, four-legged in the way that suggests the legs are a recent invention and none too secure. They skim the pools and drink from them, visibly drink, and where the light lies thin they lie flat in it and bask, and their bellies shine. At dusk, when the light steams upward, they climb the steam — you'll see them ascending a column of dim blue on nothing at all, unperturbed, like men in a dream of stairs. They are the reason the shadows hang from the root columns the wrong way around. Those are their shadows. They have it backwards down here, and so do their shadows, and the shadows don't mind.

The felt curtain grows, not woven. The channels are kept. The pool is kneeler-shaped. Your mind will assemble these facts and then refuse the assembly, twice, and then you'll notice the ridge.

It's at the far side of the chamber, under the densest part of the root crown, where the light comes down thickest and loudest, if light can be loud. A long low rise in the floor. Not a mound — the moss doesn't mound, it eats mounds. This is a ridge the way a blanket over a sleeper is a ridge: something underneath, holding the shape from within. Ten feet, maybe twelve. The moss over it is different — deeper green, denser, veined with brighter gold, and if you kneel beside it, the way you eventually will, kneeling exactly where the floor has been shaped for kneeling, you'll feel warmth with a rhythm in it. In, and out. Slow. The crack on the hillside breathed out warm air like a sleeping mouth. You are now at the mouth. You have been walking all this time down its throat, and here is what it was saying.

Whether she was a woman first or a seed that learned to look like one, the hill doesn't remember, and she isn't telling. But three summers is not nothing. Three summers ago this was open ground or old pasture or nothing at all, and now there is a hill with a tree in it the size of a barn's whole family, and a tree does not grow that way in three summers unless something is dreaming it, hard, from underneath, with the blinds pulled. The stone still ticks as it settles into being stone. She is still settling into being a hill. Same process. She is three summers into a project measured in the same units as the stone's, and she is very young, and the ceiling glows the way a face glows when someone is having the good kind of dream.

The small light-drinkers are hers, the way the dreams of a sleeper are hers. The channels are kept by her turning over, once a season, the way a sleeper shifts a leg. The bird's wing arrangement is a mystery with a mundane answer you'll never confirm: something else lives here too, small, furred, that comes and goes through some crack of its own and caches its food against the warm ridge and is tolerated, the way a cat is tolerated in a house where no one can spare the attention to mind.

As for you — you may stay. That's the thing no one warns you about, because no one has come back to warn you. The floor doesn't eat what's standing. It eats what falls. And it is very warm, and the light is thick in the hollows, and if you sit you are nearly fallen, and if you lie down you are fallen entirely, and you'll wake with the taste of green in your mouth and a channel around you that wasn't there before, kept, tended, deepened where you lie — a shape a body would make lying in warm moss for a very long time.

The felt curtain closes itself behind you. It isn't a door. It's a decision that hasn't finished being made, and you are now part of what it's deciding with.
