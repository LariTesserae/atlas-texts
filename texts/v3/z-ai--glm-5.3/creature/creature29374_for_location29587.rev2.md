# What Lives Here

Nobody lives in the Stillwater Works, in the sense you mean. But then, nobody lives in a coral reef either, if by "nobody" you mean persons, and look how occupied it is.

Here is the census, then, taken patiently, the way one takes a census of a place where every term of office is a century:

## The Pumps

The ram pumps are the closest thing to citizens. There are twelve of them — the text says a dozen, and a dozen is what they are, with the settled completeness of a jury — and they have been deliberating for four hundred years or so, one *chunk* every two seconds, which works out to something under forty million strokes each, and counting. They are not machines in the way a car in a lot is a machine, cold and waiting. They are machines in the way a heart is. They have never been off. They do not know they can be off. The concept has never been introduced to them, and so they hold the district's time the way an old man's rocking chair holds an afternoon.

They nod. That is the honest word for it. The slow bow of the arm, the patient recovery. If you stood among them long enough — months, years — you would begin to feel that the nodding is attentive, that each pump has opinions: this one metronomic and dutiful, this one with a hitch in its rhythm that it has had so long the hitch is now its signature, its gait, the thing the other pumps would name it by. *That's Lame-Jack, down in the lower gallery. He's been a quarter-beat off since the flood of whenever, and he'll be a quarter-beat off when the roots come through his packing gland, and he won't mind.*

They live on the patience of falling water, which is to say they live on nothing anyone has to give them. Their food arrives. Their work is their pleasure. There are worse arrangements; most of us are under worse arrangements.

## The Landlord

The great fig — the tree that split the flue it was born in and now stands over its ruined chimney like a proprietor over the remains of the business that first employed him — is the other resident, and the senior one, in terms of ambition. Roots down the pump-house wall like rigging: the metaphor is exact, because that is what they are. It is a tree that looked at a building and decided the building was a trellis, and three hundred years later it is not wrong.

The fig's relationship to the pumps is the relationship of a landlord to long-term tenants: entangled, structural, probably affectionate in the way of things that hold each other up. Its roots have already begun, here and there, to take over a joint, a collar, a place where a gasket gave out in the year nobody remembers. And this is the deepest fact of the district's life, the fact that visitors arrive at slowly and never quite recover from: the tree is *staff*. It is the maintenance crew. It is the successor institution. The schedule of care for the Stillwater Works was never interrupted — it was only handed, somewhere back in the fog of years, from people to plants, and the plants have accepted the position, and there was no ceremony, and none was needed.

The fig, if it thinks, probably thinks it owns the place. And in every sense that matters to a fig, it does.

## The Green Retinue

Under and around the fig, the rest of the vegetation: liverworts over moss over algae, ferns to their waists in the flooded gallery in the wet months, ferns silvered and sulking in the dry. These are the crowd, the tenantry, the parish. They are not individuals in any way a visitor can learn; they are a *condition* of the district, the way weather is a condition of a coast. They dam their own gutters and drown their own dams and grow again, and this is not, as it first appears, tragedy or comedy. It is simply their liturgical calendar. Moss that dams its own gutter and drowns has not failed; it has kept a feast.

## The Standing Water

And then there is the water, which is everywhere, and warm, and utterly sterile, and this is the thing that even the most prepared visitors cannot be told in advance — the water is the district's one genuine spookiness, its one refusal to be cozy.

Water without mosquitoes. Water without midges, without the blind little swimmers, without anything. You can look into the flooded tramway, warm to the hip with duckweed, held like a hand, and understand that no eye has ever looked back from under that surface. Not ever. It is water that has opted out of biology entirely — water that has decided to be mineral, the description says, and it is right. The standing water is the district's one inhabited vacancy, its one room kept deliberately empty. Perhaps that is what it is *for*: every household, every long-settled one, keeps one room like that, and doesn't discuss it.

You can sink into it and be held, warm, un-bitten, un-investigated by anything alive. It is the most sterile embrace available on this earth, and it is not nothing. Some people come back for exactly that.

## The Character of the Whole

So: who lives here?

A jury of nodding pumps, working a case that will never close and was never really a case. A landlord fig whose business plan spans centuries and whose rent is paid in shade and roots. A green congregation keeping a calendar of drowns and recoveries. Water that has taken a vow of mineral silence, and water falling everywhere else in a continuous silver applause.

And that is all. No animal at all — not because this is a dead place, but because it is a place that has gotten past the need for them. Everything alive here is either very slow or very repetitive or both, and all of it is in the middle of a single long task: being continued. Not preserved — preserved is what you do to the dead. Continued, which is what you do to yourself.

Stand still long enough and the chord gets into your sternum, and you understand the local law, the unwritten one, the true constitution of the Works: the place is not abandoned, it is merely on a maintenance schedule longer than yours. Somewhere in its patient arithmetic, a pump arm is scheduled to be replaced by a root, four hundred years from next Tuesday, and the pump will not notice the difference, and neither, in any way that matters, will the district.

You will leave eventually. The Works will not mark your leaving. It does not mark arrivals either. But it is not indifferent to you, precisely — it is just that its hospitality runs on a clock you cannot stay for, and it has already, warmly, blood-warmly, made room for the day when what replaces your visit is a fig root, and considers that a perfectly good use of the guest chamber.
