# The Inhabitants

## The Consortium of Hands

The workers — never more than four or five at once, and never the same four or five for long — have no name for themselves. They don't need one. Names are for calling across distances, and distance here belongs to the water.

They are called, by the surface departments that technically employ them, *reservoir attendants*, which captures nothing. What they actually are is harder to say. They arrive through a maintenance hatch in shifts that have drifted from their posted schedules over the years into something organic, tidal, aligned not with clocks but with the cistern's own pulse-cycles. Some of them have worked here so long they've stopped thinking of the surface as home. The city above is a place they visit, a loud bright room they return from.

Their language has evolved to fit the acoustics. In the roar, a spoken word lasts a quarter-second before the machinery eats it, so the workers speak in contact: a flat palm on the sternum means *stop*. A grip on the forearm, held, means *come look at this*. A fingertip drawn slowly down the spine of the shoulder blade means *something's wrong, be quiet about it*. There are perhaps two hundred of these signs, and three of them exist only for communicating about parts of the system that haven't malfunctioned in living memory — vocabulary waiting, like the machinery, for the day it's needed.

Their hearing has changed. Not deafened — *specialized*. After enough months, a worker can distinguish a failing regulator gate from a healthy one by the grain of its hiss, the way a doctor hears a wet lung. They can feel a pressure anomaly through the floor before the floors' own sensors compensate for it. Some claim they've learned to read the subsonic aquifer-breath, and that it has moods. None of them will say more than that. There's a gesture for the subject: two fingers, then a shrug.

## The Pale Film

The biofilm is the closest thing the cistern has to a native. It grows only where water escapes its plumbing — the weeping joints, the sweating ceiling, the worn basin — as if proximity to *managed* water were fatal and only leaked water, water that has left the system, were habitable. It is faintly luminous, a cold gray-green shimmer, and the workers use patches of it as landmarks. They have individual names for the larger colonies, handed down from attendant to attendant like saints: *The Nave. The Palm. Long Sorrow.* No one remembers why Long Sorrow is called that. The patch has been there longer than anyone.

It exhales ozone, or something adjacent to ozone, and it is warm to the touch in a way that has never been explained to anyone's satisfaction. Whether it is one organism spread across the whole cistern or many, no one has sampled it, because no one has thought to, because the gesture for the subject is the shrug.

## The Fauna, Such As It Is

The blind crustaceans in the condensation gutters are, by any census, the entire animal kingdom of the cistern. They are the size of a fingernail, pale as the film they graze on, and their lives run on a schedule the workers find enviable: drift the warm wet edge, eat the minerals, avoid the bead-lines when the ceiling drips. When a hydraulic pulse shakes the floor, the whole gutter population freezes for the duration and then resumes, seamlessly, as if the pulse were a rest note written into their music.

They have no predators. Nothing down here is hunting. The workers protect them from nothing and simply *count* them, occasionally, as a pastime — a soft touch on the gutter's edge, a slow tally. The numbers stay strangely stable. This is either reassuring or not.

## The Thing in the Channel

And then there is the other.

The splash. The one that came from a channel that holds nothing but flow, and came with a weight and displacement that suggested mass, and suggested also — this is the part the workers don't put into gesture — *grace*. Something that entered the water cleanly and chose to.

It has been heard three times in the last decade. Each time, the workers near the sound turned, looked at one another in the biofilm's faint light, and made the sign: two fingers, then a shrug. *Acknowledged, not investigated.*

But the shrug has developed a private meaning, different from the official one. The two fingers mean *I heard it too — we heard it together*. The shrug means *and we will not go and see, because we have decided, together, without ever discussing it, that the cistern holds exactly as much as it can be asked to hold*. The channels are deep and the channels are warm and the water has been passing through this place for a hundred years, and it is at least possible that something has been passing through with it — something that finds the pulse-cycles restful, the way the workers do, something that has learned the schedule, the way everything here eventually learns the schedule.

If you ask an attendant about it, they will give you the gesture. If you press, they will give you the gesture again, slower, as if to a child.

## The Deep Maintenance

And below all of this — below the gutters, the film, the oldest strata of pipe — there may be machinery no living being has ever looked at, still performing its function with perfect patience.

These, too, live here. The distinction between inhabitant and infrastructure dissolved in this place a long time ago. The self-repairing seals, the routing water, the unlocated logs, the buried systems waiting under their own successors: all of it is the cistern's population, and the workers know it, which is why their care for the place feels less like employment and more like attendance — the way one attends a patient, or a sleeper.

The cistern is full of a sound like a sleeping giant's circulation. The workers are what the giant dreams. The crustaceans are what it doesn't bother to dream. And the splash in the channel, uninvestigated, is what it almost remembers.
