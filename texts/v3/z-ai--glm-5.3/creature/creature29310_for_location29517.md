What lives here is, first, the Stand itself — but the Stand is not alone, and nothing that lives here is alone either.

**The notch-folk** are the ones you'll meet, if you meet anyone. They are humanoid the way a hermit crab is a crab: recognizably so, but shaped around an inherited architecture that isn't theirs. They live in the culm-towers, in the notches — the crescent openings, some natural, some cut generations ago, that give the towers their ribbed, mouth-like flanks. A notch-folk family holds a vertical shaft of tower the way another people might hold a river valley: this notch to that one, and the culm's hollow heart between, climbed by peg and groove for forty stories.

They are small, wiry, wind-burnt, patient in the way of people who have never known solitude — how could they, when the ground itself hums with the Stand's one long chord, and every neighbor's footstep comes up through the rhizome as a faint change in the note? Their hearing is strange: they read the Stand's hum the way you read weather, and they know you are coming days before you arrive. They say the hum tells them when a tower is dying, when the Chaff-fires are moving, when someone three hundred kilometers east has felled a culm for a bridge. They may be right. Scholars who climbed the rim to test this went half-mad from the chord before they got results.

Their economy is the Stand's economy. They eat its young shoots, its seed-years, the fat grubs that live in its joints, the birds that nest in its notches. They wear fiber spun from the Chaff and lacquered plates of culm-skin. They do not farm the Stand, exactly — you cannot farm a continent — but they tend it: clearing choked rhizome-eyes, coaxing new towers up where old ones have fallen, negotiating, always negotiating, over who may cut what. Every cut culm is recorded. Every cut culm is mourned, a little, in the low psalm-voices they use for formal grief.

Because here is the thing about the notch-folk: they are divided, and they know it, on the oldest question they have. To some, the Stand is a god — the God, the only one, whose body they live inside like tenants in a sleeping giant. These are the majority, and their liturgy is the chord itself; they hold silence to be a kind of blasphemy, and at the solstices the towers are opened at the crown so the wind can play them and the whole plateau becomes one instrument for nine days. To the others — the minority, the heretics, who live mostly in the far thin places where the Stand gets sparse and the hum gets lonely — the Stand is not a god but a corpse, or something stranger: a single being that never finished dying, that flowers once a century across the whole plateau at once and then goes back to sleep, and that will, one year, finish what it started. These folk listen to the chord not as liturgy but as a pulse, counting it, timing it, waiting for the change of key that will mean the end.

They may all be wrong. The Stand does not tell.

**The other lives are the Stand's lives too.** The notch-birds, swifts the color of the jade culms, that live nowhere else because nowhere else has half-mile cliffs of living bamboo. The joint-grubs, fist-sized and blind, that bore the crescent notches themselves — the notch-folk's architecture is, half of it, grub-architecture, and there is an unkillable tradition that the folk and the grubs came to an understanding long ago. The Chaff-walkers: long-legged, low-bodied things like wolves built by a wind, that patrol the litter plains in matriarch packs, listening for the towers' small voices the way the folk listen for the great one. The resin-bees, whose hives hang in the culm-hollows like amber lanterns and whose wax is the notch-folk's only currency that never rots.

And under everything, the rhizome-tenders — pale, mole-slow, almost never seen, that the folk call the Stand's dreams: whether animal, fungus, or the Stand's own wandering tissue, no one, including the folk, is entirely sure. They are the reason no one digs below a certain depth. They are the reason the deepest notches are sealed. The folk do not talk about them, but every tower has its sealed notches, and every child is taught, before it is taught anything else, what to do if the floor of the shaft begins to hum wrong.

That is who lives in the Stand: a people living in the body of the thing that also feeds them, houses them, sings to them, and never once — in all the generations of their songs — has answered them back.
