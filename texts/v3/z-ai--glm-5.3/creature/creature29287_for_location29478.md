No one lives here. That is the first fact of the place, and it takes the longest to believe, because the place is crowded almost past counting.

On the surface, the unit of life is the individual — one fox, one hawk, one self moving through a day that belongs to it. Here the unit is the crowd. The bats are a number with no useful scale attached; you do not count rain. The beetles are not a population on the floor, they are the floor. The pools hold nations. Even the stars in the return galleries are not individuals but a constellation, a device. Everything alive in this mountain has been dissolved into something larger than itself, and the mountain is full of life precisely because it contains no one.

The animal that most fully lives here, then, is assembled from millions of parts, and it hangs from the ceiling. Its skin is shingled fur. Its voice is surf. It has a heartbeat once a day. For one hour at dusk it becomes, briefly, one thing instead of a texture — a braided column pouring from the pits, visible from the bone-colored scrub above, audible as wind through a narrow place — and then the night dissolves it back into its millions, each small hot body burning fuel like a shrew of the air, each one remapping the dark in screams too fine for your ear. At dawn it reassembles, and the vault's roar climbs from surf to storm and settles. This animal is also the cave's only port. The mountain's entire economy imports through it. Everything the dark eats, the bats carried in from the sky, and the guano dunes below are the harbor where the cargo piles up and is never, for one second, allowed to stay.
