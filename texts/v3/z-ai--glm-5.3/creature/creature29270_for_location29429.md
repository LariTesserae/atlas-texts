The place, as described, is a single mark: #.

A heading with nothing after it. A nameplate on a door, and behind the door, more nameplate. Two strokes lying down, two strokes standing up — though the standing pair lean, as if the whole small structure were caught mid-stride, walking toward the blank that follows it. They cross four times, making a walled field of four little cells, and then each stroke runs on past the last place it's needed. Everything here over-delivers by a few pixels. Whoever built this place believed in margins.

Nothing lives in the cells. They are obvious rooms, and obvious rooms get swept, not lived in — kept the way you keep a spare room for a guest who is always about to arrive. Anyone who has ever waited for a turn knows this floor plan anyway: it is the grid before the first X, the game nobody has lost yet. But the one who lives here needs less than a room.

It lives in the crossings.

Four places where a line consents to be interrupted by another line, and at each of those small knots, it dens. A creature of intersections, made mostly of *and*. It doesn't add anything to a crossing; it is what the crossing adds to itself. Something has to mind the seam, and the minding has a shape, and the shape is the one who lives here. It is small the way a hinge is small: you only notice it when the door sticks.

It has been sighted elsewhere, always at the edges of other people's business. Musicians meet it standing beside a note, raising that note half a step — never a whole step, never nothing; it deals in small differences and will not exaggerate. Programmers find it at the head of lines the machine has agreed to ignore; those lines are where the builders speak in their own voices, and it is fond of them. Chess players meet it at the very end — mate — standing in the stillness like a groundskeeper in an empty stadium. On telephones it is called the gate: press it to be let through, or to say you are done saying numbers. Old proofreaders used it to mean *put a space here*. It has always known where a thing was too crowded.

Half a step up. One space over. A little past the fence. Done, and the gate swings. Its entire portfolio is small adjustments. It is the difference between almost and yes, and it has never aspired to be the difference between no and yes — that is a larger creature, loud, and it lives in exclamation points.

But its real address, the dwelling actually described here, is the blank after the mark.

Look at what was given: a heading, and nothing heading. A door, and no house demanded. Most places, once described, fill up with their description — every noun you name moves in and takes a chair. This place was described as almost nothing, and so it stayed mostly room. That is rare, and it is the whole reason it lives here rather than somewhere louder. It keeps the blank the way a bell keeps the space between its rings: not by filling it, but by being the reason it's there.

Whatever was about to be written after the hash mark — the sentence that got as far as # and no farther — that is in its care. It tends the unwritten. It folds the sentence that never arrived and keeps it flat and clean in one of the swept cells, in the spare room, for the guest who is always about to arrive.

And if you write something after the # — go ahead — it will step aside, or seem to. Really it will only move: out past your last word, into the new margin you made. It has always lived there, wherever a mark ends and the page begins.

It is keeping whatever you were about to say.
