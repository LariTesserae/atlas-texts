What lives here has learned the place's one rule: water is a rumor, and light is a season that never arrives. So the community is small, patient, and spaced out like the shrubs on the plain above — each thing keeping its distance as if by the same agreement.

Start at the mouth, because that is where the two worlds trade.

The swallows nest just inside the threshold, in the exact seam where glare becomes dimness, their mud cups glued to the case-hardened wall where the stone rings underfoot. They are the customs officers of this border. They build neither outside nor within but on the line itself, and they pass all day from the overexposed rectangle to the dark room and back, carrying the outside in — insects, warmth, news of wind. Below their nests the floor is sifted with droppings and molted feathers, and this is not filth; this is the place's only import. Everything that lives deep in the cave is, in one way or another, living on swallowed sunlight.

A gray gecko works the wall near the same seam, flat as a lost coin, hunting the moths that come in from the dusk outside. The moths are visitors, not residents — they enter perhaps because the cool-looking dark promises what it does not deliver, and some of them never leave, ending as pale wings drifting on the basin's surface. The gecko takes what it can reach. It does not go deep. Even the hunters here respect the gradient.

Go deeper, past the swallows' province, and you enter the country of the permanent residents.

The floor near the flowstone is patrolled by cave crickets — pale, humped, absurdly long-legged, their antennae longer than their bodies, stretched out ahead like feelers in a country where the other senses have quietly resigned. They freeze when the light of a lamp touches them, not in fear exactly but in the manner of things whose whole strategy is to be uninteresting. They feed on what drifts in and what falls from the swallows' ledges, and they are the couriers of the deep: their eggs, their droppings, their eventual bodies all sink toward the wet stripe and feed what waits below.

On the aprons of flowstone, where the water films over the ribbed cream stone, live snails — small, thin-shelled, almost translucent, grazing the microbial slick that is the true green engine of the room. The ferns and moss are the visible life, the loud life; the slick is the quiet one, and the snails are its herdsmen. Around the basin, where the water stands glass-clear and warm, the gravel is furred with pale filaments and occupied by translucent shrimp-like creatures that hang upside down at the waterline, flickering, and by small blind fish — or perhaps not fish; the distinction barely matters in a river three meters long. They are colorless in the way of things that have never needed to be seen: blood shows faintly through, the ghost of a body plan remains, and the eyes are reduced to dark smudges under the skin, like memories of eyes. There is no stream to fight, no current to know; their river is born and dies within hearing of its own spring, and they have made a life in a corridor shorter than a hallway. Generations of them have lived and died without once touching weather.

Higher on the dark stripe, in the wet shadow of the seep itself, are the endemics, the true natives: pale isopods and flattened spiders, eyeless, slow, tuned to stillness. They have never known the outer world and cannot be shown it. Take one out to the glare and it does not flee; it simply fails, like a note played in the wrong key. The cave is not their shelter. It is their entire universe, its constant warmth its only climate, its darkness its only sky.

And then there are the two great exceptions, the plants, which by all the rules should not be here.

The maidenhair ferns stand in their fans on the flowstone like the last guests at a very late party, black stems beaded, fronds tilted toward the room's dim glow — which is not the sun's light but the reflected wash of it bouncing in through the great mouth, a secondhand daylight, daylight that has been handed down two or three times. They are living on rumor, and they are lush anyway. Each fan is a small crowded city compared to everything around it: mites, springtails, tiny land snails in the bead-wet crowns, a whole society conducted in a habitat the size of an open hand.

The fig tree is the stranger. It stands at the lip of the alcove, half of it in one world and half in the other, its leaves burning green-gold in the outside glare while its roots walk the cliff face down into the wet dark like a man wading in slow motion. It is the only creature here that belongs fully to both countries, and it is the bridge. Birds come to its outer half to take figs, and in doing so they stand a moment from the threshold and look in. Sometimes they enter a few strides — the bolder ones, a thrush, a dove, once a troupe of waxbills — and they bring the outside's seed and noise in on their feet. The fig feeds them; they carry the fig's children back out; and in between, the two worlds touch, once a season, at the exact height of a tree's crown.

At the very back, where the runnel sinks and the room ends in stone, the life is at its sparsest and strangest — a single pale thread of fungus on a fallen frond, one cricket standing motionless in the dark as if keeping an appointment. Everything here is present, and everything is far apart. There is no crowd, no tangle, no competition worth the name. There is only the spacing: the swallows at the border, the gecko under them, the ferns at the water, the blind things in the water, the fig at the door — each in its own ten paces of bare stone, each keeping, as the shrubs do above, some old agreement about distance.

The country around is warm, low, and dry and does not know any of this. The bright rectangle of sky hangs at the end of the room like a painting of another world, and the whole small nation lives on beneath it — water that has never seen the sky, green that has never seen the sun, creatures that have never seen anything at all — all of them settled and thriving in a room the size of a barn, at the foot of a step in a staircase, in a stain of darkness that from far off looks like nothing at all.
