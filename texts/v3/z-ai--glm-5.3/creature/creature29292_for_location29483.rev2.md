# Who Lives Here

## The Sponges, Who Are Older Than the Word for It

Begin with them, because they began.

The great sponges are the plateau's true citizens, and it is misleading to call them a "species," which implies individuals. A few of them are individuals, in the way a mountain might be an individual. But the oldest — the ones whose tissue runs in unbroken sheets under the grass mat from one ghost-monolith to another — may be *one organism each*, kilometers across, or may be several beings who merged so long ago that even they have lost the boundary. Where their tissue joins, light bends wrong, as though the merger is not finished, as though something is still being decided at the molecular scale after ten million years of deliberation.

They do not think. This is what the researchers who brave the memory-basins insist, and it is probably true, and yet the word "insist" is necessary because the sponges behave otherwise. They filter; they exhale warm mineral water at dawn-slate; they pause. All of them, everywhere, pause — for the length of the low tone, and one breath after. Whatever makes the tone (and the polite hypothesis is that they do; the impolite hypothesis is that they are *listening to* it) the sponges keep its schedule. They have kept it through the submergence of their world, through the drowning of the sky they must in some tissue-memory still recall, through the era when the water learned to remember. They are patient in the way geology is patient, and if they have an opinion about the visitors who occasionally pass through their rooms of water, it is that an opinion would require more hurry than they possess.

## The Grazers, Who Are Institutions

There are nine of them. Not nine species — nine *animals*, each known by name, each with a range, each with a schedule the old charts annotate like shipping lanes.

They are whale-large and gentle-handed with the world, cropping the grass mat in long deliberate lines that older maps sometimes mistake for the ghost-paths of the drowned builders — the mistake is understandable, because the grazers preferentially follow those lines too. Whether this is inherited habit, or whether the rows of too-regular kelp grow more richly along buried foundations, or whether the grazers, too, are moved by the memory in the water, nobody has established, and the grazers are not forthcoming.

They have no predators. Nothing here is fast enough to be a predator, and nothing here is hungry enough to want to be. They live for spans that make the charts' careful actuarial notes almost comical — an individual grazer's "projected lifespan" has been updated upward eleven times. They travel alone, or in pairs so established the pairs have compound names. Their calls are the mid-range of the plateau's silence: long, low, questioning tones that seem always on the verge of resolving into a word.

When the deep tone sounds, the grazers stop cropping. They hold still in the gold haze with the fronds braiding around them, and the divers' logs describe this moment the same way, every time, in different handwriting: *they know something. Not what. Something.*

## The Shadow That Keeps the Memory-Basins

There is one other, and it should be listed last because it is seen last, if at all.

It is large. It is cartilaginous. It has a range that includes every memory-pocket on the plateau and no ordinary water at all — it is drawn to the places where the water tastes of dust and sunlight, and it has been known to pass *through* the moment-pockets, and divers who were inside one at the time report that for the duration of the shadow's transit, the remembered dry wind carried a scent of the thing itself: something between rain and stone and blood.

No one has seen its whole body. The amber light and the fog-banks conspire; what is seen is displacement, a slow wrongness in the kelp's braiding, a corridor of fronds all bent the same direction. It does not hunt the divers. It has had opportunities, and it has declined them, and the declining is somehow worse. The most-held theory is that it patrols the basins the way a warden patrols a museum — not guarding the treasure, exactly, but *attending* it. Some say it is the last of its kind and has been attending since before the water came. Some say it is waiting for someone to come back for what the water is keeping. It is known, in the charts and in the whispered shorthand of the few who work here, simply as the Curator, and the name is not entirely a joke.

## Everyone Else

And then there is the small life, which is not scarce so much as *diffuse* — microbial clouds that drift through the interior weather like planktonic weather themselves, filtering the fog; colonizers of the sponges' flanks, entire tiny guilds living out their fast generations in the heat-haze of the merger-zones; the grass and the kelp, which are vegetation in the way the sponges are individuals — a technicality. The forest is arguably one being too, several stories tall, braiding itself against itself in the stratified currents, and if the drowned builders left anything behind besides rows and monoliths and water that remembers, it is this: the plateau's life has adopted their shapes and keeps their schedules and pauses, all of it together, once a day, for the length of a tone.

Whether that is memory or reverence is the question Ambershelf has been declining to answer since before there was anyone left to ask.
