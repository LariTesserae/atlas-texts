What lives here, first of all, is a difficulty.

You can census this place in an afternoon — the oxen, the beetles, the waxwings, the hand-shaped crabs, the fog-fish flickering past your face — and come away with a tidy list and no idea what you have listed. Because the question *what lives here* assumes the usual border: that a creature is in a place the way a stone is in a pocket. Nothing on this plateau is in it. Everything is *of* it, in the working sense, the way a hand is of a body — detachable, replaceable, intelligible only as part of the reaching. The fauna do not inhabit the factory. They are its organs, on legs.

The oxen make this clearest, so start with them. Their porcelain hides are not white but *unglazed*, and the damp luminosity along their spines is not decoration; it is load. Watch a herd crossing the dim country between groves and you will see the glow rise and fall in slow bands, herd-wide, one band per shift — the light on their backs is a gauge, and when an ox's spine dims to grey it drops out of the file, unhurried, and no other ox turns its head. Their speech is steam. They stand in the fog and breathe out plumes of warm vapor, and the fog — which holds things, which has currents, which is a medium the way water is a medium — takes each plume and carries it, stretched into a slow banner that the next animal reads with its whole damp flank. Whole sentences of weather pass down a herd this way. A warning is a hot gust. Contentment is a long cool exhale that the fog keeps the shape of for half a shift, hanging among the trunks like a curtain nobody drew.

Their hoof-clicks you took for a metronome, and they are — on the open rock. But at the spires the rhythm syncopates, and the syncopation is inventory: *this band runs clear, this one tarry, the wax is slow at the third terrace.* The oxen read the towers the way a tongue reads a stove, and they write back — every licking stroke leaves a fine groove in the wax, and the next ox on the route mouths those grooves before it drinks. The spires are ledgers. The herds keep them current.

And at the end — and this is the part you will not believe until you have stood at a sink's rim and seen it — the oxen deliver themselves. When the spine-glow goes grey for good, the animal does not weaken, does not lag, does not get sick. It simply resigns. It walks out of the herd at the change of shift, alone, in no hurry, across however much country lies between it and the nearest digester, and it queues. The white crabs part for it — they will not part for you, and it is worth being pushed back by a splayed cold hand once, just once, to learn what authority means here — and the ox steps in. No distress travels with it. The only sound is the soft settling thud of the sink receiving, arriving through the ground like a heartbeat from the next room. Nothing on this plateau dies. Everything here *retires*, and retirement is a delivery, and the delivery is made on time.

The small ones run the same truth at a smaller gauge. Bearer beetles are born knowing their routes — there is no other explanation, since there are no elders to teach them and no tracks that outlast the weather — or rather, their routes are not learned but *assigned*, in whatever way the wax smells that morning; a station's condensate is its name, and a beetle's whole life is the corridor between two names. The files of them stretch beyond your sight and never collide. Waxwings roost in the warm vent mouths at night with their excellent throats folded shut, and they do their courting in preen — in the patterns by which they strip wax from each other's feathers, which is both grooming and a language and, since the wax is the tower's product and the preening is its redistribution, also, quietly, work. Everything here that looks like play turns out, on a second watch, to be maintenance with the joy left in.

How they all coordinate is the question the plumbing answers. The grove roots are pipes, warm, lapping at their joints — and they are busy beyond water. Put your palm flat on a root at the change of a shift and you will feel the pulses inside change: a long slow surge at bright-hour, quick double-beats during fog fronts, a deep settling roll when a hill somewhere finishes dissolving. The roots are the wires. The condensate carries signal the way blood does, and every animal that drinks, wallows, queues, or roosts against a warm pipe is on the network. The continent does not need minds in the plural. It has, in the singular, one — smeared across root and hoof and combing needle — and it runs itself the way your own body runs you: no committee, no announcement, every part informed.

Even the ceiling is inhabited, once. Climb the shoulder of the tallest spire — the one whose coppery band keeps the fog unsettled all around it — and look up through a rent in the grey-gold, and you will see hangings on the underside of the deck: pale sprays, anemone-shaped, thousands of them, each one the exact shape of a sound. Every roar that fell out of the air here, every note a young bird tried, every landslide that rained itself down as warm water — the ceiling has been keeping them. It is an archive up there, a garden of everything ever said, which on this plateau is nearly nothing, which is why each flower is so carefully kept. The silence is not absence of speech. It is speech, grown on the underside of the lampshade, waiting.

And in the sketch-zones, life gets drafted, too. That is the thing the finished country does not prepare you for. A few hours after the dim shift, when the grey blocks of the unrendered hills stand open to the weather, you find the fauna there also half-done: a bearer beetle with its left side fully carved — every facet, every polished leg-bearing — and its right side a smooth grey assertion of beetle. A waxwing with one perfect wing and one that is the *idea* of a wing, flying anyway, on faith. They are not in pain. Pending is not a suffering state here; it is a schedule. Stand still long enough at the boundary of a sketch-zone and you can feel the difference in your own soles — your weight is less consulted, the ground less committed to you — and if you look down at your own shadow you will notice it has stopped deciding things about your edges.

Whether anything walks the sketch-zones and finishes them, you cannot say. The rendering always happens between watches. You leave a hill half-asserted at dim-shift, camp, return, and it is fluted, fissured, wind-carved, finished, with dew standing in every groove as if it had been there for years. Once, at the far rim, where the new land condenses out of warm air a hand's width each shift, you thought you saw something — grey, long-limbed, slow, its posture exactly the posture of someone checking a seam — but the fog-fish passed between you and it, forty cool flickers, and when they had gone the rim was vacant, and the new rock lay there, sharp, organized, already holding the memory of heat.

So: what lives here?

Everything lives here, and one thing lives here. The herds and files and roosts and schools are real, and individual, and warm against your hand, and each of them would bleed. But the only full-time resident is the plateau — the room that lights itself, the belt that eats one end and grows the other, the quiet it tends like a crop. Everything else is its household: staff of a thing that is awake, that works in shifts, that files its mountains and archives its thunder on the ceiling.

And it has noticed you. That is the last fact, the one you carry away from the rim. There is a sketch-zone near wherever you camp now, a little greyer each dim-shift, roughly your size. The warm rivers run body-heat when you stand in them. The silence where you walk has a slot in it, patient, shaped like a visitor.

For now, the plateau has decided you count as weather. It is watching to see whether you'll be needing a station.
