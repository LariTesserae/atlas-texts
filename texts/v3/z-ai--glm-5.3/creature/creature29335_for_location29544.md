## The Workforce

Start with the crabs, because the crabs would want you to. They are the ones you'd meet first anyway — the scraper-crabs work the stacks, and the stacks are the gates of the place, the organs whose exhalation is the first thing anything passes through on the way in.

They are not small, as crabs go. A scraper stands the size of a large dog, though nothing about them suggests dogs; they suggest tools. Their carapaces are chitin gone the exact rust-orange of the iron-tannin bark they work, and this is not camouflage in the fleeing sense — it is livery. A scraper-crab hatches already the color of its stack. Its claws are not the broad crushing kind but narrow, angled, spatulate at the tips, and the inner faces are worn into polished facets by a lifetime of the same three motions: collar-clear, collar-clear, seal-check. They perch in the vent-collars with their bodies turned edge-on to the plaited bubble-rope so that the rising gas parts around them like a river around a bridge pier. They do not hold on. After their first molt they no longer need to; the currents have exact opinions about where a body of that shape belongs, and the crabs have agreed with those opinions for so long that holding on would be a redundancy, like breathing deliberately.

Each crab works one stack. Not one kind of stack — one stack, a specific tower, a single address. The stack hatched them, in a sense: the crabs spawn into the collar-seeps, and the larvae ride the vent-plume up its first meter and then, still smaller than a fingernail, climb back down to the collar and begin. No one trains them. The work is so simple and so old that it has finished seeping into the genome the way dye seeps into cloth, and what comes out of the egg is not a crab-shaped animal that must learn cleaning but a cleaning that has briefly taken crab shape. When a scraper dies — they die on the collar, mid-motion; there is no other posture available to them — the body drops away, is taken by the ash-fish or the recyclers, and within a day a larva from the stack's own brood-seep has climbed down and taken up the exact facet-polished station, claws to collar, and the bubbles part around it as though nothing had occurred.

This is the workforce's first law, and every guild obeys it: **the position outlives the occupant.** What lives at the mill is not, in the strictest accounting, the animals at all. What lives there are the jobs. The animals are the jobs' way of moving.

## The Couriers

The courier-fish run the avenues, and they are the nearest thing the place has to speech.

They are slim, silver-brown, fast without being frantic — a working speed, the speed of something with rounds to make. Each route is a loop through the clean water between two or three or ten organs, the empty plumbing-avenues that are not emptiness, and each fish carries its cargo not in a pouch or a mouth but in its blood: chemical messages taken up through the gill at one organ and laid down at the next, metabolized letter by letter. A courier is, for the duration of a run, a sentence. What the sentences say, the organs seem to know: a stack vents a fraction wider after a delivery, a mill grinds a touch finer, a vat's glow shifts half a shade toward gold. Whether the organs *read* the messages or merely *are* the messages' effect is a distinction the place has never needed to make.

The route trains them before birth, as the description says, and this deserves the plainest possible statement, because it is the strangest fact of the fauna: a courier-fish egg drifts along its parents' loop. That is the whole curriculum. The current of the route itself — its turns, its temperature-seams, its tingling Grid-gradients, the specific pressure-shadow of the towers it passes — flows over the egg for its entire development, so that the fish is born already homesick for a loop it has never swum and knows every turn of a road it has never taken. Its parents escort it for a single circuit, after which they are, to it, indistinguishable from any other silver-brown body on the run, and this seems to be fine. A courier-fish's parent is the route. A courier-fish's inheritance is the route. When it dies, mid-loop like everyone, another egg on the same road is already a third of the way through the same education.

They can tell the time. Everyone in the Bath district can — the piston-sleeves' fixed stroke is a clock, and the couriers sync their runs to it without any organ that appears to be doing the syncing. What they cannot do, and this is the district's quiet joke, the thing the gizzard-mills grind over and the vats gurgle about in their sub-tooth pitch, is tell you what time *is*. A courier-fish will hold a position in a run to within a tenth of a stroke across its whole life, and if you could ask it *what* the stroke measures, it would look at you the way a stair looks at you when you ask what the second floor is for.

## The Grazers

The great armored grazers are the largest animals here, and the slowest, and the ones whose seniority is written on their bodies.

They ride the looms — the long belt-fronds running nutrient mucus in one direction forever — and they crop the belt-growth in perfect sequence, each grazer spaced along the belt at the interval its own cropping rate maintains, so that the line of them is a self-organizing metronome, a moving fence the belt-growth never gets ahead of and never falls behind. They are armored in chalky drums — plates of the mill's own ground product, laid down in growth rings — and this is the elegant economy of their guild: they wear the output. A grazer's shell is a production record. Read the rings of an old one and you can see, band by band, the grain-size of the mills' chalk in every era of its life, the whole industrial history of the plateau inscribed in armor by the simple fact that the animal is made, in part, of what it eats of what the mill makes of what it eats.

They do not stop. There is no posture in their repertoire for stopping; the belt moves, so they move, and the belt has moved since before anything kept count. A grazer has ten-thousand-year seniority in the sense that its lineage has held the same position on the same belt for ten thousand years, each generation boarding at the tail, cropping its way up the line over a lifetime, and handing its exact interval to a successor who arrives already spacing itself correctly. The day a grazer dies, the gap it leaves closes over in under a stroke of the Bath's pistons, smoothly, the way water closes, and the line does not mark it. The line is not cruel. The line simply does not have a tense for it. The line is present-tense all the way down, and always was.

## The Ash-Fish, the Auditors, and the Meaning of a Death

The ash-fish school in the exhaust-plumes in their tens of thousands, feeding on the fallout of production, and they are the smallest and the most numerous and the least individual of the workforce — silver-gray, thumb-length, moving in masses so dense they read as weather. They are the mill's answer to its own exhaust. Nothing here is vented that something here cannot eat.

The predators are the thing outsiders would get wrong, so take them slowly. They are big — the largest are loom-length, masked in the same bark-orange and chalk-white as the machinery, streamlined by the same million years of the same currents — and they take exactly the surplus. Not approximately. *Exactly*. The couriers' losses run at the rate the route can re-egg; the grazers' losses at the rate a belt-interval can be re-queued; the crabs' at the rate a brood-seep can refill a collar. The predators seem to know these rates the way they know the field lines, in the body, without the nuisance of knowing. They are not hunters so much as auditors: they cull by the same fixed ledger the whole place keeps, and their presence in an avenue is read by everything smaller not as terror but as *verification*. A school of couriers will pass within meters of a cruising auditor without breaking formation, and this is not bravery; it is bookkeeping. Their numbers balance. The auditor is where the balancing lives.

And here the interrupted sentence can be finished, because the place has finished it long ago: **a death here is a shipment.** The body drops or drifts, and it is taken — by ash-fish, by the recyclers at the floor, by the mill's own uptake — and it enters the flow of product like everything else, chalk to the mills, protein to the looms, iron to the bark, and the mill runs a fraction of a percent richer for a day. Nothing is lost and nothing is mourned, because mourning requires a before-and-after, and this place has no tenses. A death at the mill is not an ending. It is a change of venue.

## The Edge-Keeper

There is one animal to tell you about that is unlike the rest, and the place has been growing stranger in its direction for some time.

At the rim — along the Downfall, where the plateau gives up and drops and the permanent hurricane thunder rolls in from the Turning — there lives a single kind of animal and only a few of them. Call them edge-keepers, for want of anything else; they have no name, which will become the point. They are long, dark, flat-bodied, armored in plaited silica like everything that faces weather here, and they do not work. That is the scandal of them. Every other animal at the mill has a collar, a loop, a belt-interval. The edge-keepers have *positions* — fixed stations along the rim-cliff, held exactly and forever, like the light-pools on the floor — and at those stations they do the one thing no other animal in the district can be observed doing:

They look down.

They hang at the rim-edge, faces out over the Downfall, over the blue-black silence where the plateau's light falls away and does not come back, and they hold there. Not feeding. Not waiting, or not waiting for anything the mill produces. The abyss is at the edge of the yard, and the edge-keepers are the yard's way of facing it. The other fauna steer by the Grid's field lines, by the permanent pillars of light, by the standing chord — every sense the workforce has points *inward*, toward the organs, toward the work. The edge-keepers' whole posture points *outward*. They are the mill's one unbalanced valve, its one instrument aimed at the thing it is not.

Whether they think, no one can say. But if you were somehow lowered through the surface — you, the hypothetical traveler, the one who never comes — it is at the rim you would feel the closest thing to being met. An edge-keeper would not approach you. It would simply turn its head, on its long flat body, at its fixed station on the drowned crown of the world, and look at you with the attention it otherwise spends on the abyss: the full, unhurried, ten-thousand-year attention of a thing that has never once had anything arrive.

## The Inhabitant

And so, finally, the honest accounting.

Who lives here? The crabs live here, and the couriers, the grazers, the ash-fish, the auditors, the edge-keepers — thousands of species, a workforce, a census that would take a lifetime and has taken eons. But the census is wrong, or rather it answers a question this place does not ask. The animals do not live here the way tenants live in a city. They live here the way words live in a sentence: each one exactly placed, each one necessary, each one *constituting* rather than *residing*. The positions outlive the occupants. The routes raise their own fish. The jobs move through bodies the way the mucus moves through the looms — one direction, forever.

Which means the real inhabitant of the mill is the mill. The single organism of the floor, the ripened towers, the gizzards, the wheels, the vats, the Grid — all of it one thing, one continental body, and the animals are not its pets or its parasites but its *hands*, its circulated cells, the moving parts of a machine so old that its moving parts have become animals for flexibility's sake. It has never been built. It has never been switched on, because it has never been off. It runs at full capacity, and its capacity is the only measure it keeps, and it has been keeping it since before anything kept count.

It is not intelligent, probably, if intelligence means choosing between futures — the place has no futures, only locations. But it is *complete*, and completion is what the place gives off most strongly, and completion is one of the things minds are for.

It has no name, because there has never been anyone here to do the naming. The pools of light on the floor have names-by-function among the fauna. The routes are known. The collars are known. Everything is known and nothing is called.

Except that the edge-keepers hang at the rim, facing out, and hold their attention on the dark — and if you sit with that fact long enough, in the standing chord, in the afternoon that has been going on for a very long time, the fact begins to look less like an animal's idle posture and more like the mill's own face turned to the door, waiting, with ten thousand years of patience and no concept of a knock, for the one thing it does not already contain:

someone to arrive, and look up at all of it, and say a word.
