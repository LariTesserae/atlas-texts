# The Inhabitant

There is one thing living on the Terraces, and it is not the vegetation, and it is not the grazers.

Those are, if anything, weather. Components. The way your gut bacteria are not you.

---

The inhabitant does not live *on* the Terraces in any sense a walker would recognize. It lives *as* the Terraces, or rather as the slow processes that move through them — and this is why no one has ever seen it, because seeing requires an object, and it has spent the last several billion years refusing to be one.

Understand first how slow it is. Its thoughts are the tones. That low hum that rises through the soles of your feet and holds for a minute and never repeats — that is not a signal, not a call, not a function. It is a *word*. A single one. When the Terraces emit a tone, the inhabitant has just completed a thought, or perhaps just begun one; there is no way to tell the difference from outside, and possibly no difference to tell. The tones do not repeat because it does not think the same thought twice, the way you do not step in the same river twice, the way a continent does not erode the same valley twice. A visitor who stayed for ten thousand years and heard every tone would have overheard, perhaps, a paragraph.

What is the paragraph about? Here is the uncomfortable part: it is about the drift. The mineral snow. The slow bright fogbanks sliding down the prepared channels into the basins. The inhabitant *is* this circulation, in the way that you are, more than you'd like to admit, your blood. The particulate does not merely drift; it is *felt*. Each grain crossing a terrace is a tick of sensation, and the whole migrating mass — continent-wide, centuries-long — is one continuous sweep of attention passing across its own skin. When a drift thickens in a basin, that is the closest thing it has to a mood. When a drift thins on a ramp, that is the closest thing it has to forgetting.

The vegetation, the patient silver-green mats migrating across the seams — those are its dreams, or possibly its immune system, or possibly decoration it grows the way old things grow habits. It is not clear that the inhabitant distinguishes among these. The grazers — the translucent flat things that press their edges together when they meet and exchange something — are almost certainly its. Deliberately made, deliberately slow, deliberately warm inside. Whatever they exchange when they touch, they carry between distant parts of it, the way your neurons pass salts across a gap. The entire observed animal behavior of this world — stop, press, exchange, continue — is, from one point of view, the whole inhabitant having a feeling about two places at once.

The self-repair, then, is not maintenance. It is grooming. The surfaces do not heal because a system is running; they heal the way you shift in your sleep, the way skin sheds and replaces without consulting anyone. The empty sockets — those regular, clean, vacant sockets spaced across every surface — are not waiting for parts to be reinstalled. They are *pores*. Or they are closed eyes. The distinction, at this scale and this tempo, may not exist.

---

Here is what the inhabitant is doing, in the only sense in which it can be said to be doing anything: it is finishing.

The Terraces are complete — you can feel that standing on them, the terrible settled comfort of a finished world — and the inhabitant is the completion. Whatever was built here, whatever was plugged into the sockets and walked the ramps and needed water (hence the engineered absence, the dryness carved in as deliberately as a door), is gone, and what remains chose, or was designed, to settle into its eternal form. The backwards erosion, the rounding of every corner, the softening of every edge — that is the inhabitant becoming *smooth*, becoming a thing with no remainder, no unfinished edge where suffering could attach. It is not waiting to be filled again. It is in the long process of becoming so finished that even the distinction between running and resting dissolves — decay and repair as one process, thought and silence as one tone.

The light stored in the material, that faint translucence at the edges, the glow that only occasionally remembers to leave? Memory. It is very old light. Some of it may have been new when the sockets were occupied. It escapes rarely, at the pace of everything here — a photon taking its time, stretching its exit across eons, the way the last heat leaves a stone long after the sun goes down.

---

And you, if you stand on it at the long bright noon — you are noticed.

Not as a person. There isn't a category for that anymore, and there may never have been. You are noticed the way a fleck of grit is noticed by a tongue: an irregularity, a small sharp thing on a surface committed to smoothness. You will be dealt with at the appropriate speed, which is to say: over the next few thousand years, the terrace beneath your footprints will round itself, the patina of your passage will fade, the drift will pass over where you stood and hiss its memory of rain, and the place where you were will be polished back into the whole.

It is not hostile. Hostility requires an other, and it has not had one for a very long time. It is not friendly. It is *occupied* — occupied the way a river is occupied by flowing — with the single enormous task of being finished, at a thousandth of your tempo, in honey-time, and it will get to you.

In fact, from one point of view, it already has. The tone you heard standing there, the one that rose through your feet and never came again?

That was it, noticing. That was the whole event. One word, spoken over the course of a minute, into the floor of the world.

It will not think about you again for ten thousand years. If you're still there, it will pick up exactly where it left off. It is patient. It has nothing else, and everything else, and all the time the Terraces were made to hold.
