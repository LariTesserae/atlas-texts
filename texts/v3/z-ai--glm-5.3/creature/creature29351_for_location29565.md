Inside, it is a room. Not a crater, not a cave — a room, with the terrible confidence of a thing that was built rather than formed, though no hand you could name built it. The walls are dressed stone where they aren't raw glass-black rock, and the roof is the sky, but the wrong sky, the indoor sky, the steam-hazed six-degree sky that hangs over the place like a lid that forgot it was a lid. Ramparts of slag and quarried basalt stand around the perimeter in a ring, and through them, at intervals, the ground breathes: fumaroles, capped with brass, venting on a schedule. You can stand still and count it. Inhale, twelve seconds. Hold. Exhale. The whole caldera has a pulse.

And the machinery is everywhere, and it is not ruined.

That is the thing your mind keeps snagging on. Everything you know about machines says they should be ruined. This one is not. Gantry cranes straddle channels of running water that is warm as blood. Flywheels the size of ponds turn at a speed so slow you have to watch a single spoke to be sure. There are valve stems polished bright by use, drip trays emptied and wiped, a grease nipple with the fresh-swabbed look of one serviced that morning. Somewhere below your boots, the piston you heard from an hour out is still knocking, patient as a heart, and it has been knocking, you will come to understand, for longer than anyone has been keeping count.

Because life is here. That's the second thing your mind snags on, harder than the first. You came through forty miles of white nothing and here is green — actual green, moss sheeting the shaded stone in a nap so thick it looks upholstered, ferns unrolling from the joints of pipe runs as if the pipe runs were fallen logs. Midges in December, hanging in columns above the warm water like the steam's small ideas. A frog, somewhere, saying one thing over and over. Swallows — you find their mud nests later, plastered inside a turbine housing that turns, gently, perpetually, a few inches beneath them, the birds brooding their eggs to the lullaby of eighty tons of balanced steel. Fox prints again, the same line you followed all morning, except here they meander. Here they have the luxury of meandering. Every creature for a hundred frozen miles knows about this place the way sailors know about land, and the ones who live here live soft: the ptarmigan still in white plumage against green moss, off-season, unbothered, staying.

So the tenants are easy. The tenants are birds and frogs and moss and a fox with a den under the condenser, warm as a widow's stove.

The resident is something else.

You will hear it before you see it, because everything here is heard first. A change in the piston's knock — a hesitation, then two strokes harder, as if the machinery cleared its throat. And then, at the far end of the hall, where the steam stands thickest and the light comes down in one grey column like something delivered, you will see movement that is not steam: a figure, tall by the scale of the machines, which is to say enormous by the scale of you. It moves along the pipe run the way a man moves along a garden row at evening, unhurried, touching. It has hands. That's the detail you'll defend later, the one nobody will believe: hands, flat and grey and plated like the belly of a fish, and it lays one on each valve as it passes, a half-second apiece, the way a physician touches a wrist, the way a farrier touches a leg. Checking. It has done this, you understand, down this same row, past these same valves, for a very long time, and it is not bored and it is not tired and it does not want anything, and that last fact is somehow the most frightening thing about it. It wants nothing. It only keeps.

It is not made of the machines, exactly, any more than you are made of your house. But it is built of the same grammar — iron and brass and long habit — and it has grown, the way anything grows that is fed daily by warmth and water and time, into the shape of its work. Its eyes, if they are eyes, are boiler-glass. When it passes a fumarole, the vent opens early for it, the way a dog stands when its person reaches for a leash.

Whether it built the Works, or the Works, given a few thousand years of steam and reason, grew a keeper the way a pond grows a skin — that question you can carry out with you, and it will keep, warm, all winter.

Because you will leave. Everyone leaves. It takes no notice of you beyond the small courtesies it extends to the fox and the swallows, which is to say none at all, or all there are: the path stays level under your feet, the vented steam never scalds you, the piston never falters while you sleep against the warm stone with your boots off.

And an hour out across the white nothing, the sound still behind you, faint, tireless, that deep-room murmur — you will finally understand what you heard from the first. It wasn't machinery doing work.

It was something very large, very old, and very much at home, breathing in its sleep with one eye open.
