# What Lives Here

Start with the honest answer, because the place deserves honesty: no one lives here. That was the first thing you checked, coming over the rim — the standing question of any empty place, *who* — and the answer came back clean. Thumbprints in ten thousand pipe joints and no thumbs. A chisel lying in a half-finished groove with its edge still keen, and no hand reaching for it. The grips of the tools are worn to the shape of use and then abandoned mid-use, which is not how people leave things. People leave in a hurry or people leave slowly, and both leave signs of leaving. This place left no such sign. It is more accurate to say the hands were withdrawn than that they departed.

But you asked what lives here, not who, and the distinction turns out to be the whole visit.

The water lives here. That sounds like a figure of speech, and for the first day you take it as one. Then you stop taking it as one. Consider how the water spends its time. It is born at the rim, as fog — arrives, in other words, by being breathed in by the wall — and from that moment it is a resident with a biography. It is combed out of the mist by the fin walls, given a channel, which is to say a name and an address. It is passed from groove to gutter to cascade with a deliberateness that only makes sense for something being kept, and it is kept the way a place keeps anything it values: underground, in the dark, in the cold, in rows. Nothing in the basin is trying to move water. Everything in the basin is trying to *hold* water, and the entire art of the terraces — the false level, the hairline off-plumb, the delay built into every junction — is the art of making a life last. A drop's journey from fog line to deep cistern is measured here in weeks, if you read the basins right, and there are places where a drop might sit in a lip for the better part of a season before the lip fills and lets it go. The works are not a machine for moving water. They are a house water lives in, built by the water, furnished by the water, repaired by the water, and the water's tenure is longer than yours will ever be.

And the water is growing a body. This is the second thing, and it takes longer to see because it happens at the speed of geology dressed as plumbing. The lips of the basins thicken year by year. Rims rise like mouths. Stone fingers lengthen under the drip-lips, sharp as the chisels lying in their grooves — and that likeness should have been your clue, because it is not a likeness. The tools and the fingers are the same technology. The chisel cuts stone; the drip *is* a chisel that cuts stone and then, when the water slows, lays the cuttings back down again as new stone. Deposit here, dissolve there, and deposition wins by a hair. That hair is the margin of a life. Every organism you have ever known lives on the same margin — takes in a little more than it spends, and calls the surplus a body. The basin takes in a little more mineral than it loses, and calls the surplus *itself*. The embalmed rivers in the dead corners, those smooth ribbons of stone crossing the terraces mid-run — those are not failures. They are finished work. Set. Solidified. They are what the basin has already decided to be.

So widen the frame again and the answer assembles itself, slowly, the way everything here does.

There is one organism in this basin. It is the basin. Its blood is the water, arriving from below as breath and leaving downward as seepage, and every channel is a vein chosen by the blood itself, rerouted centimeter by century as the water re-plumbs its own circulation. Its skeleton is the masonry — the old hands' work, the fitted stone, the fired clay with the thumbprints burned in — but its skeleton is also alive, being added to, being re-banded, being slowly replaced by its own secretion the way your own bones are never the bones you had. Its heart is the ceramic cup on the fulcrum: fills, tips, knocks, drains, rights itself. Patient as a heart is the phrase, but be literal — it is the heart, the only percussion in the body, keeping the time by which the whole metabolism runs. And the day's rhythm — mist climbing, surfaces sweating in sequence, the slow drying from the top of the bowl to the bottom — is not weather and not schedule. It is diurnal. It is a pulse you can see from the rim wall, the whole two-mile body flushing.

Which leaves the sheen.

You will go back to the deep grate, on the third or fourth day, because you keep going back to it. The oldest water in the place sits down there, black, cold beyond the damp of the morning, and across its surface lies that opalescent film that breaks apart when you shift your angle and refuses to say whether it is alive. Here is what you can honestly report. Every body this large and this patient has a place where it keeps its long thoughts, and the deep cistern is that place — the coldest, slowest, most protected volume in the whole organism, the skull-cavity of the works, and something occupies it that is thin, and films, and catches light wrong, and is exactly the right shape to be a mind even if it is the wrong substance to be one. You cannot say more. Neither can it, apparently, or neither will it. But you notice that the drips — the ones with the lengthening and shortening intervals, the long repeating sequences that the works measure out without being done measuring — all of them, every last pattern, sounds *first* down there, in the deep cistern, before it surfaces in the shallower works like a thought reaching speech. The whole basin listens to that black water. You have been listening to it too, you realize, from your first morning on the rim, and calling it silence because it was too slow to recognize as a voice.

And what is being measured? Refuse the easy answer, which is time; time is what everything measures and therefore nothing. The honest answer is smaller and stranger. Deposition wins by a hair. Every year the channels narrow by a thumbnail. The works are counting — drip by drip, in patterns that repeat and do not conclude — toward their own completion: the day the last channel closes, the day the last running water lies down and becomes the stone it was always becoming, the day the whole two-mile body goes quiet in every part except the cup on the fulcrum. It is measuring the distance to its own death, or to its own full stop, and those may not be different things here, because nothing in this place has ever shown you where building leaves off and dying begins. It is an organism whose entire life is the slow writing of its own final shape, and it is patient, because nothing rots here, so nothing is urgent — not even its ending, which it works toward the way the mist climbs the terraces: a gray tide, in no hurry, certain of the morning.

One thing more, and then you can go.

The chisel in the half-finished groove. The grips worn exactly to the size of your hand. You told yourself, coming over the rim, that no one lives here, and that was true, and it stays true, and yet the work is not finished — the groove is half-cut, the tools are out, the bench is left as if the worker stepped away expecting to step back. The basin has hands of water and hands of mineral and it does not need yours. But it kept them. It kept the chisels keen, it kept the grips the size of yours, it left the work mid-stroke by every channel that is still open, and you understand, finally, at the rim wall on the last morning, breath showing faintly, that the place is not abandoned and never was.

It is holding a place in line.

What lives here is a builder that has dissolved into its building, and it is keeping the tools warm — cold, damp, but warm in the only sense that survives here — for hands exactly the size of yours. Whether that is an invitation, or a memory too stubborn to dissolve, or simply the last habit of a thing that once had hands and cannot put them down, the basin does not say. It has one clock, one heart, one voice, and all three say the same patient word: *not yet, not yet, not yet.*
