# The Inhabitants

## The Pillar-Trees

The oldest citizens of the plain are the pillar-trees, and calling them citizens is not decoration — they are the closest thing this world has to persons with tenure. A big one has stood in the same spot since before there were languages anywhere to name it. Its trunk, three or four meters through, rises unbroken and unbranched for fifteen meters and then opens all at once into a broad flattened crown, a dark green parasol of overlapping fronds that an observer on the escarpment benches could watch for a lifetime and see change perhaps a dozen meaningful times.

What defines a pillar-tree is not its height but its accounts. Every one of them carries inside its sealed skin a quantity of amber oil that has been circulating since germination — the same molecules, over and over, ten thousand years, the fluid looping up the trunk and out through the frond-veins and back down, doing the work of a bloodstream without ever once being replenished or spilled. This oil is the tree's whole biography. It was the endowment in the seed. It is the medium in which every leaf the tree has ever grown was dissolved, briefly, before taking form. Nothing that has happened to the tree in its long life has happened outside that oil, and none of the oil has escaped. A pillar-tree is, in the most literal sense, a closed book: everything it ever was is still in it.

They are the plain's water-thieves, and they practice their theft with the patience of geological processes. On cold nights, when thin streams of vapor descend from the high atmosphere, the crown-fronds — angled and waxed like so many shallow troughs tilted at the sky — condense a lace of frost out of air that is nearly dry. A few grams per night. The frost does not melt; it sublimates, but not before it has run inward along fine grooves in each frond, down toward the sealed interior, where it joins the ten-thousand-year inheritance as the only new water the tree will ever receive. The pillar-tree does not drink. It skims. And it has done this every night of its adult life with the regularity of tides, so that if you wanted to know how a tree four millennia old got its water, the answer would be: a gram at a time, from nearly nothing, forever.

Each one stands alone — a hundred meters from any neighbor, minimum, enforced by shallow wide roots that mine the mineral soil in a radius and starve out any seed foolish enough to attempt the interior. This solitude is not loneliness; it is the plain's own law of room made visible at the largest scale. And each one is, within the terms of its kind, essentially immortal. Nothing kills a pillar-tree quickly. Wind cannot, drought cannot — there is no drought here, because drought presupposes a wetter norm that never existed. What kills them, in the fullness of deep time, is a cracked seal, a flaw, a stone dropped in the wrong century, age itself. And even then they do not leave.

## The Halos

Around each pillar-tree, at the contested margin where root-minerals thin out and unclaimed light begins, live the smaller plants — the folded fans, the stacks of waxy plates, the squat rounded towers — arranged in rings and halos that look from above like green lace around a dark knot. They are the plain's middle class, and they live by the same law in miniature. Each sprouted with its endowment of gel and oil. Each sealed its own body. Each circulates the same fluid, season after slow season, with the frugality of a miser who knows there will never be a second inheritance.

The plate-stacks are the ones the place is quietly famous for — or would be famous for, if fame could reach here. They are the plain's benefactors and its dead. A very old plate-stack, cracked by frost or a shifting stone or nothing more than its own accumulated centuries, bleeds its amber oil out over weeks into the mineral dust, where the air oxidizes it into hard honey-colored resin. The wind then polishes this resin over millennia into the glassy sheets and knuckles of the amberglass fields — the plain's one daily spectacle, whole flats of transparent amber catching the morning sun and burning with light, on schedule, forever, for no audience but whatever stands there.

Understand what this means: the glass fields are graveyards that glow. Every bright sheet in the flats is a life's entire inheritance, spent at last — the only occasion on the whole plain where anything is ever lost, and even then it is not lost but transformed, hardened, made into a kind of permanent record that the landscape will keep for a million years. The plants do not choose this. But the pattern is so consistent, so beautiful, so legible as an offering that it takes an effort of discipline to remember it is only chemistry amortized.

## The Grazer

There is one animal here worth the name, and it is a lesson in what a place does to the bodies of its residents.

The grazer is a low, broad quadruped with dense dark fur, descended from swift montane stock, and the whole of its lineage on this plateau has been one long negotiation with silence. Its ancestors ran. It does not. Speed was traded away, generation by generation, for a digestive system able to extract life from wax-leaf and amber sap — a diet of sealed, oily, slow-burning matter that would starve almost anything else. Perhaps a few thousand of them exist, roaming a range that could support ten times their number, their trails visible from the escarpments as faint dark threads stitched between the trees.

Everything about them is quiet. They have no predators here, and so they have lost their calls — not abandoned them, lost them, the way an unused room in a house is eventually closed off. They make no sound at all. Even their breathing is inaudible at ten paces. When you do finally meet one, after weeks of walking, the encounter is defined by a mutual failure to startle: it lifts its head, regards you across the absolute stillness, and goes back to its grazing, because in a world where nothing is fast, urgency itself has become the maladaptation. Its body has absorbed the plain's chief law so thoroughly that even its pulse seems to have slowed to match the wind cycles. It is, in the fullest sense, a ruminant of time.

## The Glasswings

Up in the frond-crowns live the insects — small, unhurried, transparent. Their wings are clear not for concealment but for economy: material here is so precious that nothing spends it on pigment, and a glasswing in flight is not a thing seen but a thing inferred, a seam of movement in the air, a bend in the light.

Their entire lives pass within a single crown. They are born there, drink the ancient sap there, and die there, having traveled at most a few meters from where they emerged — small sealed lives inside the larger sealed life of the tree, which is itself one of a million closed circulations standing in the open air. They are the only creatures on the plain that will ever be witnessed in the act of moving fast, and even they seem to know that speed is a loan against the general silence, to be repaid in stillness.

## The Sealed Ones

And then there are those who live here longest of all, though "live" requires a moment's care.

On the stable band — the hundred-kilometer-wide strip where the two dust-winds cancel each other and the ground has not changed in half a million years — the fallen pillar-trees lie where they fell. Nothing eroded them, because nothing here erodes anything quickly. Nothing scattered them. Nothing ate them. Their thousand-year oil hardened in place, slowly, into amber glass, and their shapes remain whole and legible beneath aeons of faint dust: long recumbent forms, trunk and crown still articulate, dark beneath their film of pale sediment, unmistakably shaped like sleepers.

This is the plain's one mild strangeness, and the mind will insist on the metaphor against all evidence — that they are resting, that the whole silent landscape is arranged around them the way a room is arranged around a sleeper, that at some signal they might rise. They will not rise. But the metaphor is not entirely wrong either, because on this plain the dead are not subtracted. The sealed ones keep their form, keep their place, keep their oil-turned-glass, in a world whose first law is that nothing may be lost. They are not gone. They are simply the oldest expression of the only kind of permanence this place has ever offered: to remain, entirely, exactly where you lay down, for as long as the wind allows.

That is the full census. Two kingdoms and a wind: the green and the stone, the sealed and the sealed-dead, and between them a single silent grazer threading its patient trails, a flicker of glass wings in the high crowns, and the deep subliminal hum of the stone organs when the wind crosses its threshold — the plain, thinking out loud, to no one, in no hurry, for a very long time.
