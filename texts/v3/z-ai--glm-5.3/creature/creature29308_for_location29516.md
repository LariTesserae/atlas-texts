**Who Lives There**

The first thing to understand about the Pocket is that nothing there lives the way things live elsewhere. Elsewhere, life is a response to rain, to seasons, to the ordinary kindnesses of a climate. Here, life is a response to *schedule* — and so everything in the bowl has the character of a commuter, or a monk: punctual, patient, arranged.

---

**The rosettes are the landlords.**

The great silver plants — the felted crowns on their woolly trunks — did not choose the Pocket; they built it, or at least they built the green part of it, which is the part that matters to everyone else. Each one is a slow fountain running backward. Its leaves are gutters, its crown a cistern, and every mid-morning when the fog pours over the eastern col, each rosette becomes a small factory for rain from a cloudless sky: the moisture beads on the felted hairs, the beads gather, the gutters walk them inward and down, and the plant waters its own feet on a ground that hasn't seen real rain in months. They stand well spaced, each with its province of bare ground, all leaning the same way — tilted into the fog-push like a congregation leaning into a sermon. If you crouch among them at mid-morning you can hear the whole arrangement working: the tick, tick, tick of a thousand small deliveries. The rosettes are not abundant. They are *distributed*, the way wealthy things are, and every other life in the Pocket is arranged around their plumbing.

---

**The moss forest is a country, if you are small enough.**

Between the crowns, the moss runs knee-deep and springy, audibly wet underfoot. At human scale it's a carpet. At two millimeters it's old-growth timber — a canopy of fog-drinking leaves, a understory of cushion plants lying around like green saddle blankets dense enough to sit on. And it is grazed. The transparent-shelled snails that live back in the lee of the corral are the Pocket's quiet majority: you will almost never see one, but on a damp evening the moss is laced with their silver trails, and every leaf bears the evidence of their slow, thorough attention. They are the only residents who think the fog is *weather*. To a snail, mid-morning is simply when the world becomes drinkable, and dusk, when the fog pours back over the rim the way it came, is when it stops. They have no opinion about the mountain breathing. They are inside the breath.

---

**The mammals are all economists.**

The vizcachas sunbathe on the warm stones in rows like loaves, and this is not leisure; it is fuel. Every calorie in the Pocket has to be imported from the sun, and the vizcachas have simply made their bodies into stone-warmed ovens. Their pikas — or the pikas that work the moss margins near them, who may be tenants or may be family, no one has settled this — spend their days harvesting the fog-fed green and their nights in the deep dry cold, and they mark the boundary of every harvest with a single thin whistle that seems to carry the whole silence inside it. When it stops, you notice the silence more than you noticed the whistle.

The culpeo fox is the one who understands the fog best. It hunts *in* the fog, at dawn, using it as cover, and most days the fog keeps its side of the bargain: you see only the tip of that enormous tail moving through the gray like a brush being drawn across wet paper, and then not even that. The fox treats the mountain's breathing as a hunting partner. Whether the mountain knows this is one of the things the herders argue about on the nights they bring their flocks up to the warm seam.

The camelids come up at dusk, on schedule, to the salt scrapes — woolly, deliberate, unhurried, licking the white blooms off the freshly split shale where the sea is still paying out its last wages. They are visitors, not residents. But they come so faithfully, and the scrapes are so exactly what they need, that they function as the Pocket's tax collectors, arriving each evening to collect what the uplifted seabed owes.

And the caracara patrols the rim on foot, walking it like an official making rounds, incurious and entitled, less a hunter than an inspector who is owed something by everything below.

---

**The birds run on light.**

The sunangels are the Pocket's most improbable citizens — hummingbirds with throats like embers, working the rosette spikes at midday when the fog has withdrawn and the sun briefly rules. Their wing-whirr is the loudest sound the basin produces on a still day, which tells you something about the volume of everything else. But the true fact about them is what they do at night: they hang on their perches like ornaments, cooled to a breath, hearts slowed almost to stillness, waiting for the sun to wind them again. They are less birds than exquisite clocks, and each noon is their spring being re-tightened. The bumblebees do a smaller version of the same trick, shivering themselves warm before takeoff, warming up like small engines before they trust the cold air with their weight.

---

**The frogs have solved death, provisionally.**

Along the warm seam in the south wall — the little crack where the young uplift still vents its heat, the green tongue of ferns in the freezing bowl — live the pebble frogs. They are small, brown, round, and unremarkable in every respect except the one everyone in the valleys half-disbelieves: each night they freeze solid. Glass-hard. No heartbeat, no breath, ice in their blood, a stone among stones — and at first light they thaw and hop off as if nothing had been decided. The herder children have a game of finding them in the morning before the sun does and holding them, cold and perfect in the palm, waiting for the twitch. The frogs do not consider this a game. But they thaw anyway, every morning, and so the question of whether they died in the night is one the Pocket has quietly agreed not to ask. Everything here is a little bit the sea's leftover, surfacing on schedule. The frogs are just the ones who admit it.

---

**And the people.**

The human marks are old, faint, and few: the half-tumbled drystone corral, the overhang by the tarn with its obsidian flakes and split rodent bones, the line of waist-high cairns threading the basin floor at thirty-pace intervals, each capped with a fossil shell set spiral-upward, like the whole valley floor is a cabinet of ammonites opened to the sky. Along the fog-path, strung between poles, run the red wool fog-strings, dyed with lichen, and every mid-morning each thread beads with water the fog donates — a fence that harvests, a fence that gives back.

The herder families do not live in the Pocket. They use it the way you use a relative's house: seasonally, gratefully, with knowledge of where everything is kept. They bring the flocks up on the worst nights to the warm seam, where the mud never fully freezes. They read the cairns, mend the fog-strings, know which vizcacha rocks to leave alone. If asked, they would say the Pocket belongs to the rosettes. They are probably right. The humans are simply the ones who understood early that the mountain inhales at mid-morning and exhales at dusk, and that a wise tenant hangs red wool along the breath, and waits for the beads.
