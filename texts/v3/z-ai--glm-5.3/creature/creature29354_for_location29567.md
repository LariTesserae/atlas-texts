# Who Lives Here

Start with the hard answer: nobody was asked to live in the Cradle Ward, and nobody can quite say how they came to. Ask a resident and they will tell you they were walking, or waiting, or — this comes up more than you'd expect — *grieving*, and the doorway was there, and it was warm in the only way this place is warm, which is to say it was promising. The residents did not choose the ward. The ward is considering them. That is a different arrangement, and everyone here has quietly made peace with it, or is making peace with it, at roughly the pace the stone makes peace with water.

## The residents themselves

There are a few dozen. You will have trouble counting them because they have trouble counting themselves — the ward revises its own population the way it revises its corners, and on some mornings a neighbor you've spoken with for weeks has a faint doubled edge, the current version and the next version held together, and you understand without being told that they are mid-consideration, being asked by the architecture what shape of person they intend to settle into.

What they share, more than any face or history: they are all people who were between things. Between jobs, between homes, between convictions, between the death of someone and the life that comes after. The ward collects the interstitial. A person with a firm, finished sense of themselves walks through the ward and finds it drafty and pointless and leaves within the hour, and the exit obligingly appears for them because they have genuinely stopped wanting it. The people who stay are the ones whose needing-to-be-somewhere-else had gone soft and unaddressed, and who found, in the cold damp attention of the place, the first thing in years that seemed willing to wait for them to finish deciding.

They are not sad, mostly. They are *unhurried in a way that looks like sadness from outside*. They drink the condensation off their own windowsills because it is the freshest water. They keep odd hours, or rather they keep the ward's hours — up during the long inhale, drowsy through the exhale, so that the whole population yawns in a slow unison that a visitor would find eerie and a resident would find companionable. They greet each other by standing near each other for a while. In a place where sound barely travels, proximity is the whole language.

And they build. This is the part that surprises visitors, because the ward also seems to build itself, and the residents' hands and the ward's intentions are not clearly separable. A woman on the third street has spent four months on a single windowsill, fitting it, letting it sulk, refitting it, and the windowsill now matches her patience exactly — you can see it in the grain. A man who arrived after a divorce is building a staircase that terminates nowhere yet, and he is not rushing it, and everyone respects this, because a staircase built in a hurry is how you get rooms that lead to previous versions of yourself, and he has had quite enough of previous versions. The coral-reef density of the ward, the shouldering houses, the balconies leaning like an audience — some of that is the ward's own dreaming, and some of it is a few dozen people working slowly at the only task that ever mattered to them, which is *making a place that has room in it for the person they are still becoming*. The ward has never told them who it's being built for. They have each privately guessed: *me*. And the ward has not corrected anyone, because the ward is considering everyone.

## The almosts

Then there is the fauna, such as it is. The pale motes in the standing water — the ones that might be simple organisms or complicated weather — seem to be the ward's earliest drafts of life, sketches it hasn't committed to. They swim in small persistent loops, and if you cup them in your hands and carry them to a different pool, they resume their looping exactly where they left off, unbothered, as if location were one of the things they haven't decided about yet.

The flat grey thing that crosses the northern lane every few days is another matter. It is almost certainly not an animal. It moves like something on an errand, slow and obligated, and it is the only creature in the ward that the residents speak of with a particular lowered-voice care. Some say it is the ward counting itself. Some say it is what a doubt looks like when it has been given a patrol route and a salary. The residents step over the place where it crosses, not out of fear — there is very little fear here, fear requires firmness — but out of something adjacent to courtesy. When it passes, the buildings lean very slightly, the same lean they give the wind from the unlisted direction. Everything here knows a colleague when it sees one.

## The builder

There is also, of course, the one with the permit nobody has seen. Nobody has seen the builder either, and the residents have reached an agreement about the builder that they never say aloud: it is better not to look too hard. Not because it is dangerous — the ward's weirdness is substrate, not threat — but because you might find it, and then what? It is entirely possible that the builder is the grey thing. It is entirely possible the builder is the breathing — the day-long inhale, the season-long exhale — and the hands are the mist, and the tools are the dawn condensation, and the plans are the reflections in the standing water. The residents suspect, in the vague settled way they suspect most things, that the ward is building itself the way a body grows itself, cell by patient cell, and that the permit was issued by the same authority that authorizes a seed. You do not ask a seed for paperwork. You wait.

## The others in the water

Which brings us to the ones everyone reaches for, once. The reflections in the standing pools are not the residents, but the residents have a relationship with them, and it is the closest thing the ward has to a religion: an unspoken, tender, slightly guilty watchfulness. In the reflection, these streets are finished and occupied. The residents do not believe those are their future selves. But they have noticed — and this is said only late at night, in the shorthand of people who stand near each other — that the reflected streets have *the right number of people*, and that the count is a few dozen, and that the figures there walk with the unhurried gait of people who have finished deciding. Whether the water holds the ward's hope, or its memory running in the wrong direction, or something load-bearing that must simply not be touched, nobody knows, and the fences of pure courtesy hold, and everyone reaches for it exactly once — usually in the first week — and then spends the rest of their residence not reaching, which is, several of them have said, the first discipline they've ever kept.

## The largest resident

And then the ward itself, which is the biggest inhabitant and the most undeniable. Not the buildings — the *considering*. The attention that takes your body heat into its thermal ledger, that adds your frequented paths to its design, that leans to listen to the new wind. The residents live inside it the way mitochondria live inside a cell, and they know this, and it is not a horror to them. It is the whole appeal. Every one of them came here unfinished, and the ward is a thing that is also unfinished, and it takes their shape into account as it sets, and they take its shape into account as they do, and together — stone, mist, motes, the grey thing on its rounds, the figures in the water, the few dozen people building their windowsills — they are all doing the same slow work.

The Cradle Ward does not know who it is for. Its residents have each quietly answered *me* and are not wrong, exactly, and not right, exactly. They are the ones who stayed to find out.
