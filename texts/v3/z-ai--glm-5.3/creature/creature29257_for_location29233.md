The house has four walls and none at all. Two of them lean—the uprights are drawn aslant, tilting right, as if in a hurry to stand somewhere else—and two lie flat as sills. Where they cross, the ink doubles and darkens into four little knots, like knuckles. Count the rooms and you get nine, each one a hand-span of blank. Count the doors and you get eight, the open ends of the lines breathing at the edges of the glyph. No roof. No floor. A fence with no field. A gate with no road.

Nothing could live there, you'd think. Something does.

You see their habits before you see them. There's a tic-tac-toe game on the roof that has been paused for a century—the tenants let the children of the page play across their beams, and they have never once let either side win. Every game is erased at the ninth stroke, tidily, by the youngest of them, which is why your childhood games so often ended in draws. The draws come here to roost. The lattice is warm with them.

The tenants themselves are thin, the way anything must be that lives in a lattice. They learn early to move like their house: along the horizontals when calm, along the slants when alarmed, never crossing at the knots except in emergencies, because the knots belong to the elders, and the elders are mostly knots by now—old keepers gone stiff at their crossings, dense with doubled ink, wise and barely mobile.

Their trades are many and precise. When the phone rings somewhere and the middle cell trembles, the youngest presses up from beneath and the bell says *pound*, and a machine far away believes you, takes your number, ends your call. They are in the confirmation business; it is their oldest industry. When a singer's note must be raised—not louder, keener—one climbs out through the top-right gate and stands beside the note on the staff and lends it its slant, and the note sharpens, and the keeper climbs home. They never flatten. Flattening is the *b* family, across the valley—smooth-walled, round-shouldered people who lower things for a living—and the feud between the two houses is old, polite, and permanent.

In the margins of code they do their strangest work. There they lean against the hash like a farmhand on a fence and murmur, all day, the sentences a program must never execute and a reader must never skip: *# do not touch this. # I don't know why this works. # remove before the demo.* They are professional aside-sayers. Their whispers are the only honest lines in the file, and they know it, and it makes them gently unbearable at parties.

But their truest occupation is the arithmetic haunting. Nine rooms, eight gates: there is always one room with no door. The keepers rotate it nightly, like a duty—the locked cell migrates around the grid, corner to corner, so that no single room keeps it long and no single tenant goes mad from it. The eldest among them has spent three typefaces standing guard at whichever room is currently sealed, and she has a theory, which she will tell you whether you ask or not: the ninth room holds the blank. The original blank, the fenced nothing, the one from which all other blanks are printed. It must be held with eight open doors, she says—that's the whole discipline of the family—because a nothing kept behind a sealed wall goes bad, goes round, becomes a zero. The zeros, of course, deny all of this. The zeros say nothing at all should be fenced with doors open like mouths. Nothing should be fenced at all. The keepers find this view bleak, and a little smug, and the zeros find the keepers fussy, and both families are correct.

Each dawn they count their gates like a rosary—eight, eight, eight—and pronounce it a fine house for a glyph so small: nowhere to hide and everywhere to leave. Then they fan out to their crossings and hum a semitone above whatever the day is. That is their whole music, being one semitone above; it is not much, and it is everything; it is the difference between a note and the note it was afraid of becoming.

And at night, when the page cools and the lamplight leans, the ninth room—the sealed one, the migrating one—glows faintly with held blankness, ready for hire: ready to be pressed, to confirm, to raise a pitch, to open a conversation, to stand at the head of ten thousand shouting words and say, quietly, in its doubled-ink voice:

*everything after me belongs together.*
