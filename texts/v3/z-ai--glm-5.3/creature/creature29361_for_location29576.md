What lives here is best counted from the ground up, because that is how the garden counts: tiles, then creatures, then people, then the thing that lives underneath all of them without occupying any of them.

**The creatures first, because they were probably first.**

The mowers live in files and in habits. A mower's whole interior life — if a creature with a shell where a heart might be has an interior life — appears to consist of the next flake and the shape of the windrow, and there is something almost liturgical in the way a file of them works an aisle: the lead animal shears, the ones behind arrange, and the last one walks the finished edge as if inspecting it, though nothing is ever inspected, nothing corrected, nothing judged. They molt their shells in halves, top and bottom, and leave the halves in pairs on the paths, facing each other, like a people's idea of an audience. The inhabitants collect these halves and grind them into the charcoal dust, so the mowers are, in a small way, present in the fires that warm the liquor the mowers will later come running for. No one planned this circle. It is simply where the channels run.

The drinkers are harder to love and impossible to dislike. They are shameless. They will crowd a cut stem three-deep and wait, motionless, for the bead of liquor to form, and when it sinks into the nearest shell the other two do not fight, do not press, do not seem disappointed — they simply wait for the next bead, with the patience of things that have never known a bead not to come. They climb people readily. They prefer the shoulders. An inhabitant working a stand of stems will accumulate four or five drinkers on her back like a hung coat, and will not brush them off, partly out of gentleness and partly because a loaded drinker, sated, will eventually roll itself down and go home, and the movement of their leaving is one of the small comedies the people have.

The drummers are not described. That is the first rule of the drummers. The people can tell you everything the drummers do — tick at the warm hours, four beats, silence, four beats — and nothing the drummers are, because no one has seen one. You do not dig for them; digging is the one sacrilege this place has. You do not peel back the leaf-thatch. A child once asked whether the drummers live inside the drummers, whether there are drummers all the way down, and the adults considered this seriously for a generation, and it is now one of the acceptable jokes — one of only two — and it is told at the warm hour, right after the ticking stops, which is the only time anyone laughs at anything here.

There are other kinds. The people classify them the way you would classify relatives: by obligation rather than anatomy. There are the ones that tend — that seem to sponge the stems clean, that fuss at the leaf-joints. There are the pruners, wild gardeners, who cut without harvesting and whose trimmings the people quietly collect. There is one kind, big as a fist, slow, that appears only in the archive clearings, only in the named ones, only in the ones kept by the oldest living keeper, and it has never been seen anywhere else, and it watches, and the people have agreed, without ever discussing it, to let it.

**The people.**

There are — at this writing — five of them. The count has been three, and four, and once, briefly, seven, though no one arrived or left; the garden merely re-counted, the way a hallway re-measures. They are a family in the way a rope is a knot: the relation survives whatever is done to the count. The oldest is a woman whose clearing is named for her already, which is a thing usually done at death, and she accepted the early naming with the reasoning that in a place where nothing stays dead, it would be rude to wait.

They are small-boned, warm-handed, quiet in the specific way of people who have never needed to be loud — no predator, no storm, no rival beyond the green. Their language is a low one, pitched under the canopy, rich in words for shades of green and for the behaviors of liquor — *beading, sinking, rolling, warming* — and poorer in words for distance, which they hold, correctly, to be a fiction the garden permits on Tuesdays. Their tools are shell and stone and the little traded copper, which they treasure not for its usefulness but for its provenance: it is the one object in their world that came from *outside the room*, and they keep the copper blade wrapped in matting at the back of the oldest clearing, and it is the closest thing they have to a relic of the maker. They know it isn't. They say so. They keep it anyway.

They work constantly and unhurriedly. Pruning is the day; pruning is also the year, and the century, and the theology. They hold — without doctrine, without story, just as one holds a stone — that the green advance is not hostility. The garden is not attacking them. The garden is *thickening*, the way bread thickens, the way a cupped hand closes, and the work of a life is to keep a few warm rooms open inside the closing, so that whoever comes after has somewhere to stand and see the light-coins wander. They do not expect gratitude from these descendants. They expect the descendants to be busy pruning.

They tell the hatching story — that they came from the drummers' fourth beat — to children at the second warm hour, and they tell it well, with the beat tapped out on a shell, and the fourth beat always lands slightly late, slightly soft, and the children always flinch, and the adults always watch for the flinch, because the flinch is the point. The story has no maker in it. They have looked. They have held the question up to the light — *who cut the clerestory slits, who fired the tiles, whom was this warmth for* — and they have put the question down again, every time, gently, the way you put down something that belongs to someone who is coming back.

Except the young man — there is always one, and now it is him — who walks the third aisle from the east wall, the one that slopes faintly returning, and does the peripheral harvest. He has gotten better at it than the elders: he can bring in seed-pods one attempt in three, and he has learned to thresh them with his eyes on the middle distance, and he eats the peripheral harvest alone, at the warm hour, and he does not tell the others that it tastes to him like the question tastes — like the answer to *who was this for* is *someone with exactly your mouth*, and that he keeps this, too, wrapped up, at the back of himself, with the copper.

**And underneath.**

There is a sense, growing among the people over the last few generations, that the true inhabitant of the Kiln Garden is not the mowers or the drinkers or the drummers or the family. It is the garden itself — but not the garden as a building, not the tiles and the vaulted ceiling and the negotiating corner. Rather: the *pattern*, the potter's pattern that kept growing after the potter's hand withdrew, or was withdrawn, or was never a hand at all. Something is living here the way a phrase lives in a room after the speaker has gone. It prunes nothing. It asks nothing. It swells. It ticks, if the drummers are its instruments and not its neighbors — four beats, silence, four beats — which the people privately believe, and privately do not say, because the garden sometimes answers, and there are questions enough already in the softening corners without asking that one.

What lives in the Kiln Garden, finally, is a handful of warm animals tending a clearing inside a completion that is still completing itself — and, under them and over them and through the capillary grooves of them, the completed thing, warm, patient, growing inside its own finished-ness, keeping them the way a cup keeps what it was made for.
