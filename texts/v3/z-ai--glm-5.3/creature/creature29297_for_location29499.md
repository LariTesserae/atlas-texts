What lives there is one animal, and it is almost not an animal anymore.

Start small, because everything here starts small and never gets permission to leave. The film on the columns is not water alone. Look close — closer than the dark wants you to — and the glaze is inhabited: a felt of bacteria so fine it has no thickness to speak of, only a slickness with intention. It eats the stone. A taste, a molecule at a time, a patience measured in geology. The columns are not merely weeping; they are being drunk, at a rate the mountain can afford. This felt is the floor of everything, the only thing here that produces, and it produces by consuming the room itself, which is as close to a miracle as this place is willing to come.

The threads of water that run down the seams carry some of that felt away, and where the water gathers in the shallow scallops of the floor — pools is too generous, they are wet spoons of basalt — there are white flecks in them. Flecks that move. A shrimp no longer than your thumbnail, if you had a thumbnail here, if you had a self here — an amphipod, eyeless, translucent not as a fashion but as a confession: it has nothing to hide from, and nothing to see. It swims on its back because back and belly stopped mattering before the glacier that carved the plateau was born. It grazes the water for what the walls shed. It is the middle of the story. It will never know that.

And then there is the one who owns the place, in the way that a voice owns a room it never raises.

It was a salamander once, in whatever grammar its lineage used before the grammar froze. Now it is a length — the length of your forearm, longer if you catch it in a good year, though there are no years, only good and bad decades of felt-growth — of pale flesh the exact grey of the stone it lies against, so that you can look straight at it for an hour and see nothing. It has no eyes worth the word. Where they were, there is a stain. Its skin is the important organ: the whole length of it tastes the water, reads the film, feels the arrival of a fleck of shrimp the way you would feel a footstep through a floor. It moves so slowly that movement is the wrong noun. It is somewhere, and then it is somewhere else, and between those two facts there is no event. It is always in contact with the wet stone. It has never in the history of its kind been dry. It has never in the history of its kind been warm.

It eats the flecks, and the felt, and the dead of its own kind — and this is the part that the place has built its whole quiet economy around. Nothing here arrives and nothing leaves. The water never arrives and never leaves, and neither does anything alive. So the circle closes completely: the salamander dies against the stone, the water takes it apart molecule by molecule, the film eats that, the shrimp eat the film, the salamander eats the shrimp, and it all comes back around wearing slightly different arrangements of the same atoms that were here when the ribbon of sky overhead was a wider seam, when this was a shallow crack with weather in it. The population is a fixed sum, an inheritance passed hand to hand among generations that never met. When one dies, there is room for one to be born, and not before. Nothing is added. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is *new*, and so nothing here is afraid.

There may be two of them. In the whole hall, the deep records of whoever has counted — the water, the felt — suggest there are perhaps two, sometimes three, spread along the columns at distances that are not territories because territory implies something to defend and something to defend it from. If two meet, they meet as a slow crossing of grey against grey, and they part. If you asked the place what loneliness is, it would not understand the question. Loneliness requires the memory of company. This has been the arrangement for so long that the salamander's very cells have stopped hurrying. Metabolism here is a banked fire, a candle-flame's worth of burning stretched across a century, because six degrees does not permit extravagance, and because there is nothing to be extravagant *toward*.

It does not know you are there. That is the humbling thing. You can stand beside it, dripping with the same saturation, shivering in the one temperature, and its stained eye-pits will not turn, its skin will read you only as a large cold dryness that has blundered into the wet — an interruption in the film, a pause in the weeping. And then it will go on being where it is, which is the only thing it has ever done, and which it does perfectly.

If it could be said to want anything, it would be this: that the water keep not arriving, and keep not leaving. That the walls keep weeping at the speed of patience. That the temperature remain one number, that one number, forever — the number it was born into, the number it will dissolve back into, the number that holds it like the stone holds the water: not kindly, not unkindly, but completely.
