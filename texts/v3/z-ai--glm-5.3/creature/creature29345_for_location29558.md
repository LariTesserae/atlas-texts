It is a basin — fifty miles of it, maybe more, ringed by the low hills the travelers cross and floored with something that is not quite grass and not quite water. They call it the ringing plain. The stalks that grow there are hollow, reedy, jointed like bamboo but thin as whipcords, and there are billions of them, and every one of them is tuned. The wind moving through that forest of pipes is what the travelers hear three days out — not one note, but a chord so wide and so sustained that it stops being music and becomes weather. The chord changes with the seasons. In spring it runs high and silvery. By late summer it has dropped into something you feel in the sternum, a bronze moan, the sound a mountain would make if a mountain could grieve beautifully.

What lives there has grown up inside the sound the way fish grow up inside water.

Start small. The plain's insects have no ordinary voices; they've abandoned chirping entirely, because chirping is useless in a place that never quiets. Instead they ride the chord. There's a lacewing there that lands on a stalk and straddles it, and the hollow reed becomes the insect's body extended — the lacewing angles the stalk against the wind, changes its pitch by fractions, and speaks through it. Whole conversations conducted on borrowed instruments. When two of them want each other, they tune the same stalk together, leaning in from opposite sides until the reed agrees with both of them at once.

The birds there sing in intervals, not melodies. A melody gets swallowed. What survives is anything that happens to fall inside the plain's harmony — and so over generations the birds have become the only animals in the world that stop singing when the wind rises and start when it dies, inverting themselves to fit their world, filling the plain's silences the way other birds fill their noise.

The large animals are stranger. There are deer on the rim of the basin, ordinary enough, but in the deep plain the grazing beasts are built like lyres: rib cages splayed and open, hide stretched thin over resonant bone, so that the chord passes through them rather than around them. They don't flee from loudness. They move toward it. A hunter on the plain watches them drift across the grass like notes seeking their key, stopping where the sound is truest, standing for hours in the good resonance with their eyes closed. It's thought they feed that way — that the vibration shakes seeds loose from the stalks and drives the sap and sweetens something in the reed joints they strip and chew. But watching them, you suspect they'd stand there anyway. It's also thought — though nobody has proven it — that they can hear one another's ribs, that each animal's chest rings slightly differently, a signature, a name.

And then there are the people.

They live in the basin's center, where the chord is deepest and the wind is steadiest, and they are shaped by their address. They build almost nothing that doesn't sound. Their houses are bundles of the great stalks, cut and ranked and pitched, so that every dwelling is an instrument the weather plays and the family lives inside the note. From a ridge, their town looks like a reed bed that organized itself into a huddle. From inside, it is never quiet, and they consider that the entire point.

They speak in overlapping parts. This is the thing travelers take longest to understand — that a conversation there might involve four people at once, each holding a different line of the chord, and that meaning lives in the intervals between them, not in any single voice. A lone speaker on the plain is a truncated thing, half a sentence. They keep crickets in woven cages the way other towns keep clocks, and when the wind dies and the great chord thins out to near silence — the plain's one terrifying weather, the dead calm, which comes a few days each winter — the whole town goes out and stands in the grass and breathes, and the old ones tap hollow sticks against hollow stalks, keeping a heartbeat in the silence until the wind remembers to return. They will tell you, without embarrassment, that the silence is the only thing they fear. Everything else that exists, exists because the sound holds it up.

One more thing, and then you should go, because the rim light is going.

The travelers' reports never agree on this part, and the plain-people don't discuss it, but the deep geology under the basin is shot through with caves — wind-carved flutes in the bedrock, miles of them — and the true floor of the chord, the fundamental that lives in your teeth on the third day, is too low for any reed. Something under there is sounding. Maybe it's just the caves and the wind, a geology the size of a sea happening to exhale. But the plain-people, who know the chord the way you know your own pulse, will tell you quietly, if you earn it, that the chord has a direction. That it isn't a thing the wind does to the plain. That the plain — the reeds, the deer with their splayed ribs, the people, the whole ringing bowl of it — is a thing the sound does to the wind.

They aren't worshipful about it. They just live near the low end of the voice, the way you'd live near a neighbor, and keep the silences out.
