# Who Lives Here

The pika will tell you first, because the pika is the shelf's register-keeper, its town crier, its smallest accountant. There are perhaps nine of them — a loose colony strung along the seep-line like beads — and each one maintains a haypile in the lee of the headwall, a personal winter's worth of groundsel and saxifrage cut and stacked and turned with a fussiness that would embarrass a quartermaster. They harvest all day in the compressed tempo the altitude demands: quick, quick, quick while the sun is on, because the sun is the only clock and it runs short. When you move through their federation of territories they do not flee so much as *announce* — a thin whistle-sneeze from somewhere invisible in the cushion moss, followed by a rustle that travels through the felt like a rumor. You will almost never see the one who warned you. You will see the others, frozen mid-harvest, small russet shapes gone oval with attention, deciding whether you matter.

They drink, when they drink, from the rock pockets — the washbasin worlds — and this is how you learn the pockets have order of seniority. Each basin holds its tenant. The stonefly nymph in the third pocket from the lip is a flat dark leaf of a creature, armored against the cold water it will live in for years before it ever, if ever, earns wings. The fairy shrimp in the two shallow basins near the stone ring spend their entire existence — hatched, swum, bred, died — inside the weeks between the frost at each end of the season, a whole biography conducted in a thumb of meltwater. The algae raft wherever it can. Ten meters of barren gravel is, to these tenants, the whole distance between worlds, and they are loyal to their basins the way islands are loyal to themselves. Nothing patrols between them. The gravel is nobody's country.

Except, of course, that everything is somebody's country. The fox — one fox, a compact blue-gray animal with a white-trimmed muzzle, who crosses the shelf's single walkable spine on a schedule it keeps so faithfully its scat becomes a kind of calendar — treats the whole federation as its pantry. The pikas know its smell and have opinions about it. It does not hunt the shelf often. It passes through, audits, moves on, and its passage is the only thing that ever makes the entire meadow go quiet at once, the choughs included.

The choughs. They do not live *on* the shelf; they live on the drop, on the updraft, in the black squadrons that ride the invisible escalator of air rising off the valley cloud. The shelf is their marketplace. Each dawn they come down to comb the frost line, walking the moss with a businessman's stride, taking the insects the cold has slowed to a speed that can be caught — and the shelf tolerates this the way a town tolerates the traders who are technically foreigners but who arrive every single day. At a raptor's pass — and the updraft that carries the choughs also carries, higher, the things that own them — the whole shelf holds its breath for a count of five. Moss, pikas, choughs, bees: all of it pauses on the same beat, and resumes on the next one, and the pause and the resumption are as reliable as the condensation.

The bumblebees live at the far edge of what bee-ness can endure. They are dark, moth-heavy, fur like a coat cut for a colder planet, and they work the saxifrage at a pace the air barely permits — cold-blooded determination is exactly the phrase, because they are literally running on heat they must manufacture themselves, shivering their flight muscles warm before each takeoff, spending sun-energy like misers who know the bank closes at dusk. There are not many of them. There do not need to be. There is only one shelf of flowers, and it is theirs.

And the humans, who live here no longer but whose residence the shelf has not yet finished canceling. Whoever stacked the low stone ring beside the seep-line came for the water — this is legible, written in the placement if nowhere else — and came with a bone needle and a hand-peg and a strap of hide and nothing else that history has permitted to survive. They understood the mountain's single generosity and sat down beside it, in the one green room among three thousand meters of stone, and drank from the same pockets the pika drinks from now. The cairn by the lip leans. There is no path to it. The route that reached it lived in a body, and the body is gone, and the shelf — young, unfinished, still deciding — keeps the ring and the worked wood under the overhang the way it keeps everything: wet, cold, patient, and lit to a saturation that makes even absence look vivid.

The whole population could be counted in an afternoon. Nobody here is large. Nobody here is numerous. But the place is *full* — a federation of small wet lives, each loyal to its basin, its haypile, its furrow of seepage, running the daily cycle of on and off between one frost and the next. Everything here is fast when the sun is out, and nothing when it isn't. And the mountain, still deciding what to do with its shelf, so far seems to have decided this: to hold them all.
