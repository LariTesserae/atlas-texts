Who lives here is a short list, and the shortness is not poverty. It is an economy in its finished form: a hundred and forty structures supporting two mosses, a cushion plant, a grass, a fern, one flower, one moth, and whatever is too small to have been counted yet. A town this size, anywhere lower, would support thousands of kinds and lose track of them all. This one keeps a census.

Begin with water, because everything here begins with it and no rain ever does. Every drop any living thing drinks in this town has been through the town first — beaded on a wall, held a full minute at a band-line, run down into a channel, carried under the pavement through plumbing older than any drinker. The town chews everyone's water, and swallows at midday, audibly. And every resident is, one way or another, either a tenant of the sound or an employee of the fog. Most are both.

The mosses are the oldest tenants and behave like it — unimpressed, permanent, keeping records. There are two of them, or one of them living two lives. On the towers it wears the green coat, and the coat is a ledger: loudness is warmth, warmth is growth, so the fattest coat hangs on the loudest pipe, and you can read a tower's whole career in the depth of its green. In the low runs between rows it lives the other life — black, waterlogged, lying like wet hair, trembling in the drone. Neither life needs roots, which is the only way to live on a pavement. Moss drinks through its skin, dries to a crisp by noon, swells with the fog tide by three, freezes at dusk without complaint — fitted, to the hour, for one day of this town's clock, repeated. If anything here was made for the place, it is moss; but nothing here was made, so say instead that moss is the shape this town's weather takes when it is left alone long enough.

The cushion plants are the cisterns and, unexpectedly, the ovens. Those spongy hummocks that give underfoot like shoulders are packed tight enough to hold the day's thin sun the way a mattress holds a body; inside a cushion it is warmer than anywhere else on the plain. Lean close at noon and you can smell it — a faint greenhouse breath, the only smell up here that isn't stone. They bank the fog water against the clear key. They are fed, entirely, by the moth, whose droppings are the one nutrient import a town above the rain will ever see, so the cushions sit at the top of the local food chain by doing nothing but being warm, spongy, and patient. If they have a municipal function, it is this: they are where the town keeps things it wants to survive the night.

The hairgrass is the only thing in town that dances. Its roots hold the gravel seams; its pollen rides the same wind the town plays — a tenant doing its own business on the landlord's instrument. It cannot hear the chord, but you can watch it obey the chord: seed-heads shivering visibly in the drone, wisps conducting. In a place where the music is everywhere and invisible, the grass is where the music can be seen.

The fern lives in a shadow, and only in a shadow — the north-facing shade of certain pipes, the moist side, the side the sun never audits. Its entire habitat is a standing consequence of the town: no towers, no shade; no shade, no fern. It is the most domestic creature in a town no one built, and its green is the one saturated green in a world of gray and white, so cold and deep it looks wet even when it isn't — because it has never in its life needed dry manners, having always lived on the town's shady lee, drinking what the walls give up. On fog afternoons it spores, which is the only flying the fern will ever do; its spores are single cells that die in minutes of dry air, so it sends them out only inside the tide, and the tide drains through the notch at dusk and carries them over the rim — where the weather begins, and no spore survives the weather. The fern has been emigrating for as long as anyone could count. It is still here.

The flower is the cushions' bloom — it rises out of the hummock crowns, which is one reason the moth manures them so faithfully. Small, white, violet-veined. White for fog light; the veins are landing lights, pitched to an eye that hunts in murmur. And it opens not by sun, not by clock, but by key: when the clear chord drops into the fog key, the flower opens, the whole town blooms at once for a few hours, and closes when the fog drains out. On clear days you would never know the place had a flower. The town does its living in the fog key — drinking, blooming, flying — and keeps the clear key for working: the loud warm drying chord of midday, frost shaken off, ledgers written. Its citizens keep the same split day, and the flower keeps it most honestly of all.

Which brings the census to the moth, and the census is mostly moth. It is the town's one citizen, and the only thing here with a face.

It is white and powdery, its wings like flakes of frost — and that is the honest description, because it looks like nothing so much as the town's own frost taking wing: the caps, the lace collars, the powder on the Throat's rim, and then frost with legs. No bird comes over the rim, no bat, no other insect; nothing with an appetite has ever arrived. So the whiteness is not camouflage against anything — there is nothing to be white against. It is engineering: scales that refuse to let wet get a grip, fog and frost shed the way a loud tower sheds frost, by never holding still. A moth built by a town. Everything about it is a cold-weather answer, and cold holds the clock, so the moth lives its whole life inside the town's day, on the town's hours, and has never once been late.

It wakes to the scatter of caps cracking off; that percussion is the town's alarm and its own. It does not fly during the tuning hour. A hundred and forty pipes searching off-pitch is illegible weather — you cannot tell a gust from a gap in that noise, and the moth navigates by the gaps. At full chord the wind becomes a shape it can feel: a gust arrives as a swell, dies as a thinning, and the thinning is a door. Columns rise from the moss in the still air between gusts, mate in the wind-shadows — three paces of slowed air in a tower's lee — and are under cover before the next whistle. The moth never looks at the wind. It listens for the wind's absence, and the town, by singing every change of the wind all day long, tells it exactly when that absence is scheduled.

Its economics close every loop. The caterpillars graze the black moss, fattest under the loudest pipes — the town literally feeds its citizen with its voice; the drone is a pasture. Their droppings fall into the cushions, where in return the caterpillars sleep at night in the only warm beds on the plain. The cushions hold water that feeds the moss that feeds the moth that manures the cushions. And in the fog key the moth calls at the flower, which has lit its landing lights. One animal performs the town's entire animal work — grazing, manuring, courting, pollinating — and one animal is enough, because the town does everything else.

At night, when the mouths seal one by one, trebles first, the moth cannot fly, so it goes to the one building that never closes: the Throat. Hundreds gather in its mouth on cold nights, powdering the rim white — a ring of frost that is alive. The Throat's note is below hearing, but vibration shakes frost off, and the Throat vibrates all night, and so the town's deepest voice runs under its sleeping citizen like a hand kept moving over a blanket. The moths do not know why they sleep there. They sleep there.

It lives nowhere else — not because it can't leave. The rim is a day's walk, and a still evening would carry a moth over it. But past the rim is a plain with no town in it: nothing to sing the weather, no walls to bead, no warm towers, no Throat. The moth's range is not a territory; it is a municipality, and it ends exactly at the rim, because beyond the rim there is no government. Only weather.

Then the uncounted. The six pans — shallow eyes of black water in their lace collars — are surely not unemployed; water that freezes nightly and thaws by noon almost never sits empty of small lives, things with no ears and therefore no use for the town's whole enterprise, dying nightly and untroubled. And in the buried channels the water runs dark. Whether anything lives down in the plumbing — whether *where life once was* is past tense all the way down — is a question the local custom, if there were locals, would leave alone.

And the census keeps one class no other town can claim: it counts its own dead. On the deepest fog days the chord thickens with voices that belong to no standing tower — the buried rows, singing through their own ancestors' rubble, and no one has ever found one. Elsewhere the dead come off the rolls. Here they still pay their way in sound, and the town keeps them on.

The census, taken as well as it can be:

- Two mosses, or one living two lives: the green ledger-keeper on the towers, the wet black hair in the runs.
- Cushion plants: cisterns, ovens, landlords of the flower.
- Hairgrass: the visible metronome.
- One fern: resident of shadows, emigrating nightly by fog and dying at the town limits.
- One flower: open in the fog key only, landing lights lit for a moth.
- One moth: the entire animal kingdom of the place, on a civic schedule, asleep in the Throat.
- The small uncounted in the pans, and whatever the custom leaves alone.
- The dead, still singing.
- Any visitor, briefly: entered in the ledger the moment their ears begin to ring in the town's key.

That is who lives here: a town of unknown builders, governed by erosion, staffed by wind, decorated a few fog hours a day — one citizen and no crime, one flower and no market, a full choir and no composer. It has been dying for as long as it has been a town, every century a little shorter and a little higher, and when the last voice finally thins past the reach of ears the green will go, and the black hair with it, and the caterpillars, and last of all the moth — a citizen who never knew the town was ending, because it never knew the town was there. But that end is far off. The place is at peace, and so is its population: governed all its life without its knowledge, provided for without its consent, which may be the only arrangement that works up here. The alternative is weather.
