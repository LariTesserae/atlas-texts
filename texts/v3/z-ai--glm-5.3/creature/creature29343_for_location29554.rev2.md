The people of the Shelf announce themselves by omission. You come up the stair through the dawn steam and the first thing you notice is what the town doesn't do: it doesn't shout. Over open water a voice carries whole, and in a lane a shoulder wide there is no privacy in sound, so the Shelf has grown soft mouths and loud hands. What looks from the punt like four hundred people ignoring one another turns out to be four hundred people talking. A mason on his roof and a warden in her lane can hold a complete argument beneath the hearing of a sleeping child: *your gutter's blocked* is two fingers and a tilt; *eels in the run* is a knuckle drawn along the jaw; *not now, she's sleeping* is a flat palm lowered, like settling water. Lane-sign runs to forty-odd gestures and is learned the way speech is learned — by being wrong in public until you're right. Strangers find it eerie for a day. Then one morning you answer a flick with a flick in the middle of your own sentence, and you have been enrolled.

The lanes have made a people to fit them. Shelf folk are narrow through the shoulder and light on their feet — what else could live in a town where you walk on other people's roofs — and they come through the world sideways by instinct, presenting less of themselves to doorways, weather, and company. A wide man on the Shelf is loved, but he is a scheduling problem. There are eight-year-olds who have crossed the town fifty times without once wading the lanes; the lanes, to them, are gutters, eel-runs, and where grown-ups go. Hands are the town's other face: cold from the dew fences at first light, calloused from the quarry, smelling of eel from the run-season until frost, so that for a while each autumn the whole island smells like one household. A laugh at marsh volume is a scandal, and the man who produces one is treated as weather is treated — forgiven instantly, held against nobody, discussed for a week. Nobody sings in the lanes. There is nowhere to put a song. Where the singing goes, we'll come to.

The stair keeps the town's records, and the town keeps the stair's scars. Every child is cut by the newest step once — it is sharp as a new tool and only new for a year — and the scar sits at whatever height that step stood when it got you. Since the island rises and the stair is carved downward, one fresh tread every spring below the last, ages here are legible at a glance, shin to shin. High scars are old; the eldest's is up among the treads worn to butter; this year's scabs ride low, a hand's width above the water that used to be everywhere. It follows that the past is downhill. An old woman saying *before all this* tips her chin toward the water; a boy saying *when the rock is bigger* tips his up toward the rim. Past tense is downhill on the Shelf, future tense is up, and the population is always, in every sense, walking upward.

Understand, too, that all of them are new. A town four generations deep, worn steps and all, that did not exist within living memory — it is a strange thing to be, and the Shelf wears it without ceremony. The founding cohort, a dozen left now, were marsh-folk, punt-born, net-calloused, who watched their fishing ground shoulder into the sky and traded nets for picks in the middle of their lives; theirs are the only hands on the island that have held both trades. The eldest will tell you at the Warm Seam, ladle in hand, unprompted, that she caught a perch where the square is. That is not reminiscence. It is orientation — the founding generation's standing duty, informing every stranger that the ground used to be otherwise, the way you'd tell a child the stove is hot. Below her are people born on stone, and a child born this year will grow up certain the island was always this size. Between the perch and the certainty is the whole population, and in the crowd of the square you can stand within arm's reach of both ends.

A family here is not a house. Houses are pegged, not fused, raiseable in a day if the day comes, and nobody pours an identity into what water could repossess. A family is a cistern — the stone-lined dark under the kitchen, older than whatever kitchen happens to be standing over it, breathing cold up through the floor, holding the name the way other towns hold land. Betrothals are conducted accordingly: the suitor's people are invited down the ladder with a lamp to look at the cistern, and they come up knowing. The household's mold history is kept honestly, like a merchant's books, because a concealed damp patch before a wedding is the local form of fraud, and the wardens have heard them all. Rank, meanwhile, is furniture — measured in steps above the seep-line where your bed stands — so consequence lives in bed-legs, and there are households in the Crown that would sell the roof before the posts. Courtship itself happens overhead, on the Dry Way, in public, among the drying laundry, where you can study a family in its wash the way you'd study a face. There is a way of hanging your shirts beside someone's shirts that constitutes a declaration. And the grand gesture, the Shelf's serenade, is to re-pitch her mother's path in secret, so the morning after rain her doorstep is dry for the first time in years. Nobody doubts a man after that. Proposals and verdicts alike, on this island, are rendered in masonry.

Justice is masonry too. The code is short and old — every roof drains to the common run; no one builds below the high-water notch; the rim is quarried outward, never down; the Seam is shared by ladle and never sold; swallows are protected; no one sleeps off the rock — and its court is the gutter-wardens, who try a wet grievance in the lane concerned, ideally in the rain, so that the evidence can present itself. Judgments are not written down here. They are pitched. A ruling arrives as workmen and a new fall of gutter-stone, and there is no appeal because there is nothing to appeal to: the water now goes where it should, and both parties stand and watch it go. The strata are simple — the Skirt lives with the eels and the kilns, the Lanes live most lives, the Crown sleeps high — but the ladder runs both ways, because the one commodity rank cannot corner is the Warm Seam, which rises at the very top and is ladled to whoever is holding a cup. You can buy the island's stone, its eels, its lime, its children's vole-skins. You cannot buy a drink of it. The man with the tallest bed-legs queues at the Seam behind the kiln-children with the moth-smell on them, and says he likes it.

Trades are plural, because four hundred people cannot afford specialists. Ask a Shelf man what he does and he counts on his fingers first: quarry in the dry months, eels in the run-season, warden's faggot-work on the west rim in frost years. His wife hauls muck-tubs, keeps the dew-count, and is the second-best mason on her lane — and masonry here is its own strangeness, because the stone leaves the face soft as cheese, cut with horn, and hardens over two years into something that argues back. A Shelf mason works in a material still deciding what it wants to be; his craft is anticipation, carving soft for what he knows will be hard. His life is visible from his own roof: moss maps a wall's wet lines as it ages, so the oldest mason alive can stand in the square and read his career in shades of grey — bone-pale at the Crown where he finished last spring, kettle-black at the Skirt where he served his pick at fifteen. Below him the morning shift is children, wringing the dew fences into jugs at first light — the town's weather department, dairy, and alarm clock, in whatever order they manage — and underfoot the Meter keeps its tick so steadily that two lanes set breakfast by it, and in a wet year it runs quick and those lanes eat a quarter-hour early all season without anyone deciding anything. The night shift is the kilns, where moths crowd the flues in soft brown constellations and the burners are known by their hour rather than their faces. The children's economy runs on vole-skins. No adult can say when that started. It has the status of the weather.

The animal census is a family, and the town will introduce you to its members. First the Banker, because he'll be standing on your step: a single heron, owner of the second-newest tread, taxpayer in good standing, his dry croak the town's dusk bell. Children learn the sign for him early — a flat palm swept low, the one for letting a magistrate pass. His holdings appreciate at one step a year. There may have been several Bankers by now; the town declines to inquire. The swallows are citizens with legal standing, the town's air corps, nesting under the Dry Way in rows the way soldiers are counted; the midges they eat are the only real predator here, and are discussed the way weather is discussed, with resignation and statistics. Black moss is the calendar. The ferns of the north seep are protected by a law nobody wrote because nobody needed to. The east terrace's willows, eleven of them, cropped like sheep, are the town's only trees and its only lawn. And at the rim the last mussels of the exposed bed gape and click and die back each season, like slow rain falling upward — the oldest sound on the Shelf, the sound the lake makes while it stops being one, and the first sound every child born here is born into.

Now the silences, because a description of these people that left them out would be the loud version, the stranger's. There are three, and they are kept the same way: actively, in company, by everybody at once. The Ear, the capped fissure at the crown, where twice a day a kneeling child can hear water moving far below with a slow push and a slow pull that no marsh should have; nobody explains it; the plank goes back on. Out in the deep marsh, some nights, a single slow displacement of water, heard, never seen; the town has agreed, without ever agreeing, not to have that conversation. And the rule that nobody sleeps off the rock, though the stilt-barns are snug and the reed-yards plenty and both stand empty every night of the world. Ask about any of the three and you will not be scolded. Something better, and worse: the politeness comes down over the faces like a shutter, and someone remarks that the eels are late this year. Lane-sign, which can say forty useful things in a flick, has no sign for any of it. The Shelf will tell you that's because there's nothing to sign, and the manner in which they tell you is the last lesson in the language.

Ask after their god and they will laugh. Then watch them touch a fossil going in a door; ladle out water they could sell and don't; kneel at a capped fissure on a dare; unbuild a dead neighbor's house gently, in a day, by hand. The Shelf keeps its religion distributed among the furniture, and its one holy day has no fixed date. It comes the first clear night cold enough to stop every drip at once. The town wakes into a hush with no bottom to it, and the children run out laughing — to hear the quiet with their own ears, the only silence they will ever own. The grown go out too. They stand in their doorways with lamps unlifted, not laughing, not saying that the island's pulse has stopped for the night, and not not saying it either. On the Shelf this is what worship looks like: attendance.

Death is a salvage operation. Nothing is poured into foundations — grief least of all — so when someone dies the house comes apart within the day: pegs drawn, boards walked off in neighbors' arms, salvage rights exercised as a rite. By week's end the dead woman's lintel is over somebody's door and the town is eating bread under it. That is not sacrilege here; it is the local afterlife: to remain in use, among the living, unembarrassed. The body goes out on the water weighted with quarried stone, the island giving back to the water what the water once gave it, and the grave-grounds around the Shelf are marked by nothing, known by everyone, fished by no one. And here is where the singing went — the volume the lanes can't hold. Over open water a voice carries whole and nobody has to pretend not to hear, so grief goes out by punt, at full marsh volume, once or twice a year, audible a mile off and unmistakable. Whatever it is that moves in the deep marsh some nights has heard the town at its loudest. This is the sort of thing you are not supposed to notice on the Shelf, and the manner of your not-noticing is called manners.

Which brings you back to the rock, because the youngest and largest thing living on the Shelf is the Shelf. The town will not put it this way — the town puts very little any way. But it is a body: fog for lungs at dawn, drips for a pulse, growth at the rim, grey scabs on the west where the frost bites. It bleeds at the crown — warm as blood, iron-flavored — into the ladle. The people patch its wounds with faggots, prune its growth eastward, drink its blood and never charge for it, and kneel, on dares, at its heart. None of this is worship, they will tell you. This is maintenance. And the eldest saying, the one they'll give you if you ask about the future, is that the stone is only borrowing the sky. The Shelf rose out of the water and will settle back someday, and everything raised on it is raised to be raisable. It makes a particular kind of people: light hands, portable grief, opinions pitched but never sunk.

Stand at the Warm Seam at dusk, with the fog deciding to stay, and you can see the arrangement entire. The eldest at her ladle, telling a stranger where the perch was. Children coming in with the dew jugs, shins scarred at exactly this year's height. A mason on his roof, resting, watching his life's work darken on schedule. The Banker on his step, taking the evening count. Somewhere below, one more tread, still sharp, waiting for spring. A child born this year will think the island was always this size, and an old woman at the Seam can point at a door-sill and prove otherwise, and between those two facts lives everyone. Four hundred people on the loan, paying it back the way tenants pay — a little at a time, at funerals, in stone, on the water — not expecting to see it settled, and not much minding.

Four hundred people live on the Shelf. Four hundred and one, counting the heron — and on the Shelf, they count the heron.
