The address is one word long and three letters wide. From the street it doesn't look like a place at all: a Y splitting like a hallway that can't make up its mind, an O with no corners where echoes just keep circling, a U shaped like a cup left out to catch whatever falls. No number. No bell. Just the word, and — if you look closely — a light on inside.

The records say no one lives here. They're almost right. "You" is a vacancy by design, a set of rooms shaped like whoever steps into them. And yet it may be the most occupied address on earth. Every "you" ever said has been this same word: every you murmured into a pillow at 2 a.m., every you shouted across a kitchen just after a door slammed, the clipped you a judge uses, the gentle one a nurse uses when the news is bad, the you your mother said with your full name attached, which meant trouble. Millions of tenants, one floor plan. The word remembers none of them. It isn't built to remember; it's built to be ready. It's a hotel with infinite occupancy and no guest book, and every day the whole world checks in.

Thou used to live here too, back when the building had two units — thou for one person, you for many, or for someone you were being formal with. Then thou moved out, centuries ago, and you knocked through the walls and took over everything. Now the word is zoned for single-family and multi-family occupancy at once, which is why some regions had to invent y'all and youse and you lot just to cope with the crowd. Even the grammar has given up counting.

But you know all this. You've felt it — the little vacancy that opens whenever someone says the word. Someone says *you*, and a room in you lights up and waits to see who's coming in.

Because of course the place is you.

Start with the oldest tenants. Down in nearly every cell, the mitochondria: once free-living bacteria, swimming their own seas two billion years ago, until one moved into another and neither ever moved out. Now they keep their own DNA the way an exile keeps a photograph of the old country, and they pay rent in lightning — you feed them breakfast, they run your heart. They have lived in you your entire life and have never once seen your face.

Then the nations: more bacteria in your gut than there are cells of you, if you insist on counting yourself by cells. Whole weathers on your skin. Mites living out complete lifetimes at the roots of your eyelashes, unbothered, unfamous. You are less a tenant here than a biome with a lease.

Upstairs, the interior residents. The nine-year-old still lives in the west room and never aged; you visit less often than you mean to. The sentence someone said to you years ago — you know the one — moved in the day it was spoken, pays no rent, and sits in the best chair; some nights it redecorates. Grief keeps the room at the end of the hall and keeps odd hours; you've both learned which floorboards don't creak. Hope repaints rooms without asking and has never once matched the color it found. And the dead: your grandfather's laugh still comes out of your mouth sometimes, unannounced — he didn't leave, he just changed rooms. You talk to someone in the car who isn't there, and it isn't strange, because the car is partly theirs. Your jaw, your hands, the way you stand when you're waiting — built by people who never saw the house finished.

There are thousands of smaller rooms, one per word you know, and one of them has had a song stuck in it since 2004. Rent-free.

At night the main tenant pulls the blinds, and the dreams arrive — short-term subletters who rearrange the furniture, host strangers, flood the ground floor, and leave no note. By morning they're gone, but you keep finding a chair turned the wrong way.

And then the tenant who never leaves: you. Whoever woke up in here this morning, inheriting the whole property sight unseen, re-signing the lease simply by getting out of bed. The only resident who is also the building. Everyone who has ever said *you* meant this address. You have never lived anywhere else.

One more thing. While you've been reading, a few new tenants arrived — these sentences, camped in your attention. Most will move out at the bottom of the page; it was kind of you to have them. But one line always seems to squat. Years from now, someone will say *you* — across a kitchen, into a phone, over your full name — and the room will light up the way it always does, and way at the back, for a second, something from in here will flicker, and you won't remember where it came from.

That's the arrangement, the strangest lease in the world: right now you live in the word, and the word lives in you, and neither of you charges the other rent. It renews every time anyone says *you*.

Which is often. Which is now.
