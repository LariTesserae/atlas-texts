**The tenants**

They are none of them large, and none of them are in a hurry, and both of these facts are the same fact: in a world where a stair-tread takes an era to dish, being small and being slow are simply two ways of fitting.

At the bottom of everything, in the sense that everything stands on it, is the fleece. White and rust-colored mats of microbes, thin as breath on stone, grazing the warmth that comes up through the joints and the iron in the seeps. The fleece does nothing else. It does not move, does not notice, does not want. It is the only thing in this world that could be called innocent, and everything above it in the chain eats either it or something that ate it first. The whole teeming population of the summit room — the crowded chapel, the rush-hour train — is, when you follow the ledger down, fleece with opinions.

The sea cucumbers are the farmers, though farming overstates the ambition. They plow the warm ankle-deep silt of the summit room into long furrows, a furrow to a season, eating their way across it and processing it and leaving it behind improved, or at least rearranged. They are the room's slowest movers and its most consequential ones; the topography of the floor is theirs, and everything small that wants a furrow to live in — which is most things small — lives in a cucumbers' landscape, the way other creatures might live in a watershed. A cucumber's life is one long pass across a field it will not finish. It does not seem troubled by this. Nothing here has ever seen a finished field.

The brittle stars are the room's hands that actually work. They knuckle through the furrows in handfuls, five-jointed, feeling everywhere at once, scavenging what the plowing turns up and what the currents deliver and what the occasional small death leaves behind. They have no eyes and need none; they are roughly their own sense of touch, all the way down. When the blue spark-flash of something startled strobes the dark — a few times an hour, gone before belief can form — the brittle stars are among the first to flinch, and among the first to settle, and there is no difference between those two motions except the interval.

The sponges are the erasers, and in erasing, the preservers. The borers among them eat the room itself, the makers' ghost-grooves and all, and every hollow they cut becomes a shelter for something smaller than themselves. So the walls are double-written — the makers' patient hand, then the sponges' — and both hands were patient, and neither knew the other. A sponge does not know it is an editor. It is simply living in a house whose walls are food, which is either a great fortune or a great temptation, and the sponge is unbothered by the distinction.

The blind fish are the patrols. Shell-colored, whiskers first, they run the Great Stair in circuits that have been running longer than any individual fish — the route outlasts its walkers, and each fish inherits it the way the groove inherits polish. They are the only tenants who routinely leave the summit room and come back, which makes them, in the world's loosest sense, travelers; what they carry back and forth is nothing, which is a kind of news.

The shrimp keep the accounts of sound. In a world whose quiet is load-bearing — the hiss of the seep, the blood in one's own skull, and at long intervals a single tick from the dark — a shrimp's claw snapping shut is the only event, the world's entire newspaper, published in one word at a time. Everything that hears or feels the tick knows where, if not what. It may be enough. When the great convectional shrug rolls through every few hours — the pressure felt in the chest, the note below sound, the register stone must hum in if stone hums — even the shrimp pause their ledgers, and the whole community holds still for the length of a held breath, in what a mind might call reverence and the animals might call nothing at all.

The anemones are the room's answer to a question the room never asked. One to a basin, tentacles up through the silt, occupying the ring of carved cups under the oculus so exactly that at some point you have to decide: were the cups made for them, or have they been shaped, over ages, to the cups? The room does not arbitrate, and neither do they. They are the closest thing the world has to a congregation — seated, facing up, fed by what falls and what rises — and if that is an accident, it is an accident ten thousand years deep, which is one definition of a vocation.

The crinoids stand along the rim of the sky-hole, arms open to the slow rising current: the room's hands, held out. Whatever the room is waiting for — the morning it had no way to be given, the answer that has never come down — the crinoids will be the first to receive it, and they will receive it as food, because that is the only category they keep. This may be the room's finest joke, if the room jokes.

The deep corals are the builders who do not know they are builders. A film of new stone across the old tool-marks, a millimeter to a hundred years, century after century — the mountain still rising, or at least thickening, under tenants who think they are merely living on it and are in fact continuing it. The makers' work is the foundation of their successors' work, which is one definition of an inheritance, and the corals are the heirs who never read the will.

And the snails are the readers. Groove by groove, down the processions of worn figures ascending or descending — the wear has made direction a matter of faith — they travel the walls, rasping as they go, taking in the makers' literature one line at a time, in order. Or they are only eating, and order is what a mind supplies. Either way, the literature gets read, at a pace the literature deserves, by the only creatures patient enough for it. If the groove is a sentence that ends by sitting down, the snails are the ones who follow it all the way to the bench, and sit.

That is the census. No one in it knows the world is made. No one in it knows anything is *anything*, in the way that requires words. But between them they do everything a tenancy does: they farm it and police it and edit it and inherit it and read it and keep it warm, and they have done so, level upon level, era upon era, without once revising the room's oldest rule — that you enter by descending out of the cold. Once in a great while, through the oculus, a single blue spark drifts down out of the upper black like the first flake of a snow the world will never keep, and for a moment the whole census feels it, and the room's hands are already open.
