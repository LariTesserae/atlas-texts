# The Gardeners

There are only a few hundred of them, which is the first strange thing: a country the size of a sky, held by a village. They are long-limbed and quiet in the way of people who have spent ten thousand years inside an ear. Their skin has gone the color of the place — bark-brown, lichen-gray, faintly green where the light-vines have dyed generation after generation of them. They speak at the volume of turning pages. Their laughter, when it happens, is a thing done with the shoulders. They have a word for loud noise that also means *wound*, and another word for silence that also means *home*.

They do not think of themselves as owning the House. The relationship runs the other way and has a name that translates poorly — something between *tenant* and *trustee* and *the part that walks around*. A child's first lesson is not language but footfall: how to place the heel so the floor-mat swallows the sound, how to move through a hall the size of a valley without waking anything. Children who cry are not shushed. They are gathered against chests, and carried, and the sticky harp-strings above them stay asleep. This is the origin of the deep, unsettling gentleness in all of them — a people shaped by the knowledge that sound is a hand reaching into the dark, and the dark has teeth on filaments.

Their tools are simple enough to be embarrassing to describe: flaked stone, thorn needles, antler hooks, clay lamps burning rendered wax. There is no metal. There has never been metal. A gardener can spend an entire life mastering one grafting cut, and the walls hold the knives of her great-great-grandmothers' teachers embedded in the grain like flies in amber, and she will find one on her rounds sometimes and touch it and keep walking. Their immense craft is invisible by design — the best graft is one the House seems to have thought of itself.

They are not melancholy, though it would be easy to be. They read the cambium rings like harbormasters reading clouds, plan pruning around next month's un-happened rain, count their lives in sap-tides. They marry slowly, in the way of people who know that time here is thick. They bury nothing; the dead are folded into the walls, and it is not uncommon for a gardener to have a favorite panel — warm to the touch, a faint shape in the grain, a grazer or a grandmother or a stranger from before the lineages — and to rest a hand on it in passing the way you'd greet someone across a room.

And underneath all their competence runs the hum: the Bud. Every one of them will die before it opens or live to see it open — the rings do not say which, only that the season coming has no precedent. This has made them neither fearful nor festive but something rarer: attentive. A people going quietly about their rounds in a vast warm ear, listening for whatever it is their ten-thousand-year house has been holding its breath to hear.

# The Others

Everything else that lives here is mute, and this is not timidity but inheritance — the loud ones were eaten so long ago that silence has been bred into the flesh itself.

The **grazers** are the gentlest. Hayrick-sized, eyeless, warm as felt, they move along the buttresses in no hurry the architecture doesn't share, lapping mineral crust off the bark with tongues like cloths pulled from a bath. They know the gardeners by footfall, which is to say by the *absence* of footfall, and will stand unstirred while a human climbs over them to reach a runnel.

The **moths** come in slow blizzards. Their wings are wet paper; they make literally no sound, and in the low fog they drift toward the sap-rise like thoughts a hall is having about light.

The **threadfish** ride the water films down the walls, transparent as intent, invisible except where they cross a glow-vine and are briefly sketched in light. The **midges** carry pollen that shines on their backs, and at a distance a pollination is a weather system — a drizzle of sparks moving deliberately through the dark.

And through the deep corridors hang the **harp-strings**, sticky and tuned, patient as the House's own attention. Whether these are predator, organ, or the House's hearing itself is a question the gardeners long ago decided not to press. The House hunts by vibration, and everything alive within it has agreed, in body, to the second rule: *nothing that lives here says its own name aloud.*

Except once a day, when the Weep comes — interior rain falling from ceilings kilometers up onto leaves, the loudest natural event in the world — and for those minutes, the whole vast organism is talking to itself, and everyone else may as well.
