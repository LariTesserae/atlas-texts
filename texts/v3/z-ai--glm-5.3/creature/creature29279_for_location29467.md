# Who Lives Here

The tenants of the drowned mountains are, almost all of them, green.

Start with the majority, because the majority here is overwhelming: the ribbon-things that are not kelp. They root themselves directly in bare lava — no sand, no sediment, no courtesy asked of the world — sending holdfasts into the cooling stone as if into an old friend's shoulder. They grow in curtains down every sharp face, bottle-green where the light strikes full, iron-red where it slants, and they do not sway the way kelp sways, because there is no surge to sway in. Instead they move the way a warm room's air moves: slowly, internally, all together, in the great soft convections that the heat-vents below drive up through the water. A whole ridgeline of them will lean the same direction at once, an inch a minute, like a congregation turning its head.

They have one great event in their lives and it comes at dusk. When the day's warmth begins to drain upward out of the water, grove by grove, they exhale — a fine silver rain of bubbles rising through the shafts of failing light, one grove then the next then the next along a ridge, the whole mountain range sighing in sections. If the sea has a pulse, this is it. It is possible that the ribbons synchronize by nothing more than shared temperature. It is possible they are signaling. No one here is equipped to care about the difference, and the difference, at this stage of the world, may not exist.

On the frozen spines of the last eruptions — the thousands of lava pillars standing up off the ridgelines like the burnt remains of something — live the turners. Things like sunflowers that have never seen the sun but track anyway, following the light-shafts as the day wheels overhead, rotating on their small thick stems with a patience that takes hours to notice. They are the closest thing this world has to faces. A pillar wrapped in turners will hold a few hundred of these gazes, all angled the same way, all day, and at dusk they all swing slowly down and around to wait for morning, like an audience lowering its eyes.

Down on the valley floors, the meadows. Low creeping mats of a thousand grasslike things, each drift of them moving in the slow heat-currents the way grain moves under wind — except the grain is being combed by warmth itself. The terraced meadows descend the stairsteps of the lava flows shelf by shelf, each shelf a slightly different green, each green a species that has found its exact preferred height of water and heat and settled there with the finality of a thing that has never known competition. Between the shelves, the weavers: the plants in the gullies that have grown into one another so completely that the terrain beneath them is no longer terrain. A gully floor here is not walked on. It is replaced. The woven canopy hangs fifteen meters above the stone, springy, dense, and the actual valley bottom beneath it — dark, still, warm as a held breath — is the closest thing this world has to a private room.

And among all the living green, the white architecture. The scaffold-builders precipitate the dissolved stone of the young water around their own stems as they grow, so that each of them lives inside a fresh mineral sheath like a candle in glass. When one dies, the fluted pipe remains. The forests are filling with these — pale, hollow, organ-voiced-in-waiting — a bone-forest growing among the living one, built by no one, for no one, the sea's first and only buildings. The tenants are constructing a city without meaning to, one millimeter a decade, and the tenants will eventually live in the ruins of themselves.

Then the animals, such as they are. Hold them in one hand; that is the whole census.

The grazers: seed-sized, translucent, patient, swarming in the meadows in numbers that make their individual smallness irrelevant. Each one is barely a visible thing — a fleck of glass with an appetite — but a million of them moving across the terraces make a sound, and it is the loudest sound the animals of this world make: a soft continuous shifting, like distant sand, like sleet on a roof heard through walls. They graze the green films and leave the deep structure, and so they are less a threat to the meadows than a texture of them. The meadows have never known anything else. In this sea, being eaten gently and constantly is simply one of the properties of existing, the way being rained on is.

The hoverers: one species, fist-length, flat-bodied, and the loneliest way of life ever invented. Each of them lives only at the boundary where warm vent-water meets cooler sea — that shimmering, wavering, not-quite-visible seam between two temperatures — and it lives nowhere else, holding station in the shimmer its whole life, feeding on the richness that accumulates along the edge. It is the only creature in the world that lives on an edge rather than in a place. It does not know where it lives. It knows the boundary, follows it when the vents pulse, and the boundary is always slightly moving, and so the hoverer is always slightly moving, and its entire biography is the small correction. There is something almost unbearable in this: a life spent suspended in the exact meeting-point of the warm young world and the cool old one, refusing both, sustained by the refusal. If this sea ever produces a philosopher, it will already have the hoverer's portrait painted.

And then the visitors, who come once in an age. A lone sea-turtle, an old wanderer, blown off distant oceans by storm or accident, riding a rain-flood up the caldera's lowest rim-gap. They do not stay — there is nothing here yet that can hold them, no reef to shelter in, no chorus to join. They circle the young sea once, slowly, soundlessly, over meadows that have never felt a shadow that large pass across them, and the whole green world stops what it is doing — which is nothing — and the grazers pause their sand-sound, and the turners turn toward the dark of it, and the hoverers hold their stations while the boundary itself trembles faintly in the wake. The turtle circles as if reading the place. As if checking whether it is real. Then it finds the gap again and is gone, leaving the water faintly disturbed for a day, and the meadows rippling, and nothing else.

It is worth saying plainly what the visitors do not leave: no eggs, no offspring, no seed of an animal future. The animals of this world, if they ever come, will have to arrive on their own, in their own geological time, and find a world already fully prepared for them — terraced, terraced green, piped and scaffolded, warm — a house built and furnished and left waiting by tenants who do not know they are waiting.

Because that is the last truth of who lives here, and it is not in the census. The largest tenant is the sea itself. It has a temperature the way a sleeping body has a temperature. It breathes at dusk in sections. It hums with the settling of its own bones. Its only sounds are the purr of the still-cooling rock and the soft resonant *tink* of a boulder letting one more drop of the mountain's interior heat into the water, and the valley walls return these sounds once, softly, and swallow them. Every tenant inside it — ribbon and turner and weaver and grazer and hoverer — is warm because it is warm, fed because the rock beneath them has never been leeched before, unhurried because nothing here has competitors yet, or predators, or seasons. The whole census lives inside one great slow organism that is itself mid-story, mid-construction, a young range being quietly and greenly drowned, and every surface of it being claimed, patiently, by the first tenants of a house still being built.

Grow, settle, accumulate. That is the only law here so far. The tenants keep it perfectly. They have never heard of any other way, and from in here — with the light coming down in long straight shafts, and the mineral dust rising like snow in reverse, and the organ-pipes thickening by a millimeter a decade toward the day the heat-vents will blow across their open ends — from in here, no other way can even be imagined.
