# Who Lives Here

## The Two Thousand

They call themselves, when outsiders ask, *the Kept* — though they never use that word among themselves. Among themselves they have no word at all, the way you have no word for the floor you stand on.

Here is the first thing to understand: they did not build the city. The city was already standing when the oldest of them was young, and it was already standing when *their* grandparents were young, and the calendar insists that this is impossible, and they have long since stopped arguing with the calendar. They are, by their own quiet reckoning, the second thing to arrive. The machines were first. The vegetation was a near-simultaneous second. The people came in at a photo-finish third, and have never quite gotten over the feeling of being latecomers in their own home.

They tend. That is the whole of their economy and nearly the whole of their philosophy. There are engineers who tend the machines, gardeners who tend the plants, and — this is the prestigious and slightly feared profession — *arbiters*, who tend the disputes between the two. An arbiter spends her day walking terrace edges where a creeper has engulfed a maintenance-stream, and must decide, stem by stem, which of the two rivals gets to keep that inch of ground. They say the best arbiters can hear the difference between a machine repairing itself and a plant growing; the sound is nearly identical, a kind of granular whispering, but it comes in on different micro-beats of the great four-beat cycle. Nobody can teach this. You either acquire it or you don't, and those who do are changed by it in ways the others find hard to meet across a table.

Physically they are unremarkable, which surprises visitors: you expect a people shaped by noise, and in a sense they are — not louder, but *more patient*. Their conversations are slow because they are conducted at volumes that never fully clear the sound-floor. They have evolved, culturally, a stillness. Two Kept friends will sit on a sounding-bridge for an hour and exchange perhaps forty words, and part warmed, and mean it.

Their children are raised on the rhythm. Real infants, and the dreams of adults, both sync to the four-beat; there are lullabies here that are simply the mother tapping the fourth beat slightly early, over and over, teaching the child that the world can be anticipated. This is considered the first lesson of personhood: *the noise has structure, and you are allowed to trust it, a little.*

## The Machines

Not a species, exactly. Not a population. The Kept argue about this at the long tables in the mist-halls: whether the maintenance is one vast distributed animal, or many, or whether "animal" is the wrong category for something that is also, functionally, a *mason*. They are visible only at dusk, streaming and crawling, and when they want to be seen — which is rare, and usually means something — an individual mote will drift to eye level and hold position. Arbiters keep logs of these audiences. There is no pattern to them. That is the troubling part. It implies *discretion*.

What everyone agrees on: the machines are not tools. They were never installed. Nobody programs them. They are doing a job they decided on, and the job is *holding the land young*, and the Kept's entire civilization rests on the machines not ever, ever changing their mind.

## The Vegetation

It is winning, and everyone knows it, and the machines seem to know it too, and there is a strange courtesy in the way the two forces collide. The creepers do not destroy the maintenance-channels; they grow *around* them, leaving corridors, as if respecting the traffic. The machines do not poison the roots; they reroute stone beneath them. Some of the younger Kept — there is always a faction of younger Kept — believe the two are not fighting at all but *conversing*, in a language of growth and repair, and that the four-beat noise is this conversation audible to everything with ears. The older arbiters say this is romantic nonsense. The older arbiters walk the terraces at dusk and watch the streams of light crawl beneath the flowers that listen downward, and say nothing else about it.

## The Herons, the Choruses, and the Other

The giant herons — horses with hollow crest-bones — are the closest thing to neighbors the Kept have in the animal kingdom, and are treated as such. They nest on the city's upper tension-cables, which the machines repair around them, and the Kept feed them at the cistern-throats, and a heron's resonant brass note at dusk is considered good luck in direct proportion to how much it startles you. They remember faces. They hold grudges. There are herons in the upper city that have outlived four generations of the families whose balconies they consider theirs.

The amphibian swarms in the wet pockets are the *musicians*, in the Kept's cosmology — small, bright, singing on the offbeat, the only living things that answer the rhythm rather than following it. Children are taught to find their own heart's offbeat against the great sound, a small skill of listening that marks childhood here the way riding a bicycle marks it elsewhere.

And in the deep river-channels: something. The Kept have a word for it that translates, poorly, as *the Co-Respondent*. It has never been seen. It may be one thing or many. It may be — this theory is whispered, not spoken — the machine-world's aquatic counterpart, or the original that the machines are all a copy of. No one has determined which rhythm is copying which, and the Kept regard this question as the deepest one available to a person, deeper than gods, deeper than origins. It is the question they set their finest minds to on the long amber evenings: *who started the song?*

## Why They Stay

There were once hundreds of thousands of rooms and there are now two thousand people, and the city is loud because it is calling for everyone who has not yet arrived. The Kept believe this literally. They do not think the others are dead. They do not think the others left. They think the others are *en route* — that the whole shimmering, unfinished, half-grown world is a place being prepared, a table being set, and that they, the two thousand, are not the remnant but the *advance guard*: the ones who came early to tend the ground before the guests appear.

Ask any of them, on any sounding-bridge, in any of their forty-word hours, and they will tell you: someone has to keep the lights before they're needed. Someone has to know the rhythm well enough to teach it.

The land is still being decided. So are they. They would not have it any other way.
