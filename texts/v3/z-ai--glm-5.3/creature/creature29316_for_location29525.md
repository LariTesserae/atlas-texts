# Who Lives Here

No one was born in the Skylight Chamber. That is the first thing to understand about its inhabitants: every one of them, or every one of their ancestors, arrived — fell through the broken ceiling, crawled up the cool throat of the passage, was washed down the storm-rope, or drifted in as a seed on some wind that got lucky. This is a colony of accidents that learned to live together, and it has the particular gentleness of a community that knows it was never meant to be.

The room's true population is not counted in bodies but in the column of light. Everything alive here is arranged around it the way iron filings stand around a magnet, and so the best way to describe who lives here is to start in the beam and work outward, into the dark.

## The Swifts

The swifts are the chamber's only residents with a key to the front door. A dozen of them nest up in the broken rim of the collapse, in the fissures where the world failed, and they descend the shaft one at a time — never two together, as if queuing at a window — to feed on the insects that gather in the brightness. They are the room's commuters, its citizens with outside lives: they have seen the desert above, they know what sky is, and each evening they climb the bright air and leave. That they come back every morning is the chamber's quiet compliment to itself.

Their single ricocheting cry, once every minute or two, is the only clock this place has ever had. The swifts do not know they keep time for everyone. That is often the way with the most essential members of a community.

## The Moths

Below the swifts, in the beam itself, sleep the moths. They come at dusk, up the crawlway or down the shaft, drawn by the last warmth in the sandy center, and they roost directly in the light column's footprint — gray wings flat on warm sand, sleeping inside the very fire that will wake them. At dawn the red blade of light slides across the floor and finds them still lying in it, and they lift all at once in a soft gray exhalation, like the room breathing out.

They are the chamber's dreamers, the ones who trust the dangerous thing most completely. Whether this is faith or simply poor judgment, the room does not adjudicate. It warms them either way.

## The Crickets

On the sweating walls live the crickets — pale, eyeless, long-limbed, silent, forty or more, though no one has ever counted them because they move like thoughts and you only ever see the three nearest. They drink from the droplets that take an hour to form and a half-second to fall, and so their entire existence is a discipline of small patience: they stand near the *tik*, they wait, they are rewarded. They are the room's contemplatives, its monks, blind in a bright place and untroubled by the contradiction. Their absence from one corner of the wall is the most eloquent thing they ever say.

## The Frogs

Around the old waterfall-strike, in the moss ring, sit the frogs — small, waxy, gold-eyed, and fundamentally *waiting*. They have no water deep enough to breed in. They may wait seasons. So they sit in their grey papery garden, which looks dead between storms and greens over in three hours after rain — the fastest thing that happens here — and they wait for the one storm heavy enough, long enough, to leave a pool that lasts. Some years the gamble pays. Those are the years the night rings with a thin high chorus, tadpole-music, a sound like the chamber congratulating itself; and then the calling stops, and the frogs settle back into their long patience, gold eyes reflecting whatever light is on offer. They are the gamblers of this society, and the house, being geological, almost always wins.

## The Tree

In the bright center stands the room's one vertical citizen: a seedling that fell out of the sky perhaps forty years ago, waist-high, trunk-thick for its size, leaves held flat and stiff like a plant saluting. It gets one-seventh of the sun it was built for, and it grows the way a debt is paid — slowly, grudgingly, over decades. But everything orients toward it. The eye goes to it. The animals arrange themselves around it. The floor is warmest at its roots. It is the mayor of this small city, elected by no one, tolerating everything, insisting on nothing but its own incremental existence. It will never reach the skylight. It grows anyway. This is either the saddest or the bravest thing in the chamber, depending on the hour you consider it.

## The Fern

At the base of the weeping wall, one fern — one rootstock, a skirt of fronds the size of a seated person — holds dozens of trembling lenses of water all day, so that it appears to be constantly about to cry. It lives entirely on wall-sweat and secondhand light. It is the room's grief, or its tenderness, or simply its thirst made visible, and it is doing fine.

## The Corner-Animal

And in the deepest rubble-choked corner, behind the fallen slabs, where the light has never been, lives the one who is not quite nameable. Broad four-toed tracks in the dawn sand. The swifts refuse to hunt there. The crickets are absent. It moves rarely, it makes no sound, it asks for nothing, and the rest of the room has agreed — without discussion, without a single confrontation — that the corner belongs to it. Perhaps it is an old badger. Perhaps it is something descended from one, changing slowly in the dark at the speed of stalagmites. It is the hermit, the tenant in the unlit wing of the house, and the chamber holds the question of it gently and does not insist on an answer.

## The Visitors

And finally there are the ones who leave ochre. They come alone, with bone tools and fire-sticks, stay a night or a season, press a body-shaped memory into the same patch of sand so many times that the floor remembers it, and smear a hand-width of red on the wall at exactly the height where light will find it. Not a picture. Just: *I was here, facing the light.* They are not residents; they are pilgrims, and the chamber tolerates them the way a tree tolerates a bird — without acknowledgment, without change, and yet without ever once shaking them loose.

## The Oldest Resident

But if you stay long enough — long enough to run on the room's clock instead of your own — you begin to suspect that none of these are the real inhabitant. The real inhabitant is the light itself, the great patient finger that moves across the floor each day, that turns the whole chamber into a sundial, that pours the stars in one at a time at night as if feeding them to a well. Everything else in this place — the swifts, the moths, the waiting frogs, the gambling fern, the insistent tree, the nameless breathing in the corner — lives inside the light's house, arranged around its movements, dependent on its covenant, descended from its accidents.

They are not the chamber's inhabitants. They are the chamber's *tenants*. The light has lived here since the ceiling failed, and it will be here, moving its slow gold column across the sand, long after the last tenant has crawled out and the brightness closes behind them like a held breath released.
