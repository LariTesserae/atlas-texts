# The Tenants

Who lives here is not a question the basin has ever had to answer, because nothing here has ever left and nothing has ever been turned away. The population of the Headspring is not a list. It is a condition of the place, the way humidity is a condition of the air. But they can be met, one presence at a time, and each one is worth meeting, because every citizen of this basin is old in its way — not as individuals, none of them, but as lineages, as habits, as *solutions*, refined over ten thousand generations of tenure into forms so fitted to this ground that they have stopped being animals in a habitat and become organs of it.

---

## The Ants

Begin with the ants, because they were here first and they know it.

The dark rivers beneath the ferns are not one colony but a civilization — a single superorganism that has occupied this basin for longer than the standing wall has existed, longer than the stone ring, possibly longer than the path. Their trails are worn into the clay as grooves, and the grooves are the oldest constructed things here made by anything alive. Some of those channels are deeper than a finger's first knuckle, cut by an unbroken relay of feet so numerous that each grain of clay removed represents a full career of a single ant, carried off one mouthful per lifetime.

They have a relationship with the dome. This is the part no one expects. The trails do not merely pass near it; several of the deepest grooves run directly to its base, to the seam where the silver rope falls, and the ants go there the way pilgrims go somewhere — not for water, the whole basin is water, but for the *cold*. The dome's weeping surface is the only cold surface in the warm world, and the ants cluster in the fine grooves of its flank on the hottest hours, touching it, resting against it, a thin dark pelt of bodies spread over the gray-green curve. The dome accepts them as it accepts the tool marks. It has been wearing an ant coat, more or less continuously, since before the wall was carried here, and there are ants whose entire evolutionary lineage consists of generation upon generation born within a centimeter of a machine older than the mountains-that-were. If anything alive remembers the dome's makers — not as memory, but as inherited comfort, as the shape of a niche — it is them.

## The Fish

The congregation in the pool deserves its name. It is a congregation.

They are small, clear, and nearly invisible — you see them mostly as *distortions*, the way you'd see heat, held in the cold green-glass water where the rope falls. A hundred or more, all facing upstream, all holding station in the outfall current with tiny corrective flicks of fins that never stop and never vary. They have been doing this for as long as there has been a pool to do it in, and the pool was carved by patience, and so were they.

What they are waiting for is the water itself. The rope of falling water carries with it everything the dome condenses out of the air — a faint, steady drizzle of airborne nutrients, pollen, spores, microorganisms, the dissolved dust of the entire sky — and it hits the pool in a column at the center. The fish face upstream because upstream is *the sky*. They stand in the cold outfall with their mouths working, filtering the sky's delivery, an arrangement so reliable that they have lost nearly everything else: their camouflage is total, their rituals are minimal, their whole species has been shaped down into a single perfect behavior — *face the gift, open, wait*. They are the basin's most devout residents. They never look away.

## The Frogs

The tree frogs are the basin's patience made flesh. Wet-leaf colored, one to a stem, motionless on the fern stalks with their throats pulsing in a slow rhythm that is not their heartbeat but something slower, almost tidal — an idle rhythm, the tempo of an animal that has never in its evolutionary history needed to hurry.

They do not call at midday. This is not shyness; it is *policy*. The basin's hush is so old that it has become a selective pressure in itself, and the frogs are among its finest products. Their dusk chorus — when it comes, brief and localized and somehow still understated — is tuned low, pitched to carry only as far as the bowl's rim and no farther, a sound that stays home. In ten thousand generations the loud ones were eaten, or dried out, or simply failed to find mates in a world that rewards stillness, and what remains is a frog whose entire posture, whose whole body plan, is an argument for waiting.

Sit on the smooth stone long enough and one will accept you. Not approach — *accept*. It will simply stop classifying you as an event. Its throat will pulse. Yours will slow to match. This is the closest thing to a conversation the basin offers, and it is enough.

## The Moths and the Bee

The pale day-flying moths are the basin's one visible gesture, its single concession to motion. They drift between the scattered white and violet flowers in their slow floating way, dozens of them, almost never landing, and their flight is unlike any other flight — not the purposeful vectoring of a forager, but something closer to *browsing*, to wandering, as if each moth is browsing the emptiness between flowers as attentively as the flowers themselves. They respect the spacing. You will never see two moths crowd one bloom; the sparse geometry of the basin's flowers has been adopted by its pollinators as a geometry of conduct.

And there is the bee. One bee. Fat, solitary, out of sight, working a flower somewhere with total absorption, its hum the only sustained voice in the whole soundscape. Whether it is literally one bee or a rotating succession of bees in a role — *the* bee, a station held like the fish hold theirs — the basin has never made clear. Its sound is so constant, so placed, that it functions as the basin's held-breath quiet made audible: one voice, allowed, absorbed.

## The Mud-Dwellers

The banks of the rivulets are the most crowded address in the basin and the least examined, because the residents there live at a scale where mud becomes architecture. The crayfish burrows freckle the clay in densities that read, from knee height, as texture; from eye height, as a city. Each burrow is a chimney to a flooded warren, and each warren is older than the wall, maintained in an unbroken tradition of digging that has been handed down through mud-generation after mud-generation without a single century of lapse. The beetle larvae breathe through siphons that break the surface in their dozens — small snorkel-tips rising from the bank like the tips of an iceberg population, the visible fraction of a citizenry that lives entirely below, in the cool dark waterline, in the one place in the basin where the world is not warm.

The mud is alive the way a reef is alive. Tread wrong and an entire neighborhood slams shut — siphons withdrawn, burrow-mouths sealed, the bank going still in a single synchronized flinch that takes half a second and then three full minutes to relax. They know footsteps. They have known footsteps, as a category of threat and as a category of *weather*, for a very long time.

## The Ones Who Sit

And then there is the last kind of resident, the rarest: the sitters.

The smooth stone by the pool is worn concave, and it was not worn by water. It was worn by an uncountable succession of occupants — human, and otherwise, species that came and sat and looked at the dome and the falling rope and then went extinct, each one adding its own polish, its own fraction of a millimeter, to the hollow. The sitters are not a population; they are a *tradition*, the basin's oldest continuous custom, older than any lineage that practices it. Whatever comes to the Headspring and has enough mind to be still — enough mind to feel the cold come up through the stone — eventually finds the stone, sits, and joins it.

The basin does not mind visitors. The dome weeps for them exactly as it weeps for the ants and the fish and the moths, which is to say it makes no distinction at all, which is its own deep form of hospitality. Nothing here is waiting for anything. But the stone is hollow, and the stone does not lie: for an unimaginably long time, someone has always, eventually, come to sit.

At the moment, at this warm bright noon, the sitting is done by a hundred frogs in miniature on their stems, by the moths browsing their spaced flowers, by the fish holding their congregation in the cold green water — a basin of several million small still citizens, each one alone in its own halo of open space, all of them together keeping the quiet so well-populated that it has become, itself, the largest resident of all.
