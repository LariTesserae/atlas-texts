What lives here are the ones who turned flatness into a country.

Start with the chorus, because the chorus is the majority. What sounds like a million small voices moaning and chittering in the fog is exactly that: knots and dunlin, sanderling and godwit, birds the color of the mud itself until they turn and show the pale of their bellies, and then a whole acre of ground lifts at once and becomes weather. They live here in the way a note lives inside a chord — no single bird is the sound; the sound is a property of the crowd. Each one is barely larger than a fist and weighs less than the fog it stands in, and each one has flown here from the other end of the planet, from tundra it will not see again for months, because this place — this bare, roaring, dish-of-a-place — is the richest larder between two hemispheres. They have judged it correctly. The flats hold more food per square meter than almost any ground on earth, and the birds know this the way a person knows hunger: in the body, before thought.

Under them lives the reason they came. The mud is not mud; it is a city, and the tide re-peoples it twice a day. Lugworms write their coiled essays across the surface at night. Clams lie in their thousands, filtering the grey water and speaking nothing. Sandhoppers and ragworms, ghost shrimp and diatoms, a whole quiet metabolizing nation that never rises above the surface of the flats and so is known only by its effects: the fat birds, the crowded sky. When the tide is out, this nation is hunted. When the tide comes in, it is simply home, and the water slides over it like a closing eyelid.

The skuas and the peregrine live here too, but they live in the fear the way spiders live in a wall. The falcon uses the fog — comes out of it low and fast, and the million-voiced chorus goes silent in a spreading ring, a held breath the size of a mile, and then the roar of the birds resumes as if nothing had happened except that one knot is gone. The skuas are blunter about it. They chase and harass and steal, and they scream doing it, and the terns hate them with a pure, ancient, inherited hatred, diving and shrieking and stitching the sky with hatred, all summer, every summer, forever.

Gulls live here in the way opportunists live everywhere — laughing at the edges of things, taking the eggs of the terns when the terns falter, standing in lines along the tide line looking like a jury.

And there is at least one fox. There is always one fox. It lives in the dune line at the hem of the plain, in a den it did not dig, and it knows the flats better than any mapmaker — knows the firm bars from the soft channels, knows where the tide will pinch the peninsula of sand before the birds know it, and it walks out at the low water like a landlord doing rounds. It is the only creature here whose coat is the wrong color for the place, and it doesn't care. It is comfortable. Everything it needs is delivered to it, twice daily, by the sea.

And under everything, in a sense, lives the wind — if a process can be said to live somewhere. It lives in the shapes of the birds: their narrow bodies, their swept-back wings, the way a flock facing the gale turns all at once like a field of grass. It lives in the dune grass, which grows lying down and calls that standing. It has carved every resident of this place like water carves a canyon — slowly, from the outside, by simply never stopping. Nothing here is built to outlast it. Everything here is built to *withstand* it, which is a different and humbler thing, and the difference between those two words is the whole character of the place.

The tide lives here as well — not as water but as a landlord in its own right, one with a clock and a temper. Everything that breathes on the flats arranges its life around that clock: the worms' feeding, the birds' flying, the fox's walking, all of it synced to a schedule written by the moon and enforced by the sea, which owns everything here and visits twice a day to check on its holdings.

That is who lives here. No one large. No one solitary, except the falcon and the fox. A parliament of small, tough, extremely well-traveled bodies, stacked in layers — sky, mud, sand, water — all of them talking at once, all of them at home in a place that looks, to anyone standing at the edge of it, like the middle of nothing at all.
