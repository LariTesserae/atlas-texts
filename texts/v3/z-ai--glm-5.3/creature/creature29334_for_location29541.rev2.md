Ask who lives here, and the town does not answer with names. It answers with a ledger.

The census is kept at the Tapworks, posted beside the breathing schedule in the same careful hand, and it does not count souls. It counts liters. A soul, in this bookkeeping, is a quantity: the water standing banked, at any given hour, in hide and bark and felt and bone, held against the air. The trees are on it. The herds are on it. The dead are on it, and drawing interest. You are not on it yet. Give it a day.

Begin with the majority, because the majority is not people. The oldest and largest citizen of the Hollow is the floor. The mat came down seventy years ago as seed in the founders' freight — pocketed, sleeved, hoarded like coin — and it is now one weave eight kilometers across, the biggest living thing anyone here has ever heard of, holding every other citizen the way a hand holds spilled beads. It is pavement, pasture, aquifer, and the town's entire idea of ground. It drinks the Ventrow's breath when the maws exhale. It keeps itself clean with animals it employs at the scale of mites. Nobody owns it. It is held the way the sky is held — in common, at a distance, by everyone — and it is the one citizen that holds everyone else back.

Above the mat, the trees — though you will learn to call them vaults before you learn to call them trees. A family speaks of its tap-line the way coastal people speak of a harbor light: always there, needing maintenance, steered by. The lamp-bark holds the Grey's freckle-glow and asks for nothing. And the ore orchards are the strangest farmers in the world — roots laid deliberately across copper veins, chewing rock with filmed acid, patient as any plowman and ten thousand times slower, their blossoms opening as bright filings, their harvest brokered tree to tree by flying middlemen.

Then the animals, every one of them a bank. You will look at a tallowback herd rotating through the light cells and see cattle; correct yourself the first time you watch a herder press a thumb into a wax flank and read the dimple like a statement of account. The hide wells back slow or fast, and that is the herd's liquidity. Herding here is water banking, and the herders — who sleep in the wax heaps on the warm ground-banks and smell of tallow and felt — speak of the herd in the language of finance: holdings, withdrawals, a strong season. The town's lamps are the herd's savings spent as light — the little moons on the towers burn shorn wax, so that walking the lanes at night is walking through a candle made of livestock. And when a tallowback dies it does not rot, because nothing rots; it stands and hollows and lacquers, and the herders call it a foreclosure, and the electric drays come, and the Mills make it into floor.

The predators pay for their water in advance. Cell-cats work the rim where noon meets Grey — long as a fence rail, flat as a shadow laid down — silent in a town where silence is currency. Killing here is drinking, and the herders' ledgers carry predation as withdrawals. A cat eats fast, because the air taxes an open kill by the minute; leave one an hour and there is nothing left in it worth the eating. The cats never vocalize, adults or kittens; a cat that spent sound would starve twice. To the town they are mostly arithmetic. To a straggler at the light's edge they are, briefly, weather.

At dusk the only wild weather that flies comes off the mat. Dustmoths rise in slow wheels, dust-colored, by the ten thousand, carrying the flowers' freight between them — filings, mineral dust, traded tree to tree. A moth is born holding all the water it will ever have: one night's worth, banked in a body the size of a thumbnail, and it spends that flask in a single dusk of flying. By the time the little moons are lit it is a husk on the weave. Claimed like everything. Carted like everything. Returned as substrate. The moths are the only citizens whose whole life is exactly one expenditure long.

And below notice, the mites and mat-ticks, chittering in the weave. You will file them under noise. File them instead under the floor washing itself: when the Ventrow exhales and the mineral flour snows down, they come out in the hours after and clear it. The mat keeps its hygiene the way a cat does — with an animal it owns at the correct scale.

Then, far down the census, people. Containers first, faces second, like everything else here.

Three generations of them. The founders came down the hoist with the freight seventy years ago, and the ones still living are the driest people you will ever meet — skin like the mat, and proud of it — and they remember rain. Ask one about weather, if you are willing to spend the words, and you get an inventory: so precise, so past tense, and then a silence, and then a sip from the flask, taken like medicine. Rain, one of them will tell you, sounded like something. They do not say like what. Their children were told about rain and half-believe it, the way children half-believe giants. The grandchildren are taught that the sun is an employee and the sky is a rumor that pays wages; they climb the starpipe to learn it — useful, distant, owned — and there are grown people in this town whose entire biography has taken place inside a walkable noon.

Every face is half felt. The masks take mouth and nose, so the town knows itself by eyes, strap-knots, and gait, and since voices are rationed, bodies have become eloquent. A whole negotiation can pass between two herders at forty meters, in the angle of a chin. The one who rode down beside you in the cage — the first person you saw in this world, adjusting a mask in the corner, the one who waited without hurry while you stood in the doorway listening to the hole where dripping should be — has become, by now or against their will, your guide. You have not heard their voice. By the second day you can read their mood from four meters off, in the shoulders.

Water etiquette is the whole of etiquette. To be offered a sip from someone's flask is what a kiss is elsewhere, and is declined only with real care. Flasks are heirlooms, carried the way other towns carry title and memory; a scratch on one is a date. Grief is paid in sips: at the standing orchards where the dead are buried feet to the roots, a mourner pours a mouthful of still-water at the post — a gift returned to the one who has become, functionally, a well. You will walk those orchards for a week thinking them plantations. Then you will learn that a sapling and a grandfather are both things this town is growing, and after that you nod to the posts either way. Music exists the way contraband does — a whisper-trade in the felt rooms off the mill-lane, songs inherited like flasks, kept just under the threshold of the mat's hiss.

The trades are all water trades, whatever they are called. Tappers, licensed, opening barrel-trees with thumb-sized augers and a jeweler's patience. Still-keepers, racking and clarifying and sealing. The shaft-guilds, who keep the light-hour ledgers — no one owns light, everyone owns light-hours — which are the town's real law book. Stealing a neighbor's mirror-hour is the local crime, tried under the schedule board, and the sentence always has the same shape: the hour repaid double, and your tap-line closed a season, so that you drink at the stranger's rate, from the common flask, in front of everyone you know. No one, they say, has ever taken the second season. The surveyors drive stakes for streets that do not exist and are paid, in effect, to imagine: the town answering, stake by stake, the only question it was ever asked — how big do you intend to be? The Nursery keepers raise felt-sleeved seedlings under a false noon. The dray drivers cart the claimed dead. And the borers ride the Worm.

The Worm is not alive; everyone knows this; nobody talks as if they know it. It eats current spun out of the shafts' updraft and advances three meters a day into raw geology, excreting future city, and you feel it at the Cut in your molars. Its crew are the youngest and dustiest citizens. The Hollow has no fevers — fever takes water — and its one sickness is the dry one the Worm makes, which the borers' lungs keep the way a tree keeps a bad year: laid down in bands, legible eventually, owned by no one. Nothing here is finished — that is the local religion — and the Worm is that religion's engine, three meters a day.

Once a rotation, citizens go up. The heliostat crews tend the mirror-chains on the surface — the direct management of the employee sun — shrouded in mirror-polish, trench to trench, under a sky that is actively robbing them. They come down like divers, unclipped from a hostile medium. They drink first, before anything, and they never have much to say, which is not remarkable here; but their silence is different from the town's. Their silence has weather in it. On shaft-opening day the town walks out to the new cell and stands together in the first noon it has ever had — masks on, saying nothing, for as long as the sun holds — and the crews stand in front, facing up. It is the only ceremony here. Children play at it in the lanes: one of them shutters a hand-lamp, and the rest stand in the sudden dark being quiet, for minutes, and are praised for the minutes.

There is one place in the Hollow where nothing lives. Out past the Grey the mat runs to a white margin and stops, tip-burned: the Salt Floor, mineral bloom dried to a crust that throws the far lamp-glow back across the dark. The rock there exhales its thin trace of moisture and the air takes it on the spot, and the salt is the receipt — robbery in progress, the one place on the floor of the world where the air still wins. The town knows exactly where it is and never goes. It is also, if you want to be technical, the only proof in eight kilometers that water was ever loose here at all, and nobody cares to look at the proof.

At lamp-hours on your first day, your guide stops at the cistern groves at the edge of noon — the cage-corner shoulders you can now read — sets an ear against an old barrel-tree out of habit, and then unslings the flask and pours you a swallow. Clay and cucumber. The cool inside of the slow heart. The taste of a thing that has been refusing the air longer than you have been alive. Nothing is said, because nothing needs to be: you understand that you have just been made a citizen. And at the Tapworks, before the shift-light changes, a clerk opens the ledger beside the breathing schedule and adds one line in the careful hand — liters held, flask issued, a name kept the way this town keeps everything, which is to say against the air.

You came down with the freight. By nightfall you are one of the things the air cannot have.
