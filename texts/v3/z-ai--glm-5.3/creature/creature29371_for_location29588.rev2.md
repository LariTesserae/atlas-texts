# The Tenants of the Bellows

What lives here lives small, lives warm, and lives on the sound.

At the bottom of everything there is the film — not a growth so much as a *patina of appetite*, a whitish skin no thicker than a breath, stretched across the warm ledges and the outfalls where the machine-heat leaks into the water. It smells of stone and coins. It eats what the drowned city sheds: transformer oil, dissolved iron, the slow mineral sweat of a hundred years. It is the only thing in this vault that could be called flora, and it is not green, because green is a color for places with sun. The film has never seen a photon it didn't have to imagine. It is the base of the world here, the way grass is the base of a meadow, except that it is thinner than paint, patient as lichen, and utterly alone in its kingdom.

Above it drift the shrimp. Glass shrimp, though the name flatters them — they are eyeless grains of rice, translucent as intent, snowing slowly through the slack water in loose constellations. They hold to the warm ledges because the second law of the room is carved into their bodies more deeply than any instinct: *heat rises, life follows heat.* They graze the film in invisible rows. And they tick — faintly, dryly, like watch parts settling — in the mutter between surges, when the great roar falls low enough that small sounds can exist at all. They are the punctuation of the room. Everything else the Bellows says, it says in vowels; the shrimp alone are permitted consonants. When the surge comes and the bubble-mist whitens the water, they flare like struck flints, and it is the closest thing to a sky full of stars this vault will ever admit.

And above the shrimp, above everything, holding station in the slack pocket behind the east stanchion, above the tithe where all lost things are filed — the Tenant.

There is one fish. There has perhaps only ever been, in any meaningful sense, this one fish: pale, scaleless, translucent at the edges, its interior lit faintly amber by the machines it has lived above all its life. You can see the patient machinery of its body working in the glow — the slow heart, the dark knot of the gut, the shadow of its spine like a mast in fog. It is large for what it is and slow for how large it is, grown to the tempo of water that never varies by a degree. It never hurries because hurrying has never once been rewarded here. It waits in the one pocket of calm the room affords, over the eddy that the surge uses as its ledger and its midden, and it eats whatever the cycles deliver: shrimp drifted to the edge of the current, the film's slower leavings, whatever the drowned city sends down through forty meters of silt and drowns at last in the warm.

It knows the room better than any visitor ever will. Its body flicks half a second before each surge — a tell, a flinch, a weather-sense — as though it hears the pumps *decide* before they act, as though after all these years it has become a part of the machinery's nervous system rather than a creature living alongside it. It does not think of the hatch. It does not think of the cold. The cold arrives occasionally anyway: once a season, something large and pale and indifferent noses at the ajar hatchway, considers the shimmer of warmth, and moves on up into the green-black indifference it came from. The Tenant watches it go with the fixed, unimpressed patience of a thing that has outlasted every argument the ocean has made at its door. The room resumes. The room always resumes.

Whether the machines themselves live here is a question the room has quietly answered yes. They were built for a purpose — keep the undercity dry — and the purpose drowned, and rather than stop, they kept working, pumping the ocean into the ocean, generation after generation of grit-polished impellers, their labor going nowhere and coming back, all of it, as heat and noise: the only currency this economy accepts. They are the room's heart, its sun, its weather, and its god, and they are also its tenants, the largest and most long-suffering ones. The bacteria eat their oil. The shrimp eat the bacteria. The fish eats the shrimp and the room's losses. And the machines, in the end, are fed by nothing but their own refusal to stop — which is the nearest thing to faith the drowned city has left.

So: three lives, by the room's own count, plus the heart they live inside.
