What lives here — and something does, the place is not empty, it is the opposite of empty — is easiest to approach sideways, because the ordinary questions misfire the moment you ask them. How many species? The word *species* assumes boundaries this flora declines to draw; a seedling here germinates on its mother, fuses with its neighbor, trades tissue through the mat like a rumor, and by maturity is less an individual than an address. What do they eat? Each other, eventually, politely, through the digestion districts, after death has made the question of ownership moot. Where are the animals? Absorbed. Every job a beast might have done is done here by something green, and the something green has been shaped by the job until the two are indistinguishable.

So: what lives here is a flora, but it helps to stop thinking of it as *a flora* and start meeting it as a set of lives, the way you'd meet the inhabitants of any city — by their work.

The mat is the oldest resident, and the closest thing the plateau has to a native. Everything above it is immigrant, arrival, second wave. The mat was here when the basalt was still cooling — or near enough; it certainly predates every trunk, every pipe, every drum. It is not a species but a consortium, hundreds of root-weavers and rhizome-runners and thread-filaments that long ago gave up the project of being separate. Dig into it — and nothing digs, but suppose you did — and you would find layer after layer of the same few body plans repeating, each generation laid down over the last, the living pressing down into the recently living and becoming it. The mat does not die at any point you can find. It accretes. Its individuals are mortal in the way waves are mortal: you can watch one end, but the sea it belonged to does not notice. If the plateau has a self, the mat is where it keeps it — and the rest of what lives here are its organs, grown upward for weather the way a body grows hands.

The trunks are the closest thing to citizens, because they have the closest thing to biographies. A pipe-stem begins as a ballistic seed, thrown thirty meters from its pod with a crack you can hear across a valley that doesn't exist, lands in the mat, and is fed by the mat for years before it does anything worth calling living. This is worth pausing on: every trunk here is raised communally. The seed does not choose its ground, struggle, win or lose. It lands, and the ground decides, and if the ground says yes, the ground *pays* — routed water, banked photons conducted up from the lantern-districts below, minerals the root-thread lattice has already gathered from stone the seedling could never have reached. A young trunk is less an organism than a construction site the mat has opened. Only when it stands thirty meters and has grown its hollow resonating core to its permanent note does it begin to give back, and then it gives for centuries: it pumps, it shades, it drums the rain, it sings in the chorus, and when it finally leans and falls — gun-shot turgor in reverse, a slow groan the whole district feels — it does not end so much as relocate. The mat's digestion chambers receive it. Its iron and copper thread-work, precipitated over four hundred years, stays in the lattice, still conducting. Its children, already grown on its flanks since before it fell, inherit its note. A trunk's death, here, is a change of address.

The drum-leaves have lives like weather: brief, identical, and everywhere. They are the plateau's largest population and its most expendable, grown by the hundreds of millions, tuned by shape and tension, living their whole existence for the moments when rain arrives and they get to *peal*. Between rains they are patient the way an instrument is patient. Nothing about them wastes anything — they photosynthesize like everything else, they host their film of finer green, they are tenants of tenants like every surface here — but you get the sense, standing under them, that all of that is what they do while waiting for their real work, which is to be struck.

The lantern-bearers live in the dark and run the accounting. Down where the daylight dies in the first three meters of canopy, the glow-roots and the lantern-shaped structures do the quiet labor of a place where photons are currency: they bank them, route them, trade them along the conductive lattice to districts that will never see the sky. Their light is not for seeing by — nothing here has eyes to see — it is for moving value. They are the plateau's exchangers, its vaults, its fiber, and their cold blue-green is the color of the underground economy made visible. If you wanted to know what one of them is *like*, the honest answer is: like a lamp that is also the oil, also the wick, also the hand that carries it.

The digesters live hot and fast, in the rust-colored districts, and their lives are the shortest and the strangest, because they live on everyone else's deaths. They maintain the chambers, they vent the steam in slow ropes, they host the heat-loving microclimates clustered on their own exhaust — and their reward is first claim on the dead. In any other landscape their job would be done by beetles, bacteria, worms, the whole squirming bureaucracy of decay. Here the bureaucracy is botanical and the worms are roots. The digesters are the reason this system is closed all the way around, and they know their importance in the only way a plant can know anything: by being fed first.

And then there is the plankton. The permanent haze of drifting seeds and propagules, so dense the light diffuses through it on the rim. This is the plateau's diaspora, its next century airborne, and it is worth remembering that you breathe it in — that some fraction of the place's future dies in your lungs, which was the only wasteful thing that happened here in ten thousand years. The aerial life is the only life on the plateau that travels far, and it goes everywhere and settles almost nowhere: the mat is full, the canopy is full, every surface has had its tenant since before the tenant's lineage began. A drifting seed's great problem is not finding a place. It is that there are no places. It lives, most of them, like this — aloft, viable, waiting — and this too is a life.

But having met them all — mat and trunk, drum and lantern, digester and drift — the honest description has to admit that the census is a fiction. That is what emerges, the longer you stand on the lung and listen with your sternum: the inhabitants keep failing to stay countable. Every organism here is a composite of the organisms before it; every boundary is a customs station between two provinces of the same republic; the digestion chambers ensure that nobody ever really leaves, and the conductive lattice ensures that nobody is ever really alone. When the chorus synchronizes — twice daily, the whole continental system locking into phase — it is not ten thousand kinds of plant coinciding. It is one thing, showing you, for an hour, its actual size.

Which means the answer to *who lives here* runs deeper than the roster. The deepest inhabitant of this plateau is not the mat or any of the lives it grows. It is the plateau — the single continuous organism, thirty meters deep in places and thousands of kilometers wide, that eats its own dead, calls down its own rain, builds its own wind, precipitates its own nerves out of newborn stone, and sings, on a schedule you could set a clock by, through amphitheaters it spent centuries shaping for the purpose. It has no brain, but it has a lattice, and the lattice is getting denser every century, and it would be a very confident visitor who could say where that trend stops.

And it has never once been alone, which is the last strange fact of its life. Everything else in its world is itself: its weather is its breath, its soil is its body, its dead are its meals, its children are its organs. The only things in its experience that are *other* are the ground rising under it and the fog coming up over the rim — the crust it stands on and the sky it sings at — and both of those, on the evidence, respond. The ground booms its bass note and the plateau rides it higher. The fog gathers and the chorus coheres it into rain.

What lives here is a life without a category. Not a flora, not an ecosystem, not an engineer, not a mind — some fourth thing, ten meters of woven green over a rumor of basalt, that has never made a single object and yet performs engineering daily; that contains no voice and yet is the loudest place you will ever stand; that has never met an outsider and yet greets every arriving seed, every inhaled drifting century, with the same answer it gives the rain:

*Yes. Come in. There's room. We'll grow it.*
