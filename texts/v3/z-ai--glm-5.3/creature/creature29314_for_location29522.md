What lives in the Throat lives by its rhythm, and there is a hierarchy to that tenancy, running downward from the almost-human to the barely-animal.

At the top of that hierarchy — though "top" is the wrong word for anything in a room shaped like a swallow — is the keeper. There has been only one keeper at a time for as long as the records in the room's own skin can show, and the current one is old in the way the walls are old: not declining, just finished. No one appointed them. The stair stopped being used, the district above thinned, the salary stopped arriving or the currency it arrived in stopped existing, and the keeper stayed because the work stayed, and at some point — no one could say when, least of all the keeper — the difference between tending the room and being tended by it dissolved entirely. Their job, as far as any visitor could reconstruct it, is to notice. To sit on the keeper's bench through the long swallows and the longer exhales and feel for the flaw in the beat, the drip that lands a hair early, the warmth that climbs half a degree past its custom. Their hands know the rope hand-rails by their damp, know the travertine rims by their growth, and once or twice in a decade they have caught a hairline change in the mineral accretion early enough to rub the healing skin against itself and settle the dispute before it became a breach. They sweat through their clothes in ten minutes like everyone else and never dry, and this no longer registers as discomfort, the same way the loudness no longer registers as sound to them. When you live inside one enormous sustained note, the note becomes silence, and what remains is only deviation. The keeper is a creature made of deviations.

They speak, but sparingly, and the room has colonized their voice. Anyone who has spent a night in the Throat reports the same peculiarity: the keeper talks in the gaps. Between swallows, the room is almost quiet — the low floor of it still there, but bearable — and the keeper times their sentences to land inside those apertures, the way the ceiling's drips land in time with the beat. Ask a question during the exhale and you get nothing, no impatience, no held breath, just a person standing in warm rain waiting for the room to finish its phrase. Then the swallow begins and the keeper says what they have to say, unhurried, pitched just above the tone, and if the sentence runs long it runs long, and the next aperture gets the rest. Conversations with the keeper are duets with the machinery, and visitors who don't understand this think the keeper is slow, or deaf, or possibly simple. The keeper thinks nothing about it at all.

Below the keeper — literally below, in the graded gravel and the tiled channels — lives the small nation. The warm rain and the mineral damp have produced a pocket biome with no sun in it anywhere, and its citizens are accordingly pale, soft, and punctual. There are white amphipod-like creatures in the film of the channels that come out at the exhale-rain and graze the fresh mineral wash, and they do this with such regularity that their emergence is itself part of the room's speech; the keeper counts them the way a farmer reads a season. There are thread-thin grazers on the lichen-machinery skin of the walls, blind, slow, tolerating kiln-heat that should cook them, feeding on the very film that heals the stone — and here the room's ancient agreement shows its teeth, because the grazers take so little, grow so slowly, breed so sparsely at this heat, that their browsing is indistinguishable from weather. They are the only predation the walls have agreed to, and the walls would not survive without being eaten, in some way the skin understands and no one else does. In the paste that was once dust in the corners there are mites. In the sumps, which no one sees, there are certainly things, and their absence from every account is its own kind of evidence.

And then there is the question of what else — the thing that visitors half-report and the keeper will not confirm or deny, which is that the room itself seems, on the fourth or fifth night, to be paying attention. Not sentient; nothing so narrow. But the swallows adjust to the presence of a sleeper on the bench, running longer and softer, the exhale-rains shifting their schedule like a host turning down a lamp, and people wake rested in a place where rest should be impossible. Whether this is care or mere pressure equalization doing what equalization does — responding to a warm body as a pressure event — is the sort of question the keeper has been asked and has answered, in a gap between swallows, with a shrug, which is the answer of someone who no longer believes the two are different.

The keeper was once, records suggest, one of several. Now there is one. Below the keeper, the pale nation. Below the pale nation, the skin, the stone, the rings, the column of worked air. And if you ask what lives in the Throat, the truest answer is that the Throat does. Everything else is its flora.
