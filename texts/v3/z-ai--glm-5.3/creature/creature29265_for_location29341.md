# The Bellfolk

They are thin people, and dense. That is the first thing you notice up close: not starved-thin, but drawn *tight*, like the heads of drums. A Bellfolk body carries itself the way a held note carries itself — nothing wasted, everything under tension, everything listening. They are dark-skinned from the glare that bounces up off the white rim, but the green gives them a faint undertone, a mineral pallor, as if the bowl's gypsum has gotten into their blood the way it has gotten into their roots' diet. Their hands are enormous. Their feet are enormous. These are the two organs of a Bellfolk face; the face itself, above the palms, is almost a formality.

They live, as the bowl lives, by substitution. Other people are mostly water; the Bellfolk are mostly sap — the cold clear spirit pressed from caskroot and chanter, drunk by the liter through the hot noon, sweated out through skin that smells faintly of struck bellfruit and wax. It makes them light-footed. It makes them, travelers swear, slightly *carbonated* — a people with a faint sweetness and pressure just under the surface, prone to sudden laughter that arrives like a popper going off, unannounced, at noon. Their children are loud the way fireworks are loud: brief, bright, and immediately hushed by a palm on the shoulder, and then translated, giggling, into taps on the nearest stone.

Because that is what a Bellfolk is, in the end: a translator. Their whole bodies are tympana. They sleep with palms flat on gypsum to read the day's messages rising through the rock. They stand in doorways with heads tilted, tasting the chord for the note of a neighbor's open flute-shutter — *there's Mava, home early* — the way other peoples read smoke or laundry. Their palm-language is quick and subtle and regional; the eastern flank has idioms the center finds rustic, all broad flat slaps, and the center's finger-flicking gossip is considered precious. There are poets. There is a whole literature, in the hand-language, of things that can only be said slowly, palm to palm, at Evening Cadence, when the pitch sinks and everything sounds like it means it.

Their dead are buried in the Silent Quarter — the Great Unclenching's scar, the one place the chord cannot go — and this is the tenderest fact about them: a Bellfolk grave is the only place in their world where a person can be truly *unheard*, and so they speak to their dead constantly, in full voice, in the open air. It is the only loud, naked, unprotected speech any of them ever produce. A visitor who stumbles into the north quarter and hears, for the first time, a Bellfolk woman weeping and talking out loud at a grave — actual voice, actual words, cracked and unlovely — understands at last what all the tapping has been for. The chord eats whispers. The chord eats everything. Only the silence is private, and the dead hold the silence, and so the dead hold all secrets.

They are gardeners, and masons, and tuners, and they do not distinguish between the three. A person's working life is measured in Tuning Weeks — *I have cut forty springs of bore-holes* — and the great craft-honor of the town is the staircase street, the climb to the First Notch, the oldest tuning cut in the basin. The one who remakes that notch each year is called, in the palm-language, roughly *the One Who Keeps the Oldest Note*, and it is a title held for life, and the holders' names are tapped into a pillar on the rim-side, and the pillar is full, and so the oldest names have worn smooth and are now only felt, not read, a texture of ancestors under the thumb.

They are cheerful in a way that unsettles travelers. This is the great misunderstanding: that a people born into deafening standing sound must be a crushed people, a flinching people. They are the opposite. They are the most *reassured* people you will ever meet. Their world hums, therefore their world is alive; the hum is heartbeat, weather, news, calendar, and god. When the chord stands at its full noon height, a Bellfolk feels what a sailor feels when the ship's timbers groan comfortably in a following sea — *all is well, the vessel speaks.* It is the Quiet, the white Flat beyond the rim, that horrifies them. Bellfolk children are told stories about it like graveyard stories. Some are about a woman who walked out over the rim to see what the world was like without sound, and the story's horror is not that she died of thirst. It's that for three days before she died, no one could hear her.

Two hundred of them now, in a town built for thousands. They do not mourn the crowd that is gone; the wide streets, the houses each alone in its pocket of acoustic shade, the enormous gardens — this is what *enough* looks like to them. Density, to a Bellfolk, is noise, and they are people who understand noise. They hear everyone. They meet no one. They would tell you, palms quick and amused, that this is the exact right number of people to live inside an instrument that is several thousand years into its composition — enough to keep it tuned, few enough to hear each tap rise clean out of the floor.

And at night they sleep on their rooftops under a sky thinned down to a single held thread of sound, palms flat on the warm gypsum, each one half in dream and half in conversation — because the taps are still traveling down there, the day's greetings and gossip and grave-talk rising through the rock like weather — and the thirteen knucklebirds sit on the topmost pipes, and the Bellfolk count them, thirteen, as they have for six hundred years, and find in it no strangeness at all, only the sweet deep reassurance of a world that is still, after everything, ringing.
