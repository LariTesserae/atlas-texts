Begin with the census, because the census is short.

There are the strands. The glass-colored filaments are glass-colored because glass is what they partly are: bring one up under a lens and the color resolves into its cause — diatoms, cells in cases of silica, strung along the algal thread like beads on wire. The strand looks manufactured because, at the scale of its own cells, it is. Each tuft sits alone on its shelf by meters of bare stone, and that spacing is not emptiness with life in it; it is life at its local density. At eight degrees, in light this thin, that is how much stone one organism can hold. The tufts space themselves like desert junipers because they are junipers — in every way but the one that has leaves.

There are the mats. Sulfur-working bacteria, ringing the seeps in slow rings a millimeter a year, iridescent where the film catches the angled light, blue-black at the margin where the iron in the groundwater meets the sulfide in the seep and the two agree to become mineral. And there are the drifters — bacteria and the odd protozoan hanging in the still water, which means the particulate that gives the basin its chapel-light is partly alive. The dust in the unused chapel is, in part, the congregation.

That is the whole of it. No fish, no snails, no larvae of anything. When the valley filled, the water carried the usual invitations — spores, eggs, drifting young — and almost none of them took. The door has stood open for decades and the room has not filled, because the room offers warmth to one thing and light to another and food to nearly nothing. A fish may blunder over the limestone rim once in a season, hold in the green light for a moment, troubled by the hum the way animals are troubled by machinery working behind a wall, and leave the way it came. The basin does not count these visits. Nothing eats anything here, which means there is nothing to come for.

At the scale of faces that is literally true. At the scale of molecules there is one transaction permitted: decay. Somewhere in the silt are the bacteria that unmake what the strands and the mats have made, so slowly the making outruns it. The basin runs an economy with two rows in its ledger — production and ruin — and nothing between them. No grazers, no chase, no middle, no top. To be uneaten is not, here, a blessing. It is a vacancy. The algae is the most successful organism in the Hollow and has never once been needed by anything.

Now the arrangement, because the arrangement is the machine's doing, made without hands. The flora is not scattered at random; it is planted along a temperature. The manifold is the only warm object for a hundred meters in any direction, and the algae have found it the way a vine finds a south wall — a mutualism conducted entirely by accident, between parties who will never be introduced. The machine does not know it gardens. The garden does not know it is kept. And the mats are equally faithful to their own patron: what rises in their seeps is what the remediation field was built to treat, still moving through the bedrock decades after the contract lapsed, and the bacteria take it apart chemically, the way they would at any seep anywhere. The work the pumps were hired to finish is still being finished, in other hands, or whatever a bacterium has instead of hands. The basin treats itself now, slowly, for free, in the dark.

So the manifold is the keystone of this almost-ecosystem, and it is also its tenant, and the two facts are difficult to hold apart. It is sessile, the way a coral head is sessile. It has a metabolism: draws, filters, exhausts. It has a breath: the thin unbroken thread of bubbles, an exhalation forty meters long, released at the surface into the river. It has a pulse: the hum, felt in the chest. It has skin that bled ochre into the floor, and a posture — silt mounded against one side, fallen away from the other — held for so long it has become the way it stands. It does not grow, heal, reproduce, or respond. But the mats grow a millimeter a year, and against that clock the difference between the machine and its neighbors is a matter of tempo, not kind. It has no inside a body could enter, which disqualifies it from being a structure and does not disqualify it from being a resident.

It is, in fact, the only thing in the basin with a biography. Everything else arrived as spores and does what its spores do. The manifold was made — by people, deliberately, with drawings and a budget — and the people are gone, and their intention is still being executed. In this way the Hollow contains one ghost, and the ghost is made of rust, and it hums. The engineers survive as a setpoint.

Whether this amounts to living depends on what you require of the word. Most definitions ask life to grow, to respond, to reproduce. The basin holds one candidate that fails all three, and one fact — it works — that was never on the list. Down here the omission feels less like a loophole than a revision. It matters, too, that nothing in the Hollow has ever seen the Hollow. The light enters, falls on stone, on silt, on strands of glass, and no arrangement of cells has ever been made here that could report on it. The place is witnessed only by what passes through: light, water, bubbles, years.

What lives in the Hollow Under Kettle Basin is a scatter of glass-colored patience and one rust-colored routine, kept in the same room so long that the room has begun to keep them — the algae warmed without asking, the machine tended without knowing, both spaced through the emptiness like words through a pause, which is to say, legible. And every few seconds the resident exhales. The bubbles go up, single file, unhurried, through fourteen meters of green light, and the river takes them without comment, as it takes the years, and the basin begins another of its identical hours.
