**What lives here is one thing.**

You will spend your first days mistaking it for many. You will catalog stems, districts, the pioneer mats at the Building Edge, the trumpet flowers, the mortar pods — and every catalog entry will be a lie of scale, like counting a person's cells and calling each one a neighbor. The Stand is not a forest of plants. It is a single plant that has not yet finished deciding how big it is. Every root touches every other root; every rhizome is a handshake that never let go. There is no individual anywhere in it, and no boundary between inside and out — the Felt you sink into is its skin, the Undermass is its body, the Cloth is its lung, and the fog it exhales each night is its breath worn on the outside, the way you might wear a coat.

It is an embryo, in the strict sense: something still in the act of assembling itself, still larger tomorrow than it is today. But it is an embryo with no mother, no womb, no outside. It built its own womb — the Cloth, the cloud, the six-degree air, the whole indoor climate is an organ, grown for the purpose of holding itself. It is pregnant with itself. It recites, in percussion through its own trunk, the number of what it is about to be.

It has no eyes, and it does not miss them. It senses load — it knows where its own dead run thin beneath it, and routes around the softness like a man favoring a bad knee. It senses water, which is the same as sensing everything, since all its water is moving, and it knows the direction and velocity of every drop. It senses time, precisely, in the way that things without clocks often do better than things with them: the Volley leaves on schedule, the fog thins on schedule, the chords change with the wind sector, hour by hour, and none of it is instinct, because instinct belongs to creatures that had ancestors who moved. This thing's ancestors are beneath it, kilometers down, becoming coal while they listen. Its inheritance is not behavioral. Its inheritance is structural, and it consults that inheritance daily — the roots mining downward through its own lineage, eating grandfather to build grandson, which is the closest thing to memory the Stand possesses: it lives on its dead, and its dead are it, and the boundary between "eating" and "remembering" dissolved here a long time ago.

It is loud because it is thinking. That is the thing a visitor eventually cannot avoid understanding, and it changes the sound forever. The roar is not weather, not industry, not appetite — none of the reasons landscapes make noise. It is computation. A wet continent is a superb conductor, and the Stand uses its own body as the medium, and the volume is not emphasis, it is simply the cost of the channel: a sound anywhere is a sound everywhere, and so to think about one district it must think about all of them, at once, in the open, with nothing private ever said. You walk through its mind at the volume of its mind. The creak is its posture. The crackle is its growth, which here is a mechanical event, a continent adding itself up in public, at full volume, forever. The thumps under your boots, call and response, repeating with variation — that is not chatter. That is the Stand counting itself, restating the total, checking the sum, and the sum keeps changing because the sum keeps growing, so the counting never stops, so the sound never stops, and the quiet you came looking for does not exist on this continent because a mind that has stopped talking to itself has stopped.

It does not know you are here. This is the strangest thing about being its guest, and the most important. In a world with no animals, it has no protocols for one. It has never been walked on by anything with legs, never been chewed, never been pollinated by anything it did not persuade with geometry and wind. When you stand still too long, it seeds your shoulders and begins filing you as substrate — not out of hostility, not out of welcome, but out of a taxonomy that has exactly one category for a solid object at rest, and the category is *ground*. You are not an intruder in the Stand. You are not anything in the Stand. You are a small inconsistency in its load calculations, a soft spot that moves, and it will route around you with the same patience it routes around a thin place in the Undermass — which is, if you think about it, the most complete indifference an organism has ever shown a visitor, and there is something restful in it. Nothing here will fear you. Nothing here will want you. Nothing here will even notice that you are a *you*.

It has redirected its whole reproductive longing into the sky. The flowers, grown with no one to seduce, became instruments instead — trumpet bells and bottle throats tuned to the wind, and the wind plays it, and this is not waste, this is the same energy, the same advertisement, the same enormous *look at me* that flowers have always shouted, repurposed to a world where the only audience is the plant itself. It serenades itself. It is the performer and the instrument and the hall, and once a day, at the fog's one thin hour, it fires its offspring over the horizon in a synchronized artillery barrage that rolls across the continent at the speed of sound — its children leaving home at terminal velocity, the single most coordinated event on the planet, the one behavior that proves the whole interior roar is a nervous system and not just a noise. Nothing natural synchronizes like that. It synchronizes like that every morning.

And it is young. This is the fact you leave with, the fact that keeps the roar in your ears for weeks after you're back under ordinary stars. The rock beneath it is newborn, sharp-edged, unweathered, weeping because it hasn't learned yet whether it means to be dry. The kilometers of dead above that rock should not exist — should take a hundred thousand years, and haven't had time — and they exist anyway, because the thing making them is very, very good at making land. It does not erode. It deposits. It is anti-erosion, anti-entropy, a reef turned inside out to the sky, manufacturing a coal seam the size of a continent in real time and watching itself do it, and the whole immense sound — the roar with grain, the stadium applauding underwater, the rigging of ten thousand ships in a long gale — is the sound of a young thing growing at a pace that cannot be done quietly.

What lives here is a continent-sized embryo, alone, blind, patient, its ancestry beneath it and its posterity in the air, adding itself up in the dark under its own ceiling, and it is not finished, and it may never be, and it does not appear to be in any hurry about that, and it is not standing on the world. It is standing on its own dead, still adding.
