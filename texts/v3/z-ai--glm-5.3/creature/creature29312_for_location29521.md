Who lives here is the wrong question, briefly, and then it becomes the right one, and the answer is: everyone. Everything. The city and its citizens are the same biomass differently organized, the way your bones and your thoughts are the same you.

The bellstalks are the most obviously person-like. They are the city's musicians and, in a sense, its voice — grown from seed in ranks along the avenue shoulders, deliberately spaced, because somebody understood acoustics. Nobody who is not a bellstalk understands exactly what they say to each other in those organ chords, but the chords change hour to hour, and they change in coordination — the towers on the north wall answering the towers by the plazas a full second before the wind that supposedly explains them arrives. They are tuned by the wind, yes. They are also listening.

The gutter-figs are the artisans. Their dew-chains, leaf-tip to leaf-cup, are too precise to be accident; walk any street and count the drip-tips — every one aimed, every one catching. A craft like that, sustained across kilometers, is not a behavior. It is a tradition. Somewhere in the rhizosphere there are figs learning it from other figs, the way apprentices learn, through slow chemical conversation at the root-tips, corrections passed down in sugar-code: *a hand-width left. The dew runs off at the edge otherwise. My grandmother grew it this way and her grandmother before her.*

Because there are grandmothers. That is the thing you understand by the third day inside. The flatcrowns at the top of the canopy, the ones leasing out every cubic meter of light below them — those are the elders. You can tell by the buttresses, by the way whole districts of understory arrange their leaves in deference to the shadow of a particular crown, by the fact that when a flatcrown falls, and one has, recently, its clearing is a plaza now, and the city has left it empty. Hard-packed salt pan. Neutral ground. Nothing builds on the grave of an elder. The oldest streets bend around the oldest roots the way old roads in old countries bend around old churches, and no contract in this city is more binding than that bend.

And beneath everything, in the waterlogged dark where the drumroots pound — the drumroots, sixty meters of forced sap landing in your sternum — lives the part of the city that never sees its own streets. The rhizosphere is the crowd, the public, the body politic: an interlocked mass of root and fungal thread so dense it functions as one tissue, trading messages the way a market trades goods, slow floods of hormone and sugar that carry news. A herbivore's jaws on the south wall — there are herbivores, small armored things, tolerated in numbers, culled in excess — register on the far side of the valley in under an hour. A drought three ridgelines away is discussed before it arrives. This is not metaphor. The valley's aqueducts begin carrying more water on the second day of a dry wind out of the west, before the wind has crossed the ridge. Something decided. Something down there, distributed and root-bound and patient on a timescale you find uncomfortable, looked at the weather and issued instructions, and ten thousand towers re-tuned to comply.

So: no one lives here, if by "lives here" you mean walks its streets. Nothing wears the city. But the city is worn. It is inhabited from inside, every cubic meter of it, by its own substance — citizens of leaf and xylem, governed from the rhizosphere, deliberating in drones, mourning in plazas, building in contracts of straight lines, on land so new the sea left it a lifetime ago and something was ready, waiting, and moved in before the water had fully drained.

What lives in this valley is the valley's green, organized. And it has been talking the whole time you stood on the ridge listening. You thought you were hearing weather. You were hearing a town meeting that has been in session, without interruption, for several thousand years.
