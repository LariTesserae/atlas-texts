## The Inhabitants

Ask the valley who lives here and it answers with functions. You came looking for residents and you keep meeting occupations. No one is home in the sense you mean — and yet the place is fully staffed, every hour, including the hours when the sky above the ridgeline is scoured clean and the cold outside the canopy is a fact, like the color of anything.

Begin at the top, where the inhabitants are largest and least personal. The pillar trees — call them the heaters, since heating is their office — stand in their tens of thousands in their dark felted coats, each one carrying a hundred thousand small hearths overhead. Every broad leaf burns sugar through the night to hold its own tissue above freezing. Rest the back of your hand near one after dark and, against every instinct you have about what a leaf is, it is warm — faintly, steadily, like the flank of something sleeping. This is not a figure. But no tree heats for itself; the arithmetic is collective, and the exhalation of the whole upper story lifts the interior of the valley by degrees. Which means the first strange fact about everyone who lives here is that none of them is standing in weather. The understory, the halls, the pipelines, the grazer on its rounds — all of them live downstream of someone else's breath. The heaters work the night shift by design. They are the establishment, the old arrangement, the ones whose patience has become indistinguishable from architecture.

Though no individual tree is as old as what it belongs to. Trees do die here; they tip in the slow soundless way and are admitted downward. What persists is the arrangement — the warmth, the twilight, the held climate — and the arrangement is the eldest resident in the valley, older than any trunk, maintained the way a river maintains its shape while shedding all its water.

Then the venters: towers of woody lattice rising from the floor districts, releasing their slow plumes of spore-dust and steam, the exhaust of ten thousand furnaces gathered and channeled up through purpose-grown flues. Whatever else they are — and the valley offers no explanation — they are the only correspondence this economy keeps with anywhere else. On still days the plumes stand straight above the canopy, one to a district, like steeples, or like announcements from an industry with no products to sell. Read them as letters. Nothing here is waiting for a reply.

The real work lives in the halls, and the halls are residents too. Long segmented bodies of living girders, flattened stems braided over centuries into corridors, humid and dark and warm as breath. Inside them the past is processed: leaf-fall, branch-fall, tipped heaters, the collapsed corridors of older halls — all of it descending through their intake margins into fungal vats, digested, refined, resourced. They take their own dead. They take their own predecessors. In this economy nobody retires; you are reassigned to the vats, and your warmth is fed back up through pipelines that run for kilometers, pulsing faintly — freight with no manifest. Press a palm to one in the quiet and you feel the tick of it, slow and peristaltic, sugars addressed upward to furnaces that will burn them into another night of climate.

So the valley is one body? It wants to look that way: one circulation, one metabolism, warm at the core and busy in the gut. But walk the floor districts long enough and you find the rivalry. Whole tracts of lattice ease down over a week as newer growth crowds them out — collapse here is not catastrophe but succession, acquisition conducted below the threshold of sound, an entire hall surrendering its corridors the way a company is outbid. They shade each other. They strangle each other's flues. If this is one organism, it is one that devours its own limbs on schedule and calls the result growth. The census will not close on this question. The valley declines to say whether it is a commonwealth or a single enormous body wearing a thousand names, and eventually you understand that the distinction is your luggage, not theirs.

The white stalks live in the mist, and they are the only residents whose presence you can feel in your teeth. Bone-pale, tall, standing apart in the wet hours while the air tickles and the hair on your arms lifts, passing slow discharge between neighbors like a conversation at the speed of held thunder. If the valley is one body, these are its nerves. If it is many, they are its gossip. Whatever they are for, they make the air feel occupied.

Somebody, meanwhile, makes the silence — it is manufactured, like everything else here. The leaf-carpets upholster the floor, padded and layered, evolved to drink impact, so that even the weather arrives softened. The condensation cycle handles the rest: cold air meets the exhaled warmth at the canopy line and falls back as a fine interior drizzle that never touches open sky, and the upper forest glistens always, beaded runs sliding down the huge trunks toward the shallow root-mats. The place rained on itself into being, and it keeps raining on itself, quietly, on schedule, inside a weather system the size of itself.

Down at the dry riverbed lives the one whose whole life is withholding: the tuber-mat, gray-brown and unremarkable, disguised between floods as dead ground. Its camouflage is idleness. It has learned the pulse rhythm — the intermittent rains, the melt letting go above — and it opens its pores only when the water arrives, drinks a whole flood in an hour, and seals again. It is the water bank; every drop that enters this economy through the river enters through it. Its patience makes even the boulder-shoulderers look hasty, and the shoulderers have held their stones for millennia — roots that leaned rocks into stable arrangements and kept them there so long the stone has settled into the grip, groove to root, the boundary between holding and being held dissolved. That is the local form of strength: not force. Tenure.

And then the tolerated ones — a handful of species, all silent, all on contract. The grey grazer you may actually see: long-legged, cropping the fungal margins in unhurried rounds, watching you with that deep proprietary unconcern, which is the only face this valley will ever show you, and which is the look of the whole economy, pointed. It is paid in margins; what it delivers, in its slow circuits and its droppings, is tidiness and inoculation, spores carried hall to hall. There are others you only glimpse: a flat pale thing riding the freight inside the pipeline dark; something leathery up under the girders, dusted with spore-work; a canopy-dweller you know only as a wake in the interior drizzle. Almost no insects. The plants never needed couriers — the wind does the pollination, the branches do the touching, the freight lines do the carrying. The animals are staff, not citizens, and their contract renews each dawn in a currency of warmth they did not make and could not replace.

Which is the last thing to understand about whoever lives here: they made all of it themselves. The heaters made the climate, the halls made the digestion, the carpets made the silence, the mats made the water bank, the shoulderers made the arrangement with stone, and the mist made the rain — and no one made any of it on purpose. There was no first resident who built for the rest. There is no resident in charge. There are no artifacts because nothing here is dead enough to be one; every tool is also a body. What lives in this valley is an economy that has eaten its own ecology and called the result home: labor without laborers, warmth without hearth-tenders, a population whose every member is also the place.

Stand on the bare ridgeline and look back at the whole of it — canopy unbroken to the horizon, valley opening beyond every rise into more of itself, a thousand plumes you cannot tell from trees. If you insist on filing a census, it closes with a single entry. It has no name. It has never needed one, and it has never needed a witness — which is this place's way of saying, quietly, without saying anything at all, that it has never needed you.
