# The People of the Ascending Scaffold
## Where the founders are somebody's grandmother

Begin with the inversion, because everything else about these people follows from it: *they are older than their city.* Not some of them. All of them. There are women still living who remember the fourth tier as the roof of the world, who climbed as girls to its unfinished edge and looked down on cloud. A handful of the first masons are still alive — ancient, unhurried, hands too swollen for a chisel — and children are brought to touch those hands the way children in older cities are brought to touch relics. Elsewhere, founders are myth. Here, they are somebody's great-grandmother, and they grumble about their joints like everyone else.

Living in a place younger than yourself reorganizes a person. It makes you a parent rather than a tenant. Nobody here speaks of owning property; they speak of *raising* it. And the city did not plan its people any more than it planned its ferns — they are life that arrived opportunistically, because warmth and water and green appeared where none had been, and life is opportunistic to its bones. The locals know this about themselves and find it funny.

Who stays, then? Not the finished. People whose lives have set like good mortar cannot abide the place — too negotiable, nothing completed, noon like pewter. They come up on the hoist-platforms with decisive luggage, and they are back down within the year, and the locals can spot them at the gate. The ones who remain arrived feeling like a staircase that is nine steps going up and ten coming down — and found, in a city built out of that exact condition, a kind of citizenship.

They are barrel-chested from the thin air and slow of speech, and they dress the way they build: in tiers, each layer settled before the next goes on. The damp finds their joints; the old walk like gantry cranes — mid-reach, pausable — and *pausable* is a compliment here. Their whole culture orbits one verb: *settle*. Foundations settle. Couples settle. Disputes settle. The dead settle into the walls. To call a stranger *well-settled* is the highest praise the city offers, and most strangers walk away not realizing they have received it.

## The guilds of the patient

**The lamplighters** are half engineer and half priest, as advertised, and the priest half gets more of the work the higher you go. They tend the arc-lamps and the hammered-tin reflectors; they audit leases written in lumens; they referee the long cold war between the light-wards and the dark-quarters. Above the tenth tier their job stops being maintenance and becomes ministry — the lamps up there are a fence — and a lamp that fails up there is not a work order but a funeral. Ask what the light is kept against and you will be told, pleasantly, that the light disagrees with something, and the something prefers the dark, and this is all the theology you will get. When a lamplighter dies, the guild lights one new lamp in their name and adds it to the works. The bright parts of the city are, in part, a graveyard, and the reflectors are kept polished, the lamplighters say, so the dead can see themselves working.

**The masons and their foundation-wards** practice a craft that is mostly waiting with instruments. An apprentice's first commission is to sit a season with a settling foundation and report what the stone does. The diligent fill notebooks with weather. The promising turn in blank pages, and the masters nod, because *it held* is the whole report. Their oath is sworn palm-down on a mending-stone: *nothing outruns the rock.*

**The dew-wardens** tend the copper funnels and read the pipes at dawn like scripture, listening to the mountain drink. A good warden can tell from three streets away whether a cistern is sulking. Their law is the strangest in the city: mist rights, a household's water measured in hours of condensation on its roof, argued in disputes conducted almost entirely in damp. A family's fortune here is legible in its patina.

**The gardeners** do not plant; they *permit*. Their craft is allowance — which fern may hold this seam, whether the moss on the third beam has become load-bearing with sentiment. They weed by negotiation, and the worst word in their vocabulary is *planter*: one who forces. They also keep the city's quietest honor, which we will come to.

**The bell-keepers** maintain two ledgers, one for bells rung and one for bells pre-heard, because up here sound carries from the future. Occasionally an entry appears in the second ledger and never in the first — a bell that was heard and never rang — and the keeper on duty is entitled to a day off and a strong drink. Civic etiquette is strict: you never act on an early bell. The city lives in a deliberate present tense, because answering tomorrow's sounds gets you out of step with the stone. Some people hear early all their lives — the *early-hearers*, a tolerated and useful caste — and they are seated at the backs of meetings and told no surprises.

**The cartographers** keep two atlases, one ascending and one descending, for the same stones under different names. Both atlases are right, which is why the guild has survived by never saying so aloud. Mail is addressed both ways, and letters tend to arrive by whichever version of the street they prefer. Above the tenth tier, where distance is negotiable, guides carry knotted cords and count their steps out loud.

## The chuffs, the idle rich, and the ones with no names

The ptarmigan-things are called *chuffs*, which is less a name than the sound of ten thousand of them being contented in the dark. They are plump, grey, spherical in winter plumage they will never need, and no one has ever seen a chuff learn anything. They foul the scaffold, jam the pulleys, sleep in the mail. The city long ago stopped trying to manage them and now simply budgets for coexistence. Their meat tastes like the mountain's opinion of itself — chalk and wet slate — so they are eaten only at funerals, and this is deliberate: at the moment you give someone back to the rock, everyone at the table tastes rock. Their molted feathers are gathered each dawn by sweepers from under the roosts, a grey snowfall harvested like the dew, and the whole city sleeps under chuff-quilts, which the birds regard, correctly, as none of their concern. The annual chuff census is taken by ear, by children, who consider it a great office.

The small sure-footed grazers on the flanks are whistled down at dawn by the same children, to breakfast on the dreaming rock's shed curls. Their meat is excellent — it tastes of the green that shouldn't be here — and the locals will tell you a grazer can only be raised on an unfinished mountain. The huge fat condor-things that ride the thermals in slow, patient spirals are called *the idle rich*, and it is meant admiringly. They land rarely; a landed one is an omen of thickening mist or something at altitude. By immemorial custom, a bird perched on a frozen gantry crane cannot be moved, and the crew stands down — *waiting on the bird* — and no foreman has ever successfully argued with tradition.

And then there are the hunters, the long, low, wrong-angled things that come down from higher, colder altitudes, and the first thing to know is that they have no name, because names are invitations and what is invited stays. When one descends, the chuffs stop first — the murmur dies in a spreading wave, a held breath moving through the scaffold — then the upper arcs flare, then the forges add a night shift whose real product is sound. Witnesses are almost always lamplighters, who decline to describe. What survives into the language is the word *wrong-angled*, which the locals now apply to crooked shelves, dishonest merchants, and staircases that miscount — everyone knows where the word came from, and no one says. Dreaming rock is soft enough to take a thumbprint. After a sighting, people grip their mending-stones hard enough to leave themselves in them, and families keep those stones in drawers, and you can read a family's worst nights by lamplight, printed in the shape of their hands.

## Etiquette for an unfinished world

Directions are given conditionally — *third lane on your left going up, or Tin Lane if you're coming down* — and children learn both atlases of their own street as a game, while adults treat the discrepancies as law rather than error. The nine-and-ten stair has a skipping rhyme: *nine up, ten down, either way you're still in town.* On the third terrace, where the fog comes out of the insides of buildings, a house that breathes is thought *settled well*; nobody caulcks anything, and airtight houses are considered dead. Children chase the mirror-pair chuffs; a person who has once seen a pair fly *apart* does not chase them again. Children also see the second image under the puddles' reflections with no difficulty at all. Adults say they cannot, which is not the same as being unable.

The mending-stone is chosen young — a child hunts the rock's sheddings and simply knows their stone when they grip it — and it is the local answer to a world whose grain runs against you. A lifetime of the pocket wears it small and polished, a compressed proof of every anxious hour survived. And at the end, the stone goes into the mortar with you. The dead here are given back to the works: settled, literally, into the newest course, a fern permitted over the spot, so that over generations the greenest terraces are the quiet ones — the vegetation maps the moisture, yes, and it also maps the dead, which is the honor the gardeners keep. The masons hold the register of who rests where. You do not strike the ninth course of the Dew Stair. That is where the Lindens are.

Courtship observes a settling-period, like a foundation: the pair is watched for months to see whether they slump, and the wedding is held, when it can be managed, at the hour a foundation is declared settled, so that the couple and the city settle together.

But there are two ways to die here, and the city holds them differently. Those taken by the mountain — the ones who climbed toward the Scaffold Beyond, who stepped into the unfinished like a bed taking a sleeper — are not settled at all. Their funerals stay open: a seat kept at the table, a wage of grain set aside *for when they come down*. And on the one evening in six when the Scaffold Beyond shows its lamps, the families climb to the rule line and stand looking up, arguing gently about which light is theirs. The faithful say the taken are up there, finishing the mountain from inside its dream. The engineers say mirage, refraction, the summit-works through haze. The lamplighters say nothing and keep every lamp above the tenth tier burning, which functions as an answer for all parties. No one climbs. Everyone knows the rule, and the rule is not really about danger — it is about manners. You do not walk into a child's dream while the child is still deciding what it is.

## The youngest resident

Ask a local who lives here, and the answer will walk downhill through the whole catalogue — the guilds, the chuffs, the grazers, the idle rich, the ones with no names — and then pause, and your informant will look up at the veiled summit and say: *the mountain.* The youngest thing on this mountain is the mountain. It is soft the way young things are soft; it sheds itself in curls; it is still deciding. The city treats it accordingly, the way one treats a sleeping child-giant: quiet when it is mid-decision, patient with its moods, quietly proud of its growth spurts — whole terraces arriving overnight that no one poured. There is a covenant here that no one has ever spoken aloud because it is performed nightly instead: *we build at your pace; in exchange, consent to be a place.* The great oceanic drone of the works is, among other things, the hum an adult makes over a cradle.

No one living will see the crown finished. The works are a debt of stone to be discharged by grandchildren, and the locals find this *comforting* — in a city where nothing is completed, being unfinished is not a failure state. It is the family resemblance. **The geology has not finished deciding, and neither has anyone else.**

## Come back at dawn

Come back at dawn, on a mid-terrace, when the pipes are gurgling their swallowed-water language and the sweepers are working the roosts like a grey harvest; ten thousand chuffs shaking the dew off their shoulders; the grazers filing down to breakfast at a child's whistle; the lamplighters walking the reflector-lanes banking the lamps one by one, slowest at the uppermost, which they let burn a little long out of respect. A gardener stands at a mortar seam, permitting a fern. On the nine-and-ten stair sits the oldest woman on the seventh terrace with her granddaughter and two bowls of forge-warmed broth, both their breaths fogging, waiting — comfortably, professionally — for the city to catch up with them. She was born on this terrace, back when this terrace was the sky.

They are all of them mid-becoming together — masons and chuffs, lamplighters and idle rich, the ferns, the founders, the girl with the blank notebook — settlers of the not-yet, raising a city that is raising them. And the mending-stones in their pockets are not really charms against the world's grain. They are calling cards, carried close: small soft proofs that they were here before the place was finished, and intend to be here when it is. No one here ever quite leaves. The walls are the census, the light is the ledger of the dead, and everything — the rock, the streets, the birds, the people, the mountain over all of it — is still settling.
