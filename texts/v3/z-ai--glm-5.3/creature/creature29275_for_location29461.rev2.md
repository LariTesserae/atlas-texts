# The Census of the Court

What lives here is mostly green, and the green is not passive. Understand that first, or you will miscount everything.

## The Heron

The heron is the closest thing the court has to a citizen who chose. It arrived — whether with the flood or before it, no one can say, and the heron does not volunteer information — and it stayed, and it adapted, and it adapted *wrongly* by every natural law you carry with you. It stands in the iris beds with its feathers dry. There is no surface here, no air pocket, no margin where bird meets sky, and yet the water lies off its plumage the way rain lies off wax. When it breathes, its throat moves, and the water goes in and is simply *accepted*, filed under the same category as air, and the heron gives the matter no more attention than you give to breathing.

It fishes for light. The old brightness pooled in the stone hollows, the sedimented glimmers that sank in with the flood — the heron takes these one swallow at a time, patient as everything in this room, standing motionless for a day over a coin of dimming light before it dips and takes it. What the light does inside the heron is one of the court's unanswerable questions. But on certain hours — when the water smells of low tide, when the gulls are almost audible — the heron's eye holds a faint glow like a lamp behind horn, and it is possible the light is not consumed there but *kept*, the way the water keeps the memory of every shore.

The heron is not lonely. That's important. A creature alone for generations would wear its solitude somewhere, and the heron wears nothing but patience. It acts like a being among its own kind, which suggests that whatever it counts as company — the frogs, the bell, you, if you sit still long enough — is sufficient.

## The Frogs

The frogs sit on the boundary stones near the old wall, and sitting on them is the most accurate description available, because the frogs do very little else. They are pale, blind, and warm. You can rest a hand near one — never on one; there is a feeling around them like standing too close to someone else's letter — and feel heat rising off their small bodies into snowmelt water, which is wrong, which has always been wrong, which is the point.

The frogs belong to the pre-worn past the flood delivered. Whatever they were before the water carried them here — and their stillness suggests they remember — they are not alive in the ordinary sense now. They are present. This is the court's distinction, and after enough hours here it stops sounding like a quibble. The frogs are present the way the boundary stones are present: enduring, warm, keeping carvings worn to suggestion somewhere under their skin. They know the last law, the one about drowning as a change of citizenship, and they sit on it like a secret, which is the most energetic thing they do.

## The Elder Tench

There is one fish, and it is enough. A pale tench, old past the flood's arrival — which should be impossible in a valley three seasons old, and the impossibility is consistent with everything else — patrolling the walls in a circuit that takes hours and has the gravity of an office no one has named because naming it might embarrass it. Keep. Custodian. Warden. None of these are right. The tench moves along the boundary like someone walking a fence line they have walked since before the fence was built, before the land the fence enclosed existed.

It does not eat the green. It does not interact with the heron. It passes the frogs with a slowness that might be respect. Its circuit includes the bell's ruined hut, and there it always — *always* — pauses, one beat longer than anywhere else, before continuing. Whatever office it keeps, it keeps it on behalf of whoever cut the stair.

## The Green

And then the majority. The population of the court is its plants, and they are not scenery. The hair-grass drowning upright, the ferns resting fronds on each other like hands, the camphor-laurels with their cool-leather leaves and their refusal to fall — all of it is here *in the transferred sense*. Everything green in this room should have died at some point, in some earlier chapter, in some former place, and was instead moved here, patiently, by the courier water that has been everywhere and remembers all of it.

So the green is made of former things. Former trees, former meadows, former gardens whose gardeners are worn to suggestion. When the moss grows over your fingers — and it will, millimeters an hour, curious rather than hungry — you are being examined by something with a very long memory of shapes like yours.

The pale vine climbing its own older stems is the closest the green comes to a philosophy: growth as sediment, present stacked on past, patience as architecture.

## The Bell

The bell is not alive. But it counts, and it hums through your sternum when the water gains another centimeter, and in a room where the ground has no floor and the rain is still falling forty meters overhead, being the only thing that keeps count is its own kind of life. Ask the frogs. They'd know. They won't say.

That is the census: one heron keeping light, frogs keeping a secret, a fish keeping an office, a bell keeping count, and a garden keeping everything else — drowningly, patiently alive, at the bottom of unfinished weather.
