Ask this place who lives here and it will answer in order of loudness — which, on this shelf, is also the order of importance.

The first and loudest answer is the grove. A dozen of them, give or take, spaced like conspirators because a voice needs room and courtesy here is measured in paces. Each one is less a plant than a citizen with a district attached: a hollow column sheathed in wax bark, a great bell of green velvet angled down at the light, and stacked on that single stalk everything a rainforest would spread across a valley — moss carpet, epiphyte cushions, hanging spore-lanterns, vines. The lushness is vertical. From the rim, the census looks easy.

The eldest is called the Deep Organ, and it is the closest thing this shelf has to a ruler, except that its entire program of government is to get very slowly lower. Its note is the floor the chord stands on; you take it in the sternum. It has been flattening a hair a century for longer than anyone has kept records worth the name, and when it drops, the whole shelf moves house into the space it makes. The finches are the evidence: half a note above their grandmothers. On this shelf, aging is not a private matter. When the Organ settles, everybody's ceiling settles with it, and the congregation adjusts the only way congregations can — generation by generation, into the gap.

Against one rim stands the youngest, and it has no name, because nothing here is named until it has a voice. It is all pith still, walled solid with its own childhood, mute as a held breath — and a few hornets attend it anyway, humming nothing. That is the only unemployment on the shelf, and it is voluntary. Some dry season soon, or ten dry seasons from now, it will crack and hollow, and the wind will find the aperture, and the shelf will christen it with its note, and its name will be a frequency.

Somewhere in the middle distance there may also be the retired one: a stalk that split crooked and went silent standing up. The crabs strip it, the moths' young polish it, the wind has already fired it — and that, under the one written law cut into the flat stone, is the definition of death. *Take no pipe the grove still plays.* Read the other way, the law says: you may take what the wind has finished with. On this shelf, death is not something you discover. It is something the grove tells you, by stopping.

Each of the twelve is a neighborhood, then. The bell's throat is carpeted — chlorophyll on the ceiling, the whole architecture upside down out of loyalty to a lamp the size of a horizon. Spore-bells hang like lanterns and pop in volleys when the crowd is in. Vines and gourd-gardens crowd the shoulders, and note that word, *gardens*, because a garden is a thing somebody tends. Into each bell go the carillon-hornets, and out they come powdered and humming the pitch of the plant they serve — which is either loyalty or a lock and key, and functions as both: a hornet finds its employer by ear in a flood, through vapor, in the dark. The Deep Organ's hornets hum so low you feel one land before you see it.

The ground floor is staffed. Sexton-crabs live inside the pipes, scouring and sharpening, paid in gloss and pollen — undertakers of the hollow, which is the correct profession here, since the pipes earn their voices by dying correctly and somebody has to keep the coffin clean. A pipe without its crabs dulls within a season, and a dull pipe is passed over, and a passed-over pipe leaves no seed; neglect on this shelf is a short story with a foreseeable ending. Shuttermoths are the audience — millions at flood, wing-clatter like applause on tin, the house taking its seats — and their children graze the wax bark, a tax every pipe pays without complaint, or at least without any means of complaining. Chime-finches supply the melody, the pipes being too dignified for anything but the held note: flocks moving overhead in running figures, a tune drawn by committee. And the front-runners are the criers — lean birds skating the flood's own skin ahead of the front, piping what is technically an alarm and functionally an invitation: the sea is leaning in, the bazaar is open. They nest in the drilled flues of the pinnacles, housing the old tuners provided; whether the flues were built to whistle and happened to acquire tenants, or built as birdhouses that happened to acquire a whistle, is the sort of question this place declines to rank.

Then the loom-oxen: the percussion section. Shaggy, wax-fleeced, up to the hocks in glow and gleaming as if they too had been lacquered by the floods, they wade in with footfalls like muffled drums and graze the gloss off the crust in long strips, a sound like wallpaper coming down. Their bellies rumble the bass line the pipes consider beneath them. And their name tells you the rest: a loom-ox is named either for the way it looms out of fog or for the loom its fleece ends up on, and on this shelf both etymologies hold, because nothing here gets a name that doesn't describe its future. An ox that dies correctly becomes a lintel, a flute rack, a wall's one bone. Nobody finds the arrangement strange. The pipes have the same deal.

Say it plainly, because everything follows from it: nothing on this shelf owns anything except a frequency, and everything else is wages. The pay is gloss and pollen. The gloss — the flood's film, warm, oily, faintly luminous, beading on stone in slow fat drops that crawl rather than run — is water, currency, and weather at once, and whether it is itself alive is a question the shelf refuses to answer. It feeds everything. It glows after dark. It moves like something with an appointment. Money here has moods.

Below the whole arrangement lives the landlord. The sea is the largest resident by any measure — uncountable small bright things laboring in it, glowing from within, with slow lanes of light moving through it at night like streets seen from a great height. It has neighborhoods; it has traffic; the flood is not weather that happens to it, it is something it does. The small bright things themselves have no names, which is consistent: names here follow voices, and a thing that speaks only as a horizon's worth of glow cannot be named by anything as small as a person. And out of the sea, rarely, the fog stands up. A heron. A school of fish-shapes swimming through air. Once, famously, a kneeling figure holding what looked like a cut reed — carved into the rimstones more than once, which is how you know the sightings are old, and seen again within living memory, which is how you know they are not finished. Remembering, mimicking, luring, or merely grazing — nobody has settled it, and the argument is conducted between people who cannot speak over the chord, which keeps it both endless and quiet.

There is a quieter theory, held by people who hold things quietly. It says the kneeling figure is not the sea doing an impression. It says it is the tuner — seen from too far, through too much vapor, kneeling in the flood to cut a reed — and that the figures collapse without drama because a person can walk out of a cloud in a way a cloud cannot walk out of a person. Nobody confirms this. Nobody exactly denies it either. Confirming anything here requires walking over to someone, and by the time you have, the flood has gone.

Because someone lives here, and that has to be said plainly too, since everything follows from it. The tuners were plural once — the rack's grammar is plural, the way they wrote to each other was plural, short motifs blown on cut reeds and ox-bone flutes pinned with hammered copper, facing the wind. Their hand-sign alphabet is cut into flat stone and you can decode it by watching the place work. Their one recorded law is half conservation and half a definition of death, and their whole ethics is somewhere inside it: find out by listening. They built a spiral wall a knee high, one stone at a time, dry-stacked, and if you ask what it is *for* you have already misread it — one stone per visit, or near enough. The wall is a calendar a person is building out of patience. You can read it the way you read the crust's bands.

If one lives here still — and the morning's evidence says somebody does — then here is how. They eat from the gourd gardens, which fill in flood season and age their contents into gloss of a grade nothing else on the shelf produces: the reserve, the pantry, the savings. Nothing here drinks; they anoint, like everything else, and are anointed. They hear through the sternum, which does not retire the way ears do. And they work in the loud — during the big swells, when the chord fills every crack of the mountain and no footfall, no chisel, no drag of stone can be told apart from weather. That is why you never hear them. That is why, in the hush after the flood, the hush feels occupied: the hush is the footprint. The wall has one more stone. The reed is gone from the rack. The flutes are singing a phrase nobody cut yesterday, and it is somebody's mail, and you could read it — the alphabet will let you — but some correspondence is not addressed to you, on any world.

Whether it is one tuner or two, old or young, alive, or something the sea has agreed to keep the appointments of, the census does not close. That is the shelf's doing. It does not explain its residents; it demonstrates them.

The deepest resident, if you want the whole truth, is the chord itself — the shelf wearing itself into itself, a skin in balance, digesting its weather into music a centimeter a century, with everything on it somewhere in that long metabolism: Organ and ox, crab and hornet, finch and figure and tuner. And you, briefly. You were counted before you ever reached the rim — the scree sang along on the stairs, and so, faintly, did your teeth; the shelf recruited you from below, the way it recruits everything, by resonance.

In the morning the reed is gone, and whoever took it has gone with it, in the direction the wind drains, into the hollows where a person can live on gloss and patience and the bass notes of oxen. The census stays open by one reed, one stone, one phrase. On this shelf that is what a resident looks like. Not something you see — something the music cannot do without.
