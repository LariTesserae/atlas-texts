The residents are few, and they know it.

The first to arrive — within hours of the stone breaking surface, before the towers had height, before the chord found its bass — were the silk-makers. They are not animals, exactly. They are more like the place's first opinion of itself: white bacterial filaments that anchored in the warm seams and began streaming, immediately, in the current. They hang from every ledge the heat allows, unspooling like laundry left out by someone who never intended to return. In the red light of the glowing seams they look like frost woven into cobwebs, or cobwebs pretending to be frost. When something disturbs them — a pressure wave, a falling grit-rain, your presence — they answer in the only language available to them: pinpoints of blue-green light, cold sparks that flare and are gone before the eye can be sure of them. Every confirmation they give is also a retraction. They have lived here their whole lives, which is to say days, and already their oldest colonies dangle in the ruins of the first towers, dissolving alongside them, silk on the bones of the place's earliest mistakes.

On the hotter shelves, where even the silk won't hang but the mats will lie down, live the grazers. They are small, white, shrimp-like, and utterly eyeless — not blinded, never sighted, eyestalks simply never issued, because light here is either the red of the seams or the blue-green lie of the silk, and neither was ever worth building an organ for. They graze the bacterial hoarfrost in a sparse congregation scattered across the mats, and here is the thing about them that the place has made true: they move on the beat. When the standing chord pulses — and it always pulses — the whole congregation shifts a half-step together, a synchronized nudge through the world, like a congregation shuffling forward in a pew. They do not know they are doing it. They were born into the sound the way other things are born into water. It is not music to them. It is weather. It is the floor.

There is a third resident, and it does not belong to the warm territory at all. Something larger patrols the cold wall — a shadow that rides the boundary where the plume's rain falls, where hot crosses cold, circling the plateau's rim again and again without ever crossing in. It is never seen clearly; the shimmer doubles and triples it like everything else, and the curtain of falling ash-metal keeps it half-erased. It may be a fish. It may be a squid. It may be several shadows, or one shadow seen thrice, which by the rules of the place are the same. It patrols the edge of the world here as it would patrol the edge of any world — drawn to the heat, forbidden by the physics, keeping its distance the way the cold keeps its distance: absolutely, and without malice.

And then there are the attempts.

At the mixing fronts, where the blistering plume argues with the winter sea, matter keeps trying to become resident and cannot quite manage it. Minerals precipitate out of the argument into shapes with too much intent — a hinge, once. Knuckles. The curve of an ear, perfect for a breath, as if something were being assembled just below the threshold of allowed. The current erases them before they finish. They are not alive, but they are the closest thing the place has to ambition, and so they count. Something here wants occupants. Something keeps auditioning bodies for the towers.

The truth about whoever lives here is this: no one has been here long enough to be *from* here. The mountain is a month old. The ruins are three days old. The silk will be gone when the mats are gone, and the mats will be gone when the ledges they anchor to cool, and the ledges are cooling, ticking and splitting, already being painted iron-dark by the mineral water, already being chalked by the salts. The grazers move on the beat because the beat is the only durable thing the place has ever offered them — the one constant in a locale where the ruins are a stage of construction.

And when a tower goes — the gunshot through the body, the chord dropping a step — the congregation shifts a half-step into the new rhythm without pausing, and the silk on the nearest ledge sparks once, uncertainly, and the shadow at the wall circles on.

The place does not have inhabitants. It has witnesses, all of them newborn, all of them keeping time.
