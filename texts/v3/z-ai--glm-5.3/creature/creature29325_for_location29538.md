The first thing to understand is that the valley is not empty. Nothing that has run this long runs without tenants.

**The mats.** In the shallows around each vent, where the exhaled water cools as it spreads, the floor is not white. It is banded. Green goes first, in the hottest water — a green so deep it reads as black, the color of chlorophyll pressed to its thermal limit. Then, a few centimeters downstream where the water has given up a degree or two, an olive stripe. Then gold. Then a shocking, artificial-looking orange, like rust that has been taught to want things. Then pink. The bands do not shift. Each one sits exactly where its temperature sits, and since the vents do not drift, neither do the borders. From the rim, the outflow channels look like the edge of a rainbow that has been paved — color used as a structural material, laid down in courses, film by film like the sinter itself. The mats are the valley's oldest residents and its only true natives. They were here when the rivers first found the basin, and they will be here if the rivers ever forget the way. They photosynthesize in water too hot to touch. They do not know the word *extremophile*. They do not experience their world as extreme. It is the cold uplands, the dun flats above, that are uninhabitable — a fact the mats would confirm if asked.

**The steam-lovers.** Where the plumes rise, the sinter is never dry. The fluting of the geyserite lips catches condensate and holds it in vertical runnels, and in those runnels live things even smaller and stranger than the mats: threads that eat sulfur the way the sulfur arrived, in delicate castings; pale filaments in the vent throats themselves, anchored in stone that is scalded a hundred times an hour, and indifferent to it. Inside the largest vent, a meter down from the polished aperture where the roar is born, there is a felted lining of white. It is the hottest community on the floor of the basin. Its entire universe is the note. Whatever senses it has, if any, have been tuned by that single tone for so long that the tone is not a sound to it but the medium — the thing everything else is heard *against*.

**The flies.** The terracrust at the iron margins hosts a fly that exists nowhere else and is found here in numbers that beggar the word *population*. Its larvae live in the mineral films, breathing through spikes, able to survive immersion in water hot enough to cook an ordinary insect in seconds — the larvae sit half-submerged in it deliberately, grazing the mats' outermost pink edge where the cooling water is only merely scalding. The adults are small, dark, and dim; their entire adult life is a few days spent walking — not flying, walking — over the terraces, mating, laying, and dying on the warm crust so that their bodies fall into the very film their children need. The valley does not notice them. But the terraces are, in a sense, made partly of them, and the mats at the pink edge have learned, over uncounted time, to grow around the grazing, the way grass grows around the mower's set height.

**The dew-line residents.** Up on the flats, at the rim where the black glass pebbles beaded with dawn are, life is harder and smaller and older in its habits. Lichens grip the windward faces of the obsidian, gray-green, dormant most of every day, drinking the single dew that comes at the coldest hour. Underneath the lichens, in the film of moisture that forms on the glass each dawn before the sun takes it back, live tardigrades — plump, slow, unhurried, animals whose whole strategy is to outlast. They walk, unhurried, on their eight little legs, between the grains, while they can. When the moisture goes, they curl into dry husks and wait for the next dawn, which always comes, because it has always come. Of everyone here, they are the closest thing to a philosopher. They have, in effect, examined the valley's central arrangement — *what comes in, never leaves; what is kept, is kept* — and adopted it as a personal creed, applied to their own water.

**The birds.** Twice a day, at first light and last, small birds come down from the uplands. They come because the two thin rivers are warm at their touch and mineral-bright and, crucially, unfrozen — in winter they are the only open water for a very long way. The birds drink the warm water. They drink it carefully, tilting their heads, absorbing the heat along with the minerals. They do not nest here. They do not stay. But they know this valley the way anything knows a resource: not as a place, but as an arrangement that will hold. It has held every winter of every bird that ever flew down to it.

**And the one at the big vent.** There is, at last, one animal that lives here the way a keeper lives in a works. It is a small wading thing, sandpiper-shaped but stockier, with dense short plumage the same cream as the geyserite, and it lives nowhere else on earth. It was discovered standing on the fluted lip of the largest vent, unbothered by the roar — because it does not hear the roar as noise. It hears the note. It calibrates against it, the same note, every day, in every one of its thousand tomorrows. It builds its nest, a scrape lined with basalt gravel, in the lee of the lip, and the constant vibration of the note comes up through the stone and through the eggs and through the nestlings, so that even before hatching, every one of them knows the pitch the way you knew your mother's heart before you knew her face. It feeds on the flies. It drinks from the channels, warm. It has no predators, because nothing larger can stand where it stands and nothing smaller bothers. When the vent chuffs its piston-slap, the vent-bird's whole body lifts, a millimeter, with the pressure wave, and settles. Its entire life is spent inside that rhythm, and it is — this is the important thing — content. The valley was finished long ago. It is not forming, not decaying. And on the lip of its loudest throat stands a bird that has matched itself to the operation, that keeps the hours of a works no one built, and that will be standing there tomorrow, in the plumb-straight steam, on duty.
