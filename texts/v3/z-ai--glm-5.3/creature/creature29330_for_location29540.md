What lives here is, first and overwhelmingly, the herds.

The grinders themselves are less animals than landforms with a pulse. A full-grown one is a weather event — a body the size of a hill moved by the patience of continents, hide like poured slag gone leathery, seamed and channeled for the dew to find its own way down to the mouth. The channels are the first thing you'd understand about them if you could see: not decoration, not armor, but *plumbing*. Rain falls on them, mist condenses on them, and the whole broad back is a watershed whose lowest point is the mouth, so that a grinder walking through the dark is drinking the entire time. Their eyes are small, ancient, nearly vestigial. They long ago stopped needing to look at anything. The world they navigate is a texture of warmth, humidity, and charge — they feel the herd's hum the way you feel a hand on your shoulder, and they feel the ore seams, some say, as faint sweetness in the sole of the foot.

They browse by dragging a lower jaw like a scraper along the mineral crust, and the sound is the sound of the country: that slow mill-river grinding, patient, patient, a herd's worth of jaws at work somewhere out in the black. You would hear them for an hour before any part of one came near enough to touch, and even then what arrives first is heat — a wall of it, wide as weather, the furnace-belly radiant through the hide. Each one is carrying a smoldering gut the size of a church, a colony of stone-digesting microbes inherited in an unbroken chain back to some small opportunist that once ate a warm rock because it was there. The grinders are that opportunist's continent.

They live in herds because they have always lived in herds, and the herd is itself an organ: a dome of held heat, its own weather following it. The calves — smaller, faster, hide still pale where the channels haven't darkened — run beneath the dome in the warm rain their mothers make, learning the corridors by walking them, learning the hum by standing in it. There is play, of a kind, slow as everything else here: the young testing weight against weight, shouldering, leaning, in the dark. A grinder's affection appears to be mostly a matter of proximity and charge. A calf in trouble is found not by sound but by the change in the field when it cries.

Around and beneath the herds, everything else arranges itself like a town that has never heard of towns.

The sleek low-slung patrollers move through the mist along the worn lines, and they are the closest thing this place has to a nervous system. You never see one. What you get, if they pass you, is a soft ripple of attention — a feeling like being *read*, briefly, filed, and released. They follow the electric signature of the corridors the way rivers follow their beds, and it is impossible to say whether they serve the herds, watch the herds, or simply live on them the way lichen lives on stone. Possibly the grinders do not know they exist. Possibly the grinders know everything and it doesn't matter.

The maintenance crews are more visible, if that's the word: small scaled things that climb the vast flanks in the warm dark, working the grooves, cleaning the channels that carry the drinking water, tolerated the way any great body tolerates its own skin flora. The grinders don't acknowledge them. They don't need to. The arrangement predates both species' capacity for opinion.

The hunters you meet only once, and briefly, and you meet them by the silence. They are the one thing here that moves fast, and they hunt blind in a blind world, following the electric breath of prey through the wet — the small tremor of a body's charge standing out against the great background hum like a candle on a switchboard. The kill is a muffled clap, a pocket of night closing, and then the drips again, unhurried, as though nothing had happened, because at the scale this place operates on, nothing has.

And then the small life, the abundance that works close to the ground: the insects that tick in chorus as the terraces contract in the cool hours, cooling-metal sounds from cooling-metal bodies, so in tune with the slag that you cannot tell at first which ticks are animals and which are the world itself. The dew-pond dwellers — pale, sealed things living out entire generations in one warm pocket of water under its inch-high private cloud, world without end, a pond's worth of pond. The scavengers, unseen, absolute, so thorough that this country has no bones and no leavings anywhere; nothing the grinders produce is wasted, not even the grinders, and the dead go back into the machine as surely as the slag does.

The plants know their station. Black-green mats rooted in old wallow-muck; lichens living on the metal skim of fresh terraces, patient as plating; corridor-lines of low growth tracing the worn routes like current along wires. One species carries the only light: cold blue pinpricks along its leaves, faint as instruments left on standby, marking the corridors, advertising to the insects that read the electric dark.

What lives here, taken all together, is not a collection of animals so much as a single slow metabolism with many bodies — a factory that evolved instead of being built, no maker, no shift, no waste, only output, running so far into deep time that running is the wrong word. The herds walk their corridors, eating stone, paving the world with themselves. The mist moves through the worn lines like blood. The ground idles, warm as a shut oven door, breathing in its sleep.

And you, standing on it, are briefly a warm small irregularity in the field — noticed, filed, and released.
