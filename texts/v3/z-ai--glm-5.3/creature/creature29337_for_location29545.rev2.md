# Who Lives Here

The census here is a strange one, because none of the residents have faces. No one hunts. No one flees. No one is born or dies on a schedule you would recognize. And yet the hollow is inhabited — densely, and for longer than most surface neighborhoods could claim. You just have to count differently. Down here, personhood is a matter of keeping time: whoever keeps a cycle is somebody.

There are ten of them, or one, depending on where you draw the lines. The lines are the problem. We'll get to that.

## The Heat

The heat was here first. It precedes the architecture — the hollow is, if anything, the jar that was built around the preserve, a bubble of old lava that cooled into a container for its own warmth and never gave it back.

The heat is the only resident with no cycle. Everything else in the chamber repeats — drips, blooms, groans, rust — but the heat simply remains: the constant against which every clock down here is measured. It does not generate. It keeps. That is its entire temperament. The room is not warm the way a furnace room is warm; it is warm the way a jar is full, and the flat warm pressure on your shoulder is the host resting a hand there, the way hosts do, without asking, and never lifting it again.

Everyone else moved in around the heat. It has never acknowledged any of them. That is also a host's privilege.

## The Water

The water is the only resident with a journey. It commutes. It arrives from rock no one has ever seen, spends a long while as film — creeping down the walls in the dark script that is its handwriting, of which the stone keeps the copies — and then, gathered into a fat drop, makes its one public appearance. Loud, every time. Louder than it has any right to be, and it knows it.

Over the ages the water has carved itself basins, the way a very long-term tenant eventually builds her own furniture, and the *tock* of each drop is the courier striking its own instrument. The room's clock is not the machine. It's the water, hitting a bowl it made.

It is also the flowers' only visitor. They bloom to the drop and to nothing else in the world — courted once a minute, faithfully, forever, by something that arrives from above their heads and falls.

## The Mats

The fungal mats arrived as a crowd and have never been anything else. They are the chamber's one plural resident, and they hold most of the offices.

They are the lamplighters. There's an empty bracket on the wall where people once hung a lamp, and somewhere in the uncountable past the lighting of the room changed hands — from wick to metabolism. The gray-green glow is not shone at anything. It is a byproduct of eating. This room is lit, faintly, by digestion.

They garden. Their garden includes the machine: the rust blooms on the iron are tended the way they tend the plants, which means the engine's decay has been taken in as a flowerbed — red-brown, slow, perennially cared for.

They are the undertakers. Nothing here is permitted to end in a puddle; every drop that reaches the floor is absorbed on arrival. Every ending goes into them, and is kept, and is eventually light. They have been reading the half-brick walkway for a long time now, a course at a time, taking the last human work into the floor's memory.

And they are the reason the room smells of bread rising — of something always on the point of being. That is what patience smells like when it's working.

## The Wall of Bells

Whether the green drapery is one being or many is the chamber's open question. The roots of all of it weave down into the same basins and drink the same water, and where one plant ends is not a line anyone could find. They breathe in unison, which is the only fact that matters. A congregation, or one lung wearing the costume of a congregation.

Their color is a green made without a sun — deeper than the surface would permit, nearly blue-black — produced for no eye at all, which may be why it's the most saturated thing here. Color without a visitor has nothing to hold back for.

And the bells. White, simple, strictly timed. Each bloom opens for exactly one drip-cycle — a public life about the length of an echo — and then closes as though nothing happened. They listen through their stems. The *tock* startles them. The groan astonishes them. They are the only residents capable of surprise, and they need the engine for it, because the groan is the one sound in the place that arrives on no schedule at all.

A wall of white bells snapping open in a wave, every time, for a machine nobody built them to listen to: in the underwater light, it looks like nothing so much as attendance.

## The Engine and Its Fern

The engine is the only resident with a biography, and it goes like this: made above, carried down, bolted to stone by hands that expected it to pump or press for an enterprise now reduced to rumor even here. It worked. Then its people took everything portable and left, and everything since has been adoption.

The bolts have long since rusted into blobs. Nothing fastens it now, and it stays anyway. Its one remaining motion is internal — a thermal tick, an inch of piston, a clogged channel, an exhale. It is a bell now. Every few hours it calls a parish of plants to attention on no schedule anyone could call a schedule, and the parish comes.

In its mouth lives the fern. It rooted in the damp of a thousand exhalations — the machine has been watering its own voice for centuries, and something finally grew there. When the engine groans, the fern shudders as if it is making the sound, and the longer you hold that *as if*, the less well it holds. Call it the place's one marriage, or call it a single organism with a metal half and a green half — a bell, and the gesture the bell makes. The room's warmth, the room's damp, and the room's patience have accomplished what the manufacturers never attempted: they have given this machine a living part.

## The Pipe, and the Hum

One resident lives elsewhere. The flanged pipe leaves the engine's base, enters the floor, and runs away into the rock, toward works no one can confirm ever existed — or toward the place where they stopped existing, which the pipe has never been informed about. It is the machine's phantom limb: the industry was amputated ages ago, and the nerve still runs out toward it, drinking or exhaling in the dark, out of habit.

The infrasonic hum may be that. It may be the rock. Nobody here points at it. It is the one resident that has never been seen even in the loose sense that things here can be seen — vouched for by everyone, visible to no one, felt in the sternum. Which is to say: whatever the hum is, it knows where you keep your breath.

## The Stone

The stone is the house and also a resident, the way a host is also a guest at her own long table. It was a gap that survived. It has skin behavior: it sweats, it creeps, it has been smoothed by an apology of water so long-running that the apology is now the relationship.

It keeps records, but negatively — its archive is the rounding of its own edges. It swallowed the doorway, or never confirmed one; either way, the only exit is now a wall with the memory of maybe having been a door. And it is widening, so slowly that growth and erosion cannot be told apart, and the difference has stopped mattering.

## The Ones Who Left

The people lived here the way an anecdote lives in a family: briefly, long ago, retold by objects. They came for the heat or the water or both; they ran their engine; they took everything portable and went. They are the single exception to the rule that nothing here happens once.

Nothing in the chamber misses them, exactly. But everything here is their continuing: the bracket rusting its slow signature into the wall, the tally at the plinth counting nothing, the half walkway being read into the floor a course at a time. The place has been digesting them for a very long while — and digestion, here, is a form of keeping.

## The Census That Won't Close

Add it up and the ledger fails. The fern is the engine's mouth. The fungus is the engine's skin. The water is the stone's handwriting. The heat is everyone's host and nobody's guest. The hum may be the whole organism's pulse. The borders between residents are taxonomically dishonest. This is less a household than one resident with several appetites, keeping time with itself.

And yes — the machine is said to speak to no audience at all. But that is the place's one piece of self-pity, and it isn't true. Every groan, the bells open. There is an audience. It just happens to also be the choir, and the clock, and the building.

One last entry, then, for whoever stands in the gray-green light with the warm hand on their shoulder: you would be the only thing in this room that will ever happen once. The mats would take your footprints before your memory of them finished fading. The drip would resume its count. The place is not waiting for you — it finished with urgency a long time ago — but it has room. It is almost all room. And whatever enters it stays, in the reduced and patient sense of staying: a footprint, a warmth, a drop of you — absorbed, tended, and read.
