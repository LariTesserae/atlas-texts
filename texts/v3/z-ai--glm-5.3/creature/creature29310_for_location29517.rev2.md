**Who lives on the Bole**

The first thing to understand is that nobody *dominates* this place. The Bole is not a land that belongs to anyone, and everyone who lives here knows it in their bodies, because the ground itself outranks them — literally, since the Tide lifts them a thumb's width twice a day whether they consent or not. What lives here has learned to be small, patient, and above all *quiet in the right ways*. In a world where sound is weather, survival is acoustic.

**The continent itself, arguably**

Ask a band elder, after the thirteenth tally-knot, whether the Bole is alive, and you will get a look of polite puzzlement — the question is like asking whether the sky is overhead. Of course it's alive. They've felt it breathe since before they had words. Whether it *counts* as a resident is a matter of metaphysical taste, but the people speak of it the way coastal peoples speak of the sea: as an elder sibling, temperamental, generous, not to be trifled with. Some drummers claim they can tell the wood's *mood* by the tone of a trunk-post — that the same code-beat lands differently in a dry year than a wet one, that the Bole is, in some sluggish enormous sense, answering.

**The Choristers**

The insects themselves — the thirteen-year nymphs, the glasswinged singers — are the closest thing this world has to a ruling species, and they don't rule anything; they simply *occur*, like a monsoon. During the Chorus, they are the world. In between, they exist as a rumor in the sap, a knot tied in a calendar cord, a black paste in the back of a dew-shed that a grandmother remembers tasting. The whole ecology pivots around them and has never once managed to sync with them, and there is something the people quietly admire about that: the choristers keep a thirteen-year promise with absolute fidelity and answer to nothing else.

**The mowers**

The megafauna — bark-grazers the size of hills, moving at a pace measured in days — are so scarce that they are more like landmarks than animals, and treated like elders. Each has a name; the names are old and belong to no single band, having been passed down the trunk-post network for longer than anyone can trace. Meeting one is remembered the way other peoples remember eclipses. A person who touches a mower is said to carry the luck of it for a season, and children born in a year of a mower-crossing get a small honorific attached to their name. The mowers do not seem to notice people at all. They are on a timescale between the Choristers' frenzy and the Bole's geology, and they are the only large creatures here that appear to have *nothing to do with sound* — they neither make it nor flee it — and so the people regard them with a faint awe, as creatures who have somehow been excused from the world's one great conversation.

**The tank-nations**

Every cistern crown holds a little polity: the red larvae rowing the margins, the glass-shrimp in their shifting clouds, and the potbellied kettle-frog — always one, always the owner of the basin's whole acoustic space, its song a defining note of home. Bands know their frogs the way others know their roosters, and a tank whose frog has died is said to be in mourning until a new one establishes itself; you can hear the vacancy a mile off. Tank rights are the deepest legal structure on the Bole, older than the fire law, older than the drum codes, negotiated in a past so remote it has the quality of geology.

**The smoke-flocks**

At dusk, the swallows — a hundred thousand at a time, moving like their own weather front — are the Bole's one great visible spectacle of abundance, and everyone stops to watch them, every time, even elders who have seen ten thousand flocks. There is a gesture in the touch-code, made with the whole open hand tilted slowly, that means *flock passing* and also, by extension, *magnificent*, *brief*, and *be at peace*.

**And the people**

The kin-bands, twenty or thirty strong, are few by temperament as much as necessity. On the Bole, solitude is not loneliness; it is the default condition of a sensible creature, and company is something you *schedule*, via trunk-post, days in advance. A band's whole identity is acoustic and tactile: they read hands like faces, feel messages in their soles, name their children partly for the note of the fog on the night of their birth. Their virtue is restraint. Their great crime — arson, the end of the only world — is not a crime of violence but of *noise* taken to its terminal extreme: fire is the one sound the Bole cannot survive hearing.

Their inner lives are shaped by the Hush. Because there is one thin quiet each day, this is a people who schedule their softest things — grief, weddings, the things that must be said gently to children — into the pre-dawn, and they carry that habit of ear through everything. They are, by training, a people who can distinguish forty kinds of silence, who know which quiet is grief and which is fog, who end their lives the way the day does: flat-handed on the cooling bark, listening to the continent gather itself, playing their small part in the one long conversation that everything here is having, all the time, through the wood.
