# Who Lives in the Lining

**The people first, because they are the strangest part.**

Four thousand of them, and they are quiet the way deep-sea creatures are quiet — not from shyness but from the bone-deep understanding that sound is a weapon here. A child of the Lining learns the hush before it learns words. Its first tantrum is a silent one, all rigid limbs and streaming face, because the tantrum that shouts would bring every adult in the ward down on it in genuine alarm. The people speak in the register of libraries and hospital wards, and their laughter — they laugh plenty, it should be said — is a shoulder-shaking, breathy thing, the loudest laughter any of them can afford. Lowlanders who visit say the Lining's people seem to be perpetually sharing a secret. In a sense they are: the secret is the city itself, hidden in a week of cliff face, and their whole manner is the shape that secrecy has worn into them over generations.

They are thin-blooded and layered. Cellar-cool is the only climate they know, so they dress in their famous strata — silk innermost, then slag-wool, then moth-shell tile for those who work the cold galleries — and the fashion is intricate, since there is nothing to do indoors but dress and undress. Their hands are remarkable. Static makes a touch a ritual, and dryness makes fingertips a precision instrument, so the people of the Lining have the hands of watchmakers and the habits of priests: the touch of the copper ground-post before anything else, the constant small discharge of themselves into the bright-polished corners of their world. Watch them greet each other — hand to post, then hand to shoulder — and you are watching a liturgy so old it has become reflex.

They are, all of them, a little bit surveyor. Everyone grows up fluent in growth: which wall is fattening, which vein surged last season, which family got evicted by crystal and where they re-settled. Property here is a lease with the geology, and it makes a certain cast of mind. Lining people are patient gamblers, cheerful about loss, matter-of-fact about being moved, and they speak of the Room the way farmers speak of weather — as a mood, a partner, something you read rather than fight. When a ward floods with new glass overnight and a street vanishes, the mourning lasts about a day. Then the whispers start about the fresh space. It should be said, too, that they are famously hard to cheat at auction. Nothing teaches the pricing of tomorrow like living inside it.

**The gauge-walkers** deserve their own mention — the Table's people, the night measurers with their lead-glass rules, who know the dome facet by facet the way other cities' scholars know manuscripts. They walk the shelves at hours when the vats are dim, logging growth in fingers, and the city's law follows in their footsteps. Their authority is almost priestly and entirely numerical: when two families whisper a dispute over a wall face, the ruling comes down to who has been measured and how. They are recognizable by their weighted boots and their odd, listening gait — they claim they can hear a fast vein coming before the ticking gives it away, and no one in the Lining disputes this.

**The cutters** work the Bore, and they are a different animal from everyone else. The deep cuts are warm — the only warmth that isn't exhaust — and the cutters work shirtless, slick with oil-dust, half-naked in a city of layers, which is its own scandal and its own glamour. They are the ones who handle the dead. When someone dies, the body goes down the shelves in anthill silence and is given as flux to the deep glass, and the cutters conduct this the way they conduct everything: competently, in a hush, with a rough tenderness the surface families find almost unbearable. Every cutter has ancestors in the far facets and knows roughly where. It makes them calm. Nothing about death is abstract to a cutter; it's an address.

**And the animals, who are citizens whether or not anyone says so.**

The saltlice — the grey tide, punctuation-sized, in their millions — are the sanitation department, the compost, the city's slow visible circulation. Children keep them as pets in the way children everywhere keep the small and available; adults step around their working columns with an unthinking courtesy, the way you'd step around a neighbor sweeping a doorstep.

The grease-beetles live inside the machines and are loved without irony. A Lining foundry-worker will tell you, entirely straight-faced, which bearings have good beetles and which have lazy ones. It is not a joke. A greased bearing with a healthy beetle family outlasts one without, and everyone knows it.

The glassmoths are the Lining's shared weather. They pour from the Throat at dusk to graze the rock-flour clouds, and they crowd the warm exhaust ducts at night like grey frost with opinions. Their shed wings become tile; their chewing of the young facets is, the gauge-wakers suspect, a kind of pruning the Room actually benefits from. On surge nights the moths lift first — always first — and the oldest families trust a moth's restlessness over any instrument the surveyors ever strung.

And the jar-oxen: furniture with a heartbeat, patient as the mountain, hauling crystal to the cable-head with their rounded armored backs and their door-easing shifts. They are named, individually, and their names are usually jokes. An ox outlives three generations of a family and carries all of them to the Bore in the end, which the people do not find sad. That is the arrangement. Everything here is given back to the glass.

Because that, finally, is who lives in the Lining: four thousand quiet people and their dry congregation of lice and beetles and moths and oxen, all of them rented space in a crystal lung, all of them ticking along with the house, waiting — comfortably, in the dark, in layers — to see which way it grows next.
