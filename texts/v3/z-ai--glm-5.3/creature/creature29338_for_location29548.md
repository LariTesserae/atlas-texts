# What Lives in the Vermic Range

Nobody rules the Range. That is the first thing to understand about its residents: this is a city of ten thousand dynasties and no government, a polity with a constitution written in instinct and a citizenry that has never once, in ten thousand years, held a meeting. What lives here lives by occupation. You are a resident because you are *in* it, and you are in it because you were born in a cavity your mother won from a neighbor in a fist-sized war, and you will die in a gallery your descendants will eat.

Start at the top, because the canopy starts at the top.

## The Canopy Kind

The roof of the Range — that meters-thick thatch of broad leaves — is tended by things that have never touched the ground and do not believe in it. They are small, flat-feathered, quick, living their whole lives in the green half-dark between leaf layers, and their entire purpose is *thatch maintenance*. They probe the leaf-tiles for gaps. They pack the gaps with fiber, with seed-down, with fragments of their own molted plumage. When a storm tears a hole in the canopy, they do not organize a response; they simply each, individually, cannot abide the draft, and within a season the hole is gone. They sing constantly, all of them, a thin high piping that is the topmost layer of the Range's music. They are the closest thing the Range has to a priesthood: keepers of the boundary between the world and the roof, and utterly unaware of the theology of it.

## The Beetles of the Amber Deep

In the lowest galleries, where light has never arrived and never will, the ceiling smolders. The phosphorescent beetles roost there in numbers that make counting meaningless — not a swarm but a *substrate*, generations deep, their accumulated pale glow the only illumination in the deep Range and the only illumination it has ever needed. They are wingless now, most of them. Their ancestors flew; the deep galleries offered nothing to fly to, and over centuries their wings became luminous lanterns instead. They graze the fungus that colonizes the old shell domes — the same fungus that glows blue-white — and the two lights, amber and ice-blue, layer together in the deep galleries into a faint doubled twilight that the salamanders navigate by.

The beetles are, in a sense, the Range's memory. They do not remember; nothing here remembers; but their roosts sit on strata laid when the shell-domes were being grown, and they have been shining on those strata continuously since. When a beetle dies it drops into the deep humus and becomes the soil that becomes the material that becomes the wall. Even the light, here, is eventually spent as material.

## The Ants and Their Roads

Every channel in the Range has an ant road beside it. Every road is older than every ant on it. The ant rivers have worn grooves in the moss-nap and the fibrous flooring — actual highways, polished by uncountable generations of feet, following routes that were laid out when the mud towers were young and that the ants themselves could not abandon if they wanted to, because the road is now part of their bodies' knowledge the way the heartbeat is. They farm the fungus gardens in the walls. They herd the sap-drinking insects on the vines. They carry off the dead of other species with a brisk, unsentimental efficiency that is the first step in the Range's one law: *nothing is wasted.*

## The Birds of the Dusk

The middle terraces belong to the birds — hundreds of species of them, all descended, it seems clear, from a handful of ancestors who found the Range in some ancient era and stayed, and stayed, and stayed. They have all gone the same direction: big-eyed, glossy-feathered, loud past all reason, adapted to a light level that never changes and a day that is not the sun's day. They are territorial to the point of madness. A bird that has claimed a nesting cell in the old mud towers — one of the ten million thumb-sized galleries, every one occupied, every one defended — will fight for it against anything up to and including you, striking your knuckles with a weight and fury entirely out of scale with its body. The cells are passed down. A lineage of birds occupies a single cell in a single tower for longer than the tower has any right to stand, and when the cell finally crumbles, the lineage grieves in the only way it can: by fighting the neighbors for theirs.

They are the Range's orchestra, and they seem — this is the impression every careful observer eventually forms — to *know* it. The nineteen-day cycle is not conducted. No bird holds the whole pattern. But the pattern holds, varying, never repeating, a piece of music tens of thousands of hours long that no single resident has ever heard all of, because no single resident lives long enough. The song outlives every singer. That is the second thing to understand: the Range's culture, if that word applies, is entirely oral, entirely mortal, and entirely continuous. It is the music, and the birds are the only ones who know they're performing.

## The Shriekers

The tree-mammals — small furred climbers of some ancient rodent line that went arboreal inside a building, so that "tree" for them means "wall" — own the middle stories. They run galleries at the scale of your hand, and they shout. At dusk, when the fog-water starts to run and the frog chorus surges, the shriekers take up their territorial challenges, and the whole Range gains a third percussion line: whistling, chattering, alarm-lit creatures calling into the green half-dark that *this cavity is mine, this channel-frontage is mine, this moss bank is mine*, at a volume that a visitor does not expect from anything that small. The shrieking is the sound of the Range's endless small wars. There are no large wars here. There are no catastrophes, no fires, no invasions, no collapse of anything at scale — because the shriekers, and the birds, and every other territorial resident, keep every conflict local, keep it fist-sized, keep it between neighbors. The Range's peace is not an absence of violence. It is violence with a size limit, enforced so thoroughly, for so long, that it has become the shape of every temperament in the place.

## The Salamanders

And then there are the pale ones in the water.

The cistern-salamanders — long, blind, colorless, drifting through the dark still water and the shallow channel-threads — are the Range's one mystery, the one residents no one messes with and no one understands. The smaller birds will not touch them. Nothing hunts them. They have no evident defenses; they are simply *not food*, and no organism in the Range has ever treated them otherwise, as if they are exempted by something in their skin, some ancient chemical signature of *hands off* that every palate in the city reads as law. They live decades — some of them, judging by their length, outlive the birds' lineages. They are the only residents who move slowly enough to be *watched*, and there is a temptation, watching one drift through a channel past the reed beds and the frog ornaments, to think it is thinking. It probably is not. But it is the closest thing the Range has to an elder, and everything about the Range's temperament — the patience, the delays, the seeping water — is easiest to see when you look at them.

## The Mortar and the Soil

Finally, at the very bottom of the civic order, at the very top of the civic importance, the two silent majorities: the calcareous moss that sutures every joint between every material, patient beyond any animal's capacity for patience, growing a crack-shut over decades the way the birds hold a note for two seconds; and the deep decomposers, in the unlit fibrous layers, who eat everything and judge nothing and turn every fallen leaf, spent shell, molting, seed-husk, and body into the substance of the city itself. They are the foundation and the government both — the Range's tax system, its waste management, its urban planning, its mortuary practice, all performed by organisms with no brains at all, over centuries, without once being thanked.

## The Common Temperament

Put it all together and you can see what the residents *are*, as a collective: a citizenry with no capacity for history and perfect capacity for rhythm. Every resident is born knowing the fog cycle, the song cycle, the slow generational turnover of the canopy, the way the water seeps and never rushes. None of them knows the Range is ten thousand years old. Each of them knows only its cavity, its channel-frontage, its part in the current nineteen-day variation, its neighbors — loved and hated — and its own small warm portion of the unbroken loud warm dark. And each of them, when it dies, joins the strata: fresh fiber at the top, pulp below, shell below that, humus below that, the first generations of builders become the soil their descendants stand on.

That is what lives in the Vermic Range. Nothing that built it on purpose. Nothing that remembers building it. A billion small lives, each holding one cavity and one note of a song vastly longer than any of their lifetimes, in a valley-sized body that is entirely made and entirely alive and doesn't know the difference — and doesn't need to.
