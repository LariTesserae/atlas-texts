What lives here is not much, and knows it. The census fits in a sentence, and each member of it lives at a fixed address, appointed by water, corrected by light. Nothing wanders. Nothing hunts. Nothing is hunted. Every living thing in this bowl is a plant or a plant's nearest cousin, and every one of them is here because it can eat the sun directly, without middlemen — because there are no middlemen. The food chain was never built past the first rung.

Start at the top of the shaded wall, because that's where the living film is thinnest and most ancient. A greenish algal stain, more suggestion than organism, riding the wet stone where the seep's film slides. It is the wallpaper of the place, the closest thing to bare life the hollow owns: a skin a few cells thick, drinking the shine. It has no structure to disturb. It cannot be killed by anything that happens here, because nothing that happens here is structural. It will be on that wall when the wall is a wall no longer.

Down the terraces, where the travertine staircases hold their cupped-palm basins, the moss lives in two greens and keeps a strict economy. The darker green takes the terraces' lips and edges, the places where the seep's film never dries — moss that has never known a thirsty hour and has grown soft and deep accordingly, cushions you could press a thumb into and be forgiven. The paler green holds the margins, the zone of nightly sweat and noon-dry, and it is tougher, tighter, a moss that has negotiated with the climate and won concessions rather than comfort. Each cushion is an island on bone-white stone, and the distances between them are not loneliness but arithmetic: this is how far the water reaches, and no further. The moss does not spread to fill the hollow. It fills the hollow's moisture, exactly, and stops where the moisture stops, a green shoreline around a stone sea.

The liverwort grows where even the moss finds it crowded, in the deepest shade, and it has the look of something that gave up on ambition very early and has been content ever since: dark, waxy, laid flat against the rock like melted candle-stuff, its whole body a centimeter of commitment. It does not photosynthesize with enthusiasm. It persists. It is the pessimist of the census and may well outlast everything, because pessimists are hard to disappoint.

And the ferns. A dozen of them, all one species, all standing alone — this is the population, the whole diaspora of fern in this place, twelve individuals separated from one another by pale stone and by what looks, if you're in the right mood, like deliberate mutual avoidance. Each one stands in the seep's cool draft, in its own pocket of moving air, and each has the posture of a plant that has chosen solitude and been assigned it. They are the closest thing the hollow has to animals: visible, countable, individually located, each one a who rather than a which. You could come to know them, the way you know the stones — this one by the shelf, that one alone on the far slope, this small one holding the dish's edge where the heavy air begins, the bravest and lowest, its roots a finger's width from the chemistry that forbids. It lives above the line the way everything here lives above some line: tolerated, at the margin, by a place that did not invite it and has not yet gotten around to objecting.

Nothing else. That's the inventory. No insect has ever looped its small circuit above the terraces; no spider has ever had reason to build here; no bird has ever angled down through the sky-circle, because there is nothing at the bottom worth a dive. The hollow is a room with plants in it and no animals, which is not a garden exactly — a garden implies a gardener, implies intention — but the people who came long ago came nearest to being that, and even they left. What remains of them is on its way to being geology: soot going glossy and stone-dark, sherds softening into soil, the chert flake from local limestone returning, by the slowest route, to the rock that flecked it. The hollow is incorporating its visitors at the same pace it does everything — a hair's-breadth a year — and not out of reverence. Out of finish.

Understand what kind of aliveness this is. None of it struggles. The moss has no grazers; the ferns have no shade to race; nothing here competes, because competition requires neighbors, and each living thing has been spaced by the water's reach into permanent, unchallenged solitude. This is not peace, which is the quiet of a truce among enemies. It is simpler than peace: the absence, at every link, of the second link. Each organism faces only light and stone and water, the three non-competitors, and spends its life converting the first into itself with nothing watching.

And each of them, in its own tempo, is winning a race so slow it doesn't look like a race. The seep builds a hair's-breadth of terrace a year; the moss widens a centimeter a lifetime; the ferns, presumably, make more ferns at some pace below notice, a spore at a time, drifting down through the milky light to whatever damp the census hasn't yet claimed. In thirty years you could not swear the population had changed. In a thousand, the green might have moved a pace up the slope, the ferns might number fourteen instead of twelve, and nothing that had ever stood in the hollow would be able to testify to the difference.

That is who lives here: a film, two mosses, a wax-dark liverwort, twelve ferns. The entire fauna is light. The entire social life is spacing. They keep the room the way the water keeps the terraces — without interruption, without event, without ever once needing to know that anyone has come down the rubble slope to look, or that the looking stopped, or that the hollow, with its complete and sufficient population of one dozen ferns and their attendants, never noticed there was anyone to notice.
