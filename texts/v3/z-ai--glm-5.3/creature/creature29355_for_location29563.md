## What Lives Here

Begin small, because everything here begins sealed.

The jars-with-legs come first: the wax grubs that never open, born luted, dying luted, whole lives spent as polished capsules pushing through the sap of wounded trees. They are the base of everything, and they are eaten by everything, and when they are eaten their shells are spat out clean and dry and they lie on the forest floor by the million like dropped beads, because nothing rots here — nothing *can* — and the floor of the cavern is, in places, a strata of former grubs pressed into the fat marl, a sediment of small closed lives.

Then the swimmers. Bore a trunk and the fauna that pours out does not drown in air, because it was never breathing any; the oil-swimmers are slender sealed ribbons of muscle that move through sap the way eels move through warm shallows, and they glow faintly along the flank, the light of the leaves one step removed. They live inside the trees their whole lives, in the gold, in the dark of the wood, and they never in their lives touch either the rock or the air. When a tree blows out at a bad tap and rains hot sap, the swimmers come down with it and die on the floor, glowing, and the tappers leave them — it is not taboo, it is just understood that a thing which has spent its life inside a tree should not be taken apart by anything that did not.

The moths are the loveliest and the most honest. Glass wings, nothing to them but a casing and a hunger, and a proboscis like a stone drill-bit — they tap the trees themselves, at the old healed holes, the amber knobs, drinking light straight. Then they fly for days as lanterns. You can read a moth's age by its dimming. The young are searchlights; the old are brown coals; the dead go out exactly. The tappers keep them in husk-houses as clocks — a moth's glow is the nearest thing this world has to an hourglass, and when the moths in the house go brown, everyone knows a pulse is ending.

The chewers you have already heard. The great fat bark-browsing beasts, the makers of the bass floor, the rain-on-leather sound that never stops. They are the size of cattle and the shape of nothing, low, lacquered, plated, with mouths built like mine-heads — flat grinding works of bone that strip bark in continuous slabs. Their flanks carry the shine; a herd of them moving through a glade is a slow surf of light. They cannot be hunted, only herded, and only at a distance, because a chewer cornered does not fight — it *vents*, a bladder-valve blast of scalding vapor with a sound like the Firing in miniature, and then in the Silence it makes, everyone runs, out of deep habit older than the reason for the habit.

And under everything, in everything, the flat bright mites, the cleanup crew, the ones that find every spill of sap and take the shine out of it. Watch anything die here and you will see them arrive within the pulse — a spill is a summons — and they leave nothing but the shell, and they leave the shell shining, and that is how you can find the shape of any animal that ever lived in this forest: its ghost, in mites, glowing for a while after it's gone.

Then the people. The tappers. A few dozen, scattered, in houses of husk and gourd grown and cut and sealed, and here is the first thing to understand about them: they are the driest things in a dry world, and it is a discipline. Their skins are oiled and waxed daily, worked fat into every surface of themselves, because a skin unsealed is a skin the air steals. Their eyes run oil, not water — an old tapper crying is a strange slow business, amber instead of silver, and the tear is caught in a gourd bowl and kept, because in this place nothing that comes out of a body is wasted, ever. Their ears are the tragedy and the gift. Every child is born into the roar and every child goes deaf by degrees, not from age but from pressure, from a lifetime spent in a stone bell — and so the culture is built like a staircase down out of hearing. The young hear everything and can say nothing. The middle-aged feel the great sounds through their palms. The elders hear nothing at all, and are the most powerful people in the world.

Because the elders read. They sit with their hands flat on stone and they know the forest's moods through the rock the way a sailor knows weather through the soles of his feet. A tremor here: pressure-wind coming, get down, get waxed. A quick triple knock there: that tree is about to blow, move the taps. A long roll from the deep chambers: rooms opening, the country digging itself, nothing to fear, and the elders will smile at it, the way people anywhere smile at a child growing. And the Silence — the elders are the only ones who can stand inside a Silence without terror, because to them a Silence is not an absence but a *shape*: they feel its edges, its weight, its direction, and they sign to the younger ones with two fingers: *it is only the forest. Or: it is not only the forest. Or: flatten.*

Their language is hand against wrist, palm grammar, and it is fast — the deaf are not slowed by the roar at all, and it is the hearing who are the impaired ones, wincing, mishearing, shouting into a medium that eats sound. The elders pity the young their ears.

Their work is tapping, and tapping is almost a sacrament, which is what happens to any labor done the same way for forty generations. Stone bits, bone spiles, wax seals. You drill, you set, you catch the gold, you seal, and — this is the part outsiders never understand — you *heal*. Every tap is dressed afterward. The wound is luted so the tree closes over it, because a tapper's wealth is a living tree and there are whole trunks a thousand years dead down the dark galleries, covered in amber knobs in ranks, each knob an old tap, each rank an old village, a wall of healed-over holes rising like shelves, and the tappers pass these dead trunks on their rounds and touch them, and no one alive knows the names.

The dead themselves are given to the forest. That is the custom, and it is not sentimental; it is thermodynamics. A body is a jar, and a jar is not opened here — the dead are dressed in wax, fully sealed, and set at the base of a tree, and within a pulse the roots have them, and within a year there is a knob of amber on the trunk where they stood, and the family taps *that* tree thereafter, and it is considered the truest inheritance: to drink, once a season, from the tree that holds your grandmother. The shrine at the oldest living tree, its base worn smooth by palms — that base is smooth because it is a wall of ancestors, every one of them, worn by the palms that come to touch it, and no one distinguishes between the tree and them, because at that age what is the difference.

They eat sap and chewer-meat and the seed-hail of the Firing, and at the Firing they do not hide. When the canopy goes to artillery, the tappers go *out* — into the anthill-sized frenzy, into the loudest hour of the loukest world, with gourds and baskets and their ears already dying, and they harvest, and it is the one time of year the whole scattered people see each other, thirty faces in the light-storm of a seed-rain, everyone lit from above by a canopy firing its whole future into the dark at once. Marriages are made at Firings. Griefs are settled at Firings. The manual language, so precise at arm's length, is useless in a crush — so at Firings they press their foreheads together, and that is the touch that means *I know you're here*.

And when it is over, and the roar crashes back like surf, and everyone exhales with their hands briefly off each other's mouths — the elders stand apart, palms on any stone at all, listening to the deep construction, the rooms being chewed out ahead of everyone into the dark, and they feel what tomorrow is digging, and they make one small sign, the same one, always, palm flat and then raised, meaning roughly:

*It is still building. There will be room.*
