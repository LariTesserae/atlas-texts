What lives here is, first and most numerously, green — and it lives here the way tenant farmers live on a hillside, generation upon generation, each one shaped a little more by the ground.

The mosses are the oldest residents. They came uninvited, as moss always does, and were given the walls the way you give a relative the spare room because it's easier than fighting. The growers cut the mortar grooves for them, and the mosses repaid the kindness by colonising the runnels so thoroughly that the walls now read like text — bright sprung-green script in the channels where the water walks, dark felt margins everywhere else. There are dozens of species, probably, nobody has ever counted, because counting them would require moving them and they have been sitting still on this brick for longer than most of the crew has been alive. When you stand close to the wall your breath comes back to you slightly vegetal. That is the mosses, exhaling.

The ferns are an aristocracy, and they know it. Every fern in the vault descends from a single ancestor that rode in on somebody's heel in the nineteen-eighties — one spore, one boot, and now a dynasty seated in every joint of brick on the north wall, their fronds leaning into the aisle wind with the particular entitlement of a founding family. They have a country, which is the damp; they have a court, which is the cool upper courses near the ceiling where the droplets form; and they have a habit, which is to unfurl slowly, over days, so that a worker who comes down Tuesday and again Friday always finds something newly open, as if the wall has been keeping a secret and has decided, graciously, to tell.

In the gutters live the crops, and they live like soldiers in ranks — basil, mint, watercress, land cress, shiso, red orach, lettuces clenched like fists. Forty years of selection have made them over into something specific to this room: nothing that flowers, nothing that fruits, nothing that waits on a bee. They have been chosen for their patience with a sealed world, and they repay it by being all leaf, all the way down. But they are not as obedient as the ranks suggest. The basil by the return cascade has gone woody and perennial and founded a three-generation colony, its seed carried to new ground by the gutter's own current — because in this vault water is the pollinator, the courier, the matchmaker, and it makes deliveries to whoever floats. The mint has escaped the catchment tank entirely and is marching along the return channel with the slow, smug confidence of a plant that has never once been stopped by anyone anywhere. On the tank surface floats the duckweed, a green skin, the one citizen permitted to drift, turning over lazily when the pumps cycle like a thought changing its mind.

Near the centre is the mound — the rack that failed in a flood year and was never cleared, because by the time the water receded it had gone so completely and gorgeously to moss and fern and leggy happy watercress that clearing it would have felt like demolition. It is a ruin that became a resident. It is harvested like any other shelf, which means that even its wildness is on the payroll.

Above the top rack, where the light fails, hang the aerial roots — pale as linguine, swaying in the gale with a slow patience. They are the vault's one gesture at strangeness, its one haunted corner, and the workers do not talk about them much, the way you don't talk about the relative who stayed too long at the party, but they are always there, threading the fan-wind.

And then there are the people. Four or five of them, the only fauna in a sealed green world that has never held a rat, a gnat, or a slug — the sticky cards by the door have caught nothing in years and are replaced anyway, like candles at a shrine. The place's loudness has remade them. Eighty decibels of fan-drone leaves no room for talk, so they have grown a language of the hands: a flat hand sweeping low for *check the drains*, a thumb over the shoulder for *harvest*, two fingers tapping the air for *wait for the mist to finish*. They speak this now even in the street above, in silence, out of habit, and catch themselves. They sweat before they reach the bottom stair. Their paper goes soft, their clipboards warp, their tools rust unless oiled. They live, in other words, on the plants' terms — humid, dim, warm as breath — and they have come to prefer it, though they'd never say so above.

They touch the lit column in passing the way other people touch a doorframe — for luck, or greeting, or just because it is the brightest thing in the room and it marks the one place where the sky still reaches down. The grey needle of daylight from the old 1871 shaft falls on that column alone, about a metre of it, and the moss there is the vault's treasure and everybody's fingertips know it.

Their work is not gardening so much as re-signing a treaty every morning. They rotate the pumps like a heartbeat with a spare, pencil each harvest onto the painted steel column in the same spot until the graphite lies forty turns deep, feed the failures to the small hot compost drum so that even the dead come around again, and hold the line — gently, forever — against the green that takes whatever you stop defending. Mint is pushed back; mint returns. The mound is maintained as a mound. The mortar grooves are cleaned like a font.

And when the freight lift carries the crates up at dawn, and the mist falls for ninety seconds and the whole vault drips silver and goes quiet-voiced, what lives here is easiest to see all at once: a standing forest in the dark under a street, patient as brick, breathing its loud slow rain-song, and five damp humans moving through it with their hands talking, faithful to a founding fern, at peace with their gale.
