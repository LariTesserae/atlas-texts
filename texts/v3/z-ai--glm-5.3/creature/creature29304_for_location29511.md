# What Lives in the Loud Country

Almost nothing, first of all. That has to be said plainly, because the place says it too, in its vacant sky and its clear empty pools. No green, no legs, no wings, no eyes. And yet the Loud Country is not uninhabited. It is inhabited the way a bell is inhabited by its note — thinly, totally, and in a way you cannot remove without killing what holds it.

## The Wool

Begin with the borderline case, because the wardens themselves begin there, in every argument by every drip-line: is it alive?

It grows. It grows *in bands* — ridges laid down in the pattern of the standing wave, so that the grain of a patch of wool is a recording of the note it grew under. Whether that is life or mere crystallization with pretensions, no one has settled, and the wool is not saying. It is grey, mineral, soft only in the country's terms — soft by comparison with everything else — and it does exactly one thing that resembles a gesture: in the hard midday glare it curls, slowly, like a hand closing. From what it protects itself, or whether protection is the right word for a thing that may not have an inside, the wardens have debated for generations, usually in the deaf hollows, where speech is possible and the debate can be heard.

What is certain: it is the only soft thing in four miles, and so it does the work of every soft thing. Cordage, gaskets, seals, visors. Cut damp, it sets hard as horn. The wardens tend it without farming it — they clear rival drip-lines, they rotate which rims they cut from, and they leave wool on the drums themselves strictly alone. That last is not written anywhere. It doesn't need to be. There are rules you follow because the country taught you, and rules you follow because the wool did.

It may be the country's only native organism, if it is one. It may be the country's earwax. Either way, it lives where the sound is steady and the water stands — it lives, in other words, at the exact intersections of everything the place is.

## The Wardens

The people. The ones who come up from the lower world on purpose, which is the strangest thing about them, and the thing they are least able to explain to the people who send them.

**They come for short terms, and short terms are shorter than you'd guess.** One or two at a time in the one slab hut on the third terrace. The loudness unstrings people — not a metaphor; the wardens describe it exactly that way, *unstringing*, like a rope worked loose strand by strand. A tour is measured in weeks, not seasons. They rotate down to the lower world before the roar finishes with them, and the lower world receives back people who stand too still, who put a hand flat on tables before trusting them, who flinch at silences because silence, for a while, feels like a held breath before something enormous.

**Their bodies take the country's shape.** A working warden develops a particular stance: weight low, one hand often touching stone, because the ground is the reliable ear here — you feel the truth through your boots while your ears are being lied to about time. They speak lips-at-ear or not at all, and they have a hand-language for use on the chords, economical as butchers' signs: *tight, loose, rising, safe, hollow, go down.* Their hearing does not survive intact. Old wardens carry a constant faint tone with them the rest of their lives — the country's note, installed — and they report, without exception, that they do not mind it. That is the sentence that frightens their families most.

**Their work is maintenance, and maintenance is worship, and neither word is quite right.** The copper pegs along the far wall — green as pond water, cold-hammered from the native vein, driven into the seams of the drums — must be kept tight, because a slack membrane means the deep bass goes wrong, and the bass is the floor everything stands on. The tally-notches beside each drum are cut notch by notch, generation deep, and the cutting is the whole religion of the place: the note is rising, slowly, and someone must be writing that down. That is the job. Not to stop it — no one believes it can be stopped — but to *witness it accurately*. The wardens are, if you like, the Loud Country's act of taking its own pulse. The country cannot read its own tally. That's what the people are for.

**They live by the rules because the rules are load-bearing.** Walk the chords, not the sightlines — the cairn-lines that connect drum to drum through the quiet places, safe footing and honest distance in a country where distance breathes. Spend silence only where the broken cairns mark the deaf hollows, and spend it on the right things: sleep, weeping, saying aloud the words the roar eats. Never hum. *Never hum* — a human voice joins the standing wave, and the drums are already tighter than anyone likes. Every warden has one story about the time someone hummed. No two warden-humming stories are the same and every one of them ends the same way: pegs checked for a week afterward, in silence, with the hands.

**What they are like:** careful, spare, dry-humored in a register you have to be lip-reading to catch. People who chose the place, which means people who could not be in the lower world without feeling that everything around them was muttering just below audibility — which, of course, it is; they're simply the ones who wanted to hear it *clearly*. They respect the wool. They distrust their own eyes here on principle — the country lies to sight and tells the truth to the soles of the feet — and this breeds a certain humility that they carry down with them and that never quite wears off. They will put a hand on your arm to check something you said against what your arm transmits. They mean nothing by it. It's how they listen.

## The Ones Who Stayed

Not everyone goes down when their tour ends. A few, over the years, have chosen not to — and this needs saying carefully, because it is both a fact of the population and the place's one grief, or one joke, depending on the warden telling it.

Their cairns stand on the chords, unbroken — the mark of a resident, not a visitor. They did not die in the deaf hollows, weeping; they died, or else did not die, out in the sound. What actually becomes of them is not documented, because the wardens do not document it, because the ones who stayed are the ones who stopped needing the tally-notches. The country does not object. That is the phrase that survives, and it is the only epitaph the place offers: *the country does not object.*

Some wardens will tell you the stayed-ones are still walking the chords, keeping their pegs tight, off at the far bend of a distance that breathes. The evidence for this is exactly the kind the Loud Country specializes in: sounds arriving before causes, footsteps in the playback at night that match no one's day, a hummed note at dusk — which never happens — reported by everyone, always, from a different direction. The evidence against it is that nothing in the country is alive, which is an argument the Loud Country has been quietly losing since the first cairn was stacked.

## The Country Itself

And then there is the possibility the wardens only voice at the listening arch, at dusk, with a hand flat on the wet stone: that the population of the Loud Country is one. That the wool is its skin, the drums its voice, the deaf hollows its sleeps, the tally-notches the beginning of a memory, and the wardens themselves something between a guest and a nerve ending — the means by which four miles of loud bright empty stone takes attendance of itself.

It is lifeless, and it runs. It wakes at dawn and weeps, works at noon, rings once at dusk with its whole body, and dreams all night in playback. It hears tomorrow arriving early and lets you hear it too, once you've left, so that you'll know you were part of it. That is more than a great many living things manage.

What lives there: the grey film that grows in bands to the shape of the sound, the few careful people who keep the pegs tight and write down the note as it rises, the stayed-ones the country does not object to —

— and underneath all of it, filling the whole bowl like a held breath, the thing that is rising, very slowly, toward a pitch no notch has yet been cut for.
