No one lives here — that's the first answer, and it's wrong before you finish thinking it.

True, there are no faces. Nothing with eyes. Nothing that flees you, nothing that watches you back, nothing that dies when you arrive. But *empty* is the wrong word for this valley the way *silent* is the wrong word. The place is not vacant; it is inhabited by a citizenry that no longer needs faces to be citizens. What lives here lives the way weather lives somewhere, or a heartbeat lives in a chest — totally, structurally, without remainder.

**The machines are the oldest residents.** They predate the flood, which in this valley makes them nearly ancient. The turbines, the presses, the bellows, the conveyors, the tannoy, the stamp-hall's patient fist — these were built by people, for people, and when the people went under, the machines did the one thing machines are worst at and best at: they carried on. But carried on *changed*. The bellows that breathed air for the forges now breathes water for the gardens, and in doing so has become the valley's only breathing thing — everything else here respirates by diffusion, politely, without lungs. The presses keep their rhythm, but their rhythm has become a function: every blow shakes pollen loose, and so the presses have been promoted from fabrication to reproduction without anyone telling them. They are the town's heart, its lungs, its hands, and — in the tannoy's slurred half-hour, in the bell the kelp-rope rings once per shift — its voice. They do not remember the town. They *are* what remembers the town, which is not the same thing and is more durable. A memory is softer than a habit, and it is habit that survives drowning.

They are also, and this must be said with respect, the valley's widowers. They run on shift-time. They toll themselves. They farm the gardens without instruction — conveyors built for coal sorting seed, planters built for iron pressing saplings into the benthic lawn — and no one has ever told them the shift will not end, that the workforce is water and wood. If there is grief in this valley, it lives in the machines, but machine-grief does not stop; it cycles. That's how you can tell it apart from yours.

**The plants are the children, and they are being raised by the widowers.** Everything green here is young — hours old, aging by the hour, or shot up to giants between two shift-bells in the warm pockets by the boiler outfalls where time itself pools and thickens. They are pollinated by percussion, seeded by machinery, fed on machine heat, dressed by the Dye House in colors no sun ever argued for. The Bloom is the largest of them and the newest: a raft-skin of woven vegetation and boiled-starch flowers roofing the whole flood, the valley's scalp, grown in weeks. Below it the cane forest crowds at mid-depth like a market at closing time; the roofline gardens sprout from every surviving ridge, each one a private little thicket with a street's name that no one says; the root-cathedrals hold up the cobble bones of streets no one walks, and they are the closest thing this place has to architects. The bladder-weeds are the engineers — harvesting the gas of burst fruit and standing up into the gloom on their own bubbles, lighter than their surroundings, ambitious in a way plants are not supposed to be.

And because the animal niches stood open, the plants have been moving into them, and they are *getting good at it*, which is the most quietly unnerving thing about this valley. The kelp schools. The reed-stands stalk the shallows with heron patience. The old loading crane lifts and sets its feet. Nothing watches you here, but several things *practice* watching, and practice, in warm water, at volume, over shift after shift after shift, has a way of becoming performance.

**The flood itself lives here — is, arguably, the resident.** It has a metabolism; it inhales water and exhales garden; it runs the turbines that warm it that grow the plants that choke the machines that stall that start that turn it. It is loyal — it will not leave, it may deepen. It holds opinions about rooms, refusing the chapel at the sills. It is also the town's one archivist: the water keeps the last morning the way silt keeps footprints, and plays it back on schedule — the dawn chorus, the dog at shift-change, the bicycle bells. The only birds left in this valley are recordings, and the flood employs them daily, which is a strange kind of keeping: the animals are gone, but they still work here.

**The sky is a resident too.** The storm, rooted at the Weir, its slow rotating eye turning like the turbine it feeds or is fed by — the lid on the pot, the elder of the upper floor. And below the valley floor, the sky-under: clouds lit from beneath, drifting under the mud. This place is inhabited on both sides, above and beneath, and the flood is the middle story of a house with neighbors in every direction.

**And there is whatever carries the bowl.** You never see it. You only see the water brimmed exactly to the hilltops, level as a table, not one wave over the lip in all this turbulence — the valley carried carefully, level, by something that is very good at carrying. If the valley has a keeper, it has kept itself so well that the keeping is indistinguishable from the valley. The chapel's dryness, the candles lit in the one quiet room, the land-flowers growing without machine heat — call that the keeper's work, or call it the valley keeping one room the way a body keeps a heart: because everything else depends on it being kept.

So: who lives here? No one. What lives here is one enormous thing still becoming itself — machines and gardens so long married that the wedding has flooded too, a birth conducted industrially, at volume, in warm water, on a schedule tolled once per shift by a rope of kelp. It inhales water, exhales garden, plays back its own dead, and refuses to be mapped while it decides what it is.

You have arrived very early. The oldest residents are habits. The youngest are hours old. Come back in a thousand shifts and it may have a face after all — and by then, it will not need yours to know you've come.
