No one is born in Sough. Start there, because everyone in the town does. The water is kind to what is already made and cruel to what is still becoming — kind to leaves, cruel to eggs — and so the Kettle holds no beginning things. Frogspawn lies in the warm shallows and crusts over with lime by morning, candied, and never hatches; the children gather the little white failures off the pool lips and keep them as pocket-pebbles, which tells you something about how they're raised. Hens live up on the rim, above the sweating line, and eggs come down in baskets packed in dry reed, the one import the town handles tenderly. Couples who want a child walk out over the causeway together — they call it *going up to be soft* — and the baby is brought back down when it has its shell on, by which they mean weaned, walking, done with being an egg. Everyone you will ever meet in Sough arrived. The town's own saying, offered to strangers with the water: *nothing is born here, everything finishes here.*

The people nearest the heart of it are the Counters. Under the low end of the bowl, where every channel in Sough runs together and drops into a dark mouth in the floor, the Throat swallows — that is your heartbeat, six strokes to the minute, ten seconds apart, the gulp that keeps the Kettle from filling. The town is named for its own drain; it is named, in other words, for its salvation. Over the mouth stands the Listening House, and in the Listening House a stone table called the Drum that taps with every stroke, and at the Drum there is always someone seated, day and night, in an unbroken line the town calls the Chain of Ears. Mother Sike has held the evening watch for sixty years and sleeps with her palm flat on her floor at home, and she will tell you she can feel a miscount through her feet from the market. The tallies go into the Ledger of Strokes, shelf after shelf of chalked strokes going back further than the paper does. In the whole living memory of the town the beat has failed twice: the Year of the Longest Gap, when it stopped for two minutes and the water rose three steps on the Pump Stair, is dated like a war and retold like one. Outland engineers came twice to explain that the Throat is only a siphon. The town agreed politely both times. The Throat does not care what it is called, and neither, much, do the Counters. They speak to each other in the gaps between strokes, and they hear under your words while you are still saying them.

Then there are the waterwrights, who tend the silver hiss. That sound you're inside of is not an accident. Every gutter, laade, culvert, and riffle in Sough is tuned — a pebble shifted here, a lip of sinter trimmed there — and the town's water is kept, collectively, as an instrument. A channel that silts up is said to have *gone mute*, and re-opening it is *giving it back its word*. Children do their lessons blindfolded, naming channels by ear: Beck, Dub, the Long Hiss, the Laughing Rill. The deaf children do best of all. They feel the town through their bootsoles and the seat of their bones, and in the fog — and Sough is half fog — they lead the sighted. You will be told not to whistle in the channels. It isn't superstition. It's courtesy: the town listens to itself.
