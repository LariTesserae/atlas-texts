What lives in Gallery Twelve lives by ear, or by the loss of one.

Start with the moths, because they are the closest thing to a native population. They are small, gray-gold, unremarkable, and they have not touched a solid surface in more generations than anyone has counted. They live in the warm column at the gallery's throat, where the air rises all day and all night, and they have made a nation of it the way other creatures make a nation of an island or a river. They court in the updraft. They mate tumbling. They lay their eggs in the fine lint that gathers on the intake screens, and their caterpillars never know silence. When they die they drift — the airflow is too steady for falling, they are *conveyed* — into the fin-ranks, and the fins take them the way the fins take everything: into the channels, into the compost, into the soil. The garden is, in part, made of their bodies, and the garden in part feeds the airflow that carries their grandchildren. They are the gallery's circulation. And they land in the same places every evening, on the same curve of the wet-end rail, thousands upon thousands of feet touching down on one polished bend, until the handrail gleams like something in a shrine. Nobody oiled that rail. The moths did, with their lives.

The swifts live on the beat. That is the plainest way to say it. The fan turns at three revolutions to the minute, and the whoomp of each pass comes up through the floor, and the swifts have folded their whole hunting rhythm into that clock. Whoomp: the insects scatter upward in the pressure-wave. Wings shut. Dive. Whoomp. They hunt inside the sound the way tide-pool fish hunt inside the surf, on the interval, in the gap the machine leaves them. They nest in the clerestory gaps where the louvers never quite close, and their clicks arrive at your cheek as pressure before they arrive as anything you could call hearing, because hearing has been taken from everyone here and repurposed as touch.

The geckos live in the machine's warmth — literally in its insulation, brooding their eggs in the lagging wool wrapped around the hot downcomers. Their nursery is a pipe jacket. Their mother's vigil is the temperature of water going to the lowlands. They are the only residents of the gallery who chose the dry south end, and they chose it for the same reason anything is chosen here: because the machine made a niche, and life is a liquid, and niches are depressions.

The frogs are the exiles. Somewhere in the deep past the mountain's amphibians found the one cast-iron stairwell behind the banyan's trunk where the fin-tuning, by accident of geometry, cancels itself — the single pocket of quiet in the whole cathedral, the size of a confessional. They gathered there and they never left, and now the entire frog nation of the mountain lives inside that pocket, singing in the only room where singing is possible. What they sing they cannot hear well either; they feel one another through the iron. But they sing, and a person who climbs the trunk-ladders all day and stands an hour in the roar and then puts a hand on that stairwell's railing will feel the chorus come up the cast iron like a second pulse, and it is — there is no better word for it — a relief. It is the gallery apologizing. It is the one place the mountain admits a voice.

The falcon lives at the failed louver, and she is the gallery's monk. She comes each year through the gap that should have closed, nests in the blanked-off vent, and never cries. Not because she is mute — because her line's cries were swallowed so long ago that the calling simply stopped being inherited. A falcon's scream is a territorial claim; what use is a claim in a room that answers everything with the same vast nothing? So her descendants hunt by shape alone, turning and stooping in the fan's throat, and their silence is not an absence. It is a garment. She wears the roar the way her lowland cousins wear the sky, and she is, in her way, the most adapted thing here: the only creature that treats the noise as if it were merely weather.

The bees are the garden's industry. One colony, in a junction box whose indicator lamps burned amber for longer than anyone can say, working the figs and the orchids and whatever blooms the condensate coaxes out of the dead rake-ranks. Their honey is the gallery in a spoon — fig-sweet, but underneath it a mineral note, a taste of rust and rain-on-hot-metal, the terroir of a machine. Whether it is good honey no lowlander has decided, because no lowlander has been able to climb the mountain and stay the night and come down with a jar and remain objective about it.

And the garden. It would be wrong to leave the banyan out of a census of residents, because the banyan is not a plant in this gallery so much as a citizen. It arrived by accident — a seed in a clogged gutter, sometime in the last century — and it took the terraces the way a strangler takes a host, except that the host was already alive, and did not seem to mind. Its canopy streams north in the permanent wind like a flag. Its roots hold the intake screens clear, which is to say it performs maintenance. It swallowed a cluster of brass gauges whole, decades back, and the needles are still in there, standing in the wood, still faintly reading the humidity of 1941 — the tree has archives. Whether the figs are good this year is evidently a matter of record, because somebody writes it down.

Which brings us to E., who is the hardest one to describe, because E. may be one keeper or a dynasty of keepers, initials passed down like a parish office. Someone climbs. Someone reads the ledger, or fails to climb for a decade and then climbs again, and pencils the only facts that matter to whoever comes after: *Figs good this year. Fan slower, I think.* E. does not live here — E. visits, the way a doctor visits, the way a priest visits — and yet E. belongs in any honest census, because the ledger is part of the gallery's metabolism too. The garden grows because the machine runs; the machine runs because the garden keeps it; the ledger keeps the knowledge of the keeping alive across generations of people who mostly forgot the Works exists. E. is the gallery's memory. Sparse, punctual, patient, arriving through the roar like everything else does — as pressure, felt before it is understood.

But the truest answer to what lives here is larger than any of these, and it is this: the gallery itself lives. Not in a mystical sense — in a plumbing sense, which is more binding. Roots clear the screens; lint builds the soil; soil grows the figs; figs transpire into the intake air and raise the harvest of cloud; the harvest of cloud condenses, runs down the fins, tops the grease-cups that keep the fan's bearings fed. Somewhere in its centuries of running the Works crossed over from being maintained to being metabolic, and now it is one organism with a brass skeleton, a fig for a heart, moths for blood, bees for hands, a falcon for a silence, and one stairwell, behind the trunk, where it keeps its voice.

Everything in Gallery Twelve is shaped by the roar. Everything in Gallery Twelve is the roar, arranged.
