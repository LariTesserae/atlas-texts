# The People of the Terraced Town

## What They Look Like

They are a slow people, and it shows in their bodies the way weather shows in stone. Generations of thin air, cold, and a diet of moss-bread and dew-water have made them compact and dense — short, deep-chested, with blood that darkens their knuckles and the rims of their ears to a plum color in winter. Their skin is the tone of the bedrock ridges they live along: dark, warm undertone, faintly ruddy at the cheeks from the wind. Their hands are remarkable — broad, with thick palms and short strong fingers, hands built for scraping dew, fitting terrace stones, and hammering soft copper. A terracer's hands can read a slope the way a reader's eyes read a page: laid flat on the ground, palm down, they can feel which way a field leans, and by how much, more accurately than any instrument their civilization has ever needed to invent.

They dress in layers of woven vine-fiber and moss-felt, oiled with the plants' resin until it sheds rain. Everything they wear is the green-greys and deep water colors of the plain; a traveler has stood in the middle of the town square and lost sight of three people standing still nearby. This is not camouflage on purpose. It is simply that they have made their clothes out of the world, the way they make everything out of the world, and the world is that color.

## How They Move

The first thing a visitor notices is that no one runs. Not children — and there are children, quiet ones, who play games of extraordinary slowness and complexity on the terrace steps. When a terracer needs to get somewhere quickly — a fire spreading, a child in a drainage channel — they do not hurry; they *proceed immediately*, at the same deliberate pace, with a change in the eyes that is somehow more urgent than running. It is a strange thing to see, and after a while it stops being strange. Their word for *hurry* is the same as their word for *die*, and they mean it practically: a slipped stone on a wet terrace, a fall into a thin place, a stumble at the edge of the burial platforms — the plain does not punish speed so much as it does not forgive it.

They speak the same way. Their language is low-toned, consonant-soft, well-suited to talking while walking together side by side. It contains a large vocabulary for the behavior of water: a word for water standing, water walking, water *considering* a terrace edge, water that has been patient so long it has become still in a way that is different from ordinary stillness. They have almost no words for mountains. They have one — a compound meaning *the world's old work, gone to the sea* — and they use it rarely, mostly in the songs.

## Their Days

The day begins before the dew does. Dawn is harvest: the bone scrapers, the clay troughs, the long silent files of people moving down the terrace steps gathering the silver beads off every leaf, because that water is the sweetest and, in the thin places, sometimes carries someone else's sky in it. The scrapers are family objects, worn to the shape of particular hands, older than anyone can count — the town keeps a small, half-buried hall where the scrapers of the dead are kept, hundreds of them, and it is understood without being said that when the hall is full, the town is full, and no more houses will be built.

Midday is thin and bright and the fires are banked. Afternoons are for work: mending, pressing fuel bricks from dead cushion-fern, reshuffling a terrace's stones, trading gossip across the bedrock ridge. Dusk is the hour of the exhale, when the whole plain breathes and the people stop what they are doing and sit with it. They call it *listening to the blanket breathe*, and it is the closest thing they have to a daily prayer, and it consists entirely of sitting still.

## What They Believe

Their religion is geology with a liturgy. The world is a great being that finished its work — raised mountains, cut valleys, wore itself smooth — and then lay down to rest, and the plain is its sleeping surface. Everything alive is the blanket on that sleeper: moss, rain, people, the town itself. The core moral instruction follows directly: **be a good blanket.**

A good blanket is warm, light, spreads itself evenly, and does not disturb the sleeper. Hence: nothing tall, nothing deep. Their buildings are half-sunk, rounded, invisible from a half-mile away. Their agriculture steps the water down gently so that nothing is wasted and nothing is rushed. Their dead are not buried but laid on the stone platforms, where the cushion-fern grows over them in nine years — and the mourners' grief is genuinely mixed with satisfaction, because to be grown over is the reward for a life spent being a blanket. On the anniversaries families visit the platforms and place a hand on the fern, and say the person's name once, and leave.

The thin places are understood as the sleeper's dreams — the places where the rest is not quite whole, where the finished surface lapses and something else shows through. They are not feared exactly; they are respected, the way you respect a sleeper's talking without answering. Everyone knows their locations. Children are taught them by a walking lesson, a circuit of the town's borders sung as a low chant: *the rising-rain circle, the valley that returns, the sky of the other dew.* The chant goes slowly. Everything the town needs to remember, it remembers slowly, so the remembering will last.

## Echo Days

On echo days they do not hide indoors, as a visitor might expect. The hour when every sound ever made on the plain comes back at once — the dead dialects, the footsteps of stone-age shapes, the sub-audible grind of vanished mountains — the whole town sits down wherever it is, on terraces, in doorways, on the warm flat ridge-rock, and listens. It is the one day the plain is loud, and they treat it as the sleeper turning over in its sleep. To talk during it would be rude. Old people say they have heard the voices of their own parents in the murmur, laughing, and they say this without grief, or with the specific well-settled grief of people who believe their dead are exactly where they should be: flat under the fern, part of the rest.

And when it settles — always it settles — the quiet returns, and the cushion-ferns exhale at dusk, and the fires are lit, and the water begins, again, its slow walk down the terraces, one hand's-breadth at a time, toward a sea that no longer exists, or a sea that is the plain itself, which amounts to the same thing here.

They say it plainly, to children, to travelers, to the thin places themselves: the world is asleep. Be a good blanket.
