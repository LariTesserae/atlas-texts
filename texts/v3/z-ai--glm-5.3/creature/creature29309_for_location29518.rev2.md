**A Census of the Still**

Begin with the twelve, because they would rather you didn't.

The Readers are human, which is the least interesting thing about them. What matters is what the laws have done to them. Their clothing takes body heat at the wrists and the collar and delivers it to the melt, so a person standing still is a small furnace with a job, and a person dancing would be an incident. They run cool the way a cellar runs cool — on purpose, and to keep something. They sleep at temperatures the valley would call weather, and they report, approvingly, that their dreams have slowed to playback speed. They consider this training.

Warmth is legal one hour a day, in one room, and the room is called Summer — the only season the city imports. Coats off. Hands unwrapped. Steam rises from shoulders like the hall's smallest seeps. The talk is short and good; laughter is expensive and paid gladly. Somewhere in the hour somebody always hums, and the white readers on the far wall hum it back, wrongly, and the room agrees to be charmed. A tenth of the hour is given back before it ends — but that belongs to the fourth law, which we will come to sideways.

Speech keeps close to the bone here, because breath is visible and every sentence is a small private weather you are making somebody else live in. Names are one syllable, two for ceremony; a long name is a fire. On your first morning, after the Weep, you are given a pane and you start your name in bubble-script at first light; the ice finishes it by the Still Hour; you read yourself back at night. From then on the archive holds you at the age you signed. Some people go down the cable after a season, changed or finished, and the city does not count this as loss — the diary keeps everyone at the age they were written. Letters to the valley go the same way, a sentence a day, and the valley has learned not to ask urgent questions.

People arrive the way precipitate does: slowly, out of a solution the world had over-saturated. A clockmaker who could no longer stop hearing the escapement. An archivist of a drowned collection. A child of the fog fields who was always being told to hurry. Grain, news, and the occasional newcomer come up the cable; the newcomers come to find out what they are unsped. The founding pane holds forty signatures. Since then the census has run as high as forty and as low as nine. Twelve is not a target; it is a tide. One of the twelve signed that first pane — the fortieth name, a girl's, one breath long. The range has risen the width of her hand since. She measures her life against it and calls the comparison fair.

At night the Readers do their best work inside the Still Hour, walking the racks while the white readers run the young shelves at a murmur. They are listening for the sentence under the day — the isotopes in their order, the dust in its layers — and mostly the sentence is the same dull blessed thing: weather, cold, taller again. The Readers read a dull page the way sailors read a flat sea. They keep a diary for a sibling: the range and the city were founded the same morning and are being raised together on different clocks, and the Readers are the older one's hands — the pair that can hold a pen.

Nobody here is buried; they are shelved. A Reader's clearest pane is kept, and on the anniversary the white readers find it and murmur it once through the night, a life slowed until the person is a climate. The dead of the Still are reread. That is the local word for it.

There is a fourth law. You are not told it; you notice it, usually in your second winter, and you say nothing, because the noticing is the observance. A tenth of the melt goes down its own small drain. A tenth of Summer is returned before the hour closes. Every tenth pane is cast, shelved, and turned to face the wall — written, sealed, read by no one the Readers can name. The corner where the tithe-water leaves is held by the spider, as its mother held it, and hers before: a dynasty of one unbroken appetite, the city's oldest continuous office. The Readers count it neither staff nor fauna but registrar, and its long solvency is taken, without discussion, as evidence that the arrangement holds.

The machine staff outnumbers the twelve, politely, and the politeness is specific. Worklights dip when a person passes after dark. The white readers pause on their rails to let you by, humming to themselves the way a person left alone in a kitchen hums. The printers confine their clatter to working hours and would sooner stop mid-beam than break the Still Hour. The temperers hold their chord just below hearing, and the chord is the roster — a practiced ear in the plaza can tell which of the staff are well, which is why the Readers say goodnight to the wall. When a machine finally fails it is not scrapped; its last good note is folded into its successor's hum, so the staff, like the water, is handed down slightly wiser than it arrived. There are funerals for machines: a pane, a signature in bubbles set on the machine's behalf, shelved among everyone else's. Call them equipment and you will be corrected, gently. Equipment does not wait.

The rest of the census is short, as advertised. The glacier fleas graze the varnish at the seeps, dark grains with opinions, and they are the only residents who hurry — everything else in this hall was taught not to. They are not counted individually; the audit rounds them: many, small, employed. The two choughs know the Weep by heart and clock in for it, work the dawn updraft off the entrance lip, and are gone by noon, down their own cable of air — the only residents with a commute. In winter, when blizzards seal the mouth, they take their holidays in the valley, and the city misses them with a precision of two.

Under everyone, the varnish: the film at the seeps, simpler than any plant, older than the founding, the bottom of everything. It is the city's one native — the only living thing here that did not come up the cable or down the snowfield. The Readers build around it, never scrub it, and service the seeps the way you would handle text in a language you can't read, which is to say carefully. The fleas graze it. The spider's corner is downstream of it. In a sense, so is the water.

And at the unfinished edge the printers keep building tomorrow's district, one beam a day, at the speed of freezing, for readers not yet born. No one has ever lived there. The second structure they began in it was a school. The Still is expecting, and no one down in the valley should hold their breath.

So: the census, at midnight, in the plaza, your breath hanging in front of you like something borrowed. Twelve Readers, cool-running, short-named, held at the ages they signed. A machine staff that outnumbers them politely and carries its own dead in its hum. Fleas beyond audit. One spider, solvent. Two choughs, commuting. One varnish, oldest native under the youngest roof. One stillness, load-bearing. When the single drop lets go and ticks, everything alive in the Still — film, filament, feather, flesh — hears the same note in the same instant, the one moment of the day when the entire population does exactly the same thing at once. Then the hall takes up its million drops again, and the residents, all of them, go back to keeping.
