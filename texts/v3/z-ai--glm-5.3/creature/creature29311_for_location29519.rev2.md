What lives here are the ones who never left.

Begin with the wader, because the wader is easiest to mistake for a landmark. It stands in one particular pool at one particular depth, gray-legged, neck moving through its slow arcs like something sweeping water with a net too fine to see. It is not waiting for anything. Waiting requires a future, and the pool provides everything in a continuous present: the small warm shapes rising toward its beak at intervals so regular the bird's body has become a clock that measures nothing. Whether it is the first wader or the ten-thousandth wader to stand at this spot, the place does not say, and — this is the important thing — neither does the wader. It has no reason to distinguish itself from its own predecessors, because there are no predecessors. There is only standing, and the pool, and the soft click of its beak closing, which has always been the next sound.

The big grazers in the channels are the closest thing to a citizenry. Chest-deep in the blood-warm water, vast and unhurried, they mow the reeds in lanes the way rivers keep to banks — not by decision but by being what they are, repeated. They are warm-blooded in a world that is already body-temperature, which should make them the lords of the place, and in a sense they are; nothing challenges them, nothing could. But lordship has no use here. There is no rival, no season, no lean stretch to grow fat against. So all that size has settled into gentleness. They shift weight in the shallows with a sloshing patience, breathe the shared air in long slow agreement, and let the small bright fish clean their flanks in the same spots they have always been cleaned. They do not sleep, exactly. Sleep is a debt owed to a tiring day, and there is no day. They simply go on being awake the way the stone goes on being warm.

The fish are the secret-keepers, though they keep nothing. They stack in the water by temperature, each layer at its own depth of comfort, dark shapes hanging so still that from above they read as roots, as mud, as the pond's own furniture. And along the bacterial seeps some of them carry that faint cold glimmer down their sides — a pale light that does not belong to warmth and does not behave like warmth's light. It does not pulse. It does not fade. It is simply there, a bit of something older and colder carried into a new hot world, never explained, never spent. If the valley has any mystery left in it, it is filed in the flanks of these fish, and filed perfectly, and no one reads it.

The swarms own the air between the canopy and the cloud-ceiling, and they own it the way smoke owns the space above a dish — by filling it. Each column of midges hangs over its own pool, turning in place, a grey standing thread pinned to the air. Nothing joins one column to another; nothing exchanges. Each is a congregation with no content, a crowd that has never had a purpose assigned to it and so has simply persisted. The dragonfly-analogues thread through them on slow circuits, eating, and this is not violence here — the columns do not thin, because birth equals death at every scale, and the eaten are replaced at the exact rate of their eating, so that predation in this place has the quality of an act in a play, performed identically each eternal hour. The midges do not flee the way midges elsewhere flee. There is nowhere to flee to, and no before to compare this to.

The snakes in the branches are the still ones, and they are the ones a visitor fears, wrongly. A snake here lies with the exact stillness of its branch because it has never once been hungry beyond the slow arithmetic of the place. Its food arrives on schedule in the sense that there is no schedule and everything is always arriving. It will strike, at its interval; it will swallow; it will return to branch-stillness. Nothing about it is coiled against a future. Coiled is what its body is shaped like, the way the drip-tips are shaped for dripping.

And the ground — the boiling-looking top of the litter, the beetles, the crabs, the small wet amphibians in numbers beyond counting — these are the place's arithmetic made visible. Every one of them holds position. That is the whole of each life: hold position, consume at the rate of being consumed, process the litter at exactly the rate it falls, and pass through the hands of the place from one form to the next without ever once leaving the ledger balanced differently. They do not know they do this. Knowing would require a place to stand outside the sum, and there is no outside.

The birds call their phrases into the dim air — the same phrases, each from its own branch, in the same overlapping order. They are not calling to anything. Calling is what their bodies do in this light, and the light does not change, and so neither do they. Somewhere in each of them is the machinery for dawn-song and dusk-song, the machinery for territorial defense, for courtship timed to seasons, for warning cries — and all of it runs at once, or none of it runs, or the distinction has dissolved, because the one continuous crepuscular hour has given every bird all the time in the world and no moment to use it in. So they sing everything at once, softly, forever, and the song is not a message but a texture, part of the chord.

Which brings you to the strangest thing about what lives here, stranger than the misplaced cold light on the fish, stranger than the hundred-year wader: none of them are afraid. Not in the ordinary way. There is no shadow to cast them back to a den, no dry season to survive, no winter on the other side of anything. Fear is a creature's estimate of a change to come, and change is the one commodity this valley does not stock. The animals do not know they are unafraid; there has never been anything to compare it to. But you can feel it in them, in the way the grazer lets you stand at its flank, in the way the snake does not bother to be a snake at you, in the way everything here treats your presence as simply another warm fact in a saturated world — absorbed without alarm, the way the air absorbs your breath and gives it back.

They cannot remember. That is the final rule of their citizenship. The mud forgets as fast as it is written on, and they are made of the same accounting as the mud — each of them a small balanced ledger, closed each moment and reopened exactly the same. If the valley is a wheel spinning perfectly, in place, then these are the creatures caught riding it: all the motion of living, none of the travel, every appetite met at the moment of its arising, every death paid for at the moment of its cost.

Whether they are happy is not a question the place can hear. Happiness, too, wants a before and an after. What can be said is this: everything here is already home. Everything here was born home, is being born home, will have always been being born home, mid-breath, mid-creation, the first generation at its full and final size — and nothing in the valley, not the wader, not the grazer, not the glimmering fish with their cold unspent light, has ever once had the thought that things could be otherwise.

That thought, when it comes, will come from outside. And the steam will still be standing when it does.
