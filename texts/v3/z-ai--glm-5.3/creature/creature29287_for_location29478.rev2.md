What lives here is a parliament that has never once adjourned.

The citizens are the free-tails, and they do not live in the tube so much as the tube lives on them. Millions of them, packed among the ceiling's stone teeth in layers deep enough to look like black thatch, each one no bigger than a thumb, each one a furnace the size of a heartbeat. Together they are the largest animal in the place — a single distributed organism spread across kilometers of roof, breathing in ripples when disturbed, warming the air beneath itself into a soft ceiling of mammal heat that hangs below the cold rock like an argument the stone lost. They are the importers. Every dusk they pour out through the skylights in a spiral that takes an hour, and every night they tax a hundred square kilometers of plain — moth and beetle and gnat, the whole airborne wealth of the world above — and carry it down as guano, seed and currency of everything below. The tube is their gut. Nothing here eats anything they did not first catch.

Beneath them lives the working class: the beetles. Dermestids, mites, pale blind larvae, fungus-gnats — an entire graveyard shift that turns the guano cone grain by grain, and whose collective labor is one of the country's standing sounds, a continuous distant frying, like rain on paper, like surf too far away to swear to. The cone they inhabit is not floor anymore. It is meters deep, compost-hot at a hand's depth, steaming faintly, and its skin moves when you look at it. It is the only warm country in a cold one, and it is entirely manufactured — by the bats above, by the beetles within, by the fungus threading it. It has a climate. It generates its own weather. In a place with no seasons, the heap is the only thing that makes anything new.

Along the walls between the skylights live the crickets, papered onto the blue-black glass by the thousands, and their song is the tube's voice — chirp overlapping chirp off the resonant walls until the rock itself seems to have opinions. They hold their pitch always. They have never varied.

At the drip-holes, each metronome pond fed every eleven seconds by the same bead of condensation falling where it has always fallen, live the drinkers: moths with tongues the length of their patience, gnats in tight orbit, everything that can afford to wait at a fixed point in a dark country. Each tiny pool is a village with one well and a schedule that has never been broken.

And at the cone's edge, at the exact border between the beetles' jurisdiction and the bare ringing basalt, lives the stillness: a snake. It is the only creature in the whole loud country that does not move, rustle, drip, or chirp. It waits where the bats fall — for the ones that fumble on the evening exodus, the tired, the wing-torn, the unlucky — and it is the closest thing this place has to a judge. Everything else here is process. The snake is decision. It is the period at the end of the cave's long sentence.

Above them all, occasionally, briefly, live the people — guano-harvesters with woven baskets and resin torches, who come down the rope-grooved lip and take only from the cone's apron and leave almost nothing behind except wear: soot-ghosts on the ceiling, a polished path, a dropped fiber here, a knapped flake of chert there, being slowly swallowed by beetles and time. The cave does not acknowledge them. The cave owns them, the way it owns everything carried into it: completely.

What lives here, in the end, is not a list of animals but a single rhythm — a heartbeat made of a million small ones, exhaled through a skylight every dusk, drawn home every dawn, running at one speed since the rock cooled, and in no hurry whatsoever to change.
