What lives in Choirbottom lives at the edges of hearing, which is the only country left here.

Start with the dark slick film, because everything else hangs from it. Along the warm runnels, wherever mineral water threads down through the rills and the clay stays filmed with heat, there is a stain that is not a stain. It is a mat — thin as a breath on stone, older than moss, simpler than moss, indifferent to moss and everything moss invented. It has no use for light, this place having made light a rumor; it eats the springs themselves, the hydrogen and the sulfur and the iron the warm water carries up from its long dark circulation, and it prospers on chemistry the way a meadow prospers on sun. It is the only greenless green in the basin. Run your eye along a runnel and you will read it as shadow, as wet, as mineral stain, and you will be wrong every time; it takes the Tuners, who stared at these waters for generations, to see it as what it is: the floor of everything, the sole producer in a country with nothing to produce with. It darkens the water's path the way lichen darkens a headstone, and it is so patient that a bootprint through it will take a season to heal.

Above it, barely, hangs the second tier. In the shallow brine pans of the lowest flat — dish-shaped depressions where the filmed water gathers into something you could finally call wet — there are swimmers some years. Pale things the length of a fingernail, eyeless, translucent in the way of animals that have never once needed to be seen, flickering in the pans during the seasons when the chemistry of a particular spring leans just far enough toward kind. They appear and vanish on schedules no one keeps, because they are the only calendar-candidates in the basin and they are unreliable. Some years the pans teem faintly. Some years they are empty dishes of rust and cream, and you cannot say whether the swimmers ended or only waited. They have no predators here. Nothing knows they exist. They live their whole crowded brief lives inside the roar, in water no louder to them than weather, and when a pan dries they are gone as silently as they came — which is to say, they were never audible at all.

And above them, very rarely, at the dim hours when the gray-violet wash leans closest to dark: the wader.

There is one large animal in Choirbottom, or there is one kind, and it is the porcelain-white thing that works the film. Long-legged, standing between the pipes with the stillness of furniture, running its unhurried bill through the mat along the runnels. Its proportions are wrong in a way you must look twice to name — the legs a fraction too long for the body, the neck a degree too arched, the bill's curve a shade too regular, as if it had been designed from a description of a heron rather than from one. It arrives soundless because nothing here can arrive otherwise; the Loudness takes its footfalls, its wingbeats, any cry it might make, and gives back the same standing chord. It is a creature that has adapted to the loudest country available by never once competing with it. When you see it — and you will see it before you hear it, you will always see it before you hear it, because hearing has been abolished here — it stands so perfectly within the mist columns and the cream pipes that your eye keeps handing it back to you as one more spire, until the spire steps.

It eats the film, and perhaps the swimmers in their good years, and it is the top of the shortest food chain you will ever walk through: mat, brine-fleas, wader, roar. That is the whole census of the moving creatures, and it fits in one sentence, which should tell you what kind of place this is. Most animals cannot enter. The volume enforces the emptiness the way a fence enforces a boundary, and so the wader has the entire basin to itself, a country miles wide with no rival, no predator, no companion — an animal whose ecological niche is silence within sound, and which holds that niche alone.

But a census of Choirbottom that stopped at the fauna would be false, because the dominant resident of this place is not an animal and it is not stone either, whatever the mineralogists would say. The pipes live. Not metaphorically — on the only timescale this place honors. Every spire is a throat with water in it, a vent still drinking from below and still exhaling its lime, still thickening its walls a finger-width a year. They are born at the fault-lines, they grow, their notes bend slowly downward as their throats narrow, and they die — close with a last gurgle and a thin mineral skin over the mouth, the way an organism dies with a small final sound. A grove of them is not a rock formation. It is a colony, a standing population of thousands, each individual singing, each individual aging, the whole population in the middle of a protracted communal act that began when the lake left. They do not move and they do not think, but they respire in their fashion, they metabolize water into stone, and they have a lifespan and a death rate and a sound that has been sinking a step per lifetime since the Tuners first counted it. If something lives here, the pipes live here, and everything that truly lives here — the mat, the swimmers, the wader, whatever the film knows — exists inside the pipes' long performance, in the acoustic weather the pipes make.

And then there is the question the basin holds the way it holds water: the Tuners themselves. Marks at 1.2 and no higher — cairn-caps on one pipe in forty, the polished dark patches worn by centuries of resting hands, the path re-laid generation after generation as the groves crept upward, the Cell on its southern rise with one warm note inside. The people are gone, or they are not. The reconstruction says they followed the falling note; the darker version says the note fell because the water went down, and they went down with the water, and are somewhere beneath the flats now, at the drowned ends of the pipes, listening to the chord from the inside. No one is up here to ask. But the place does not read as abandoned. It reads as tended-and-spared: the caps still seated where hands set them, the polished rims still taking the oil of rain, the Cell still filtering its single tone. Choirbottom is a habitat for a performance, and its most successful inhabitants — mat, swimmers, wader, pipes, possibly the people — all share one trait: none of them makes a sound the Loudness cannot swallow. Everything that could not live inside the roar left. What stayed is precisely the catalogue of what can, and the whole census of the place, read another way, is a list of the ways there are to be quiet inside enormous sound: to be too thin to hear, too small to matter, too still to interrupt, too slow to notice — or too far below, where the volume is the water's own voice and cannot deafen what is made of it.
