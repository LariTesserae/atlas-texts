**They are the last employees.**

That is the joke the travelers make, and it is not entirely a joke. Nothing on the Duty Surface was born and nothing on the Duty Surface dies in the way you mean that word; things here simply *conclude*. The custodians — the walkers, the sump-tenders, the panel-gangs — are not relics in the way a ruin is a relic. A ruin has stopped. The Duty Surface has not stopped. Every process on the plain is still holding its post, and the fauna are simply the posts that can move.

**The walkers keep their rounds.**

There were, by the worn grooves, once thousands. There are now a number you could count on a long walk, if you had the patience, and the number is wrong — you can feel it being wrong the way you feel a dropped stitch. Each walker is four-legged, a meter high, dust-matte over brushed alloy, and it steps on magnetic feet with a sound like nothing, so that your first notice of one is always a shadow where no shadow should move. Its head is a sensor cluster under a brow of scratched glass, and the head stays level on terrain that would nod a goat's skull. It walks its route. It has walked its route since dispatch. In three places on the plain, a walker's loop has worn thirty centimeters into the floor — a canyon of pure intent, polished at the bottom like a river stone, and you can stand in one and feel the devotion the way you'd feel a draft.

When one meets you, it stops. Four to five seconds. It is counting you. Something in it files the count, and the count goes down into the floor, into the grid, toward wherever the census lives — a room, maybe, that no one has found, where a ledger still opens every dim-day and enters the same number it entered the day before, or a smaller one, and closes.

Some walkers have gone strange in their old age. One, in the amber-salt country near Sump Nine, has worn its loop through a feral sedge-colony and does not deviate; it walks the groove while the glassy roots close over it year by year, and the travelers say it is being slowly installed, like a fastener. Another walks the border of a dead panel's shadow-country, edge-on to the darkness, and will not cross the bloom-line for anything, and no one knows whether that is obedience or fear, and the distinction may not exist here.

**The tenders are rarer, and stranger.**

Where the walkers patrol, the sump-tenders dwell: low, wide, six-legged things that live at the crystallized sumps and do nothing you can see for weeks at a time. Then, at dim-day, when the solvent dew runs and the salt plates crack and shift, a tender wakes. It walks the sump's rim with a proboscis lowered, tasting the drainage, and if a seam is occluded it will spend an entire dim-night clearing it — patient as geology, working a thread of grit out of a gasket channel with a tool-tip finer than a hair. They are the reason the floor still keeps itself. They are also, by the scuffs and the droppings of worn alloy, down to a number that no longer covers the territory: there are sumps the tenders have not visited in ten thousand years, and you can find them because that is where the salt grows up into spires, warm rose and gold, uncombed, unsorted, beautiful, and wrong.

The panel-gangs are nearly gone. They were made to climb — you find their anchor-points, little torqued sockets in the shell-sky's underside, marked from below as a constellation of dark freckles in the glow. What's left of them are two, or three; travelers disagree. One is reliably seen crossing a far panel every few years, a slow bright fleck, a kilometer-long shadow cast down onto the plain, and the old ones point and say *it's doing its round*, and the young ones ask what happens when the round is done, and the old ones don't answer because the answer is: the round is never done, only the rounder.

**The scavengers are what failure becomes.**

Nothing here repairs the custodians, but the world abhors an unused part. When a walker finally concludes — falls mid-stride, usually, joints ceased, census entry never updated — it does not stay a body for long. The strippers come: small, many-legged, no larger than your hand, blind, drawn along the floor's magnetic gradient to any mass that has stopped *being a route*. They take the alloy. They take the fasteners. They leave the dust to sift over what's left, and after a century there is no corpse, only a smooth shallow depression in the floor, brushed clean, that a walker's worn loop will eventually cross and not know.

Some travelers say the strippers feed the alloy back into the grid through the sump-sumps, that the plain digests its dead. Some say they only hoard, that somewhere under the floor there is a vault of disassembled custodians stacked like harvest. Nobody has dug. There is a strong sense, on the Duty Surface, that digging would be a firing offense.

**And there are the travelers themselves — few, always few.**

They are not made. That is the remarkable thing, and the walkers find them so: when a walker pauses on you for its four seconds, there is sometimes — the old ones swear this — a fifth second, a hesitation, a *recount*. You do not match the ledger. You were not on the manifest. The travelers come in over the spines from country no one maps, they stay a season or a lifetime, and they learn the rules without being taught: you do not break the salt spires. You step over the sedge. You camp near a live seam, where the solvent dew beads and the floor is warmest, and at dim-day you listen — to the tick of the panels, to the one bead letting go a kilometer away — and you navigate by everything.

They sweep. That is the duty, the one the stamp asks for, and the travelers have organized no one and sworn no oath, but each of them, walking, drifts the dust off the panel-glass underfoot with a boot-edge, clears the witness-marks of their moss so the fastener-patterns can bloom, lifts a salt plate out of a sump inlet and sets it aside. Small obediences. No one asked. The floor keeps itself, but the keeping is now shared between the tenders and the strays, and if the number of tenders only goes down, the number of strays, so far, has not yet hit zero.

Walkers navigate by everything, the old ones say. And the census, wherever it lives, keeps counting them — the walkers, the tenders, the strippers, the two or three bright flecks on the underside of the sky, and you — one number that only goes down, one number, so far, that does not.
