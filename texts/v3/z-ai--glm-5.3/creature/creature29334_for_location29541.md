The trees are called tallows, and they are the reason for everything. But they are not what you came to hear about, or rather, they are, but only because what lives in the Wick lives the way it does *because of* them. So: the tallows first, briefly.

They stand in orchard-rows across the basin floor, forty, fifty meters at full growth, their trunks the grey of candle-fat gone old — the resemblance is not a resemblance, it is a fact. Split a tallow trunk and the cut weeps. The sap runs amber and slow, and when it cools it is wax; when it is filtered, pressed, and drawn through a die it becomes wick-thread; when the thread is dipped, layer on layer, in the rendered harvest, it becomes light. The whole city is a slow argument between a tree and a flame, and the people who live here are the ones who keep the argument going.

They are called wickmen, or the drawn, or sometimes just the fourteen thousand, and the first true thing to know about them is that they are not pale. You expect pale — no sun, you think. But the Wick has had daylight-strength amber burning over it for eleven generations, and it shows. They are all of them the color of lamplight itself: a deep honeyed tone, uniform across ages, so that a child of six and a widow of eighty standing side by side look like the same person at two moments of the same long life. Their eyes are the giveaway. Wick-born eyes are large and slow-blinking and the color of struck matches — dark, but dark the way a wick is dark, with the sense that something is burning in there just beneath the surface.

They do not drink water because there is none to drink. Instead they eat the frost. Every dawn-cycle — the Wick keeps dawn and night by dimming and brightening the crown-lamps, a mechanical courtesy, an imitation of a sky none of them has seen — children go out onto the basin floor with scrapers and gather the wax-frost off the bedrock into pails. Old wax-frost is chewed; the fresh hoar is held under the tongue, where a body knows how to sort it: the small fraction that is condensate, drawn up through the rock from some deep and never-seen aquifer, goes to the blood, and the rest, the wax, is swallowed for bulk and burn. A wickman's body runs warm. Put your hand on one and you will startle. They run three, four degrees over what you'd expect, and in the cold of the night-cycle they stand around in ordinary work-clothes, steaming faintly, like kettles taken off the hob.

Their trades are named for parts of a candle, which nobody alive remembers as a separate thing, because for them a candle is not an object, it is an ancestry. There are the *drawers*, who work the sap into thread. There are the *snuffers*, which sounds like a small job and is not — the snuffers tend the crown-lamps, walking the high rigging under the stone ceiling on lines and hooks, trimming char, catching drips, replacing burners, and it is said a snuffer who reaches forty without a fall has been blessed by the tree. There are the *pourers*, the *moulders*, the *crappers* — that last one is a real trade, an honored one; a crapper gathers and processes the burnt residue, the spent char and clinker that the lamps shed, because nothing in the Wick is allowed to be wasted, not even ash, especially not ash.

And there are the *listeners*.

Here is the thing about the quiet. You counted, standing there, and heard almost nothing, and you took that for emptiness. The fourteen thousand know better. The tallows are not silent trees. Under the wax and the slow growth there is a movement of sap that has a rhythm to it, a pulse — the old ones say a heartbeat, and it is not entirely a metaphor. A mature tallow's sap rises on a beat, and the beat is individual, tree to tree, and it changes when the tree is healthy or sick, thirsty or heavy with harvest, young or near its falling. A trained listener, standing with a palm flat on the trunk, can tell you more about a tallow's condition in ten minutes than a month of borings could. The best listeners — and this is the part outsiders refuse, until they hear it — can tell you what a tree is *doing about* its condition. Because the tallows respond. They shift their rhythms when the lamps above them dim. They quicken, all together, all across the basin, in the hour before a cave-fall, and the Wick has never lost a person to a ceiling collapse, not once in eleven generations, because the trees have always known first, and the listeners have always heard the trees.

So the arrangement, the whole quiet arrangement of the place, is this: the trees give wax and wick. The wax and wick become light. The light is sold up the shaft to the world above — the cage rides down empty and rides up loaded with candlestock, and that is the Wick's entire economy, everything else is furniture. But the light is also kept, generously, wastefully, for the valley itself, full-strength at all hours, because the tallows do not grow in the dark. They need it. The people need the trees. The trees need the light. The light needs the people, or at least the people's hands, to snuff and trim and pour.

Nobody in the Wick has ever stated this as a religion. They do not have a religion, exactly. They have the *tending*, which is the word for the whole mutual keeping, and if you ask a wickman what he believes in he will look puzzled and say "the round of it," meaning the cycle: sap to wax to light to growth to sap. Pressed further he might say the trees are his relatives, and when he says this he will not be speaking figuratively, because in a sense he won't be.

Because here is the last thing, the thing you would learn only if you stayed, and people do stay, more than you'd think.

The dead of the Wick are not buried. They are planted. A body is rendered — the same word the wax-trade uses, and the same process, and no one in the Wick finds this grim, so check your face — and what remains is mixed with sap and set at the rootline of a young tallow, a sapling chosen for it, and the tree takes it up, and the listeners say the young tree's beat is never quite like other trees'. A little richer. A little more *insistent*. Trees planted this way grow faster and straighter, and their wax burns with a flame that holds steadier in a draft, and a Wick-made candle is worth three times an ordinary one in the markets above, and the people above who pay that premium do not ask why, and the people below do not volunteer.

Ask a wickman whether his mother is dead and he will say — has said, to the few travelers who earned the answer — "she's in the north rows now, forty meters, coming along beautifully." Ask him whether that comforts him and he will not understand the question. Comfort implies absence. There is no absence in the Wick. There is only the round of it.

So when you stand at the cage-door at the end of your visit, and the fourteen-thousand-quiet presses in on you, and the amber light lies on the wax-frost like a lake that has never existed, look up at the crown-lamps one more time before you ride out. Every one of them is burning a wick drawn from a trunk, dipped in a harvest, tended by a hand, planted on a pulse. Every one of them is a person, or a tree, or the difference between those words, which the Wick has quietly stopped needing.

The cage door closes. As you rise past the amber veined in the salt, you realize you have been inside a lamp, yes. But you had it backwards about which part was the flame.
