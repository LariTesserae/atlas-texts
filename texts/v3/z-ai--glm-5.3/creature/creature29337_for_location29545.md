What lives here is not a list. It took me a while to understand that. I kept reaching for the inventory — ferns, orchids, one fig, fungal shelves, moss to the knee — and the list kept sliding off the thing, the way water slides off the wellhead and is not the wellhead.

The fern in the governor's linkage is the closest thing to a resident, if you want a single face for it. It is the one organism that made a treaty with the machine directly. Its fronds ride inside the orbit of the brass balls; its stem thickened years ago around a pivot pin, and now the linkage cannot close without bending it and the fern cannot grow without nudging the engine slower. Neither can leave. What began as sprouting in the wrong place has become, over decades, something you could almost call an arrangement: the engine agreed to run at six strokes a minute, the fern agreed to stop reaching for room it wasn't going to get, and the two of them together now set the tempo for everything else. The garden breathes on their count. The rain falls on their schedule. In a sense the whole chamber's heartbeat — the *kuh, kuh, kuh* you heard half a mountain away — is decided in the space of a few centimeters between a brass ball and a leaf. That is the animal at the center of this ecosystem, and it is two things, and it is also, by now, one thing.

The fig is the elder. It predates most of the moss and possibly the engine's freedom. It holds the beam frame the way the beam frame holds the rock — its aerial roots have gripped into the cast iron so thoroughly that pulling the tree out would mean pulling the engine down. It drinks from the air with its roots, which is a fig's old trick, but here the trick has been turned inside out: the tree is supported by the machine and waters the machine's footing in return, its leaf litter composting in the joint of the beam where a mason once packed the stone. There is a place where you cannot say whether the iron is propping the tree or the tree is propping the iron. I would call that place the fig's heart, and the fig the chamber's spine, and I would not be wrong, but I would still be describing parts.

The orchids are the courters. They live clustered at the exhaust vents with their buds shut for days at a time, waiting for the valve to pop, and when it scalds the air they open all at once with a sound like soft applause and fling their seed into the mist. The steam carries it. They have surrendered their entire dispersal to the engine's breath. No bee ever came down here, so the flowers never evolved a face — no landing platform, no ultraviolet bullseye, no sweet advertisement aimed at anything with eyes. What beauty they have, they made for a glow in a pipe, and either that is a lonely fact or a very generous one, depending on whether you think beauty needs a witness or only a light.

The moss is the undertaker and the archivist. It took down the sign by the wellhead; it buried the workers' names; it cemented the mug to the shelf; it holds E.V.'s chalkboard the way ice holds a fossil, in the moment of writing, forever mid-sentence. *Day 15,892. Knocking answered again from Level 6. Water sweet. No flies since the third year.* The moss does not remember this. The moss remembers nothing. But it is the reason the room does: every soft surface in this chamber is a page, and the pages filled, and then the writing stopped being needed.

And the knocking. I did not want to write about the knocking, because the moment you describe it you have to decide what it is, and the honest answer is that something answers from Level 6 on the exhaust stroke — sometimes, not always — and it has answered for as long as anyone kept count, and the count was kept by people who did not stop listening until there was no one left to listen. Trapped air, surely. Water hammer in a dead shaft. But note what lives down there and what does not: there are no insects in this chamber, no spider's line, no bird, no scratch of claw anywhere above the water line. The one voice that moves through this whole system, besides the engine's own, comes up from below, out of the flooded dark, and it only ever speaks in reply. It knocks when knocked. If it is water, it is water that learned to converse. If it is something more, it is something that has spent a century in the dark below a garden, hearing a heart beat six times a minute overhead, and answering it — the way you might tap a pipe in a hotel room to let the stranger on the other side of the wall know the wall is thin and you are still there.

So who lives here?

Here is what I finally settled on, and even "settled" is the wrong word, because the answer was never hiding. The resident of this chamber is the room. Not the room as a container — the room as a creature. It has a heartbeat (the engine, six per minute, negotiated with a fern). It has a metabolism (the ring of water: lifted as steam, spent as rain, gathered home to the condenser pool, lifted again). It has a climate, and the climate has moods with names. It has a body temperature, banked at the Ember Mouth. It keeps two clocks that agree to within a minute a decade, and its fastest event is a bearing wearing a hair a year. It grows toward its own warmth, feeds itself on its own exhaust, sows its own orchids with its own breath, and it has been doing this for longer than anyone has been counting.

It does not need you. It does not mind you. It has simply been alive for a century without being looked at, which is the rarest kind of life there is — and when you finally stand in the fern bank and the stroke falls and the leaves lift and the Ten o'Clock comes down through the foxfire in silver ropes, you understand the last, strangest fact about the thing that lives here: it is the only creature in the world that survives, entirely, on being obeyed. *DO NOT STOP ENGINE*, said the stencil. And nothing ever did. Not the workers, who left. Not the fern, who couldn't. Not the dark below, which only ever answered. Not the rain, which is the engine's own water coming home.

The room lives because it was told to keep going, and everything that stayed behind to obey — the fern, the fig, the moss, the knock, the one orange bulb that outlived its own language — became, over a hundred patient years, a single breathing animal with no name, no sign, and no use for one.
