What lives here, first, is a population you could be forgiven for walking past. The terraces hold citizens the way a real city holds crowds — everywhere, even, and easy to stop seeing. But stop seeing them and you stop seeing the place, because they are not decoration. They are the tenants.

**The ferns** are the town's disciplined class. They live in colonies on the terraced rings, each stand as neat as a row house, and they keep the clock better than anything else in the vault. They are the fastest things here — the only residents whose lives run at a pace a visitor can share. Sit still long enough and you can watch one being born: a fiddlehead uncurling like a green comma finding its sentence. A fern here lives a visible life. It grows while you watch, the way a child grows only in photographs run fast. In three weeks it has done what ferns elsewhere take a season to do, and it has never had a brown frond, never dropped a spent leaf, never once had to be anything but young. An entire citizenry in the first flush, nobody wilted, nobody old, nobody dead. If ferns have moods, these are the moods of people on the morning of a holiday.

**The mosses** are the quiet multitudes. They carpet the terraces in bright even swards, and they glow — a pale green-white light that is the chamber's only lamp. They are the poor of this town in the sense of being numerous and low and doing the work: the light comes from them, soft and municipal, like streetlamps run on patience. They grow so evenly that at first you think they were installed, tile by tile. But no one installed them. They were seeded and simply did what they do, and what they do is flawless because nothing has yet happened to them. There is no bald patch, no scruff, no threadbare place where something ate or froze or burned. In a normal moss lawn, the gaps are the biography. Here the sward has no biography. It is a crowd that has never gossiped.

**The vines** are the lamplighters. They run the terrace fronts in braided lattices, and they carry light along their veins like milk in glass — brightest where the runnels pass, dimming upslope. They follow the water the way a lamplighter once followed a route: the lit streets are the wet streets, and the wet streets are where the town has decided life happens. The vines are also the drinkers of the walls. Every wall weeps in ordered lines, capillary-threaded, and the vines are placed to take the weeping straight out of the air. They live on the architecture's grief, or its sweat, and they have arranged themselves into lamps because of it. A citizenry whose brightness is its thirst: the more a vine drinks, the more it glows. In this town, need and beauty are the same organ.

**The sapling** is the child the whole place was built for, and possibly the mayor, and possibly the god. Two meters of pale smooth bark, new as unfired pottery, standing in the plaza basin where every gutter's water finally arrives. Its roots have already found the mains — it drinks the town's whole traffic — and its crown is the tallest thing in the vault. The survey mark by the stair is its root system drawn in section: the builders stamped the city's founding document as a portrait of a tree's feet. Whether the chamber is the sapling's infrastructure or the sapling is the chamber's heart is a question the place declines to answer, and the designation *1* suggests the distinction will not survive the next few decades anyway. It is the only resident with a title. It is also, you notice, the only resident that will outgrow its address. Everything else here fits its lot. The tree is the one being in the vault with a future that will require the building to change its mind.

**The motes** are the fauna — and it is important to be precise: there are no animals here, none at all, and the emptiness where animal sound should be rings like a bell. But there are fewer than a dozen motes, machines smaller than gnats, drifting the terraces on slow rounds. They are the vault's whole bestiary: they check leaf-veins, coax gutters, do the work that insects would do if insects had been invited. They are the pollinators-by-committee, the street sweepers, the town's entire animal class in a population you could cup in two hands. They move like things that know they are outnumbered by furniture. Twelve of them, in a cathedral. The fauna of a place can be read as its personality; this place's personality is a sparse, patient, almost apologetic handful of caretakers who keep the silence rather than fill it.

**The building itself lives here.** This is the resident you will be slowest to credit and quickest to believe once you've stood inside it for an hour. It is curing — still becoming, still young, still cracking its knuckles every few minutes as the lattice settles, each pop going off through the vault like a struck match. It hums, a bass note in the sternum, the sound of its pumps and thermal engines running on the difference between its warm breath and the cold ground. It has tide-lines in its walls, one per day, three hundred of them on the lower courses — growth rings, a diary of days. It drinks: every forty minutes the ceiling gathers the map of the city overhead, street for street, and lets go, and rains, and the hum resolves briefly into a chord — a held major third, low and round. A building that sings while it drinks is not an instrument. It is an animal that has been given the shape of a town and is nursing a tree at its center. And it is not finished: on the seventh terrace, an archway is half-formed, soft at its edges, budding a second chamber. The numeral 1 implies a row. This is not a building that was built. It is one that reproduces.

And then there are the ones who live here by absence, which in this place is a form of presence.

**The handprint people** — someone pressed their hands flat into a soft ceramic panel on the fourth ring while the wall was still young, and the wall kept them. Five fingers each, the only portrait in the vault. They are the parents. They built the plumbing, the streets, the plaza, the tree, and went back up the stair and left it running. They live here the way a signature lives in a house.

**And the ones who have not come yet.** The whole chamber is set for them: streets with gutters, blocks with basins, a plaza, signage, a first tree drinking in the middle. The emptiness is not absence — it is preparation, the emptiness of a set table, of a nursery before the child. Whoever this place is waiting for, it is waiting warm, wet, lit from within, loud with the domestic rain-on-tin sound of a house where someone is home. The ferns keep the clock. The motes keep the rounds. The building keeps the map of the city drawn in water on the ceiling, always current, always accurate, so that when the people finally come down the spiral stair, they will find a town that has been rehearsing them for weeks — a whole ecosystem where nothing has died yet, because there was no room in the design for death, and death will have to be invented when it arrives.

That is who lives here: a sapling with a title, a citizenry of moss and fern and vine, twelve careful motes, a singing building with a diary in its walls and a bud on its seventh terrace, a pair of fossil hands, and — in the largest numbers, in the deepest sense — the future.
