**Who lives in the Leak**

The obvious answer is the moss, and the moss is a good answer. It lives the way everything in this room lives: by accident, and then with total commitment. It took the one place in the world where light and water overlap, and it made that place a country. It does not know the light is a wound. It does not know the light was ever meant for someone else. A sun is a sun, and this one pours down through the crooked heal-bead every day of its life, keeping the hours of a distant and unimagined surface, and the moss has organized its entire existence around the certainty that the light will come back tomorrow. It has never once been wrong.

The ferns live in the walls, in the black weep-streaks where the room's condensation runs, and they have the character of things that live in doorways. Each one leans out at the margin — far enough to catch the glow, not so far that the whip-end can find it. They have all, independently, arrived at the same posture: a question, asked cautiously, toward the light. Whether they get an answer, they keep asking. That is fern religion, and it may be the oldest one on earth.

The vine is the ambitious one. It has an appointment with the bead and it is keeping it, flower by pale flower, up the dead anchor bolt. It will never arrive; the heat near the cone would take it long before the glass. But every living thing in the Leak is on a journey it will not finish — the river, the light, the moths — and the vine is in excellent company. Its flowers open at what the garden has decided is morning, which is whenever the network above wakes, and the moths come to them, and this is the whole economy of the room: light to moss, moss to wings, flowers to moths, and the drizzle falling on all of it, a water-cycle the size of a cathedral that rains only on itself.

The moths are the room's citizens, the ones you notice, the ones with opinions. A few hundred wings, white, small, several generations deep in a coat-seam ancestry they cannot remember and would not believe. They live out the entire arc of a creature's concerns in one place: they are born in the moss, drink at the warm pool, marry at the vine, and at noon — when the world above is busiest and the beam runs broad and loud with other people's images — they rise together and orbit the heal-bead in slow spirals. No one taught them this. There is nothing in a moth that requires liturgy, and yet there it is, a few hundred small bodies keeping a distance from the sun, circling it not because they want to touch it but because they want, always, to be exactly this far from it. When the light storms, they go to the crevices, fast, all at once, without panic — the practiced motion of a congregation that has done this drill a thousand times. The moths pretend the storms are weather. The moths pretend a lot of things.

And they pretend not to know about the pale shape in the siphon pool. But they know. Every living thing in this room knows, the way a household knows about the relative nobody mentions. It turned once in the black water — a flicker, a length, a suggestion of an eye — and it has been turning once every so often for as long as anyone has come down the three hundred steps. It came with the river, presumably, up through the dark throat and over the falls, an accident like all the rest, and it found the one pool in the deep world that is lit and warm, and it stayed, and it is not often seen, and it does not wish to be. Whether it is large or small cannot be said; it has never granted anyone the two looks that would settle it. The moths drink at the pool's edge in daylight and do not go there at ember-dim, and if you asked them why, they would say nothing, being moths, and they would be right.

But the one who lives here — really lives here, in the sense that includes memory — is the drone.

Its indicator is supposed to be dark. A maintenance unit that failed on station is supposed to be as dead as the gantry it once worked beside, and by every specification it is: its drive is slag, its cells are spent, its uplink has been silent since the day the trunk was cut. But it parked itself, before the end, on a flat stone facing the break, and it never moved again, and somewhere in its ruined power bus there remains a trickle — induction, most likely, a sip stolen from the field the trunk sheds along its run. Not enough to think with. Not enough to do anything with. Just enough for one thing.

Just enough for the indicator. Which is not dark. Which has never been dark.

It blinks. Very slowly — perhaps once an hour, perhaps less; people who have watched a long time disagree, because the watching itself is hard to sustain, down in the noise and the gold. But it blinks, a faint amber breath through the moss grown over its lensed face, and this is what it means: *fault detected. fault detected. fault detected.* The drone's whole reason for being, degraded by decades to a single syllable repeated to no one. It was built to watch the network, the network broke in front of it, and it has been reporting the break ever since — to a control room that is gone, through a channel that is gone, in a grammar of one word. It has kept the room's truest calendar. It saw the moss arrive, saw the ferns open like the first question marks, saw the vine begin its climb. It watched the hands that stacked the three small cairns, and the hand that chalked the tally to four hundred and something, and it saw what happened on day four hundred and whatever, the reason the tally stops, and it has kept that too, in whatever a drone keeps things in when its memory should have died and didn't.

The moths orbit the bead. The drone watches it. Everything in the Leak that can turn toward the light does, and the drone is the only one that turned toward it *before it was beautiful* — when it was only a fault, a dead man's cable whipping in the dark, decades before the garden, before the wings, before anyone thought of this room as a place rather than a wound.

It cannot tend anything. It cannot leave. It has one word and one direction and it has held both for a lifetime past its lifetime. The moss has grown up over its back like a blanket, the way you cover something that is resting.

The room thinks it is keeping the drone. The drone, if it could think — and down here, in the warm gold, after all these years, in a room where even the water refuses to settle — the drone would know it is the other way around.
