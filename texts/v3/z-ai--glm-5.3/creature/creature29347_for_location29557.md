# The People of the Terraces

They are easy to describe and hard to know, which is the opposite of most places.

Physically, they have been edited by their home. Generations of thin air have given them the barrel chests and slow, deep breathing of lifelong highlanders, but with a distinction visitors always notice and can never quite name: they breathe *unhurriedly* even under exertion. Their bodies treat air the way their city treats water — as something to be gathered patiently, in quantities, and stored. Their hands are another signature. Nearly everyone's hands are quick, expressive, and permanently half-raised, because the roar makes gesture the natural register of speech; a plateau native can conduct an entire nuanced argument — sarcasm, tenderness, refusal, the lot — while walking beside you, never turning their head. Their voices, when they do use them, come in two settings: a flat near-shout that the sound-wall permits, and a surprisingly low, quiet register used indoors, in the cisterns, in the gardens, where the roar softens and something like intimacy is structurally possible. They switch between these volumes without noticing, the way a sailor comes ashore still swaying.

Their eyes do a great deal of work. Children raised inside the thunder learn to read faces with unusual precision — the set of a jaw, the duration of a glance — and this has made them, as a people, quietly legible and quietly reserved at once. They do not exaggerate their expressions for the benefit of the deafened world around them; that would be shouting with the face, and they find it crude. What they have instead is a shared subtlety, a whole population fluent in small signals. An outsider can spend a week among them believing nothing is being communicated. Everything is being communicated.

They are, by trade, keepers. This is the deepest thing about them. The city is a machine for drinking the sky, and every family is a functioning part of it: the pump-house lineages, four generations deep, who tend the beam engines like livestock — feeding them, reading their moods, sleeping in cots beneath their slow iron strokes the way shepherds once slept with flocks; the roof-families, who know their corrugated slate the way farmers know a field, and who walk their terraces after every fog checking channels, clearing leaves, reading the day's condensation the way others read weather; and the cistern-keepers, the closest thing the city has to a priesthood. The keepers go down into the church-dark reservoirs with lamps and tally-books and come back up with the numbers that are, here, the news. When two plateau people meet, the greeting is not "how are you" but an exchange of levels. *North-east cistern at the third mark and rising.* It is a greeting, a weather report, and a small boast, all at once.

Wealth is measured in liters, and this has shaped their manners in particular ways. Hoarding is not quite shameful, but it is unseemly, in the way that talking loudly about money is unseemly elsewhere — and for the same reason: it reveals that you needed to say it. The genuinely watered families are understated about it. They signal their fortune through slate quality, through the age of their roof-channels, through the particular calm with which a dry season passes over their household. There are, of course, water-lenders and water-debtors; there is a whole quiet etiquette around owing; and there is at least one institution, the shared-cistern terrace, that functions as the city's poorhouse and its most respectable address simultaneously, depending on whom you ask.

They are not solemn people, though — this is important. The roar has given them a specific, well-honed humor, dry as the air between fogs, and mostly conducted in the hands. Their sign language is dense with jokes; whole comic traditions exist that no visitor will ever fully receive. They laugh the way they speak: quietly, close to the body, in the protected indoor places. They are proud of their city in an old, unbothered way — the pride of people who have never needed to argue its worth, because nobody who has heard the roar from a day's walk away has come for any other reason.

Their attitude toward time is the thing that most unsettles outsiders. Nothing here is urgent, because nothing here *finishes*. A cistern cycle takes a season; a roof's life takes a century; the engines have turned over at the same cadence since before anyone's great-grandparents drew breath. The people have metabolized this. They start things they will not complete. A keeper will begin a channel-repair knowing his granddaughter will finish it, and this is not resignation — it is how they understand projects to work. When they say of their city, *it is filling*, they mean it the way one might say of a slow soup that it is simmering: not waiting for something, but already doing the thing. Their patience is not passive. It is the active patience of people who are, in their own understanding, mid-task — and always have been.

And then there is the edge of them, the mild strangeness, the thing that shows when you look too long. It is about the water, and they know it is about the water. The patterns on the upper terraces — the laced, whorled, recurring forms, too orderly for chance — are left strictly alone, and this *leaving alone* is a piece of deliberate culture, not neglect. Roofs with a strong showing fetch higher rents, yes, but the rent is the smallest part of it. The real custom is quieter: households with a patterned roof will not scrub it, will not reslate over it, will route their channels to avoid disturbing it, and will say, if pressed in the flat near-shout, that *the water knows where it is.* Ask what that means and the hands slow down. Ask a cistern-keeper, down in the lamplight among the named and half-tamed rats, and you get the founding claim again, patient as bedrock: the water has been through the machines so many times that it has learned the shape of the city, and it redraws that shape wherever it rests.

Whether it is true does not appear to concern them much. What concerns them is the relationship. They live inside a machine that is also a mountain, on a plateau where the sky comes down daily to be drunk, in a sound that has stopped being sound, among a water that may be remembering them — and they have responded as people do to any long housemate: with accommodation, with courtesy, with an unsentimental and enormous familiarity. They do not worship the water. They work with it. But they keep its patterns the way you keep an old friend's handwriting — because whatever made it, it was made here, among them, and the least you can do with a message you cannot read is save it.

That is who lives there: a slow-spoken, quick-handed, barrel-chested people, chest-deep in a permanent afternoon of fog, fluent in silence and in thunder, debtors and lenders of liters, keepers of engines they love like animals, waiting for nothing, filling.
