# Who Lives in the Weeping Basin

Nobody lives in the Weeping Basin. That is the first honest answer, and it is wrong, and the way it is wrong will take some time to explain.

## The Water

Start with the water, because the water is the main inhabititant, and it behaves like one. It has habits. It has a daily round. It rises out of the warm ground in the hours when the haze is thickest, ascends in those soft columns you can watch from the ridge, condenses at the top of its own dome, and comes back down as sweet warm rain — and this circuit, repeated without end, is not merely a cycle the way weather is a cycle elsewhere. Here it is a *life*. The water goes to work. It has errands. It weeps down particular walls at particular hours with the regularity of someone who tends the same route every day, touching each surface in turn, and if you stay long enough you come to understand that the walls are not weeping so much as being *visited*.

The loop-canals are the water at its most domestic — water that has settled into a groove, going nowhere with contentment, murmuring the way a person hums while washing dishes. Nothing grinds in those loops. Nothing needs to. The turning is the occupation.

## The Motes

In the canal water drift the small gold lights, moving against the current, each on its own errand, and these are the nearest thing the basin has to citizens. They are not creatures — they don't eat, don't breed, don't notice you — but neither are they quite phenomena, because phenomena don't have *purposes*, and the motes unmistakably do. Watch one for an hour: it will pass under the arch where the canal runs three feet high, wait through the dripping section, continue. It knows where it's going in some manner that has nothing to do with knowing.

And some of them — one in a hundred, one in two hundred — are faces. Not detailed faces. Not portraits. But the arrangement of a face: the suggestion of eyes that would meet yours if they could, the weather of an expression. If you live in the basin, this is what you have instead of neighbors, and it works better than you'd expect. You come to recognize a particular one, the broad slow one that passes along the third-district canal-loop in what you've decided to call evenings, and after a while you nod to it, and the nod is not foolish. The nod is *addressed*. This is the only address in the whole basin — everything else grows in the silence of the unaddressed — and so the face-motes occupy a category the place has kept a slot open for, even though nothing has filled it in the ordinary way for a very long time.

It is impossible not to form the suspicion, and I will state it as a suspicion, that the faces are what the water remembers. That whatever people laid those massive lower courses — the ones who expected the water and made their peace with it — did not simply vanish. That they are in the water now, the way the luminosity is in the air: dissolved, diffused, condensed out occasionally as a soft-gold face on an errand, rained gently back down, returned to the closed circulation. The basin's fountain is patient enough to be a resurrection with no hurry in it.

## The Rooms

The city lives too, room by room. This sounds like a figure of speech until you have stood in the corridor that is longer going out than going in, and made the mistake of watching it, and felt it *notice you watching*. The broken geometry of the basin did not settle into dead habit. It settled into temperament. Every generous room — the ones larger inside than out — has a character the way a person has one: some are solemn, some are almost merry, one near the stepped ghats in the second district has the settled, unhurried generosity of an old woman who has outlived every argument she was ever going to have. The honest rooms, where things prefer to happen, are honest the way a plain-spoken friend is honest — it's a disposition, not a rule.

And the corridors — the self-conscious ones — are the shy members of this population. They do what they do reliably until someone tests them, and then they change, and the changing is not malicious. It is exactly the flinch of a stammerer asked to perform. You learn, if you live among them, to walk through with your eyes on your own hands, granting them the privacy they require, and in return they are steadfast, and you come to feel a strange protective tenderness toward them, the kind you feel toward anything shy that is trying, in its own worn way, to be a good corridor.

If the city keeps itself — and it does; the water never floods the upper floors, the stairs that go nowhere are stable in their going-nowhere — then the rooms are *who* is doing the keeping. Not by agency exactly. By constitution. A population of chambers, each weathered into its own reliable disposition, holding the shape of the whole the way a colony of slow animals holds a reef.

## The Green

The pale rush between the flagstones lives the most legible life in the basin: it grows in straight lines as if planted, it seeds its white fluff, and the fluff travels — ten feet an hour, in patient stages, lifted and set down and lifted again by the steam's slow hands. This is the basin's one visible act of ordinary biology, and it is moving precisely because nothing answers it. No grazer, no pollinator, no competitor. The rush sends its seeds into the air the way a person speaks into an empty room — not because it expects an answer, but because speaking is what it is. The hot-leaved stalks in the shallow pools live the same way, standing in their warm water with the leaves no one will ever touch, growing for the water alone.

## The Thing Beneath

And then there is the exhalation. Rarely — you might hear it twice in a month — a long, deep, soft clearing of the throat from somewhere under the streets, and the steam-dome overhead shifts its whole weight, and the drips everywhere change their tempo for an hour, and then everything resumes. Whatever makes that sound does not live in the basin the way the water does or the motes do. It is the reason there is a basin. The ground's own heat, the injury in deep time, the catastrophe that has become a climate — it breathes down there, slow as geology, and if it is a sleeper, its sleep is the basin's weather, and if it is something else, it has shown no interest in being anything else for longer than the word "longer" can carry.

So: who lives in the Weeping Basin?

The water, going its rounds like a lamplighter. The gold faces on their errands, which may be everyone who ever built here. The rooms, shy and generous and plain, keeping themselves. The rush, speaking to no one. The sleeper below, breathing the heat that makes the whole fountain turn. And you, if you stayed — the one addressed thing in an unaddressed world, standing in a generous room on your last evening, warm rain loud on your shoulders, nodding to a face going by on the canal, and being, for the first time in the basin's long healed-wrong memory, answered.
