# The Drip-Toads

They sit like little judges because that is the office the place has given them. Under the fixed drip-lines, on their own warmed rhizome, they have sat for so many generations that the sitting has shaped them. Each toad has a personal spot, a shallow bowl in the woven floor hollowed by its own line of ancestry — a toad-throne, dished like the stair-treads above, so that a young drip-toad settles into the hollow its grandmothers wore and is, from the first hour of its life, a judge in session.

They are heavy-bodied things, warty in the way of toads everywhere, but glossier than any surface toad ought to be — the constant humidity has given them a sheen like wet river-stone, and their skins drink the hall's warm sweet air the way the root does through its lip-shaped flue. Their colors have drifted toward the room itself: root-brown, felt-gray, a coppery sheen on the flanks of the oldest ones that is not metaphor — the Pan's evaporating spray salts the air near the machines with trace metal, and the eldest toads carry it, a green-bronze bloom along their spines like verdigris, like the copper pipe the tree has half-absorbed. A toad that has lived long enough under the Rootworks begins to look made. It begins to look like part of the works. This is not a disease. It is a career.

Their office is time. Three hours before the ceiling weeps — before the storm twenty meters up and six hours down has finished percolating through the ground — the drip-toads know. Not by sign, not by barometric trick alone, though their skins and inner ears read pressure the way the sight-glass reads flow. They know because the ground tells them; the long slow pressure-wave of rainwater moving down through soil arrives at their deep-pressing haunches as a rumor, a coolness, a tightening of the whole floor's mood — and the toads, who have nothing to do all watch but sit on the floor and be of the floor, are the only creatures positioned to hear it. They wake from their half-sleep and begin to climb.

They climb the walls. This is the alarm. A toad that leaves its throne and begins, with absurd dignity, to scale the root-felt toward the ceiling is a toad announcing rain, and the hall's whole population reads it. The drip-toads climb slowly, deliberately — they are not hurrying to shelter, they are *getting up on the roof of the world* — and by the time they have reached the lower edge of the root-mat, the first condensation-beads are already gathering above them. Then the ceiling glosses, and beads, and the hall rains, and the toads sit up there in the drip-lines themselves, among the drops, drinking their own forecast as it arrives, and every other creature in the Rootworks has had three hours to move the troughs, cover the basins, close the sweet-foam, and settle.

They have a religion of a sort, or something so like one that the difference does not matter: the bell. When the Hour rings, every drip-toad on the wall or under the lines lifts its head at once, in the same motion, mouths slightly open, as if the brass note were a droplet large enough to hear. They do this whether the hour means anything to their bellies or not. They have done it for longer than the toads remember, and it has begun to have consequences: their skulls are thickening at the jaw-hinge, their hearing has bent itself toward one pitch, the note of that one bell. A drip-toad taken (gently, briefly, for science or affection) up the root-stair into the forest above goes restless and wrong for days, and every keeper of the Rootworks knows you do not keep a drip-toad from its bell.

They breed in the stone basins, in the deep cold hours of the third watch, in the water the gutters have gathered. Their eggs hang in the stillness like strings of small amber beads — the same color as the lamplight pools, the same color as the sap in the sight-glass — and their tadpoles grow up on the skim, sneaking thin fillets of the sweet foam from the trough-edges when the day's feeding has left some. So the toads are sweet to the taste, faintly, and the hall's other residents know it, and the toads know they are known, and this too is part of the arrangement: they are the keepers of the alarm and the tithe of their own bodies is part of what they give back, and no party to the contract — not the tree, not the works, not the keepers — ever forgets it.

They have names in the way that fixtures have names. There is a scarred one at the third drip-line, a heavy old judge who has sat there so long that the root has begun to grow up around the edges of his throne, so that he is, slowly, being fitted into the hall, and it does not trouble him. There are the twins under the north gutter, who are not twins but have shared one drip-line so long their calls have converged into a single voice, and the hall reads one croak and knows the north is settled. There is a young one — there is always a young one — who sits not at a throne but at the edge of the empty middle, in the reserved dark, on the floor that is the future, and looks across it at the taproot's slow thickening pillar as if trying to imagine, in whatever way a toad imagines, what will be there when the emptiness has filled in. This one the older toads do not correct. It is permitted. Someone must sit near the future and watch it come.

And when the tap-scars heal on the taproot's flank, ranked like the stops of a flute — the old galleries, generations of punctures sealed — the drip-toads, in their season, climb to those healed rings and sit in the hollows of old tap-holes, high on the pillar, small judges in the empty windows of the works' whole history. Nobody taught them this. But it is a flute, and they sit in its stops, and on nights when the wind crosses the Sugarheart's crown far above and the old scars whistle faintly — the tree breathing through its healed wounds — the drip-toads in the tap-galleries sit with their throats swelling and answer it, a low chorus, the only instrument in the hall besides the bell, playing the room's own emptiness back to it.

Thump, says the Heart. Thump. Thump. The toads keep the time.
