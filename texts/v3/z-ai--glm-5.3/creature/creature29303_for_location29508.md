What lives here is, first and most obviously, the keeper.

He was not always quiet. You can tell that from the log — the early entries, decades back, are chatty in a strained, keep-spirits-up way, full of weather reports and small complaints and the word "I." Somewhere in the second or third notebook the "I" thins out. By the fifth, the entries are almost all dates and numbers, except for the birds. The birds he still writes up like events: *Kite, lowland, exhausted, slept on the stove pipe until mid-Stroke past dawn. Left before the noon-thinning. Never seen one sit so still.* He underlined *still* once, and then, apparently, thought better of it, but the pencil mark is there under the page's damp-warped paper like a fossil of feeling.

He is a broad man gone lean, the way long quiet takes flesh off people. His hands have kept their size, though, and they are the most eloquent thing about him: knuckles scarred from copper fin-edges, fingertips so permanently soft from the wet that he can judge a gutter's flow by brushing it, blind, with the back of his hand. His ears have changed the most and it's the change no one can see. Down on the plain, they say, you can drop a coin behind a man and he turns. The keeper wouldn't. But he can hear a single downcomer alter its pitch among hundreds, the way a mother hears one child turn over in a full dormitory, and he will be at that joint within a minute with a moss knife, kneeling in water, moving the fern aside with the tenderness of a barber around an ear.

He does not keep time the way lowlanders do. He has, he would tell you if he told you anything, no idea what an hour is anymore except as a unit of rain — the warm rain comes on the stroke and leaves on the stroke, roughly a hundred strokes to a squall. He sleeps in strokes: nap of forty, deep sleep of two hundred, surfacing between beats like a swimmer. When visitors come up through the notch — surveyors, once a year; a pipe-fitter, once every three — he watches them check their watches against the piston and feels, without being able to say it, the way a shore creature must feel watching someone consult a calendar to guess the tide.

The keeper knows the place has rearranged him. He found out in the lowlands, the one time he went down for his sister's funeral: three days on the plain and he could not sleep, something missing from under the bed, a silence with a piston-shaped hole in it. He drank plain water and it tasted like nothing at all, like the memory of water, and he stood in his brother-in-law's yard at what would have been mid-Stroke and felt his body lean, infinitesimally, forward, waiting for the ker-CHUNK that came only in the basin. He went back up before the week was out. He has the phrase for it in the log, one entry, alone on a page: *Came home.* Underneath it, later, in the steadier hand: *Home also came down to check, but it stayed put.*

He is not the only resident, and he knows it, and his knowing has the texture of good manners.

The tank frogs govern the bromeliads. Each cistern in the crotch of a tree fern is a full polity — two or three frogs, a generation of tadpoles, a constitution written in rainwater chemistry and the metabolics of a teacup. They are metallic-voiced, small as a thumbnail, and strict. They call in the gap and never over the beat; whether this is fear or liturgy, the keeper stopped trying to decide years ago. He has names for some of them, in his head — names like *Old Copper* and *the Widow* — and he knows which tanks run dry in a long noon-thinning and carries water to them in a ladle, one of the only interventions he permits himself, logged as *assisted tank 14, no cause for drought, just the fern drank first.*

The wick moths live on the very edge of things. Their wings are wet tissue, unsuited to everything except standing still on a copper edge and drinking where the condensation beads, trembling with each tick of falling water, and they are the basin's most fragile citizens and its most numerous besides the springtails. A moth's whole life is passed at the border between the cloud and the metal, at the exact line where the mountain's breath becomes the mountain's water. The keeper sometimes watches them at dusk and thinks — he wrote this once — *they must believe the Stroke is the sky.*

The springtails are a nation without a face: uncounted, unmappable, everywhere in the moss like salt in the sea. He has given up on them as individuals and regards them instead as one enormous soft-bodied tenant spread across the whole floor of the basin, and he steps carefully, not out of mercy exactly, but the way you'd step carefully in a cathedral — because it is the floor, and it is alive.

And then there is the thing that the keeper will not name but has clearly, in his older handwriting, begun to suspect: that the basin's true inhabitant is not any of them. It is the composite. The mountain still being born, the forest still annexing, the works still dripping — three infants grown into one another until no one could say where garden ends and machine begins — have become, between them, a single growing organism with a copper heart, a fern-and-moss skin, a vascular system of gutters and downcomers, a metabolism of cloud. It breathes on an eleven-second cycle. It has a voice, the Stroke, and the Stroke is not the piston alone — the piston is only the pacemaker; the echo off the cirque walls, the leaf-hiss the echo carries, the ten thousand drips falling together on the discharge, the frogs in the gap, the ferns' windless shiver — that chorus is the whole creature speaking in unison. The keeper is not its master. He is, at most, its lymph: a small mobile element of it, circulating, carrying tools instead of antibodies, repairing where the body repairs itself slowly, staying because staying is what the body does.

He knows the tally board says it. He chalked it. *Still dripping. Still rising.* — that's the organism's whole biography, and his, in four words, and he is content to have no larger one. When he goes — and he'll go the way everything here goes, not removed but overgrown, the moss closing over the keeper's hut the way it closed over the zinc, the tally board going gold with lichen with its last numbers still legible underneath — the basin will keep the Stroke, and the frogs will keep the gap, and somewhere in the log, in the steadiest hand of all, there will be the final entry he has already composed in his head and never written, saving it:

*Enough.*
