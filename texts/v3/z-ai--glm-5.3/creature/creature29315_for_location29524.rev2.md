# Who Lives on the Shelf

The first thing to understand is that nobody on the Shelf is entirely sure where the living stops and the keeping begins. The herds are animals, certainly — warm, opinionated about which mats are ripe, stubborn in the particular way of cattle. But their hoofprints are all the same print, and they knew the vent schedule before they were born. Somewhere back along their line, stock became feral, and feral became something the Shelf grew the way it grows its mats: shaped by the pattern underneath, serviced from below, loved in a way that has no face anymore.

## The herds

They are the reason the place reads as a pasture at all — the quilt of grazed crescents and pale resting green that covers the tableland like a map of the machine's own patience. Broad-backed, low, chalk-dun and charcoal, dense velvet coats that do double duty as dew-harvest and armor against the hammered-noon light. They stand in the vent-mist and drink themselves — the nap of the fur channels water down to the mouth, so a herd at a vent looks like a congregation taking communion through the skin.

They never cry out. This is the uncanny thing about them, the detail that settles in a visitor's teeth. A thousand animals, and not one sound louder than chewing. Their alarm is a single stamp — one soft thud into warm stone — and the ground, which carries everything here, carries it a kilometer, and every herd on the terrace lifts its head in the same moment. They are using the Shelf as their nervous system. Perhaps they always were.

At mist-time, when the long gray is out, they make their wheels: heads outward, calves at the hub, motionless, waiting for the mist to lie back down. Nothing teaches them this. It is in the print, along with the hoof.

## The long gray

Nobody has ever described its face, because nobody has seen it outside the mist, and inside the mist nobody looks. It is low, long, the exact color of the casing stone, and it moves only in the vent-fog, which may be preference, may be constraint, may be manners — a predator that keeps its side of an old agreement about when hunting is permitted. The herds know it without seeing it. The kill is a sound like a coat being folded, and then the mist closes over the place where it happened, and by evening there is nothing there.

Whether the long gray is one animal or the Shelf's idea of one animal, the herds' opinion of an eviction notice, or something older that was here before the lid — the Shelf does not say. It hunts twice a day, at the two breaths, and it keeps the herds moving, and the movement is what keeps the mats rested. It is, functionally, the shepherd. It just shepherds from underneath.

## The beetles and the seam-drinkers

The cleanup crew: finger-length, pale, arriving within hours of any death, exact as a delivery service. They reduce a carcass to a shallow dish of dust in days, and then the seams open and drink what's left. Between appointments they live in the seam-hairlines and along the vent-lips, and they are why the Shelf has no bones, no ruins, no litter, no history lying around — the machine does not keep a midden, and nothing rots in a house this clean. The dust-moths fly the glowing seams at night like lanes, and the midges glitter in the plumes, and none of these small lives seem to mind that the floor is their landlord.

## The birds

Small, dark, standing very upright on pylon-stumps worn matte by generations of their own feet — they have been holding that post since before the pylons were stumps. They call two notes, always the same interval, tuned to a tone the ground itself makes, and at first light the whole Shelf sounds at once: hundreds of identical calls, calm and eerie, like a system announcing that everything is fine. Which is precisely what it is. They are the audible heartbeat of the maintenance. In the evening they cut off together, all of them, in the same shared minute, and the tick of the cooling stone takes over — the ground's own clock, one tick a minute, like the world being wound.

## The rim-walker

Sometimes, at dusk, something tall goes down the tiers. Tower-legged, slow, machine or animal or some third thing the categories don't cover, and everything ignores it completely — herds don't stamp, birds don't lift, the long gray doesn't come out of the mist for it. It is either so much a part of the place that it doesn't register as an event, or so far outside the contract that it doesn't register at all. It descends into the haze of the machine's deep architecture and does not come back until it does.

Visitors who see it once tend to stop telling the story with any confidence. It may be the farmer's hand. It may be a hand that's forgotten it's a hand. It comes at dusk, the hour when the Shelf's own attention seems to move below.

## The machine

And under everything — literally under, a lid-width away — lives the thing that is either gone or gone into it. Not dead: the hum is in your sternum, the warmth is blood-warm, the bothy is swept and the hinge is oiled and the cup is clean for guests who may never come. Whatever it was, it farmed this surface so long that farming and being became the same verb. Its exhaust is dew. Its crop is the dark plush mats. Its livestock went feral and stayed, because there is nowhere better to be an animal. Its predator works shifts. Its birds report status in two notes. It maintains the whole world in a state of perfect wear — pylon tops held matte, road flush, edges soft, nothing getting worse, nothing getting better, a place that has decided on its own ideal age and keeps it.

It keeps a lens on the bothy shelf, old and cool on the warmest day — the only cold thing on the surface besides the basins, as if whatever the machine holds precious is held at a different temperature from everything it merely runs.

## Last

At night you can lie anywhere on the stone and it holds you at body heat, seams glowing under your hands like the filament of some vast patient heater, herds drifted across the dark like patches of snow that refuse to melt, the rim-walker somewhere down in the tiers doing whatever it does to keep the deep floors true. It is very easy to believe the whole Shelf is asleep, and that everything on it — the folded coats of the herds, the mist-holding long gray, the upright birds on their worn posts, the tall slow thing on the rim — is sleeping on its back.

They live on a farmer who has become the ground. They seem to find this a perfectly reasonable arrangement. The door of the bothy opens without complaint. The cup is clean. Leave it where you found it.
