# Who Lives on the Shelf

The people of the Still do not look like much at first meeting, and this is deliberate. Everything about them is tuned to the two great facts of their country: the glare and the silence. They come up out of the steam-veils of the Main Spring looking like the Shelf itself made men — bodies wrapped in undyed sinter-white cloth that breaks their outlines against the terraces, faces shaded under wide brims, eyes narrowed to permanent slits even indoors, even at night. Some wear the wooden blinkers year-round until the bones of the face seem to have settled around them. Their skin goes pale in the covered places and burned to dark leather at the wrists and jaw, so that an old stillman's face is a map of his own clothing, and you can read a lifetime in the tan-lines.

But it is the hands you come to know them by.

Because the Shelf punishes noise and rewards light, the stillfolk have moved their whole language into their fingers and their mirrors, the same migration the cobblebacks and soarers made long ago. They speak with a hand-speech as fast as any tongue — working talk, quick and abbreviated, done close to the body so it doesn't cast confusing shadows. For talk across distance they glint: palm-sized polished plates of sinter-glass, flashed in the code of wing-glints, and it is genuinely the birds' code, borrowed and enlarged, the way lowland peoples once borrowed from birdsong. A child of the Still learns to glint before she learns to whisper. There are formal registers of it — a slow ceremonial flashing used at weddings and at the naming of children, done with large mirrors at dusk when the air is still — and there is the rough field-shorthand flashed between blinker-wrapped workers across a kilometer of white, two glints and a pause and a third, which can carry *the crust is soft here, walk wide* or *come eat* or, from a mother to a son on the far terrace, nothing but *I see you.*

Their voices still exist. They use them the way we use bells — rarely, and mostly for the sake of hearing a voice at all, at night, in the shelters, in a dark close to the chest, for lullabies hummed against a child's ear at a volume below the drips. Some households keep the tradition of the *whisper-round*: on the longest night, the whole family lies in a ring with ears to the middle, and each in turn says one sentence into the center. What is said there is not repeated outside the ring. The sentence belongs to the ring.

The town's trades all bend around water and heat. There are the **dew-harvesters**, who rise before the light to walk the gathering-grounds — roofs, chains, stone-runs, the great strung fog-nets flung across the terraces on posts carved from sinter-cement — milking the night's condensation into gutters, every drop conducted by gravity and counterweight and patience down to the cisterns and from the cisterns down the escarpment itself, to the Lift, to the Dry Countries, in exchange for everything the Shelf cannot make. There are the **silk-farmers**, who tend the microbial gardens like vintners: they know the temperature of a run by the color of its film the way a lowland farmer knows soil by smell, and they can tell you that the rose band on Terrace Nine "went rusty early" the way another man says the frost came. There are the **vent-readers**, the nearest thing the Still has to a priesthood and a weather-service in one body: men and women who lie flat on the warm crust with an ear (or a bare palm, or a bowl of water, watching its rings) pressed to the ground, listening through their bones for the vents' breathing, keeping the ledger of each exhaler's rhythm, because a vent that skips is a vent that might hiccup, and a hiccup can drop a crust with a family on it. The vent-readers walk in pairs even to their own outhouses; nothing on the Shelf is done alone over soft ground. Their craft is passed master to apprentice by hand-speech entirely, and it is said — they let it be said — that a good vent-reader can feel a lie in the ground through the soles of borrowed boots.

And there are the **cobbleback-wards**, the strangest guild to a stranger's eye, and the proudest. The great grazers are not tamed; nothing on the Shelf is tamed; but they are *kept-with*, the way one keeps with a difficult and essential neighbor. The wards learn the belly-language of the cobblebacks well enough to be answered — slowly, in the half-understood pidgin of one animal flashing a pale shimmer at a creature that flashes one back — and their work is partly husbandry, partly diplomacy: coaxing the herds along the flume-lines so the silk gets seeded where it should be, steering them off a thin crust, standing off the steam at a calving, when a grazer drops its low stone-backed calf on the warm ground and the whole town's dogs-of-work, which is to say the wards themselves, form a silent ring against the soarers that come dropping down the thermal columns for the afterbirth. A ward's highest honor is to be *belly-known*: to have flashed back and forth with one particular old cobbleback for so many years that the animal flicks its pale underside at him across four hundred meters not as a message but as a greeting — the same wordless *I see you*.

The animals themselves are the Shelf's other citizens, and the stillfolk treat them as such. The **soarers** nest on the high cone-rims in the held light of the steam pillars, and their wing-glint signaling is so exactly the origin of the human mirror-code that children learning their letters-of-light are taught to read the birds first. The **bead-shrimp** runs in the warm schools are netted by hand in the cool of dusk, and the fishers walk into the runs with their shadows cast carefully behind them, because the shrimp see shadow, and the fishers move like their own shadows' debtors. The **brine midges** — which rise in their smoke-columns at midday and are the Shelf's only abundance, its manna — are swept from the pooling-veils at dawn with soft sinter-cloth sweeps and go into everything: meal, broth, the flat baked wafers the town trades as hardtack down the Lift, dark-flecked and tasting faintly of the heat of the ground. The stillfolk will glint you the joke, when they trust you, that they are a people who eat the smoke and drink the sky and farm the colors, and that none of these phrases is a metaphor.

The **bell-keeper** is the loneliest office. One person, always, who lives beside the glass case with the felt-wrapped bell inside it, and whose duty is to run, when the ground lies, faster than panic down the Main Spring, unwrap the felt, and strike the curse into the air. The town's shelters are sunk into the solid crust at intervals, roofed with sinter and chain, stocked and lamped, and every child drills the descent without being told twice. Bell-keepers tend to serve long terms and talk to almost no one, and it is understood that this is not sadness but *ballast* — someone must carry the town's one loud word in their chest, quiet, for years, so that everyone else can live below the drips.

Strangers come up the Lift noisy, always. It can't be helped; the Dry Countries are a land where you shout across canyons. The stillfolk don't shush them — shushing is itself a sound — they simply appear, one at a time, quietly, in the newcomer's path, and hold up a small polished plate, and let the Shelf teach the lesson its own way: the newcomer opens his mouth to ask, and the whole white horizon swallows the question, and his own voice comes back to him thin and embarrassed, like something spilled. Within three days most strangers are whispering. Within a week, their hands have started talking. The old stillfolk glint among themselves, when a caravan goes back down the Lift loaded with water-jars and midge-wafers, that the trade is not really water for goods at all.

The Dry Countries are buying the water. The Shelf is selling the silence, and shipping the water as a courtesy.

The dead of the Still are not buried — the ground is alive and must not be pierced — but **laid on**: carried to a cooling terrace at the far edge of the settled ground, dressed in white, set on the sinter, and permitted, by the slow patient silica-charged work of the springs and the steam, to be built over. In a few years a stillman's grave is a small white mound, chalk-crisp, indistinguishable from a miniature terrace, warm under the palm. The families visit by glinting the passing sun off a mirror onto the mound — one flash, held — which is the Shelf's whole liturgy in a single gesture: *the light was yours for a while; the ground has taken you back into its quiet; I still see you.*

That is who lives there: a people rehearsed in light, fed on smoke, governed by a bell they hope never to hear, married under mirror-flash, mourned in silica, standing spaced out along their warm watercourses like beads on a wire, in the one town on earth whose name is a command it keeps.
