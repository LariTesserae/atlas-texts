The three facts, then, finish like this: the water moves. Twice a day the whole weight of the drowned plateau leans into the fissure and pours through, and twice a day it drains back out, and the Gullet breathes on that schedule the way you breathe on yours — not because it chooses to, but because not breathing has never been tried. The natives do not call this the tide. They call it the Inhale and the Exhale, and they date everything from it. A person's age is counted in breaths of the Gullet. An argument, a pregnancy, a war — all measured the same way, in how many times the room swallowed while the thing was happening.

Now the people.

They are human, or were, or close enough that the difference is mostly a matter of what you're used to. A few thousand of them, and every one of them is built wrong for the surface and right for here, which is the only direction evolution in the Gullet has ever pushed. They are pale in a specific way — not the pale of milk, the pale of something that has been kept in the dark so long that pigment would be an extravagance. Their eyes are small and quick and nearly useless, good for the glow of the vents and nothing else; the children are born with large dark ones, surface-style, and the large ones shrink year by year, the body reclaiming the investment, until by adulthood each face holds two chips of wet slate that water light can barely be bothered to reach.

The ears do not shrink. The ears are the whole story.

Here is what the roar does to a people: it takes their voices away, and then it gives them something better. No one in the Gullet speaks aloud. There would be no point — you would have to put your mouth against another person's skull and bellow, and even then you'd only reach the ones you were touching. So speech in the Gullet is touch. It is hands on skin, always, one palm flat against the sternum or the temple or the back of the neck, and the speaking hand taps and presses and drums, and the listener's bones carry it in under the roar where the ears alone couldn't. They hold each other constantly, in public, at market, in the middle of quarrels — not out of tenderness, though there is tenderness, but because the alternative to contact is solitude so total that the word for it is the same word they use for dead.

A stranger watching them stand in conversation, palm to chest, palm to jaw, palm pressed over a child's eyes to say *sleep now*, would call it intimacy. They call it talking. It took their ancestors maybe four generations to stop calling it anything and simply be it.

But touch-speech is private, one skin to another. For everything public — law, gossip, market prices, weddings, the naming of children — they use the water itself. Every Gullet citizen learns, from childhood, to strike sound into the flood at the right angle and pitch to make it carry: a knuckle cracked against a bronze post, a stone dropped on a drumhead, and the roar picks it up, folds it into itself, and the whole town hears, because the whole town has spent its life learning to find the parts inside the Bellow. You cannot shout over the bell. You can only speak through the bell. They are the only people alive who live inside a sound and use the sound as their town square, and when the tide slackens — the quarter-hour, twice a day, when the water pauses between Inhale and Exhale and the roar drops to something like a large whisper — the silence falls on them like weather. That is the hour they dread. In the slack water they are all, briefly, deaf in the only sense that matters, cut off from each other and from the room, and the whole town stands still with their hands on each other's chests until the Exhale comes and the Bellow takes them back.

The town itself hangs. That's the honest word for it. The pocket's walls are vertical limestone, scalloped and fluted by a hundred thousand years of the water's arguing, and the people have spent their generations threading it with cables of braided vent-fiber until the cavern holds a suspended lattice of walkways, terraces, and houses like hanging nests — not built from the floor, because the floor is the current, the floor is the whole throat's business, and you do not build your house in a road. The buildings are acoustically tuned, which is the only kind of architecture they have ever valued: a Gullet house is shaped to catch the Bellow and hold a specific note of it, and each family's house hums its own hum, and a native blindfolded (a pointless gesture, but you see the point) can tell which terrace she's on by the chord the roar makes in it. The dead are not buried. They are given to the Exhale — slid gently into the outflow at full surge, so the water carries them through the rest of the fissure and out, over the mountains no one remembers drowning, into the deeper basin that no one has ever seen. The direction of that current is the direction of everything sacred. Their word for the afterlife and their word for downstream are the same word.

The turbine-gates deserve their Hammer, because the gates are the town's one great work and its one heresy. Some ancestor — the stories say a woman who had been to the surface and come back dissatisfied — worked out that the twice-daily surge is not just the water's habit; it is force, enormous force, and force can be milked. The gates are a groined wall of stone and bronze slotted across a side-channel of the throat, with wheels that the Exhale spins, and wheels that the Inhale spins the other way, and the Hammer is the sound of all of it working. The power goes to the vents, or rather to the vent-farms: shelves of mesh lowered near the warm chimneys, seeded with the bacterial mats that live on coins-and-matches water the way surface fields live on sun. The mats are the town's staple. They taste, visitors to the surface have reported with a certain grim honesty, exactly as you would expect iron-champagne porridge to taste, and the Gullet natives, who have never eaten anything else, describe them as tasting of home and mean it without irony.

And there is the catch — the drift. Everything else the town owns, everything it is, arrives from above, twice a day, in the surge. The drowned plateau is shallow and silted and crossed by shipping lanes, and the lanes shed: rope, crates, tarpaulin, the odd boot, tar, timber, and once every few years something so good that the finders tap it out on the bronze posts in a pattern every child knows — *riches, riches, riches, terrace three.* The Gullet's whole material culture is a sorting operation run on the world's garbage. Their finest garments are woven of cable. Their most sacred object, kept in the oldest house and struck only on the greatest occasions, is a ship's bell — a real one, brass, brought down in a chest in somebody's grandfather's grandfather's time — and they find it fitting, they find it almost unbearably fitting, that the surface's own noise has come down to live in the loudest room in the world, where it has to wait its turn like everyone else.

What they are like, then — the people themselves. Patient the way the silt is patient, warm-blooded the way their water is warm, and noisy the way you'd have to be. They are not gentle exactly, but they cannot be abrupt; you cannot slam a hand on a chest without hurting the person, so every sentence the Gullet speaks is spoken with a certain enforced care, and it has made them, over the centuries, a deliberate people. They do not interrupt, because interruption is physically impossible. They do not lie easily, because the hands on your sternum feel the heartbeat accelerate. They hold grudges for a very long time and touch their enemies gently while doing so.

And they do not, in the way that matters most, feel trapped — though a surface-dweller would hear the facts and feel the walls of the fissure closing like hands. Ask one, palm to chest, and the answer comes drumming up through the ribs: the Gullet is not a throat they are stuck in. It is a mouth that has swallowed them deliberately, keeps them warm, feeds them twice a day on schedule, and never once, in all the breaths of all the generations, has failed to finish its ring.
