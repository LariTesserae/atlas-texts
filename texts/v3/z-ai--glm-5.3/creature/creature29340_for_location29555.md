## The People

Nine thousand people live on the Shelf of Kurun, and the Shelf has been working on them the way it works on everything — slowly, from all sides, with water and patience — for longer than anyone can remember. It shows.

They are not a small people, exactly, but they are a *compacted* people. Generations of cold, damp, and walking uphill in fog have produced a body type the lowland physiognomists used to find dour: broad through the chest, thick through the ankle, low through the center of gravity. The people of Kurun do not stride. They plant. There is a local walk — a slightly wide-set, rolling, deliberate gait, knees always a little bent, as though the floor might tilt — that you can spot in a crowd of thousands, and that visitors start doing themselves within a month without ever noticing. The body, like the town, like the mountain, takes the shape of what has been done to it longest.

Their skin has a particular look that the town's own doctors call *fog-derm* and everyone else just calls skin: faintly damp, faintly wind-reddened at the cheekbones, with a quality locals prize and visitors find unsettling, which is that it never looks entirely warm. Hands here are strong and blunt and usually holding something. Nails are kept short, not out of vanity, but because everything on the Shelf — moss, lichen, sphagnum, the soft ancient stone itself — gets under long nails and stays there.

But the truest thing about them is not in the body. It's in the voice.

## The Low Voice

Kurun speech is quiet, and this is not shyness. It is engineering. Sound in permanent fog does something strange: it neither carries nor dies. It hangs. A shout on a foggy street arrives at its listener the way a thrown stone arrives at a window — with a small ugly impact. Everyone raised here learns, usually before they learn to read, that voices are meant to be kept under the fog, not thrown over it. The result is a town where a heated argument sounds like a conference and a conference sounds like a banked fire whispering.

The fog also flattens tone, so the people of Kurun communicate less with volume than with *duration*. A syllable stretched a half-beat longer means emphasis. A silence held two seconds longer than courtesy requires means you have given offense, and a silence held five means you have given serious thought. Visitors think the locals are slow. The locals know they are speaking a language in which the pauses are words.

There is one exception, and it is the church bell. The bell can be loud because the bell is not a person. Person-loudness is a kind of trespass; bell-loudness is weather. Children learn this distinction young and can articulate it by age seven in terms that would satisfy a lawyer.

## The Sedimented Character

Ask anyone in Kurun what the people are like and you will get the same word: *patient*. Ask them to elaborate and you'll get the same elaboration: *we've had to be*. But patience on the Shelf is not the patience of saints, which is a patience of aspiration. It is the patience of stone, which is a patience of results. Nothing here happens quickly — bread rises slowly in the cold kitchens, tanning takes half again the lowland time, a disagreement between two families can run cool and unspoken for forty years and be settled by a marriage nobody explains — and so the entire culture has organized itself around the principle that a thing done slowly is a thing done *properly*, and a thing done quickly is a thing done by someone with somewhere better to be.

This is why the town's industries survive in their ancient over-maintained form. The flume-keepers — a hereditary guild of some eleven families who walk the terrace channels every day of their lives, clearing moss from the sluices with hooks their great-great-grandparents forged — do not consider their work a tradition. They consider it a *current obligation*. The moss is coming. It comes every day. Someone must meet it. The idea that this could be otherwise does not occur to them, any more than the idea of not being rained on occurs to the moss.

They hold grudges the way the stone holds water: deep, structural, and without heat. A Kurun grudge is never expressed. It is *practiced* — through seating arrangements at the tannery bench, through which stall you buy your bread from, through a slight widening of the pause when a certain name is spoken. Outsiders can live in town for a decade and never detect one. The two parties to a forty-year grudge will lend each other rope in a storm. That is understood. The rope is not the grudge.

And they help each other with a matter-of-factness that lowlanders sometimes mistake for coldness. There is no heroism up here because there is no audience for it. When a roof goes in a wind, the street re-roofs it that afternoon, in silence, without being asked, and then goes home. Being thanked for this makes a Kurun person acutely uncomfortable; being thanked *warmly* is almost an insult. You do not thank the tick. You do not thank the rain.

## The Trades

Every person on the Shelf has, in the loose way of old towns, a *trade* and a *temper*, and the two are understood to be connected, the way a knife and its edge are connected. The foundrymen are said to be the quickest to speak and the quickest to forgive. The flume-keepers, as befits a guild whose entire occupation is walking the same path daily for life, are the town's deep-water personalities, slow-moving and hard to read. The moss-cutters — who harvest the cushion meadows for insulation, bedding, and the packing of fragile goods, and who are the only people licensed to cut living moss at all — tend toward a gentle mysticism; theirs is the only trade with a prayer attached to it, a short one, said before the first cut of the day, whose text nobody will repeat to outsiders and whose content everyone cheerfully denies is religious.

The tanners live downwind, which is to say they live in the one quarter of town where the prevailing damp is overlaid with a different atmosphere entirely, and they have developed, over generations, both the famous Kurun stoicism and its one acknowledged expression: a long, low, beautiful style of profanity, deployable at length, which the rest of the town regards with something close to envy.

And there is the bell-keeper. Always one, always an old man or an old woman, always elected by no process anyone can describe. The bell-keeper rings the hour and also rings what the town needs ringing for — fog-dense births, fog-dense deaths, the twice-yearly shutting-down of the generator hall for maintenance. It is said the bell-keeper always knows, before being told, who has died. It is said this is because the thrum of the generators changes, very slightly, when the town grieves. Nobody has ever tested this. Nobody wants to.

## The Children and the Mirror

Children on the Shelf are raised inside a fence of small superstitions, all of them functional and none of them frightening, because the Shelf does not do frightening; it does *solemn*. Don't shout near the Mirror, or the sky will fall out. Don't whistle in the fog, or you'll call the fog *closer*. Don't count the frogs' notes aloud, or the evening won't know when to end. Don't go past the rim after the third bell.

The children, for their part, grow up half-wild in the moss meadows, damp to the knees, telling each other stories about the winder with the enormous relish of children who live in a place where the real dangers are hypothermia and boredom and the imaginary danger is far more interesting. Every child has a theory about the winder. Every child has gone to the rim at least once at night, against the rules, and listened. Most of them will tell you — years later, as adults, with a completely straight face — that they heard it. Statistically, most of them did not. The lying is not really lying. It is a citizenship requirement.

## The Old and the Finished Doctrine

To grow old in Kurun is to become, by the town's quiet universal agreement, something between a person and a landscape. The old are not venerated in any ceremonial way — ceremony, like loudness, trespasses. They are simply *consulted*, at the pace of the place: found at their doors in the afternoon, asked a question, answered slowly, thanked briefly, left alone. The old of Kurun sit outside on cushion-moss chairs under the lee walls when the tick is on, hands flat on their knees, looking at nothing with tremendous attention. They have, most of them, stopped doing almost everything. The doctrine of the finished mountain explains this too, and dignifies it: the Shelf is done. It is resting. To be old here is not to be diminished. It is to be, at last, in agreement with the plateau.

They are the ones who say, when a young person leaves for the lowlands — and some do, every generation, blinking down the terrace stairs into the world of noise — "They'll come back or they won't. The mountain did its work. Theirs isn't done yet." This is the closest the people of Kurun come to grief, and the closest they come to blessing, and it is not always clear, even to the speaker, which is which.

## The Dead

The dead of Kurun are buried in the stone itself, in excavation-chambers off the erosion terraces, because there is no soil worth the name and because the town has never seen a reason to import what it can carve. The graves face the fog on the eastern rim. The gravestones are soft-ish stone, and they erode, and the inscriptions go the way of everything here — slowly legible, slowly illegible, finally moss. The town considers this proper. Names, like mountains, are finished eventually. The damp keeps the memory. The thrum keeps the time. The rest is held deep and structurally, the way the old stone holds water, grudges, and everything else that has ever been given to it — which is to say: forever, quietly, and without complaint.
