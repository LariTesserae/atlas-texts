Ask the Shelf who lives here and it will answer the way it does everything: eventually. But begin with a difficulty, because the categories you brought with you — plant, animal, building, tenant — arrive and find no work. Life on the Shelf runs along a single spectrum from standing to walking, and everything alive is somewhere on it. The fan-plants stand but turn all day. The pearls walk but might as well be stones with opinions. The town does not shelter its residents so much as consist of them. And counting individuals helps even less: a colony of grazers behaves as one animal wearing many bodies, and a cloud of fliers is less a swarm than a single gregarious creature that never learned to be alone. You will not meet an individual on the Shelf. You will meet fragments of one.

## The pearls who read with their feet

Fat, grey, soft-shelled, and rounder than anything that walks has a right to be. They do not walk so much as consider walking, one body-width at a time, rolling along the stalk-ropes on broad foot-pads. Nothing here bites. Nothing chews in haste. The pearls have no mouths at all, and have grown a town without them — no mouth ever chewed a wall here, and no mouth ever said a word.

They know the world through their feet the way you know a thing by tasting it, except for them there is no *except*: to stand on something is to taste it. When one rolls onto your open hand, it is not being friendly. It is reading you — your warmth, the drumming of your pulse (through a foot-pad, your heartbeat must be the loudest event in the Shelf's short history), and some flavor of you that has no precedent here. Then it files you and rolls on, unalarmed. Nothing on the Shelf has ever needed to hurry away from anything.

Their traffic is the town's chisel. Where the pearls habitually walk, the grown floor polishes smooth. Where they habitually pause, secretions pool and harden into wall. The settlement is their routine, mineralized — the standing map of every preference several generations have ever had. You are walking through their habits. The habits just happen to have doorways.

## The tall ones who keep the light

Six-legged, thin as reeds, glass-eyed, tall enough to rest a head inside a fan-plant and leave it there for a working day. Raising that head takes an hour; you could clock your visit by it. If the Shelf has a unit of time, it is the head-lift.

Their patience in the fans doesn't look like feeding. It looks like attendance. The fan-plant turns a fraction, clicks, settles; the browser is there for it; perhaps that is how the grove keeps its clock — sundials with legs. Whether the fans gain something from the visit, no one has stayed long enough to learn; on the Shelf, experiments take years.

On the dark side of the arc, their clear heads catch the amber underglow and shine faintly — the only streetlamps ever grown. Their glassy eyes will see you, but at their frame-rate your whole visit is a flicker. The image resolves slowly, and by the time it does, you have been gone for a while. They will remember you the way weather remembers a bird: something that crossed, quickly.

## The dust that says now

Seed-small, and the fastest citizens the Shelf has, moving at what any other world would call a stall. They land in agreement — hundreds at once on one plant — and the plant visibly leans, and the lean tips a little more sap down the stem, and downstream something tastes the tilt. This is as close as the place comes to a nervous system: the fliers are its sparks, the only part of the whole plateau that can say *now*. At the day's turn they land everywhere at once, and the entire grove settles a fraction under their combined weight. That is nightfall here. That is the Shelf tucking itself in.

## The hidden trades

Some residents you will never see, only live with the results of their work: the borers, soft-shelled, heads like patient augers, who spend a lifetime making one corridor — every tube between the chambers is somebody's biography — and the pale threads down in the sap-age drains, the town's entire plumbing department, blind, busy, keeping the old sap moving.

Because old sap does what old sap does: darkens, thickens, sets into the mineral of the walls. The town's oldest quarters are its darkest ones. Age, here, is a color; the settlement's floor plan is its own chronology; run a hand along a corridor wall and you are reading strata.

## The ones who became rooms

It goes further than that. The chambers are not so much *inhabited* as they are former residents. A pearl walks for a lifetime, and then one season it leans against a wall and does not go on, and the wall grows around it, and in a few years there is no telling the dweller from the dwelling. This is not quite death. Call it a change of occupation. Some of the gourd-chambers are elders who spent their second lives climbing toward the light, which is why the high rooms are the brightest ones.

If the town has a capital, it is a single fan-plant near its center: the eldest living thing on the Shelf, a spore in the buried soil that rose with the rock and opened, first of everything, into daylight nothing was ready for. The browsers queue at it. At dusk the fliers land on it before anywhere else. Its settling click is probably the local heartbeat.

Death exists — the rim takes a few: a drifting cloud that hangs a moment past the lip in the thin air, a pearl rolling past the last smooth path — but the dead are not removed. They are continued: grown over, included, made load-bearing. And notice what is missing. Nothing flees. Nothing here has learned to be eaten yet. Fear has not been invented, and you can walk through a thousand residents without causing anything but weather.

## What they came out of

Where did they all come from, on a rock lifted yesterday? Up. Inside. The Shelf was the old world's underside, and when it rose it carried its buried things with it — tubers, eggs, spores, a full passenger list resident in the soil that used to be down and is now skin. The first generation was born underground and arrived by elevation: daylight delivered before anyone was ready, which is one reason everything here still moves like something catching up.

It also explains their devotion to the young rock's warmth. A colony pressing its flank to warm stone may be performing the closest thing it has to memory — the under, the before, the old warm dark. When you lay your hand flat on the rock and feel that faint deep heat, you are touching what they think of as home.

## The one they are becoming

And now the possibility the place has been edging toward all along: that the town, the groves, the pearls, and the dust are not a community but a body — one continuous slow thing, still assembling, a being the size of a plateau whose blood is amber, whose skin is the grove, whose thoughts travel as weight and tilt, whose first thought is still in progress at the speed of a tide that takes all day to come in. No tools, no devices; every artifact here is a body's decision about where to stand. Its one long engineering project, generations underway, is a lip growing along the southern rim — a finger-width of banister per season, a hand slowly cupping itself around its own contents. At current rates it will never be finished. Or it will be finished eventually, which on the Shelf is the same word.

Which leaves you: the fastest, loudest, wettest thing ever to happen here, a body made substantially of the one substance the Shelf does not have. Every foot-pad that read you tasted that wrongness, that flavor with no precedent. They will remember your visit the way a place remembers a storm — as a layer. Somewhere in a wall, laid down this season, there will run a stratum slightly off in color, faintly strange to whatever passes along it for a tongue: the day the weather walked through, breathing.
