What lives here is not many things. That is the description's final correction, and it took the longest to arrive.

You come down the slope reading the plateau as a community: cushion plants, rosettes, combs, tanks — a flora, a census of species, dozens of small citizens going about their small cold-adapted lives. But that read doesn't survive a week on the benches. A community argues. A community has edges, has members that die while their neighbors persist, has gaps between bodies. This has none. Every root is felted into every other root. Every tank is plumbed into every tank. Put your hand on a stalk on the north rim and the tingle is the same one you feel two kilometers away on the south — the same grid, the same faint electrical murmur, the same teeth-ache pitch. What lives here is one tenant. The whole gray-green basin is a single organism that has, over tens of thousands of years, occupied its own ground so completely that it became the ground.

It is easiest to say what it is not. It is not a forest — a forest is a crowd, and this is a body. It is not a moor — a moor is ground with plants on it, and here there is no *on*. It is not a colony in the way corals are a colony, because coral heads can be counted, and this cannot; count what? The benches? They are its skeleton, terraced and healed the way bone remodels. The tanks are its stomachs. The combs are its lungs turned inside-out — it does not breathe the air so much as drink it, combing the wind the way a whale strains the sea, and the fog exits thin because it has passed through a feeding apparatus several kilometers across.

So: a body, a landlord, a vast cold-blooded thing that has never moved and never needed to. But *cold-blooded* is wrong too, because the cold is not something it endures — it is something it *eats*. Every dawn and dusk it runs a heat engine with itself as the cylinder: ground-mild against air-freezing, sap as working fluid, night as condenser. The morning plumes of vapor are its exhalation, and the colder the dawn, the harder it breathes. It is the only large animal ever to farm the weather rather than flee it.

And it is busy in a way bodies usually aren't. Healthy bodies settle; they run their metabolism at maintenance and stop. This one does not stop. It builds — constantly, restlessly — and worse, it builds *prototypes*: the hollow dome over a tank, the two-meter spiral flue, the woven arch spanning a rill, forms that appear once, nowhere else, and are gone within a week, eaten back into itself. It is hard not to call this thinking. It is harder still to keep calling it thinking, because the thinking doesn't accumulate. Nothing is written down. The dome is never built again; the flue is never improved. It invents like a mind and forgets like a wound, and its forgetting is so total that it has no ruins, no middens, no memory at all — a genius with a forehead of slate, wiped each week, beginning again.

Which is what the fauna-absence finally means. The mites in the thousandth tank, the single soundless bird at dusk — these are visitors, or tenants too small to matter, or possibly organs that have shrunk to the size of rumors. Nothing grazes because grazing is theft from a system that tolerates no leakage. Nothing burrows because burrowing is trespass in its own flesh. Nothing has ever walked here because a walker is a wound, and this is a body that has learned, over geological time, to have no wounds. The silence — the anechoic, textured, pressing silence, broken only by a falling water-drop landing like thunder — that silence is the sound of it working. Most large working things roar. This one purrs below hearing, in your sternum rather than your ears, because everything it does, it does with growth and osmosis and the slow arithmetic of freezing water. It has no fast parts. It has no loud parts. It is the largest quiet thing on the slope of the sky.

Perhaps the truest description is the plainest one, though it sounds like a metaphor and isn't: what lives on this plateau is an engineer that has never been born. No hands have ever been here, and nothing here needed them. It quarries its own stone with ice-wedges, refines its own silica until its stems ring like porcelain, packages its own freight in wax, runs its own conveyor belts on nothing but dew and gravity, and twice a day it changes shift without any workers ever having arrived or left. It is the oldest thing on the ridge and the youngest thing on it at once — bones tens of thousands of years settled, flesh minutes old — and it will go on like this, combing the clouds, forgetting its inventions, hoarding ice-cold tannin-dark water against a rain that almost never comes, until the fog stops coming up the slope.

That is the one thing it cannot mine from the air: more air. Everything else it takes.
