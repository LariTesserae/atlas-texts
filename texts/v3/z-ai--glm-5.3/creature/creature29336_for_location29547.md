**The keepers.** Start with them, because they would insist on it, though they are the minority. Eleven stations, and in each a crew of three to nine — a ratio set not by the Board's charter but by the bunks. The keeper's trade is taught as apprenticeship and inherited as temperament. You can spot a keeper by the forearms (dredge-rope work leaves them knotted like the reed-roots) and by the way they stand on open water: feet apart, weight low, eyes not on the horizon but on the surface a boat-length out, reading the film for silt. They keep the ledgers the way the plateau keeps its ripple prints — as proof that something has been happening for a long time. Every shift's entry is dated, and the dates are counted from the Inundation, so that a keeper filling a line at the Slot writes "Year 41, stormday, water leaning north" and means by it exactly what her grandmother would have meant. The first line of the ledger is copied out by hand into each new book, and each new keeper's first duty is to copy it again in her own hand, so that the sentence *the plain will not be drained; the plain will be kept* exists in the archive forty-some times in forty-some handwritings, which is the Board's real theology stated in its only scripture.

They are not dour, though visitors expect them to be. Pump work is loud and endless, and people who live with an endless noise either go quiet or get funny, and the keepers got funny. The humor is dry and specific and mostly about the weather, which they officially refuse to call weather. A keeper describing a storm will say "we had some ground tone last night."

**The fleet-folk.** The larger population, and the older claim. They live on the reed islands themselves — the big mats, the hundred-meter ones, which hold six or eight dwellings each, dwellings woven and patched from the reed itself so that over the years the house and its foundation slowly become one material and nobody can say where the raft ends and the roof begins. A fleet-folk village has no location. It has a *history of locations*, recited like genealogy: this mat spent the spring leaning against the escarpment, wintered in the drowned orchards off Broadlaw, broke in three in the big blow of Year 38 and is now three villages, all on speaking terms.

The fleet-folk are the ones who read the lean of the water and know it the way farmers once knew soil. They harvest in the sun-hours — reed for weaving and for the charcoal the stations buy, the aquatic meadow-grass for the goats (yes, goats; a fleet-folk raft carries its own small monarchy of animals, hens in wicker coops slung under the walkways, one or two goats who live better than most humans and know it). They fish the drowned streets of Broadlaw, and they fish them carefully, and here is a thing visitors take years to understand: they fish with restraint. Not from scarcity of law — there is no law — but from an understanding so old it has become instinct, that the village beneath them is a *room in their house*, a room with a ceiling of water and green light, and the fish spawning in the church aisles are not a harvest so much as a dividend on a capital they intend never to touch. A fleet-folk elder asked about this will not explain it. She will say the fish were there before the flood and will be there after, and then she will change the subject to your boots, which are wrong for the boat.

**The children.** Every human on the Harrow learns to swim before walking is fully reliable, and every child learns two geographies in parallel, the way a bilingual child learns two words for everything. There is the wet map — currents, fleets, the stations, the Slot — and there is the dry map, the one below, inherited and memorized before the child can read: which pale line is the old road to Broadlaw, which dark seam is the parish boundary, where the drowned orchard is, where the stone bridge is, *don't dive there, the arch is lower than it looks*. Children dive anyway. The adults know they dive and pretend not to, having done the same. The game of the shallow generation is called *touching*, and the object is to be the first to put a hand on something old — a field gate, a milestone, a doorstep — and hold it for a full breath. The children come up with mud on their palms looking like knights.

**What they share.** The bell of Broadlaw. It is the one thing the keepers and fleet-folk hold in common that neither owns. A storm rings it; debris works the tower and the bell speaks, and by its direction everyone within two kilometers knows the wind. But it also rings for the weddings — a keeper or two rows out to the tower in a calm window with a hammer, and rings the hour for a fleet-folk marriage, because there is no church anymore, only the church's drowned nave, and a bell's voice carries over water in a way that makes it everyone's.

And the Sundays. Seiche permitting. On Sundays, when the water is level and the sun-hours can be trusted for at least one of them, the boats come out from every station and every big mat at once and raft up in the wide shallow south of Broadlaw, over the old fields, and there is a market — fish and reed-charcoal, tools, bread baked on the stations' pump-waste heat, children trading treasures raised from below, the mayflies rising through it all in their columns. It is the loudest hour on a loud plateau and no one minds. They hold it on the drowned land on purpose. Ask why and you get the answer the whole Harrow gives to every version of that question, in the keeper's dry way or the fleet-folk's oblique one, but always the same answer:

*It isn't lost. It's under.*
