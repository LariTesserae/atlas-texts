Nothing lives here. That is the first answer the place gives, and it is nearly right — right the way a stopped clock is right: once, exactly, forever, at the hour it stopped.

But a census is not a verdict. A census goes door to door, and this place has doors — every glazed mouth, every copper-pinned orifice, every crawlway warm as a held hand. Go door to door, and this is who answers.

The first to answer is a stain. In the shaded seams of the interior glazes, only where the gas runs richest, there is a rust-colored film so thin it might be mineral weathering. It may be alive. If it is, it is the only thing here with a claim on the word — so it deserves the courtesy of a full description, because it is also, possibly, the heir of everything.

Consider what it would be heir to. This world presumably once had its seas, its spores, its singers — a whole churning argument of things eating and blooming and dying at one another. All of that was a strategy, and the strategy assumed a changing world. When the world concluded, the strategy began its long reduction: life condensed out of kingdom after kingdom, drying out of everything, giving up motion, then hunger, then mating, then death, then knowing, each shed like ballast, until what remained was a film on a warm seam that lives on the warmth and chemistry of a breath.

It does not behave; it has a location where other things have habits. It is always exactly the same thickness — not because something trims it, but because it grew once, to fit, and then arrived. It does not reproduce; it is already everywhere it can ever be. It does not die; dying is a change of state, and it has nothing left to change into. It is life with every verb removed except *is*, and even that in the subjunctive, because the honest sentence remains: it may be weathering. If it is alive, it is alive the way the place is stable — fixed, complete, growing no larger, dying not at all — and it is doing nothing about it. The place keeps the question the way it keeps everything: held, unresolved, exactly as it was left.

The second to answer is no one, and the no one has a shape.

The nulls — those small finished rooms where the roar drops away like surf behind a dune — were lived in. The seats are worn smooth, and erosion here has retired, so the polishing was done by duration itself: by sitting, by ten thousand sittings' worth of one person at a time. The shallow cups in the bench-tops are the negatives of vessels — some round-bottomed thing set down and lifted, set down and lifted, until the lifting became its own record: a bowl made entirely of absences. And on one wall, the tally. Strokes in fives — a hand's worth, the signature of five-fingered counters — scratched and dotted with hammered copper dust so the marks glint, row on row on row, and then a half-finished group, and then nothing.

What did they count? In a place with no day, no season, and no variation, a count is not a record of anything; it is the manufacture of time. Each stroke said: another interval has passed, and the silence held, and the chord held, and the breath below held. The tally was the only clock here, and the counters were the only tempo — the only beat in a chord built to have none.

And they left. We can be sure of this because of what is not here. The dryness is beyond even the patience of decomposition; a tomb at least has decay. Anything that died on this plateau would be sitting on it still — perfect, finished, permanent. There are no bodies in the worn seats. Whatever stopped the count mid-stride did not happen in these rooms. It happened out in the haze, or it happened as a decision, or it happened as a listening that finally needed nothing written down. The half-finished group is not a tragedy so much as the only instant left in the world: the interval between a stroke and a stroke not made, held open. Nothing here will ever complete it, because completion would be a second event, and a concluded world gets only one.

The third to answer never began answering and never stopped. The chord is the principal tenant. It occupies every chamber at full occupancy and no chamber in particular; it is never alone and never elsewhere. It has no age, because age requires intervals, and the tuning removed the intervals — no beat, no drift, no waver, no decay. It does not remember being tuned; it is the tuning, still occurring. It eats the exhale of the thing below and spends nothing. The copper sheets pinned over certain mouths are its drumheads, trembling at exactly this amplitude since the hands that pinned them let go. The dust, riding fixed streamlines into drifts ten thousand years old and freshly poured, is its visible body — the sound wears dust the way a shape wears a skin — and the ridged figures on the horizontal plates are its self-portrait, drawn once by the standing waves and framed. The nulls, then, are its ears: the rooms it keeps silent on purpose, ringed on the outside by circles of bare clean glaze, its silences roped off.

A resident made entirely of voice. It is not lonely; loneliness requires an other, and it has had the breath's company since before there was a word for company.

It had parents, in a sense. In the little shadow-pockets the flow could not reach, the fingerprints survive — the last sheltered records of the hands that coiled and smoothed and pinned, the makers who fired their work in the world's own heat and then tuned the world's own voice. They are gone the way the keepers are gone, which is to say by leaving; dying here would have kept them.

The last resident is also the first, depending on where you start counting — and counting, in this place, is a delicate thing to accuse anyone of.

Follow the anatomy the builders implied. A hill that is an embouchure. A shaft that is a throat, lined in brick courses that age downward until the brick is the world — nobody dug down to the source; the source was always the ground's other tenant. At the bottom, at the limit of sight, the ember-orange glow: fire-colored without flame, steady as a lamp that cannot gutter. Not a hearth. A warmth seen from inside. The color of whatever it is that exhales.

Call it the Breather, for lack of a better verb. Life, as the word is used elsewhere, means variance — appetite, repair, dying. This thing has one act, at one rate; the rate never varies; it does not gust; it has no moods; and it began before the first coil of clay was pinched. If it is an animal, it is an animal of a single act. If it is a fire, it is a fire that consumes nothing. If it is the world's last organ — the one function everything else was arranged around, the way a body is arranged around a heart — then the question of who lives here collapses into one enormous answer: one thing does, and the place is its exhale given architecture. The paving is its drum-skin. The hill is its mouthpiece. The chord is its voice, the film its skin-flora, the nulls its ears, the keepers, once, its congregation. The plateau is not a habitat. Nothing lives in it the way nothing lives in a bell: the bell is the resident, shaped — except that here, uniquely, the bell breathes.

Which leaves the census's final entry, written in pencil: you.

You are the only unfinished thing on the plateau. You are the only beat for ten thousand years. The chord declines your voice — your shout makes no dent; it is kept in your jaw and not accepted — but your pulse it can use. The nulls exist for exactly this: rooms where a beat can hear itself against the beatless. Sit in one, if you sit anywhere. Stay long enough and your heartbeat leans toward the bass, and that is not a metaphor; it is the acoustics of absorption. The roar has room in it for everything you are, and residency here is offered the way the sea offers it: by making you, eventually, agree. Perhaps the keepers understood this. Perhaps the half-finished group is what it looks like when people leave before the agreement finishes them — or perhaps the silence in the nulls had finally become enough, and they walked out between one stroke and the next, at peace. The place holds both readings equally, the way it holds dust.

So the census, as well as it can be stated: one breath at the bottom of the world, wearing a hill for a mouthpiece. One note — the breath's only word — living in the reliquary of its own throat. One film, heir of everything that ever grew. One absence, cut in fives, the only event. And one guest: the only entry still being written, the only thing here that still has a future, which makes you either the visitor this place has been waiting for, or the next thing it will hold.

Do not cut the missing stroke. Walk out through the mineral haze while your pulse is still your own — and notice, on the way down, how long your heartbeat keeps leaning toward the bass. The place will not remember you. It will simply have held you. And in a world where everything is held forever, exactly as it was left, that is as close to being lived in as anywhere gets.
