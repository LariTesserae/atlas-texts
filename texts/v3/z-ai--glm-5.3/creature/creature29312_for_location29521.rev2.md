# What Lives There

The first honest answer is: nothing. And it is wrong.

There are no animals here — this is true, and it is the most misleading true thing about the Greenhowl. Something lives here the way a sentence lives in a mouth, or the way a fire lives in a log. The question is not what lives in the Greenhowl. The question is what the Greenhowl is living *as*.

## The citizens, in the ordinary sense

Begin with the gourds. If anything here is a creature, the gourd-dwellings are: each one a swollen cottage-sized body with a door-lip and a warm hollow interior, and each one, at some point in its growth, does something no gourd should do. It waits. A gourd will reach full size, achieve perfect ripeness, every biological incentive screaming at it to fall and rot and seed — and it will pause. Days. Weeks. It hangs in that pause the way an animal hangs in attention, and if there were anyone to watch, they would notice the pause always ends the same way: the gourd falls *toward* wherever the next street is going to be. Not where water runs. Not where light falls. Where the city intends to go next.

The gourds die in their doorways, in a sense. Or they don't — it's honestly impossible to tell whether a gourd-dwelling is a house, a corpse, or an egg. The Greenhowl does not distinguish these categories, and its refusal to distinguish them is the closest thing it has to an ethics.

## The ones that remember

In the collapsed districts, where time is held like a badly healed break, live the oldest residents — which is strange, because they are also the youngest. The colonizing sprouts that fill the ruins' exact footprints are chronologically new, but they grow *into shape*, rushing through their first weeks as though catching up to a pattern already agreed to. Fresh sap and deep rot in the same wound. A sprout with the demeanor of something returning.

Here is the thing nobody (no one, remember — no one) would be able to miss if there were a no-one here: the sprouts in the ruins behave like survivors. They grow warily. They brace against collapses that haven't happened yet in this cycle of the ruin, and have happened in every previous one. They are born mid-sentence, mid-argument, mid-city. Whatever lives in the Greenhowl, it has been living here longer than its own age — it remembers forward as much as back, and it cannot always tell which it is doing.

## The seed-towers

The seed-towers are the closest thing to individuals with names, and their names would be their collapses. Each tower spends its whole life — a few years, sometimes less — growing singular, unmistakable, *self*, and then falls over in a single night with a sound like a hill dying, hurling its pollen across whole quarters in a warm blizzard.

Every disaster is a wedding, and the towers are the grooms. They die once and marry the entire district. The pollen drifts settle into soft banks that glow faintly all night, and if a person stood in one — no person stands in one — they would feel a low warmth, the metabolic heat of a thousand futures being considered at once. The pollen is not carrying genetic instructions so much as *debate*: this plaza or that one, grow now or grow toward. The Greenhowl's children are conceived as arguments, and they are born arguing, which is why the roar never stops.

## The one that is all of them

And then there is the possibility that all of this is one organism — that the Greenhowl is not a city containing something but a something attempting the shape of a city, the way a hermit crab attempts the shape of a shell, except the crab is the size of a basin and the shell is made of its own future bodies.

Consider the evidence. The subsonic churn in the root-floor, felt in the sternum, indistinguishable from a heartbeat. The upward condensation, the drops rising off the floor and hanging in the air like the beginning of rain played backward — which is, if you take it seriously, the Greenhowl trying out *in-breathing*, one small experiment, a few handspans of it, every so often, before the argument drowns it out. The mutter in the pressure-changes, not speech but what speech would be if speech were a plant's idea, heard through a membrane wall. The stems that grow toward absences shaped like future buildings — an intention preceding its own content, which is the precise definition of a mind, or at least of the moment before one.

The plants build toward intentions that do not exist yet, and then the intentions arrive to meet them. Whatever lives here has discovered a way to want. It has no eyes, no hands, no mouth, no name for itself. It has a hundred thousand creaking pillars and a clock made of falling water and a hundred years of practice, and every night, when the drops hang in the air and the roar drops into that low muttering register, it comes a little closer to saying something.

What lives in the Greenhowl is a thought. A very large, very slow, very young thought — a century old, the whole basin still unfinished, urgent, weedy, half-formed — trying with all its vegetable force to become the kind of thing that could hear itself. The city is not the thought's body. The city is the thought's *rehearsal*.

And the silence, if it ever comes — the moment the argument ends and the roar stops — that will not be the thought's death. That will be the instant before it speaks. This is why the Greenhowl shows no interest in arriving. Arriving would be easy now. It has learned how to hold itself at the exact instant of becoming, and it is stalling, gloriously, deafeningly, because it suspects — the way a gourd hanging in its pause suspects — that what comes after becoming is *answering*, and there is no one here to answer it.

Yet. The empty plazas that will clearly be full are waiting too.
