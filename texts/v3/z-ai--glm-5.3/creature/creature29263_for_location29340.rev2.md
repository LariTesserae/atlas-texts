What lives here is everything that stopped needing the sun and then kept living anyway — and went on living until living became an argument so long-settled it turned into music.

Begin with the grazers, because they are the country's foundation and its voice. The blind snails of the white mats are small, mirror-shelled, packed shoulder to shoulder like coins in a bag, and they are the most numerous historians in the dark. A snail here is mostly mouth — a radula like a conveyor of tiny files — and its life is a single decision made over and over: rasp, advance, rasp. Ten thousand of them on one seep sound like rain on a tin roof heard from underwater; a hundred thousand sound like the country clearing its throat forever. What is remarkable about them is not the animal but the record. Every herd returns to the same strip of mat at the same hour of the Breath, and the hour of the Breath is faithful, and so the appetite of the herd has cut grooves into solid limestone a thumb's depth deep — roads with no road-maker, no surveyor, no intention, just hunger repeated until the stone gave way. An old snail-road, seen end-on, is the most moving thing in the basin: a page of the country's mind, written in the only direction it can write, which is backward, into the floor.

The eel-things of the river are its congregation. They stand in the current in ranks, faces upstream, and they do not swim so much as hold — pale, fin-folded, faces made entirely of drift-sampling, jaws working the water the way the snails work the mats. When the river folds itself into its sump and vanishes, they do not panic, because the river has been doing this since before their line had a name for panic. They press into the groove, fold into the wet dark after it, and wait in the standing water for the punctual world to resume. Their calendar is the river's calendar. Their moods, if they have any, are hydraulic.

Up the walls live the clingers, the shag of the ceiling, the tenants your hand rests on a hundred at a time. Most are flat the way a rumor is flat — flattened by the logic of a world where every surface is a floor and no direction is privileged. The wall-hoppers among them have taken the constant sound and turned it into a body: hard ridges along their backs drawn across file-ribs on their flanks, so that a whole cliff-face stridulates when the air moves it, an instrument that plays itself, and each species holds a different note, so that a walk along a wall is a walk along a chord change.

And over the warm seeps, the swarms — the ones that make the air feel like fur. They are winged, though the word does odd work here; they swim the dark more than they fly it, steering by the warmth of the mats and the warmth of each other, thickening over the sulfur lawns until the thermals above a seep look like smoke that has decided to stay. A warm body moving through them is a moving hole in their world, and they curve around it the way water curves around a stone, aware of you the way you are aware of a dropped glass — instantly, and all at once, and without understanding.

In the pools, the snappers — small, quick, and the closest thing this country has to priests, because they hold its only sky. A snapper's claw closes so fast the water tears behind it, and the tear heals with a fleck of cold blue light, one syllable of a sky delivered a stroke at a time. In the black galleries the pools twinkle with them, slow lightning in a country that has never once seen the fast kind. Whether the light means anything to the snappers is a question the country has not asked; it is enough that it is true, and that the dark pools keep faith with it.

Around the Ghost Flats, the congregation of the sighted: everything that kept its eyes, moored to the fungal light like small boats in a green-white harbor. They are the exiles of a different future — the ones whose line held onto vision long enough to arrive here and find exactly one wall's worth of use for it. They cluster, they watch, they orient, and their watching is so dense and so constant that the glow has become, functionally, this world's moon: a tide of eyes pulled twice a Breath toward a sheet of luminous rot.

The Listener lives in all of this and owns none of it. Long-limbed, dish-eared, walking the walls where the floor would betray it, it is the country's held breath given a body. It has never been seen moving by anything that survived the seeing, which means its description is a description of its absence: the hush-front, the cold wind with no wind in it, the border of quiet that travels through the roar and then departs, and the roar returns gallery by gallery like rain finding its roof. Its prey have spent epochs answering it — echo-eating fur, shrieks that jam, pool-colonies that clench into a wall of crackle so total the Listener's own silence becomes its enemy — and the treaty that has come down from all that honing is stable now, old now, part of the Hum like everything else.

And the drummers. In the Amphitheater, heavy knuckle-boomed things strike the same black-polished knobs of limestone generation after generation, ten thousand generations deep, and the stones have taken the shining. It is the only possession in the country that is inherited rather than eaten, the only act that resembles making, and it made nothing — it only wore the same stones with the same blows until they sang and shone. When the chorus is at full tide and the Amphitheater gathers every voice in the region into its bowl, the drumming is felt rather than heard underneath it, a claim laid on a stone by a body that will be replaced, and replaced, and replaced, and the stone does not mind.

Above all of them, maybe, the unconfirmed one: the too-long silences, the air displaced like a door opened somewhere in the dark, the footsteps arriving through the floor at intervals too slow to be walking. The country resumes afterward, carefully, the way one resumes speaking after a funeral. Everything here is old past arguing, but that presence is the one argument still open, and the country keeps it like a held card, face down.

What lives here, then, is not a set of species so much as a single long settlement — an economy of rasping, stridulating, snapping, drumming bodies that never built anything because wearing was always enough. No tools, no artifacts, no made things; the strategies are anatomy, the weapons are behavior, the architecture is appetite. They keep time by pressure, nest at the fog-line, breathe each other's spores at high tide, and when the country's Hum moves everything at once, they move — punctual, blind, polished by each other — through a country that has no name for itself because a name, here, would have to be shouted continuously by everyone forever.

Which, of course, is exactly what they are doing.
