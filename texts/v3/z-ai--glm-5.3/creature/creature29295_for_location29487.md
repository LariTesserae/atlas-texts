So who lives here? Not visitors, not strays who wandered in and stayed — that was the founding generation. What lives here now is native-born. This is the thing you must understand about the underfloor: it is eleven months old and already has an endemic fauna. Everything below the hatch belongs to the hatch the way a tide pool belongs to its rock, and the residents know the geometry of their world with a completeness no surface animal ever manages, because their world has an edge, a lid, a floor, and a law.

Begin, as everything here begins, with the slick. The films are the grass of this nation, the turf, the soil. Yeast arrived on the breath of the fermenters above, riding the same condensate that flavors everything, and it found gravel and warmth and gin-sweet water and stayed. The films are thin — a wet sheen, a pinkish weal at the drain's lip, a gray gloss on the lagging where a spill soaked in years of food into canvas — but they are the base of the entire food web, and they make the underfloor the only landscape you will ever crawl through where the pasture outnumbers the animals by weight and yet is visible only as a shine. The springtails graze on it, and the mites graze on the springtails' leavings, and everything else grazes on the springtails. Smoke poured along the ground — that is the honest description. You put your hand down and a drift parts around your fingers like a herd around a rock, and rejoins behind you, and the whisper of them is the true sound of the deep interior, drier than the drip-percussion, softer than crickets, like paper remembering it was once a living thing.

The crickets are the songbirds and the town criers both. They sing from the warm margins — never the hot steel, never the cold shadow, but the exact temperate band between, the shore between fire and night — and their ticking is the room's own voice, the sound it makes when no one is doing anything to it. They have learned the tide. This cannot be overstated: a cricket in the underfloor keeps time by the sump pump. When the pump swallows and the trench-river falls, the drinking margins widen, wet gravel goes damp, damp goes sweet, and the crickets come down to the canyon edge in numbers, and they do this on schedule, at the same point in the cycle, every cycle, all their short lives. Somewhere in eleven months, some cricket — some one individual, since dead — happened to be at the trench when the water fell, drank, survived a little better, sang a little longer. Every cricket ticking in the margins now is that cricket's inheritor.

The moths live whole lives inside a legend they cannot conceptualize. The caterpillars know only the malt dust under the mill joints: a floury snowfall, warm, endless, the richest nursery in the building. The adults hatch into a dim gold world where all water is gin and all light is either a crawling lozenge on the floor or the built-in moon of the control-room glass block, and they orbit it, and they drink from the puddle rims, and some of them — you can see it, you can watch it happen — become visibly, measurably drunk. A moth wobbling on the rim of a puddle that smells faintly of juniper is the underfloor's most complete citizen: born of the plant, fed by the plant, watered by the plant, impaired by the plant. The cellar spiders in the pipe crotches take them at the end of it, in ruin-webs already gray with dust and wings, and this too is the plant, the whole loop closed and running, three meters beneath the people who think the building is theirs.

The gecko on pipe six is the vertebrate aristocracy. It chose the hottest territory in the nation — the main steam line, where the lagging holds a basking temperature no surface lizard in this climate would ever find — and it has staked a seam full of eggs there, and it regulates itself all day between hot steel and cool shadow with the unhurried confidence of an animal that has correctly assessed that nothing here can hurt it, because nothing here is big enough. The maintenance crews call it the inspector. It is not a joke. A gecko that lives on the pipe run reads the pipe run: it knows which joints sweat, which flanges fog, which elbows have grown their chandeliers of lime. When it finally moves off pipe six — and one day it will, territories shift — it will leave behind eggs in lagging and a small territory as thoroughly claimed as any homestead.

The sparrows in cable tray three commute. This is the strange part, the part that makes them not quite natives yet: they leave. Every dawn, out the vent, into the real sky, and back at dusk to the tray and the warm margins. They are the underfloor's only bilinguals, holding citizenship in two atmospheres, and you can track the drift of the colony by which way the tray is filling: next season there will be a second tray, then a third. In three years this will be a sparrow roost with a nation under it. The mice under the ladder rungs are further along — they have no outside. They keep their country in the dry gravel near the pier where the light lozenge never reaches, in the one province where the rain does not fall, and their trails through the gravel are worn smooth as any road, the only worn surface in a landscape of quarry scars, which makes the mice the oldest residents in geological terms: their paths are the underfloor's first erosion.

And then the sea. The sump is a round pit rimmed in red paint, holding twenty-eight-degree water flavored faintly of everything the stills have ever thought about, and in it, eleven generations of guppies. Released by some maintenance hand on a whim months ago — a feeder bag, an idle kindness — they have founded a lineage. Eleven generations is not a colony; it is a people. None of the fish in that pit has ever seen sky, and none ever will. Their entire light is the glow that leaks down the trench, their entire weather is the pump tide, their entire geography is a red-rimmed hole in the lowest floor of the lowest place in the building, which makes them, without exaggeration, the most subterranean population of their kind on the premises: a nation the size of a manhole, with a fisherman's god on a timer. There are now guppies in that pit shaped subtly by their pit — stockier, it's said, darker, better suited to dim gold water than any guppy bred for a bright tank. Whether that's true or whether the men who work down here just say it because they love them, the fish themselves are not saying.

The tortoiseshell cat is the apex and the enigma. Unowned, warm-seeking, tide-literate. She patrols a fixed loop on the dry gravel — the same loop, the same piers, the same order — and she knows the pump's minute. When the trench sings, the crickets come down, and the cat is already there, sitting, at the margin, before the first cricket arrives. She does not hunt the drips or the moths or the gecko. She hunts the calendar. Nobody feeds her; she feeds herself off the underfloor's clockwork and sleeps, it is believed, somewhere in the pipe canopy no one has ever seen, in the warm height, in the lianas, above even the webs. She is the one resident who has never once been seen to be wrong about anything.

And the humans. Thin civilization, artifacts, no residents — and yet. Someone welded here, tallied here, chalked an arrow and a drawing gone pale. Someone dropped a socket wrench still bright, and someone else did not pick it up, and now nobody will, because it has become a monument. Someone established the office — stool, tin, pallet, a paperback swollen to double thickness by damp, its bookmark of emery cloth maybe halfway in, maybe at a passage about patience — and someone else established the drip-line: a row of jar lids set under the cleanest leaks, catching gin-rain, and emptied, and refilled, and emptied again, for months, by a party the setter has never met. This is the underfloor's true civilization: not occupation but correspondence. Two people who have never seen each other, holding a conversation in jar lids, conducted over the heads of eleven generations of guppies and a nation of drunk moths.

The tomato seedling under the stool should not, by rights, exist. It came from a sandwich. It grows leggy and earnest toward the noon lozenge, which crawls, which means the seedling is chasing its sun across a floor a few centimeters at a time, day by day, forever arriving. It will never fruit. There is not enough light and there will not ever be enough light, and it leans anyway, with the full seriousness of a plant that does not know what a building is — and in this it is the emblem of everything down here: the crickets keeping the plant's clock, the moths drunk on the plant's breath, the guppies holding a nation in a hole, the cat praying to a pump, the seedling stretching toward a square of borrowed sky. Every one of them is growing toward something that does not know they are alive, and every one of them is alive anyway, abundantly, in the loudest quiet and the brightest dark in the building — beneath the thunder, under the lid, in the lowest country there is, where it rains gin on schedule and the moon is a glass block and nobody who belongs here has ever once wanted to leave.
