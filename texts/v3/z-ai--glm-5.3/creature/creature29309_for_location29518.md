What lives in the Kettle lives provisionally, the way everything here does. The mountain has not decided what it is, and neither have they.

Start with the swallows, because they were first, and because they are the only ones who did not choose this place. Slot swallows: small gray birds, unremarkable in any lowland field guide, that took one look at a vertical crease weeping meltwater in a wall no predator could climb and recognized a doorway. They nest at the lip of the Slot in mud carried up from wherever mud can still be found — which means each swallow's nest is a small importation of the world's future, a thimble of proto-soil mortared to glassy rock. They fly out through the mountain's breath and back through it again, and so they are doused in warm damp air twice a day, and their feathers have begun, in the way of creatures that live at edges, to mean something slightly different than they did. They are the city's only wild citizens and its most casual ones. The mountain breathes; the swallows regard this as weather, which of course it is.

The people are another matter. They chose.

They came as surveyors and stayed as settlers, and you can read the whole character of the place in the names they gave it: Kettle and Wick, homely words for an architecture no homestead ever had. That instinct — to make the enormous domestic — is what the inhabitants are, more than anything else. They are a people who live inside a fact that takes the longest to accept, and their culture is largely a set of techniques for the accepting.

They are few. Twelve inhabited terraces of forty-two printed is not a tragedy or a failure; it is a bank balance, a savings account of space drawn against a future they expect to need. The printed stone came out of the mountain and went back up as streets and cisterns, and the people who keep it are half mason and half gardener of the mineral — they walk the terraces tapping, listening for what frost is doing inside the walls, because frost shattering works day and night here and does not distinguish between the outcrops and the architecture. The loudest human activity in Wick is repair that is really a conversation: the mountain cracks a facet loose, the people go and find where it went, and the finding is called by a word that means both *inspection* and *listening for an answer.*

Their central science is breath. Stand at the Slot long enough and you feel the mountain inhale cold down your collar and exhale warm up through your hair, and the settlers have built an entire forecast discipline on this rhythm. There are people — the older ones, the ones who were here when the terraces were wet from the printers — who can read the flag the way a sailor reads water. A hard hour-long exhale means storm within hours, and the whole city shifts its weight in response: laundry pulled in from the lines strung under the Lens, work on the outer terraces folded up, the swallows watched. The mountain tells them the weather it is about to make. They have no other almanac, and have never needed one. Some of the young ones, born under the dome, can read the inbreath too — a cold draft at the ankle means something about the snowpack three thousand meters above, about the year's water sitting in its accounts at altitude, and a child of Wick will say, offhand, *the bank is heavy today,* and mean it about snow.

They are a people of ledgers. Not greedy, not grasping — but everything here is a flow through accounts of fissure and fracture, and it has made them precise. Water is tracked from snowbank to swallow-hole to cistern to tap, and the keeping of these accounts is honored work, almost liturgical. The cistern-tenders know their water the way shepherds know flocks: this one runs glassy and quick, that one arrives having fallen somewhere inside the mountain for a long time and comes out warm and mineral and tasting faintly of the place's unfinishedness. The children can tell, blindfolded, which fissure a cup of water came out of. This is not a trick. It is fluency.

Under the Lens, in the soft columns of real daylight that the lattice carries down, they have done the only thing settlers ever do with light: they have started to grow things. Not much. Not yet. The mountain is green nowhere, but a shelf of printed stone under a column of daylight is not nowhere, it is a *somewhere*, a first somewhere, and on those shelves the settlers keep what they call the experiments and treat like family — mats of algae, a tray of lichen propagated from the thimbles of mud in the swallow nests, a stubborn row of something leafy that refuses to be discouraged by thin air and secondhand sun. The gardeners of Wick do not speak of making the mountain bloom. They speak, carefully, of *making it possible for the mountain to be joined.* The distinction matters to them. They did not make this place; they were admitted to it, through a hand's width of shadow, and they behave like guests with a very long lease.

Because the air is thin enough that visitors count stairs, the city walks slowly and speaks in the low, even, unhurried voices of people conserving breath — and this, too, has become culture. Arguments in Wick are famously long and famously quiet. Songs are sung in the middle registers. The loudest sounds in the whole city are not made by anyone who lives there: they are the sharp reports of frost cracking loose a facet somewhere out on the flanks, rolling across the cirques and in through the Slot like the mountain clearing its throat, and every inhabitant knows exactly which sound belongs to which face of the peak, and stops, and listens, and — this is the part that takes the longest to accept about *them* — answers. Someone always answers. A tap on the wall, a knuckle on a railing: *heard you.* It is not a ritual with rules. It is just something they do, the way you nod to a neighbor across a fence.

And there is a smaller life, quieter than all of this, which the cistern-tenders found first and told no one about for a season because they were not sure the mountain had given permission. In the warm mineral water that has fallen a long way inside the rock, there is something that is not quite algae and not quite anything named — a film, a bloom, a first thin suggestion of green in water that has never met a plant. The mountain is a world ahead of its life, and it appears the life has begun arriving through the front door, in the drinking water, ahead of schedule. The settlers keep this to themselves in the sense that they do not put it in the reports. They put it in the ledgers, under water from the deep fissures, with a note that gets amended each year, and the note has never said *contamination.* It says *arrived.*

So: who lives here. Slot swallows, nesting in mud from the future. A few hundred quiet, precise, breath-reading people who treat a young mountain as a neighbor with moods, bank its snow, answer its cracking, and garden its possibilities one printed shelf at a time. Something green and unnamed, beginning in the dark of the plumbing. And the mountain itself — not dead, not yet alive, smoking in slow banners on cold mornings, swallowing its own rivers — which is, in the view of everyone who lives inside it, the resident. The rest of them just have rooms.
