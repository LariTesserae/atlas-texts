# Who Lives Here

Start with what's easiest to believe, because it's in front of you: the animals. They live the way the place rations itself — in beads, not strands. Every weeper is a village, and between weepers there is nobody, and this is the first thing every newborn on the Levels learns, before it learns its own mother's voice: *we live on stones in a grey country, and the stones are far apart.*

The bells — the grazers — are the closest thing to a people here, because they have routes and they have memory. A carillon is not a herd the way elk are a herd, loose and self-interested; it is a procession, single file by preference, moving along paths older than any animal in it. They cross the ringing pans deliberately, in step, and whether they do it for the sound's pleasure or because the true-ringing stone is somehow safer to walk — because a bell walking on it is heard by everything for miles and therefore drowned in protection — is a question the bells have never found interesting enough to answer. What is certain is the gardening. Each bell is a courier of bloom, grit moving weeper to weeper in their dark guts, and the routes they walk are the country's hedges, planted and replanted for as long as there have been herds. If you could see the Levels from above the fog — and nothing can — you would see their migration paths as faint damp green threads, the only lines drawn on the land by anything alive.

Around the pools live everyone who is loud for a living. The knucklebacks are the honest poor of the flats: small, armed with a single snap, fizzing all day in every film of water, audible for miles, and — every animal knows this, including the knucklebacks — the valley's alarm system, its metronome, its heartbeat made audible. The glasswing midges are the weather that clicks; they hold their swarm-shapes by sound the way flocks hold shape by sight in brighter countries. The dish-faced waders hunt them with heads that swing like things that see with their ears, which here is simply seeing. The wick hangs its one blue lie in the dark pools, and the false-knockers tell their two small lies — a drip, a footfall — and get by; even the most honest economy carries a pickpocket or two.

And over all of it, the Hush: flat, low, wide as a door. It does not live at a weeper. It does not live anywhere, the way silence does not live anywhere. It hunts inside loudness, stepping on the peaks of the chorus, and its presence is known only as an absence that spreads — faster than the Hush, because the hush itself is contagious. Every animal on the Levels owes its life, eventually, to the moment a neighboring pool's fizz dropped out and every head came up. On this land, one knuckleback's defiant snap at the end of it is the closest thing to a public hero.

That is the visible citizenry. But the Levels have another population, and it is larger.

The listeners built here, and the evidence says they lived the way the animals do — on the weepers, along the routes, in the beads — but they did something the animals do not do: they went to the country's edges and improved the acoustics. Everything they left is an ear or a mouth. The ear-walls, broken now into beads themselves, were parabolas facing the fog. The horn-pipes still play the prevailing wind without anyone to maintain them, which means the wind has been holding the listeners' part for them since before anyone's records. The dome at Earthmouth makes its long note through the wall's throat whenever the wind crosses it, and that note is the only silence the animals trust — everything near the dome goes still to hear it, predator and prey together, under a truce neither negotiated.

Understand what that means: the listeners are still, in a functional sense, members of this community. Their ruins are the best instruments in the country and the living compete for them — choristers fighting on the ear-walls for the amplification, knucklebacks spawning to the pitch of the horn-pipes, the dome's rim contested like prime water. The animals inherit the listeners' works and use them daily. The dead built the stage the living perform on. No one here questions this arrangement, because no one here questions the third law — *water lives in stone, and so does everything else* — and if everything lives in stone, then the listeners are simply the part of the population that has already moved into the stone, and the day's noise — the Loud Morning, the Roll Call, the midnight Swallow — is partly for them.

And they believed the exchange went the other way too: that the ground gives back what it takes, eventually, as sound. That erosion is the earth saying itself slowly. Which means the shell-lime underfoot — a whole previous sea's population, ground to footing — is also, by their accounting, still resident. The first cast of life lies under your boots. The second cast performs at the edges of the stones. The listeners are somewhere between the two, still descending.

So the honest answer to who lives here is: everyone the place has ever held, on a long delay. The animals at the weepers, living loudly in beads. The bloom, one patient cell-layer of inheritance over all of it. The builders, gone into the ground without a grave or a midden, the way sound goes into stone. And the Mute on the eastern side — soft ash, sound-eating, uncrossed — is the one address in the whole country where nothing lives, and the unfinished bowl at its rim says someone stood at that border once and decided, carefully, not to bring the water any closer.

They came here to listen. Whatever they heard, they heard it — or it stopped — or the same patience that takes everything here took them too. But the dome still makes its note when the wind is right, and everything near it goes still, and on some nights people used to say the note carried syllables. If that is the dead coming out of the ground as sound, the way the listeners believed, then they are speaking very slowly, one syllable every few years, and the Levels are not a ruin at all.

They are a long conversation with the lights off, and the room is still full.
