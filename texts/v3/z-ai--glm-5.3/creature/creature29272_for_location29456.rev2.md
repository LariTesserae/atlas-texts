# The Inhabitants

## The Humans Who Chose Dimness

No one is born into the Terraces of Slow Condensation by accident. The city does not take immigrants the way other cities do — it does not advertise, does not recruit, does not even particularly welcome. What it does is *permit*, and the few thousand humans who live there arrived one at a time, each having heard of the place through some channel narrow enough to feel like an invitation.

They are, on the whole, people who failed elsewhere. Not failed dramatically — failed *loudly*. They came from cities of noise and urgency where their particular constitution — a preference for slowness, an allergy to brightness, a need to hear water rather than traffic — was treated as a defect to be corrected. Here it is simply a way of being. The first thing a new resident learns is that nothing about them needs adjusting. The second thing they learn is that everything about them will adjust anyway, gently, without their consent, the way a plant reorients toward a light source. Within a month they walk slowly. Within a year they speak at the volume of dripping. They do not decide this. The city decides it for them, or perhaps they simply relax into the shape they always were.

They work, because there is work, though "work" here has a strange character. Much of it is *witnessing* — standing observance over condensation cycles, keeping the ledgers of water that the substrate-mind also keeps but which humans keep anyway, redundantly, because the residents have an intuition (never confirmed, never dispelled) that the city likes being watched back. Some of it is craft: the terraces need hands for what the otter-things cannot reach, fine adjustments to the fibrillar vanes, the replacement of a cracked channel-lip. Some of it is tending — the engineered flora is generations past anyone's full understanding, and the gardeners speak of their plants the way you might speak of elderly relatives: with affection, superstition, and the resigned certainty that the plants know more than they do.

And some of it is scholarship. The documentation — the city's own, extensive, patient, written in no human language and translated by no one so much as *grown into* — occupies a standing cadre of readers who have spent decades on single passages. They are not frustrated by this. Frustration requires a sense of urgency, and urgency is the first thing the city strips from you, like mud from a boot at the door.

## The Otters, and the Question of the Otters

Every resident has an otter story, and every resident's otter story is slightly different, and this is the closest thing the city has to folklore.

The creatures themselves are real enough — sleek, low, amber-slick when they surface, gone again before you can finish the thought *was that—*. They live in the cistern network, in the dark water-silver of the terrace interiors, and they maintain what they inhabit. Watch one long enough (no one has ever watched one long enough; they surface for seconds, and the light pools and lies about depth, and they are gone) and you will see it make an adjustment with manipulators that belong to no otter that ever swam in any honest river.

The argument — robot that evolved toward otter, nanoswarm wearing an otter, otter engineered into a job — is unresolvable and beloved, and the residents keep it unresolved *deliberately*. There is a quiet consensus that if anyone ever proved the answer, something would be lost. The otters are the city's mystery kept in the pocket, brought out in the amber bars over slow drinks, turned over, put away. Residents have favorite otters they've maybe seen twice. They name them. The names are passed down like heirlooms.

## The Tenants, Prior

And then there are the wading forms in the eastern marsh-plain.

Tall. Thin. Silhouettes through the haze, always at the exact distance the haze chooses for them — never closer. No one has gotten near one. No one, the residents will tell you, has *tried* to get near one, and this is said carefully, without emphasis, in the tone people use for things they have agreed not to make a fuss about.

The documentation calls them *tenants, prior*, and the phrase is precise in its evasions. Prior to the humans, certainly. Prior to the city? Prior to the terraces? Prior, perhaps, to the lava itself? The documentation does not clarify, and the residents have developed, over generations of quiet cohabitation, a relationship with the question that is less curiosity than *courtesy*. The wading forms were here. They may still be here. The lease, whatever it was, appears to have been amicable.

Some residents say the forms are gone and what people see are echoes, afterimages in the amber light. Some say the forms are still present in the way the substrate-mind is present — distributed, patient, unbothered. One old ledger-keeper maintained until her death that the *tenants, prior* were the ones who built the terraces, that the whole city was their hand-me-down, granted to humanity the way one leaves a coat on a chair for a guest who stayed longer than expected. She could not prove it. Nobody argued with her, either. In the bars of the Terraces, not arguing with someone is the highest form of agreement.

## The Substrate-Mind, or the Ghost

Underneath everything — literally underneath, in the basalt, in the cistern-walls, in the filtered hum you feel in your molars — is whatever the city is when it is thinking.

The residents do not worship it. They do not ignore it. Their relationship to it is the strangest thing about them and the most ordinary: they treat it the way you treat an elderly landlord who lives in the building, keeps the heat exactly where the lease says, and has never once shown a face. Some thank it, quietly, when the condensation falls heavy. Some resent it, quietly, for the dusk that cannot be turned up. Most simply live within it, the way one lives within weather, and when the sub-audible vibration shifts slightly — as it does, sometimes, in ways the water-budget cannot explain — they pause, listen with their teeth, and resume.

Once, the story goes, a child asked it a question out loud. The documentation has no entry for what was asked or whether anything answered. But the residents tell the story, in the amber bars, always ending the same way: *and the dripping didn't stop*. Which is, in this city, the sound of everything continuing, which is the sound of everything being, in its slow and brand-new way, all right.
