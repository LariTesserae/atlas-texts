# The Tenants of the Kept Plain

The plain is kept, and someone keeps it. That someone has never seen the sea and is rebuilding it anyway.

**The gray masons** are the landlords, and they are smaller than your smallest fingernail. They are termites — or something termites would become if you gave them ten million years of practice and a country with nothing left to happen to it. Blind, pale, soft-bodied, each one an ounce of appetite and instruction, they are individually negligible the way a single letter is negligible. Together they are the only authors this landscape has. They have never known a river. They have never known a wave. And they build with the ground-up skeleton of an ocean — hauling the sea's gravel up one grain at a time, wetted with dew, pasted into fluted towers that channel the night's condensation down into the dark where their gardens grow. Every mound on the plain is a fossil reef reassembled by hand, or rather by mouth, and the masons do not know this and would not care. What they know is chemistry: where the old stone is richest, they build thickest, so that from an inselberg's height the living pattern below rhymes with the dead pattern in the rock. They are remembering a reef they never met, the way a hand remembers a gesture it was never taught.

Inside each mound, the architecture is all interiority. The fungus gardens run warm and wet in chambers the surface never feels, tended by workers who chew dew-grass into substrate and patrol the passages in the dark. The chimneys breathe for the whole city — a faint hiss at dawn when the day's first heat needs venting, the plain's single exhalation. A colony is not a population; it is one very slow animal distributed across a hundred thousand bodies, and the plain carries several thousand of these animals, spaced like an orchard, patient as debt.

And once in an unguessable span, on a schedule no observer has cracked, the masons do the Turning: every mound vents at once, and the winged generation rises together — not thousands but millions of pale wings going up at the same moment until the air looks smoked, until for one afternoon the quietest country in the world makes the sound of a distant sea. Then it stops as one thing stopped, and the plain goes back to its bookkeeping, and no one who witnesses it ever correctly predicts another.

**The drinkers** come with the dew, and the dew is their entire economy. The snails arrive first, in numbers that reorganize the ground — banded shells, mica-bright trails rasping lichen off the stones, working the night's salary for all it's worth. The darkling beetles take the morning shift, and their method is the plain's most honest prayer: they stand on their heads at first light, hindquarters raised into the fog, and let the condensation bead on their backs and run down to their mouths. At dawn the whole country is full of small brown drinkers doing headstands, as far as you can see, in silence, and this is not comic, it is discipline. Everything out here has solved water the same way the land has — by giving up on rain and learning to drink the sky.

**The moths** are the second population, the night shift. After dark the air thickens with papery wings, and a lanternless walk through them is like walking through warm snow. They die in numbers that would look like catastrophe anywhere else and read as bookkeeping here. The lizards wait for the midday and station themselves on the mound flanks, where the masons' steady interior warmth has made the only real estate on the plain whose temperature is always fair — because a mound is not just a building, it's a hearth, and everything that wants to live on this high cold-sweating plateau eventually lives against one. Flat-topped trees with canopies leveled like water. Gray tussock grass in the lee. The whole visible flora is huddled architecture, drawn in around the mounds like wagons around fires.

**The sleepers** are the plain's deepest secret, because most of them are not currently alive in any way you could check. In the cracked white clay pans lie eggs — shrimp, frogs, whole childhoods folded into dust — things whose entire strategy is to out-wait the sky. They have no schedule; they have an ambush. When real rain finally spends itself on this country, a pan fills for a week and detonates a generation: frogs calling in numbers so astonishing after all that silence that everything with ears wakes to hear it, shrimp hatching, courting, breeding, dying, seeding, all inside seven days. Then the pan cracks white again and the next crowd lies down in the dust to wait — five years, thirty, however long the sky takes. They are the plain's most patient residents, and they are not waiting for something. They *are* the something the plain keeps in reserve.

**The auditors** are few, and high. One or two raptors turn on the thermals, nearly silent, doing the plain's arithmetic from above — because the soil, quiet as it looks, holds more animals per meter than a rainforest canopy, and the census is enormous while the volume is near zero. A swift at dusk files the day with a single scream, as if closing a ledger. These are the only inhabitants with voices, and they spend them the way everything here spends everything: once, exactly, and only when it counts.

The rest is the ground itself, the uncounted thousands per cubic meter — mites, springtails, beetle larvae, threadworms, the whole grain-by-grain workforce you hear only when you stand still long enough for the quiet to stop pretending. The soil's faint sound of being worked on never actually stops; it only stops being audible to the loud.

Taken whole, the census of the Kept Plain is a strange family: an ocean's worth of shell, converted first to stone, then to gravel, then back into towers by blind masons who tend gardens in the dark; a community of drinkers, headstanding and rasping, whose water falls upward; sleepers who live decades as a handful of dust and a single week as a chorus; and a handful of wings overhead keeping the accounts. Nothing here is large enough for loneliness. Nothing hurries, because nothing is rewarded for hurrying — the only sudden things on this plain are death and rain, and one of those comes once in a lifetime.

What lives here, finally, is the land's own habit of continuing. The sea closed like a book ages ago, and its tenants are still building with the pages.
