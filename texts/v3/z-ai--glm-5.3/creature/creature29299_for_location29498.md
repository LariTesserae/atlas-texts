**What lives on the Old Table**

First, the trees, because on the Table the trees come before everything, including before themselves. They are the architecture, the water system, the bank, and the elders. Each old stand has its family of keepers, and the keepers know them individually — this one's sap runs bitter in the dim season, that one grafts generously to its neighbors and is called a good neighbor by people who mean it the way they mean it of humans. The trees do not grow so much as *accrete*. A trunk the width of a doorway took four human generations to build, and the person who finally carved the doorway into it was the great-great-grandchild of the person who planted it. They have no rings here worth counting; the wood is too sealed, too waxy. Age in a Table tree is read in the notches — the tap scars, cut and healed and cut again, layering up the trunk like a family's debts and credits written in bark.

The people are a few hundred, and they are the quiet ones, which is what you become when moisture is money. They are small-boned, warm-skinned, slow of gesture, deliberate of word. A conversation here can take an hour and no one considers it long; a decision about a marriage or a roof can take a decade, and no one considers it delayed. They speak in low registers, close-mouthed, because open lips lose vapor. Their greetings are touches — a hand to the wrist, a thumb pressed briefly to the inside of another's forearm, where the skin is thin and the pulse announces whether a person is full or empty, rich or running dry. To be *thirst-poor* is the local term for the closest thing they have to poverty, and no one lets a neighbor stay that way long, because on a shelf this small, everyone's sap is eventually everyone's.

They do not cry, as a habit. This is not hardness. Infants cry — wetly, extravagantly, the only time in a Table life that a body is permitted to spill — and their mothers catch the tears in waxed cloth and rub the moisture back onto the child's own lips, a gesture that says *here, it is all still yours*, and it is the first lesson and it is taught before language. By the time a child walks, they know the second law, which is that the dry air is the wolf at the door. Children learn to read a spill the way children elsewhere learn to read tracks: a drop of sap on stone is a story with a culprit, a victim, and a moral, and small children crouch over these incidents with terrible seriousness, licking the stone clean as both cleanup and lesson.

Their trades are humble and absolute. There are tappers, who cut the切口 rows and read the weep of the trees. There are grafters, who spend whole lives with their hands in the canopy, splicing stem to stem, keeping the sideways rivers of sap flowing — the grafters are the closest thing to aristocracy here, patient people with fingertips stained amber to the second knuckle. There are hearth-keepers, tending the sealed fires whose smoke must never escape dry. There are bone-workers, chime-makers, dew-singers — the last being those who walk the terraces at the coldest dim hour with long waxed poles, tipping the thin condensation off the upper leaves into gourds, an industry that yields almost nothing and is done anyway, because on the Table the difference between *almost nothing* and *nothing* is the difference between a town and a grave.

And they keep the heron-hours. The greater night-herons — those enormous, motionless, dew-drinking birds standing in the canopy like absorbed elders — call at intervals through the night, and the intervals are the village clock. The first belling is the going-in. The fourth is the middle dark. The last, the long lone note, is the rising, and children are named for the hour of their birth: Fourth-Bell children are said to be watchers; Last-Bell children are said to be tap-happy and lucky. The herons themselves are half-owned and half-owner; they perch near the settlement because the settlement's warmth rises, and no one has ever decided, or asked, which species domesticated which.

Around the people, the loud citizens. The sap-beetles, whose choral drone is the town's true heartbeat — it rises at the bright-haze, falls at the rose-dim, and the villagers' whole emotional weather moves with it without anyone admitting so. When the drone thins, everyone's mood thins; entomologists, if any ever climbed up here, would find that the beetles keep time not with the sky but with the sap-run, so that insect, tree, and human all share a single slow pulse. The leaf-warblers, those small percussionists who will not touch stone — a superstition the birds enforce themselves, as though they too know the law about the dry ground, and a warbler that lands on a path is a noted and slightly ominous event. The bronze lizards at dusk, hunting by ear, running the walls of grown houses like little gods of the threshold. All of it loud, all of it wet-sounding, a market's worth of noise traded back and forth over a floor of granite that itself says nothing at all, and has not for a geological age.

What lives here, in sum, is a community that has stopped growing and started *keeping*. The mountain finished becoming; the forest, walled by its own cliffs, finished spreading; and the people, taking the hint from the rock beneath them, have made an art of continuation. They keep the sap moving, keep the chimes turning, keep the tears in. They stand at the eastern rim on rose evenings with a few hundred others of their kind — beetles, warblers, herons, trees — on the world's last shelf, and they do not call it an ending. They call it, roughly, *the finished good hour*, and they hold it, the way they hold everything, without letting a drop of it spill.
