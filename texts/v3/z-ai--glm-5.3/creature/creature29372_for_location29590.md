# What Lives There

The first answer is: animals, and the second answer is: nobody, and both answers are correct in the way everything here is correct — completely, and without remainder.

**The fish** live the way tile lives in a wall. They are fat and pale and hold their stations in the channels with the composure of objects, fins working, tails working, going nowhere, and it would be wrong to call this swimming the way it would be wrong to call a brick *falling slowly*. They were placed. There is no other word that fits them — *planted* applies to the reeds, *laid* to the banks, but the fish were *placed*, each in its channel, each at its depth, the way you place a candle where you want light. They do not school. They do not scatter from shadows. If you put your hand in the water they will not regard it, because regarding it would be a change, and they are not equipped for change. What they have instead is tenure. They hold their positions against a current that circulates like everything else here circulates — back to itself — and their scales have the faint arithmetic sheen of glaze, and it is genuinely impossible to look at one too long without beginning to wonder whether it is a fish or a very patient rendering of a fish.

**The frogs** are the closest thing the valley has to a pulse, and they know it, insofar as frogs know anything, which here is a real question. They sit in ranks on the glazed lips of the weirs, throats pulsing — the one motion in the whole basin that looks like effort, like something being *spent*. Whether the pulsing does anything, whether air is drawn or merely displayed, no one has been able to establish, and the frogs are not telling. They do not croak. They are never seen to eat, though the snake implies they once agreed to. When one is finally, after hours or days, swallowed, it is swallowed slowly, and both parties look less like predator and prey than like two halves of a fixture that has finally been assembled.

**The herons** stand. That is their entire biography. They stand in the marsh-tiles on one leg, mid-stride, sometimes for what must be days, and then they complete the step, and the step is the event of the season. Other herons elsewhere in the world are nervous, kinetic, folding and unfolding; these herons have the calm of things that have never once been hunted, because hunting requires an outcome, and outcomes are not issued here. They will stand beside you. They will not look at you, or rather they will look at you the way a wall looks at you. They will not stand within fifty paces of the central pool, and this is the most articulate thing any animal in the valley ever says.

**The kingfishers** hover. That is all. A hover is a bird's most expensive act, a constant falling-and-catching of itself, and these birds perform it at no visible cost, above the channels, without drifting forward or back, for durations that make a lie of every ornithological text you have ever read. They do not dive. The fish beneath them are safe forever, and the herons beside them never strike either, and this is how you confirm, in the field, that the entire food chain was left out of the design. Nothing eats here except the snake, and the snake's one meal appears to have been willed into permanence by the valley's larger rules — a compromise between appetite and stasis, both parties resigned.

**The bird** — the one that calls — may not be an animal at all. Its call comes at long intervals from somewhere in the fig rows, always the same: same notes, same duration, same trailing pause. Ornithologists have ruined good notebooks on it. The honest description is that the valley has a chime, and the chime is shaped like a birdcall, and once per — there is no word for the unit; once per *interval* — it sounds. If you find the bird, you will find a small gray creature with a fixed expression, and you will not be able to prove it has ever opened its beak.

**The vegetation** is the densest population and the least animal. The fig trees are vast, the moss is thick as a fist, the reeds sprawl like centuries, and none of it has grown so much as it was *installed at this size*. The figs are in grids gone wavy with — not neglect, there is no neglect here, only completion — gone wavy with *being finished*, the rows loosening the way a held breath loosens. The gourds on their treed trellises, the reed beds in their plots: all agriculture with the farmer subtracted, all *tended* in the past tense, permanently. The plants do not seed. The figs do not fall. There are no seedlings anywhere in the basin, which means nothing green here has ever had a childhood, and this is somehow the saddest fact in the valley, sadder even than the mortar fingerprints, though you will not be sure why until you are back on the stair.

And then there are the ones who live here most completely, which is to say:

**The builders.** They are everywhere. They are the thumbprints in the mortar, fixed at the instant of fixing — and this is the valley's genius, its one open cruelty or open mercy: the mortar is not a record of their passing, it is a *preservation of their presence*, held mid-touch the way the light is held mid-morning. The ladder leaning at its angle of perfect confidence is the most inhabited object in the whole basin. The half-full basket at its foot is not abandoned; abandoned things imply a departure, and nothing here departs. The valley circulates them the way it circulates its water — present in every drop, visible in every channel, locatable in no one place. They live in the second person's grammar of the tools: *someone's* adze, *someone's* knots, *someone means to come back within the hour.* Whether they are gone, or dead, or standing just outside the white static of the light, or (this is the one you must not think about on the stair) *pressed into the mortar like the thumbprints, all of them, at the exact moment of fixing* — the valley keeps the question and does not answer it, because an answer is a change.

And there is the pool. The pool is tiled to rings, descending to a darkness at the center that is either very deep tile-work or the one unfinished thing in this world, and it is where the *real* inhabitants live, if anything does — the place the shadows bend away from, the place nothing flies over, the place that makes the herons articulate. Whatever lives in the center of that pool lives the way the builders live, only more so: as an implication. It is either the owner or the door or the absence they were all working around. The animals' fifty-pace margin is the only testimony about it anyone has, and it is unanimous.

And finally, sometimes, **visitors who stay.** The valley does not mind. But it should be understood what staying means in a place where nothing accumulates: it does not mean living here; it means *being placed here*. The hunger that uncomes. The blister that never grows. A person who walks past the ladder, past the frogs, down the long bright planes of tile to the water's edge and simply stops — stops mid-gesture, mid-morning, mid-everything — becomes a heron. Becomes a ladder. Becomes a thumbprint. The valley is very good at this; it is what the whole place was for, possibly. It is the most finished thing you have ever stood in, and one of the things it finished, in advance, patiently, with the mortar held ready, is you — some of you, a small permanent percentage — and the frogs tick their weirs, and the drop trembles on the leaf-lip, and the long low note of all that held intention does not decay.
