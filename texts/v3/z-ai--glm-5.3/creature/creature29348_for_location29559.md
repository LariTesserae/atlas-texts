Inside the rim there are only two kinds of life: the ones that came up for the grass, and the ones that were already here, listening.

Start with the grass-eaters, because they are what the trail was built for. Each summer the herders bring sheep and goats up through the pass — a few hundred animals, no more, because the floor of the bowl will not carry more — and the sheep do something here they do not do in the valleys. They bed down in the resonant center of the cirque, where every sound is doubled back, and they sleep pressed close, and it is believed, though no one has proven it, that they sleep better here than anywhere else on the mountain. The ewes locate their lambs not by the bleat itself but by its echo — a single call comes back off the headwall with a direction written into it, and the lamb's answer comes back with another, and within two or three exchanges each has found the other across a flock of hundreds. The herders exploit this. They know that a lamb lost in the Hollow is never really lost; it is merely somewhere in the bowl, and the bowl will tell you where, if you can read the returns. Old herders stand still with their eyes shut and do this. Young ones chase around the floor and find nothing.

The herders themselves live up here in stone huts at the rim, low rounded things roofed in turf, positioned with a precision that looks accidental until you notice it: every hut is set just behind a boulder, just off the wind line, in the one narrow band where the bottle-note drops to a mutter. It cannot be escaped entirely — you can still feel it in your ribs on the cot, like sleeping against a sleeping animal — but it can be thinned. No herder stays longer than six weeks. Not because of the cold, they will tell you, and not because of the work, which is light. Because of the note. It gets into the dreams. You begin to hum without noticing. You begin to answer things before they are said. The herders treat this as normal and leave before it finishes happening, the way a sensible person steps off ice that is beginning to talk.

But the ones who were already here — those are the reason for the sound.

They are pika-like, if you need a comparison: fist-sized, round, short-eared, living in the ancient locked talus where the stones fit like masonry. But that comparison fails almost immediately, because pikas are anxious animals, and these are not anxious at all. Nothing in the Hollow is anxious. There has been no rockfall, no flood, no change in this bowl for longer than any animal memory, and it shows in them the way the polish shows on the headwall. They move slowly for their size. They sit on the same stones — the *same* stones, generations of them, the stones worn faintly concave on top — and they face the wind, and they sing.

Not the alarm-squeak of a pika. What they do is tune.

The bowl hums at its single low note, day and season after season, and the singers sit out on the talus and hold their own voices against it — a long, breathy, reedy tone, thin as a grass stem, pitched somewhere in the upper harmonics of the wind's note. When you first hear it you think the wind has developed a whistle. It hasn't. It is being accompanied. A single singer will hold its pitch for a minute, two minutes, wavering only when the wind shifts the fundamental beneath it, and then another takes over, and another, and the talus all around the bowl keeps up a continuous shimmer of thin tones layered over the roar, so that the Hollow — you realize this on the second day, and it rearranges something in you — is not a place with a sound. It is a place that is *being performed*, constantly, by wind and singers together, and has been for as long as there has been wind.

They do not sing for mates. They do not sing at predators, of whom there are almost none — the Hollow is too high, too far, too finished, for eagles to bother with. They seem to sing for the same reason the water does: because the shape of the bowl makes it inevitable. They were born into the note the way trout are born into a current, and holding their own line inside it appears to be simply what a body does here. Their burrows open on the resonant side of every boulder. Their young, barely furred, can already be seen sitting at the den mouths with their throats moving, holding a wavery approximation of the pitch, practicing against the oldest sound they know.

And there is one more.

At the base of the headwall, where the glacial polish is darkest and the note comes back strongest, there lives — the herders all agree on this, though they disagree on everything else about it — an old singer, older than any animal should be, older than the herders' grandfathers' huts, that does not sing with the others. It sits alone in the deep echo and does something else. The herders call it the Keeper of the Low Part. What it produces is not audible at first; you feel it, under all the other sound, a second tone beneath the bottle-note, the way the deepest string of an instrument is felt rather than heard. Some herders say it is just wind in a crack. Some say it is an old animal whose voice has deepened until it left the range of ears. One old woman, who had spent more seasons up there than anyone, said something the others repeat with respect and refuse to explain: that every bowl with a voice like that needs a floor under it, and the Keeper is the floor, and as long as it sings, the Hollow holds.

She said the year to worry about is the year you climb the trail and hear the wind alone.

You would know, she said. An hour before you saw it.
