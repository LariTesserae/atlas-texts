What lives here is mostly weather.

The tallowmoths are the hall's population, its light, its rain, and eventually its fuel, and they do not know any of this, which is the only way the arrangement works. Each one is a small ember-colored nothing — body dense with the fat that gave them their name, wings powdered with the same warm dust that makes the air feel like it has grain. They were bred for the rendering vats, once, by the billion. The billion are still here. No one renders them now except the boiler, which takes its tithe only after they are finished being alive. In the meantime they swarm — not aimlessly, though it will take you hours to see the pattern. They bank and brighten on the Diligence's stroke, four seconds to a breath, and they drift in slow fronts like weather systems, bright fronts passing over the gantry, dim fogs pooling under the Packing Room shelf. A moth's life here is short and entirely occupied: burn for a season, mate in the braidwork of the ceiling, fall. The falling is not tragic. It is logistics.

Beneath them, the grubs — pale, fat, regimented — graze the root-lawn in mowing-lines that heal behind them almost as fast as they're cut. They are the sound like pages turning, like fat quietly sizzling, the only chorus this hall has ever needed. When they've eaten their fill they climb the warm pipes of Nursery Lane, where incubator rooms once ran and where the heat still arrives on schedule, and they spin. The cocoons hang in decades-deep layers, grey-gold, sagging, insulating the machinery they depend on, in a mutual patience that nobody negotiated.

The hall keeps a small aristocracy. The two albino geckos by the boiler door are blind, enormous by gecko standards, and unmistakably pampered — not by anyone, by circumstance. The warmest real estate in the hill is theirs; moths come to them; they eat by the dozen with the unhurried dignity of animals whose food arrives as climate. Beside them, in the basin where the swarm's breath condenses, the colony of blind white shrimp drink and breed and die in water that is literally the exhalation of their neighbors, and have never known another, and do not know this one. The crickets chink in the brickwork, the mites ride the moths like dust rides dust — a population of tenants too small to see, kept too comfortable to leave.

And there is the Diligence. It's on the nameplate as a machine, but every living thing in this hall treats it as an animal, correctly. It is the heartbeat they brighten on, the summer their sward grows fat on, the drum that keeps a day twelve thousand strokes long. It eats the dead because the dead taught it to. It has not gone out in longer than memory because there has never once been a shortage. Of all the citizens of the Works, it is the only one still working, and it works for nothing — ships nothing, produces nothing — except continuation, which it turns out is enough.

That is what lives here, in the end: a loop with no designer and no purpose, held together by small strict laws. Heat grows fodder, fodder feeds grubs, grubs become light and rain and fuel, fuel keeps the heat. Everything finished changing long ago and began continuing. And the whole population, animal and iron together, is slowly upholstering its own hall shut — every hatch another layer of silk, every century another hand's width — spinning, without knowing it, one enormous cocoon around the machinery and the tree and itself, to emerge as whatever it is becoming, in whatever kind of spring comes after centuries underground.
