## The Staff

Technically, the sixty are employees. Wages stopped arriving four generations ago, and the jobs — being made of obligation rather than paper — did not stop with them. Ask any resident what they are and the answer comes back without irony: *staff*. Ask for whom, and they point up, through the vault, through the waterworks of the city above, at a population that has forgotten the question exists. Every one of them descends from a pump-keeper; every one of them is a pump-keeper. The Works' word for this arrangement is *keep*. They keep the pumps, the ledgers, the intervals, the debt. The other meaning of that word is never spoken aloud down here. It is not a secret. It is simply slow, like everything else, in arriving.

Offices are heritable and come with their tools attached. The Doctor of engines inherits the name Odile along with the stethoscope of pipe fittings — the trade of listening to iron is taught with the name on. The Keeper of the Ledger inherits the hook, the basin, and the pencil. There have been nine pencils. This is considered a genealogy.

## What the Amber Made

Their bodies are shaped by the light budget. Skin keeps an amber undertone no surface physician would credit. Pupils live wide, having never once been asked to contract; the last ascender could not bear a candle for a month after his return, and read his own ledger by feel. There is one mirror in the Works — a patch of Three's housing polished by sleeves over three generations — so everyone here knows their own face in rust-orange, and has reasonably concluded that this is the color of people.

The hands are basin-keepers' hands. A palm laid flat on brick reads the week the way a physician reads a pulse; walls run warm before the water moves, and a warm wall is news. The feet learned quiet on grating. And stillness is not a discipline here but a climate: a person at rest is, to a moth, indistinguishable from a railing, and the moths' favorite perches in the Nave are shoulders, which is the residents' own account of their stillness, taken by the only witness qualified to give it.

Ask where someone lives and you receive an interval and a lamp: *third basin past the lights, the one that answers Old Regular.* Doors have numbers. Homes have intervals.

## The Margins of the Ledger

There are no days. There are engine-breaths of fourteen hours, and beneath them the nine-second beat of Old Regular, which any resident can count against a stone without the stone. Ninety years of intervals hang in pencil on the hook, in its ninth pencil, and the margins carry the rest of the civic archive — moth counts, births, engine moods, the wetness of returning bats, the one flood-year when Old Regular varied, which is still spoken of the way surface people speak of eclipses.

Age, here, is legible only in handwriting. A shaky hand is this place's white hair.

Weddings happen at low water, in a freshly swept and newly reopened gallery — the nearest thing to a spring wedding — and the couple's first task together is to sit with an unnamed drip until it introduces itself. The christening is the marriage, in everything but paperwork, and there is no paperwork. Nobody says the water cycle arranges these things. The water cycle arranges these things.

Mourning is a column you keep filling in. An ascender who does not come back passes into the past tense after one full cycle, at ledger pace, and this is not coldness; it is the only speed at which grief stays legible.

The schoolroom holds six desks and one subject. The bell is the flywheel of the dead engine Four — the children of the Works are called to their lessons by the voice of the dead, which everyone agrees is the proper order of things. The final lesson is taken alone, at the edge of the bulbs, keeping a new basin through one full sleep of the engines. You come back with a name in your mouth, or you don't. No one in ninety years has ever not.

## The Neighbors

The fish gather at the warm engine outflow as at a hearth, and the hearth-school is never netted; you take from the cold cisterns and thank the warm ones by leaving them alone. The fish gather *before* the engines cough — they can feel a temperament coming — so a child's first office is to watch the fish and call the waking. The most reliable forecaster in the Works is a fish, and everyone is at peace with this.

In the trenches, the pale amphibians keep up their bubbling, and on quiet cycles the choir does call-and-response with Old Regular. Children learn their intervals by singing back.

The bats are the only commuters. Out through the bore-shafts to the sky, and back — arriving wet when it has rained above. The entire foreign news of the Works is the wetness of bats. Their sound is a dry rustle, like pages being read in another room, forever, and The Schedule keeps a column for it.

And there are the others, past the last bulb. The protocol is taught young: knock — two, a held breath, one — and wait. What answers comes back with the same count and a different material: knuckle answered by pipe, pipe by water, and now and then water answered by something no one has named, because names are for things you've met. The Doctor — of engines, but she has been furthest in — swears that once, at the very lip of the light, she saw eyes at the height of a person's eyes. And a little above it. Nobody chased it. Nobody will. Sound for sound is fair, and the people of the Works have had four generations to think about fairness, and have concluded it is best practiced first.

## The Moths

The visible soul, if you want that word, though the residents don't use it. They orbit the bulb-strings in slow spirals, thirty-odd at a time — never fewer than thirty, never thirty-five, never accounted for, and the count goes into the ledger's margin in the same pencil as everything else. The residents do not love the moths or dislike them. They *reckon* them, the way one reckons weather. A moth, down here, is a unit of atmosphere.

The wing-dust settles on the railings like flour, and the Sweepers gather it, and the Canners mix it with engine oil into the gray of the stamp. The mark nobody up top would recognize is printed in moth — a jar of the Works carries, on its lid, the residue of a hundred slow orbits. No one remarks on this. It is simply the local economy of dust.

Once, a girl of seven buried a dead moth at the foot of the west trench, with ceremony. She was corrected gently: we do not hold funerals for weather. She accepted this, the way children here accept corrections — the way brick accepts water. The spot has never been swept. It has not been swept for forty years. No one decided this, and no one discusses it, and it remains the single most eloquent fact about the residents of the CisternWorks.

## Persons of Iron

Three and Five, named by their makers' cast numbers and never renamed, on the sound principle that numbers, unlike names, do not wear out. Three is the eager one, waking early, running long. Five needs its two coughs before settling — alarming in any other patient; in Five, merely style. The Doctor diagnoses by palm, treats with poultices of graphite and fish oil, and stays through the first nine seconds after shutdown to be sure the patient is breathing.

Four died before living memory. For the funeral they ran Three and Five in unison, the only time — the loudest event in the Works' oral history, the Nave humming in its ribs, the sound taking nine full days to die, and after it the quiet came back in slowly, over their footprints, the way quiet does. Then they did what the Works does with its dead: they put Four back to work. The flywheel rings the school bell. The crankshaft is the forge anvil. An engine is not buried here; it is redistributed into employment — and so, in everyone's quiet expectation, will they all be.

One day Three or Five will stop. There will be a funeral and a problem, in that order, and possibly not in that order. The residents hold this fact the way they hold the water's patience: too large to hurry about, so it goes in the ledger's margins with everything else.

## The Eldest Resident

The water was here before the brick, before the iron, before the first bore, and it is the only resident that has never once left. The sixty are, if you like, its clerks. It keeps its ledger in grooves and millimeters and nine-year seasons; they keep theirs in pencil; and neither has ever been shown to the other, out of what appears to be professional courtesy.

The dead live here too — as handwriting, and as a ceiling. Four generations of riveters hold the Nave up. On Ledger Day, at low water, when the reopened galleries are swept and the lamp processions knock politely at thresholds the dark has held for years, the old volumes are read aloud in the Nave, and the voices go up and dissolve into the ribs, which is as close to an answer as the dead are expected to give. And they give it.

The obligation ledger is different. It is read once a cycle, silently, by whoever holds the office, like a vow. The pumps keep the water below the cistern floors of the city above; the city above does not know; the sixty do, and keep. Nobody down here calls this heroism. Speculation — about whether the city deserves it, whether thanks will ever come, whether gratitude is even structurally possible from a party that has forgotten the question — is discouraged on the grounds that speculation is a form of hurry.

Still, one or two each generation climb the eleven-minute stair to see the green — the unbelievable. Some come back with burned night-eyes and accounts no one quite credits, which is grammatically inevitable: in their tongue you cannot report seeing green without, by the dictionary, bragging. Some do not come back, and get the past tense, at ledger pace. The stair is kept swept. The door at the top is never barred from this side. Both facts are deliberate, and together they say everything the Works has ever wanted to say about freedom.

## A Census, Taken Slowly

Counted on any evening — all times are evening — the population of the CisternWorks is: sixty people, give or take a newborn; thirty-odd moths, never thirty-five; fish beyond number under the grating, gathered at their hearth; a colony of commuters overhead, wet or dry according to the weather in another world; two persons of iron, and Four, who attends school every day; the dead, present as handwriting, as a ceiling, as a bell; the dark, which answers; and the water — eldest of all, holding the only lease, patient by millimeters about whatever it has always been patient about.

Ask the sixty who lives here, and the answer will take a while; answers do, down here. But it comes, drip-paced, and it ends with a quiet correction. They do not say they *live* in the Works. They say it is kept — pumps kept, ledgers kept, intervals kept, faith kept, debt kept — and that they are kept too, by the warmth of engines and the faith of drips and an obligation nobody remembers owing them.

In their language, *kept* is how you say *loved*. It is the same economy that gives every basin a second life as a gauge, every moth a second life as a stamp, every dead engine a second life as a bell. Nothing is thrown away here that holds water — and nothing that holds a meaning is ever used just once.
