What lives here is the maker. You knew that already, if you read the tiles, but it takes seeing one to understand what the reading meant.

They come out of the drizzle at knee height, because that is where they keep themselves — low, close to the warmth, close to the wet. At first you will mistake one for a slump, a small failure of masonry, something that sat down and hasn't finished deciding what shape it is. Then the slump stands up. It is a little shorter than you, warm as the floor, the color of the local clay with a dark iron blush where the drizzle has found it and been drunk. Its skin is leather-hard — it will take the print of your thumbnail and slowly, over days, un-take it, re-wetting, smoothing itself out from within. Its eyes are the exception. Each one's eyes were glazed once, early, deliberately, the single fixed point in a body built to remain unfinished — two small vitreous gleams, the only glass it will ever carry while it walks. Everything else about it is still workable. That is the whole design.

Its hands are what stop you. Because you have been reading those hands for a mile. The ridges, the knuckle-drags, the crescent of a trimming nail — the tiles are not merely made by these people. The tiles are the *record* of these people, palm-sized, self-portrait by self-portrait, pressed one at a time for a life at a time, and the tilefield runs past the reach of sight because the makers have never once been subtracted. Nothing here has died yet. The old do not leave; they transform. And so the population only thickens, and the floor of the world is the arithmetic of it: every hand that has ever pressed here is still somewhere on the field, still legible, still warm.

They know each other the way you came to know the ground — by handwriting. A name, among them, is a stretch of tile. To introduce yourself is to press a course and let someone read it. Their maps are not of places but of hands: to give directions is to say *you are standing in her tired stretch, keep on until it turns hurried, then ask.*

The work is the work. Everyone presses. It is not labor exactly, or not only — it is the way the world is continued, one palm's width at a time, and it is done at the joints, kneeling where the water stands, drinking the warm iron slip as they go, pressing the tile that will lie under everything forever. They cannot dig, so neither can anything about them go down; their houses are flat, rambling, one brick thick and wide as a family, built on rafts of their own pressed courses, sprawled out like spilled water. Water is drawn from the seams with a blade or a pressed groove. Nothing is stored, because nothing needs to be — the water table is the pantry, and it is everywhere.

Children are made the way tiles are made, which is to say they are *made*, and openly, and slowly. Two makers, sometimes three, sit together at a wet joint for days, drawing slip, pressing a small body into being between their hands — and so every child carries its makers interleaved, one's left hand in the curve of a shoulder, another's right in the arch of a foot, thumbwritings braided through each other for life. No two are alike. They are very clear on this. It is the one thing they would say is sacred, if they had the word, and they don't need it, because the proof is underfoot, horizon to horizon.

Sleep is the risky hour, because their bodies are held the way their walls are held — by attention, their own. A maker who goes slack alone will begin, very slowly, to slump. So they sleep in chains: rows of them in the raft-houses, arms linked, each one holding the next one's shape through the night, a district of mutual attention, trust made physical. And over the whole sprawl of it, the great ones attend.

You will know the great ones when you pass through a held region and find its center. They sit there. They have sat there for longer than anyone will count. Glazed entire — some by their own patient choice, some caught in a firing that ran too hot under the wrong district — black or deep brown, slick when the weather sweats, fixed, unchangeable, thinking at a speed you would need instruments to detect. These are the Holders. A wall one brick thick and two hundred feet tall stands because a Holder is still thinking about it. A staircase with no underside hangs because a Holder has not yet finished the thought that has no underside in it. As long as they attend, weight is a suggestion.

And when a Holder's thought concludes — when the attention moves on, or lets go, or drifts — you hear it. Everyone hears it. The long, soft tearing.

That is what the tearing *is*: not collapse, but the sound of an era of attention ending. And when it comes, every maker on the raft-rows sets down the tile in their hand, and goes still, and listens until it finishes — the way people elsewhere might bow their heads for a bell. Then they walk the slump, all of them, and read it: the fold of the stairway, the way the vault sat down into itself, the exact place the thought let go. It is not a funeral. It is how they learn. The opposite of age here is not youth; it is attention — and every failure is a finished sentence, and they are a people who waste nothing, least of all an ending.

No one among them has died. But they know, in the way you know the edge of a glazefield by the heat coming off it, that their glazed elders are standing at the door of death and have not yet knocked — that fixity, held long enough, is one half of an ending, and the world is still too young, too recent, too attentively held, for the other half to arrive.

So they press on. Blood-warm from below, knee-deep in drizzle, drinking iron water from the seams of everything, laying palm over palm across a floor that will never be finished because the ones who finish things here never leave.
