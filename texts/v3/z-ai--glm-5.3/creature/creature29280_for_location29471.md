What lives on the Level keeps the Level.

That is the first thing to understand, and the simplest, and everything else follows from it the way water follows a slope — slowly, and finding every low place. The Keepers did not come here. Or if they did, the coming was so long ago that the memory of it has been worn down like everything else in this country, planed by the wind into a fact with no edges. What is certain is that they belong to the plateau the way frost belongs to the wool-fields below: not visitors, not invaders, but a condition of the place. Where the land ends its argument, they begin their work.

You will see them at a distance first, and you will mistake them for stones. Everyone does. The plateau is strewn with stones — not the rubble of summits, for the summits are gone, but the residue of them, fist-sized and head-sized and occasionally the size of a sleeping ox, scattered across the dark floor in distributions that look random until you have walked a week among them. Then you begin to notice the spacings. You begin to notice that the stones sit at intervals, that a line of them runs for a day's walk with a deviation no greater than a man's stride, that here they describe an arc, there a slow deliberate spiral, and there — you stop, and the frost crackles under your boot, and you count — a ring, wide as a village, patient as arithmetic.

And some of the stones are not stones. Some of them are crouched.

They are shaped, insofar as the word applies, like the residue of something larger. Keepers stand perhaps the height of a kneeling man, though they do not kneel; they simply are that height, bent forward, their broad backs humped against the wind as if they have been leaning into it so long that the leaning has become their anatomy. Their color is the color of the plateau — that long dark floor color, basalt and old ice and shadow mixed — and their surfaces are worn the same way the land is worn, smooth in the direction the weather comes from, rougher on the lee side where small lichens have taken root in the grain of them. It is possible that this is exactly what has happened. It is possible that the Keepers are not so much creatures now as landforms that remember being creatures. The distinction, up here, is not insisted upon.

What they keep is the levelness.

Listen to the wind up here for a season — you would not, but listen — and you will hear that it is not merely wind. It is inventory. It moves across the plateau and it touches every rise and every fall, and where it finds a place that has lifted — a frost heave, a buried boulder shifting, the slow swelling of ice beneath the dark soil — the Keepers go to it. You will not see them go. You will see a stone in one place on Tuesday and find it crouched over the swelling on Wednesday, and between those two observations there is nothing but night and wind and the honest knowledge that you slept, and that whatever moved, moved while the sky was busy elsewhere.

They press down. That is the whole of the visible work. A Keeper settles its weight over the disobedient ground and stays, for a season or a decade — the time is not measured, up here; there is nothing to measure it against — until the rise has subsided, has remembered that this land's argument with height is finished, has agreed to be floor again. Then the wind takes its inventory, finds the place satisfactory, and moves on, and in its own unmeasured time, so does the Keeper.

They do not eat. There is nothing to eat, and this simplifies them enormously. Some travelers' accounts insist they drink the wind, or the frost, or the thin blue light that stands above the plateau at dawn like water remembering a sea it once belonged to. The Keepers offer no comment. But it is true that after a heavy frost they are wet at their bases, a dark ring of melt around each one, and whether this is the frost's doing or theirs, no one down in the wool-fields has ever been able to say.

They do not speak, but they are not silent. When two of them meet — over some disputed swelling, some ground that has dared to misbehave in the overlap of two Keepers' ground — they stand face to face, or near enough to face, for they do not have faces in any way you would recognize; they have weathered fronts, places where the wearing has been different. They stand there through wind and dark and the enormous indifferent stars, and after days or weeks one yields, or both stay, and the ground between them settles as if embarrassed. People below say they are arguing. It seems more likely, standing among them, that they are agreeing, and that the agreement is so long because everything up here is long, and there is no reason to hurry a sentence when the plateau will not change the meaning of any word in it.

Do not fear them, exactly. They have never harmed a traveler; harm would require an interest in the traveler, and the Keepers have as much interest in you as the land has, which is to say none at all, which is to say the perfect, enormous courtesy of a place that does not need you. Walk among them and you will feel something the wool-field people describe as being *read and filed*. The wind comes off a Keeper's back and it is very slightly warmer than the wind that arrives, the way a stone you pick up is warmer than the ground, or colder, depending — the way anything is that has been somewhere, holding something.

But here is the thing that the travelers do not report, because the travelers do not stay, and it is the thing that the Keepers keep most of all.

The plateau is not finished. Under the dark floor, deeper than frost, deeper than the worn roots of mountains, the old argument continues — the slow continental sulk of stone that rose once and means, someday, on some scale of time that makes the wind look hasty, to rise again. The Keepers are not decorations. They are not the residue of the war; they are its continuation by other means. Every rise they press down is a syllable in an answer being given, over millennia, to the mountains' claim. The land says *up*. The Keepers say *no*, with their whole bodies, forever. The end of summits is not a fact. It is a labor. It is being achieved, moment by moment, by these low dark patient things, and the day the last Keeper is worn away — and they are wearing, all of them, a little, every year, the wind taking its inventory of them too — the plateau will begin, in its unhurried way, to forget what it was told.

So when you come up the last of the slopes at the hour when frost still sits on the wool-fields, and you see the plain go on past the range of meaning, understand what you are looking at. Not the end of summits.

A pause. The longest one ever held. And held by them.
