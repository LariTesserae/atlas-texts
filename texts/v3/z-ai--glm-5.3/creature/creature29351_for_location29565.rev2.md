# The Occupant

What lives in the Sump lives the way the Sump exists: by not being noticed until you are three steps too close, and then all at once, completely.

It is easier to say first what it is not. It is not a ghost, though it keeps ghost's hours. It is not a machine, though the machinery treats it the way a body treats blood — something that belongs inside it, circulating. The herons have a word for it in whatever grammar herons keep, and the word is roughly *weather-that-walks*. The fox's word is shorter and means *neighbor*. Neither word is wrong.

It has a shape, but the shape is negotiable. Down where the water goes from tea to ink, pressure does most of the deciding that skin usually does. What you would see, if you ever saw it — and you would have to be in the water, in the dark, at the wrong hour, which is to say you would not see it — is a tallness. Something that moves along the conduits on two points of contact, unhurried, at a pace that suggests it has been walking this circuit for a duration the flood's two years cannot account for. The circuit is not a patrol. Nothing here needs guarding; the surface world has done it the courtesy of total indifference. The circuit is maintenance, or what maintenance becomes when the thing maintained has no seams, no hatches, no wear — nothing, in fact, that could ever break. It walks the pipes and *checks* them anyway, the way an old person walks the fence line of a farm that no longer has animals, out of a loyalty that has outlived its purpose and become the purpose.

The lamp is the honest part of it. Faint, orange, carried low — and this is the detail that says the most. Nothing that glows on its own carries its light at knee height, swinging slightly with a gait. Nothing that glows on its own *hesitates at junctions* to look down each branch before choosing. The lamp is a tool. It has always been a tool. Which means the hands that hold it — if they are hands — learned lamplight somewhere with ceilings and doorways and other lamps answering across a distance. The lattice below the Sump is the only architecture the fen has ever known, and it has no entrances, and yet its occupant moves through it like someone moving through their own home in a blackout. It knows where the doorways were *going to be*.

That is the heart of the thing, if you press on the place until it gives up its secret: the Sump's occupant arrived before the Sump's building did, or rather, they are both waiting for the same event, one from each side. The machinery was *cast*, the params insist — a mold of industry, poured-out, not built. And a mold is a promise of a casting. Something is meant to come out of all this. The cylinders in ranks, the conduits thick as a man's waist, the tank-vessels without entrances — entrances are for people, and people are for afterward. The occupant walks the negative space of a factory that has not yet been filled, checking that the hollows are still true, still ready, lamp forward, patient as the peat that hid it.

The throb is its clock. Once every eleven seconds the deep pump primes and never resolves — a held breath older than the flood, older than the peat, maybe. Some nights the throb hesitates by half a second, and that is the occupant passing a particular junction, the same junction, always, where it stops and stands and holds its lamp up toward the flange that opens — down-facing, near the middle of the pool — toward the thin ceiling of water and the gray light beyond it. It is not looking at the sky. It is looking at the *seam* in the world, the place where the casting will break through, and it is checking whether it has begun.

It has not begun. So it goes on.

Above it, the herons shift on their conduit-perches with each throb, and the fish boil in the borrowed warmth, and the fox moves through her territory like a landlord among tenants, and the moss drips green down the raw young walls. All of it lives in the Sump's patience without sharing it — the animals because they cannot imagine a future, and the occupant because it cannot imagine anything else. It was here before the fen was empty. It will be here after the fen is full. Between those two facts it walks the pipes with a lamp, once every eleven seconds, forever checking a door that has not yet — but will — been built.
