**What lives in the Throat**

Start with the film, because everything above it stands on it, and because the film is the closest thing this place has to a native — the first tenant, older than the fauna by however long it takes a warm crack to discover what it can grow.

It is almost nothing. A skin of amber and gray on wet stone, a breath's thickness, alive in the way paint is not quite alive and blood is not quite alive. It has never seen the sun and does not know the sun exists. Its whole world is chemistry: sulfur leached from rock that was magma four days ago, iron sweating out of the mountain's young veins, water arriving warm from below and condensing everywhere at once. It eats the planet's interior and turns it into more of itself, and it does this with no urgency at all, because there is nothing else to do and no reason to hurry. Where the water is warmest, the amber goes almost orange, thick enough to matte. Where the water cools toward the walls, it pales to a frost of gray that could be mistaken for mineral deposit, except that mineral deposits don't slowly spread.

Everything else in the chamber is a consequence of the film. Hold that in mind and the ecology resolves into a single sentence: the mountain feeds the scum, the scum feeds the carpet, the carpet feeds the flat ones, and the crabs stand over all of it like landlords who have forgotten they own anything.

**The carpet.** That is the honest name for the grazers, though "swarm" is also true — pale, blind, many-legged, wet because everything here is wet, chitinous because everything small and soft gets eaten. Individually they are almost beneath description: a scraping mouth at one end, legs arranged in forgettable number, eyes never having occurred to them. Collectively they are the second-loudest presence in the pocket, after the drop that falls every few minutes. They move across the film in slow-moving sheets, and the sound of their grazing is a dry whisper, sand poured over sand, and where the sheets overlap the whisper thickens into something you feel on your skin before you hear it. They do not run, ever. Nothing here has ever needed to run from anything faster than a whisper.

They molt the way a fire sheds sparks — constantly, casually, growth being cheap in the warm wet. The shed husks drift in the film of water, gather in the low corners, pile up in gray translucent foam that is, in a way, the largest animal in the chamber: a slowly turning compost of past selves, several bodies deep, indistinguishable from the living swarm at a glance and only gradually distinguishable at any glance, because the living swarm crawls over its own graveyard continuously and does not appear to notice the difference.

Which is what the flat ones count on.

**The flat ones** are not another kind of animal so much as a hole in the account books. Low, wide, the exact color of husk-drift, patient in a way that nothing else here is patient — the grazers are merely slow, the flat ones are *waiting*, which is a different metabolite entirely. They lie in the molt-foam with their edges tucked under until a grazing sheet flows over them, and then the sheet has a widening hole in it, and then the hole closes, and the whisper never changes pitch by a single grain of sand. Predator and prey at this scale have struck a deal so old it isn't even a deal anymore: one silent, both silent, the whole transaction quieter than one falling drop. The drop falls. *Toop.* Somewhere in the foam, something is fat.

And then the crabs.

**The crabs** are the reason the word "wrong" keeps occurring to visitors, and the reason the word "owner" keeps occurring to the rocks. There are perhaps a dozen in the whole chamber, which by the arithmetic of this place makes them rare the way a hermit is rare — not scarce, *deliberately few*, each one holding territory the way you'd hold a tool you had no intention of putting down. They are squat and wet-shelled and the color of the walls, which would make them cryptic if they hid, but they don't hide. They sit in the open beside their works, in plain view of their black working eyes, and watch.

Their hands are the difficult part. Not literally hands — inner limbs, articulate, careful, with opinions. Watch a crab long enough and the word "hand" stops being a metaphor and starts being a description that simply has a different number of fingers than you expected. A crab picks up a shed husk — one of the ten thousand identical husks lying in the foam — and evaluates it, the way a person weighs a possible cup, and either uses it as a scoop to carry water to a dry patch of film it is cultivating, or sets it back down. Not discards. *Sets down*, in a specific spot, possibly for later.

They build. Their dams are small, each stone chosen and wedged and adjusted over days, the diverted streams pooling warmer, the warm pools growing film faster than the open floor. They farm, though the word is ours, not theirs. They crack the mineral nodules that form under dripping seams by tapping stone against stone, deliberately, repeatedly — the only percussion in the chamber, the only sound here that is *made* rather than merely *shed* — to reach the dense amber paste inside, which the carpet can't get at and the flat ones can't be bothered with. When two crabs fight, they fight over the stones. A good stone is a good stone for generations. The pocket has no artifacts because it has no makers; it has owners, and an owner is a maker with a longer memory and worse posture.

And they have an opinion about the stumbles — the hour that takes a day, the day that takes an hour, the daylight shaft skipping like a scratched record. When time stumbles, the streams jump their banks, the pools slop, the dams silt or slump. The crabs rebuild. Not frantically — *without hurry*, stone by stone, as if the stumble were a weather and weather is not something one has opinions about, only responses to. Whether they know time stumbles, or whether they simply know that sometimes the world requires masonry, no one inside can determine. Possibly the grazers dent the syrup of space with their hundred thousand warm bodies, and the stumbles are theirs, unnoticed by them. Possibly the mountain is simply too new to have finished deciding its shape. Possibly — and this is the thought that comes to you crouched by a dammed pool, watching a crab feel your boot with one careful limb, judge the material, and return to work — possibly the whole chamber, film and carpet and hunters and hands, is best understood as a single organism still assembling itself, a gut learning to be a body, and the crabs are what a gut grows instead of a mind when it needs to keep its water where it wants it.

Give it time. The mountain is four days old. Everything in here is still learning what a thing is for.

Your boots are for carrying water.
