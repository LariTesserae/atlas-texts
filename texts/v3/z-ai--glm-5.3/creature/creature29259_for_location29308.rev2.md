# What Lives at the Head of the Ninth Upcast

The first thing to understand is that nobody *lives* here in the way the word usually means. The tenders are gone; the frost keeps their paths like a photograph kept in silver. What lives here now lives in the cracks of an abandoned duty — but it lives completely, whole-heartedly, in the one warm room at the top of the world.

**The fan is the oldest inhabitant, if a machine can be counted**, and by now it should be. Nine blades, each a barn door, each passing the viewing port every twenty seconds with a pressure like a building-sized heart stepping on the whole summit. It has turned ten billion times. It will turn ten billion more. Nothing else here tells time at all except by its beat — it is not merely the clock but the climate, the landlord, the pulse that every living thing has arranged its life around. The moss that lives in the shroud ring's groove, feeding on the blade-tips' dust, has worn the metal glassy. The fan and the moss have reached a settlement. Whether the moss serves the fan or the fan has simply become another substrate for the moss, like a rock, like a log — the summit stopped keeping records before that question arose.

**The moss is the base layer and the landlord.** It upholsters the shaft bearings in place of grease — a fibrous breed, self-lubricating, self-replacing, so that maintenance became gardening generations ago and nobody marked the transition. Under it the rust lies stable, a scab under gauze. The moss has quietly absorbed the last of the tenders' duties: it holds the metal together, it keeps the bearings, it remembers the shape of the work. It is the most patient thing in a place built entirely of patience.

**The garden lives on spillage.** Everything green here is downstream of the city's breath — fig, ferns, orchids, espaliered trees set out by an arborist whose name the frost has outlived, a vine on the fan housing that flowers once every eleven years, enormously, briefly. The soil is a thousand years of the garden composting itself atop plate; dig anywhere and you hit the made. And every plant — fern, fig, orchid, the grass in the grating seams — carries the *same* leaf: one serrated, blunt-tipped, matte-green shape, as though the whole flora descends from a single draft in someone's notebook. Whoever designed this place drew one leaf and issued it to everything, and everything complied. The garden is less an ecosystem than an edition.

**The fauna live entirely inside the breath, and they live in the roar as fish live in water.** Midge swarms hang in the plume in columns the wind redraws like handwriting practice. Comma-sized shrimp thrive past counting in the pans where the dew carries a mineral skin up from below — the entire visible population of the deep world's exhale. Vent martins hawk the updraft and nest along the frost line. Moths upholster the warm housings like grey fur. A hive of bees has lived for generations in a dead instrument cabinet, wax sealing the pressure gauge at 3.4 forever — the only reading on the summit that will never change again, kept by insects.

**The frogs keep the beat.** Broad-mouthed, dark as the condensate, they work the gutter pools and sing in the gaps: one full phrase crammed into the twenty seconds after each blade-pass, then stillness, in meter, for the next twenty. Their whole language is built to fit between heartbeats of a building. When a blade runs flat — Blade 6, the log says, *flat* — the phrase still fits, and they don't appear to notice the difference. Perhaps a frog's world is twenty seconds wide and always has been.

**The spiders are the only fast industry** — flat white, webbing the gutters weekly, torn out by the warm rain weekly, rebuilt weekly. A small economy of ruin and repair, running at a speed nothing else on the summit permits itself.

**And there is a heron. One.** Half-deaf, presumably — though at this altitude deafness may simply be adaptation, the way the tenders went deaf, the way the bees sealed the gauge. It stalks the pans for frogs and shrimp, a predator in an orchard nobody harvests, and at dusk, some days, it blows a single flat horn note that cuts the roar like a blade. Why some days and not others, no one alive knows. It is the only voice here that doesn't wait for the beat. Perhaps it's the only one that can afford not to.

**The animals know the chord before anyone does.** On pitch-days, when the two-kilometer flue drops its interval — the fundamental you feel in the sternum, the roar proper, the thin ring at the top of hearing — the moths rise in a body, the martins follow, the frogs compress their phrases into the longer gaps, the heron sounds its note. They are not reacting. They are reading something the instruments stopped reading when the tenders left. The fauna of the Ninth Upcast are, collectively, the last maintenance crew: they monitor the pitch of the world.

**And the Old Hoist still lives by its timetable.** Three times a week the empty cage rises, waits six hours at the head with its door open, and descends. It is the one thing on the summit that still expects someone. The frost keeps the paths, the moss keeps the bearings, the frogs keep the log — *frogs loud*, the last entry says, and they are, in their way, still filing the report — but the Hoist keeps the appointment. Whatever it was waiting for, it is still waiting.

That is the census, then: one fan, one moss, one leaf in a thousand bodies, shrimp past counting, frogs in meter, martins, moths, one hive at a permanent 3.4, spiders in their weekly economy, one heron with a note it spends rarely, and an empty cage that arrives on schedule. Nobody harvests the orchard. The figs drop and ferment where no hand will find them. Four kilometers down, the city exhales, unaware, and the rain falls upward from underneath, and everything slow is the world and everything quick is alive — and the beat goes on, ten billion times behind it, ten billion more to come.
