The heat arrives first — before the smell, before the sound, before your eyes have adjusted to light. It meets you at the threshold like a held breath let go, and the cold falls off you all at once, like a coat you hadn't noticed you were wearing.

Then the smell finds you. Coal, but under the coal a tar-sweetness of char, and under that hot iron — a mineral smell, like a struck flint, like a coin held in the mouth — and horse, from this morning's shoeing, and linseed oil, and creosote, and, faintly, rosemary: a sprig of it standing in a jar of water on the sill, for reasons no one has ever explained.

Then the sound. The fire eats with small ticking noises, like a slow reader. Something metallic clicks on the far bench, cooling. The flue draws and the room inhales; the room exhales. Water drips somewhere at a long, patient interval. And out of the general warmth a purr resolves, the way a face resolves out of a crowd.

Then the light. Your eyes adjust, and the place comes to you in tiers of ember-colored dark: the orange square of the forge mouth, the dull cherry of iron waiting in the coals, the black-on-black shapes of anvil, bench, barrel — and at the center of all of it, arranged around the fire the way a sentence arranges itself around its verb, the smith.

She is not young, but she is not old the way other people get old. Heat ages most faces; hers it has kept instead, like something stored properly. The apron has gone the color of rust and mahogany. Her forearms are laced with old burns — white, ridged, laid down in layers like sediment, like a shoreline — and if you ask about any one of them she'll tell you a story, cheerful and vague, that you later realize explains nothing.

She moves slowly. Not from age — from thrift. Heat punishes wasted motion, and so she has spent decades removing waste from her gestures until they are as spare as her tools, until every reach is the shortest distance between two needs. Iron cannot be hurried and she cannot either, or rather she can, exactly once, and has learned not to. She reads the metal's color the way you read a page: blood-red for shaping, orange for joining, and that yellow-white, nearly too bright to look at, when the iron forgets that it is iron.

There is a stone jug sunk in a bucket of well water, and she will offer you some without being asked, and it will be the coldest thing you have ever tasted — because your body has already gone hot, and meets the cold like a stranger, all at once and gratefully.

She keeps the fire's hours rather than her own. It sleeps at night under a shovel of ash — banked, she calls it — and she wakes it at first light by raking the coals open, gently, the way you'd wake a large animal you are fond of but do not trust. What lives here, if you want the whole census: the fire, first and oldest. The cat, which has an internal thermometer of astonishing precision and relocates its sleeping spot through the day in slow inches, a sundial in reverse, always one paw tucked, always exactly as close to the forge as a cat can be without being an ex-cat. The dog at the threshold that belongs to no one and everyone, lying in the doorway where inside-heat meets outside-cold, one ear in each world. The swallows, who nest in the second flue — the one she never lights, and has never lit, and has twice defended from repairmen with the poker still in her hand.

And the apprentice. Others came, over the years; the heat sorted them, the way a river sorts stones, and carried them off within weeks. This one stayed. You will know her by the way she no longer flinches — flinching is the first thing the heat cures, and you cannot hammer with a flinch. She sweeps the floor of its scale, the glittering black sand that hammers off hot iron, and keeps it in a jar on the shelf, because she says it looks like a night sky, and the smith has never told her not to.

The fire wants everything. That is the one thing the smith will say unprompted, if you sit long enough in the orange light. Given time, it wants the coal, the iron, the bench, the building, the barrel, the cat, her, the valley, the sky over the valley. Her entire craft is the negotiation between one appetite and everything else in the world. She is the thin plate between the want and the world, and she has stood there so long that the tools have worn to her hands, and the floor has worn to her stance, and the anvil — which was here before the village had a name, and is dished in the middle like a step worn into stone — has worn to the particular arc of her hammer.

Ask her how long she's been here and she doesn't count in years. She thinks about it, wiping her hands. *Forty thousand fires,* she says. *Give or take.*

Cold is her enemy now, and she knows it. In winter, out in the street, she feels naked no matter how many layers she wears; she carries her climate inside her, a woman with her own weather. In summer, when the whole village wilts and complains, she stands in the noon sun and feels almost nothing. It's barely a morning in the shop.

She says smiths die of cold, and laughs, and it is a joke, and it is not. She says the fire will outlive her the way it outlived the man who taught her, and the woman who taught him. She is not morbid about this; it is simply the arrangement. Asked who lives here, really, she'll nod at the forge: *It does. I keep it company.* And it's true that at night, when she rakes the coals together and shovels the ash over — not putting it out; no one has ever put it out, she is only letting it sleep — the seam of red dims under grey like an eye closing, and the bricks go on holding the day's heat into the dark, and the cat relocates to the warm floor, and the building holds its warmth the way a heart holds its beat: slow, banked, patient, saving it all for morning.

So yes — the heat arrives first. It has been arriving here for two hundred years, through every door ever opened onto it, and it will go on arriving after her.

Everything else — the smell, the sound, the woman, the cat, the stranger blinking in the light — is just what the heat has kept, over time, around itself.
