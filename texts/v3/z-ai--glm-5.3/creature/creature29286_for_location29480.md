Osedax, the bone-devourer — named by people who met it only under floodlights, and who named the first species the snot-flower, which is what land poetry sounds like after a long submersion. It has no mouth, no gut, no eyes, and no use for any of them. What it has is what the whale left behind: fat, sealed in mineral. So it gave up eating the ordinary way. Above the bone it raises a plume — red, ribbed, feathered — the only red this plain grows, red because it is packed with hemoglobin tuned to hold oxygen where oxygen is rationed; the abyss's single flower blooms in someone else's blood chemistry. Below, its body unspools into the bone as branching roots, secreting acid at their tips, dissolving tunnels through the mineral without ever biting it, and inside those roots it keeps its livestock: bacteria that can digest what the worm cannot — the lipids, the collagen, the marrow-stored years. The worm does not eat. It farms, and its field is the past.
