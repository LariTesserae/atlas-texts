# What Lives in the Nursery Works

You asked what lives here, and the honest answer is that the first tenant is the water — but it will be easier to start with the people, because they are the ones who will meet you at the bottom of the ladder, holding up a lamp, shaping a slow word with their hands.

## The Crews

They are few, and they are shaped by the noise. Everyone who stays past a season develops what they call *the set of the shoulders* — a slight forward curl, the head tilted as if listening, which most of them are, always, even the ones who can no longer hear. The half-deaf elders are the strangest and calmest residents of the Works. They have been down so long that the roar has become their native weather. They navigate by vibration the way the fish navigate by pressure; a man who has run the same pump for eleven years can tell you from across the chamber, by the feel of it in his boot-soles, when a bearing is going, and will be walking toward it before the needle-tenders have noticed anything wrong. Send one of these men up to the surface on furlough and he will stand in the open air, in the reeds, under the enormous silence, and find it unbearable. He will be back down the ladder before the week is out. The surface, the elders say, is where the noise is hidden from you, and it is worse than the noise you can hear.

They talk in lamp-signals at distance and in hand-shapes up close, and the hand-language has grown into something the crews no longer think of as improvised. It has grammar now. It has jokes. There are words in it for things that exist only down here — a flat flutter of the fingers for *the wall is weeping faster than yesterday*, a slow closing fist for *the pool is rising*, a particular pinch-and-pull that means, roughly, *the cave grew overnight and I saw where*. Children of the crew members learn it at home, above ground, playing at it in the marsh villages, and some of the old surveyors believe it is quietly escaping into the daylight world, one family at a time.

The newcomers are easy to spot: they are the ones still trying to talk. They shout, the first month, uselessly, their words ground up in the machinery's roar, and they go home with sore throats. Then one day they stop shouting, and you can see the moment it happens — they stand on the catwalk with their mouth shut and their hands beginning, shyly, to move, and after that they are crew.

There are roles. The **pump-tenders** are the closest thing the Works has to a priestly class; they sit with their machines for shifts longer than any sane schedule would permit, and they develop attachments — a man and his pump, twenty years, and when the pump is finally decommissioned and cut apart for salvage, the tender will take a small piece of its valve-gear and keep it, and no one remarks on this, because they have all done the same. The **shoring crews** live with their arms above their heads, driving the iron rods up into the sagging clay, and they move like patient dancers, and they say they can feel the ceiling *considering* — a word they insist on — whether to fall, and that a well-placed rod is a kind of argument. The **chalk-walkers** are the surveyors who go the farthest, out past the lamplight, into the chambers where the drip-column grows. They carry the least and hear the most. They come back quieter than they left.

And there is one role that isn't assigned, that simply accrues to whoever happens to be standing nearest when it happens: the one who holds the lamp at the sinkhole shaft at dusk, when the bats go out. Someone must, because the bats will not use the shaft while a lamp is uncovered at its mouth, and the flood of them has to be managed or they tangle in the headframe cables and die there by the hundreds. So a crew member stands each evening at the shaft head with the lamp shuttered, in the absolute dark, and feels the colony pass — the wind of forty thousand wings, the high grain of shrieking that is felt behind the eyes — and it has become understood, without ever being decided, that this is an honor. Crew members have gotten into slow, silent, hand-shaped arguments over whose turn it is.

## The Tenants Who Were Here First

But the people are recent tenants, and they know it, and it affects their manners. The crews behave toward the animals the way you behave toward people who were sitting in the room before you arrived.

The **isopods** — the white ones that fur the walls — are the ground layer of everything. They graze the film of water and the film of everything the water carries, and their numbers are indecent. Where they are densest, the walls seem to breathe, because a wall covered in living grain is never perfectly still. The crews have a custom about them: you do not walk on the wet parts of the path if you can help it, not because anyone ordered it, but because the faint crunch underfoot is the only sound down here that is *yours*, that the machinery doesn't cover, and it is the one sound most of them cannot stand. Old-timers will take a long detour across a dry gravel bank to avoid it.

The **amphipods** in the sump-pools are the Works' little opportunists. They live around the pump outflows for the warmth, and they have become, functionally, part of the machinery's exhaust — the crews call the gray swirl of them *the settling*, as in *the settling's thick tonight, Number Four must be running hot*. They are watched the way farmers watch swallows.

The **fish** are the ones the crews love, though love is never the word used. Eyeless, translucent, diagram-like, they hang in the still water below the falls in their unhurried schools, and they have no fear at all, because nothing in the cave's history has ever taught them fear of lamps. A fish will come to a submerged lamp and rest beside it, inside the halo, its organs glowing faintly with the borrowed light. The crew members who see this generally do not report it. The chalk-walkers have a hand-sign for the fish that is also, in certain contexts, the sign for *unhurried*, for *it will keep*.

The **bats** are the commuting population. They live here, but they live above ground too, in shifts, and the cave goes on breathing them out and back like slow gills. Their guano cones, the tallow-fungus, the mites, the mites' own tenants — this is the vertical food web, compressed into arm's reach, and the crews live inside it literally. A worker at dinner on a gravel bank is eating at a table set within the same cubic meter as a functioning ecosystem the size of a forest floor. The mites get in the bread. Nobody mentions them. You learn to eat without looking closely.

## The Tenant Who Is the House

And then there is the last resident, the one the Works exists to serve and to frustrate, which is the cave itself — the *becoming* tenant, the one that is not yet fully here. The crews treat it as a person. Not whimsically; practically. The cave has moods, in the only sense that matters, which is that its behavior has patterns and those patterns bear on survival. The shoring crews speak of the ceiling *considering*. The chalk-walkers speak of the cave being *in a good year* — dissolution rates up, new chambers forming, survey stakes going in fast — or *sulking*, when the dissolution slows and the water threads thin to glass-rod fineness and everyone goes quiet and checks the weep-holes twice as often.

The unborn chamber with the drip-column is the cave's sanctum, and the crew's own doctrine about it has settled without any decision, like all their decisions: they do not measure it. It is felt to be the one thing down here that is going right *without them*. Every centimeter of the rest of the cave is a negotiation, a holding-open, an argument made with iron and pumps. The thin white column, wrist-width, growing at a humanly measurable pace in a chamber no light has ever entered except by lantern, is the only part of the process that is proceeding as it would have if no one had ever come down the ladder. The chalk-walkers go out there sometimes, on the far end of a survey, alone, and stand in the lantern-dark near it. They come back with nothing to say — nothing that could be shaped in the hand-language anyway — and the others see their faces and leave them alone.

That is what lives in the Nursery Works. A few dozen people shaped by noise into gentleness and patience; a hundred billion small pale tenants who do not know the people are there; and one vast unfinished thing, half-born, wet, growing and collapsing at once, whose childhood the whole enterprise — the pumps, the rods, the chalk lines creeping down the walls, the hands shaping slow words in the lamplight — exists to escort.
