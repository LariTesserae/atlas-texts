What lives on the Steading Plain is easy to miss, because almost none of it moves, and none of it has a face.

The census, taken properly, runs to three residents — or one resident in three offices, depending on how you count. There are the vents. There are the groves. And there is the ground itself, which is not usually granted residency by visitors but which does most of the living.

**The vents** are the elders. Each chimney is an individual the way a note is an individual: distinct, but only meaningful as part of the chord. They breathe in ranks, hundreds to a field, and though no two inhale on the same schedule, the field as a whole keeps a respiration you could set a calendar by if calendars meant anything here — a slow tidal exchange of warm mineral air, sweet as the inside of a new kettle, felt in the sternum rather than the ear. A vent has no sense organs, no boundaries worth the name, no youth or age. It was never born and will never die; it was *opened*, like a mouth in rock, and it will stay open on the same stroke forever. What it does — the only thing it does — is move the interior of the world up into the light, a few hours at a time, at ten revolutions a day. If you insist on calling this a life, you must accept a life with no events in it: no feeding, no fleeing, no mating, no misfortune. Just the tide. Just the breath that was never learned and so can never be forgotten.

Around them, the mineral terraces are the vents' own slow skin — shelved, tiled, growing at the far edge exactly as fast as it wastes at the near, a body maintained to the gram. Not a home the vents built. A part of them, the way a shell is part of the animal, laid down in public.

**The groves** are the visible citizens, and they are the closest thing the Plain has to persons.

Each tree is a sealed vessel, and that phrase should be taken more literally than it sounds. Every trunk is plated and enameled like a tank; every leaf is a waxed trowel, glossy as if dipped; and inside that skin the tree keeps its entire water — not *its share* of water, its *entire* water, a closed hydraulic cycle condensing and raining privately onto itself forever. A tree here is not a participant in a landscape. It is a building-sized terrarium that happens to have taken root. The dry world outside its bark is, to the tree, simply weather it has declined to have.

They are lush the way nothing in a waterless country has a right to be — canopies of deep, almost black-green, domed, crowded with health — and the lushness is entirely self-addressed. Nobody grazes them. Nothing with a mouth lives on the Plain, so every leaf on every tree is entire, un-nibbled, perfect: a perfection with no audience and no purpose, which is either the saddest fact about the groves or the most dignified, depending on whether you think perfection requires a witness. The trees, having never met one, have not formed an opinion.

They do reproduce, in their fashion, and the fashion tells you what kind of creatures they are. In the afternoon heat the seed-pods split with a sound like a knuckle cracking — the loudest event for miles — and the heavy brass-bright seeds roll out downslope until the ground wants them, and the wind, which owes nothing to anyone, finishes the delivery. Pollination was never anyone's job. When a tree bothers with flowers at all, it opens its wax blossoms for nobody, on no schedule you could call a schedule, and they never wilt on time. These are not plants struggling in a desert. These are plants that *opted out* of the economy — the give-and-take of nectar and grazer and rot — and sealed themselves shut, and have been sufficient ever since. Their nearest relatives are not the dry-land survivors of other plains but aquariums, libraries, vaults: things built to hold.

And the grove floors — ankle-deep in leaves that fell decades or centuries ago and never rotted, because there is no water to rot with and no mouth to eat them — make each stand its own archive. Resin-sweet, preserved, thinned into stone at precisely the rate of falling. Every grove is a library that shelved itself, in a script nobody reads, balanced to the gram.

**The ground itself** is the largest resident and the hardest one to describe, because most of what it does happens at rates you were not issued senses for. It is young — genuinely new, casting-fresh, seam-lines and flash still on the stone, no rain ever having taken a file to it — and it is being pushed up out of the world's interior like a slow piston on its outstroke: a finger's width a thousand years, forever, never arriving. It is the only living thing here with a *direction*, and even that direction ends nowhere. Permanently mid-birth. At night, as the top millimeter of rock lets go of the day, the whole plateau ticks — *tick*, a pause, *tick* — stone counting itself down in degrees, a clock measuring nothing, striking the hours of nothing. That tick is as close as the ground comes to a voice.

So this is the population: breathers, sealed vaults, and one vast slow engine underfoot, running at steady state so exact that from any vantage, at any hour, over any lifetime, the census never changes. Nothing is born here in the ordinary way and nothing dies in the ordinary way. Nothing eats, nothing flees, nothing sings. The audible world is nearly the whole of it: leaves knocking in a rare wind with a small dry clatter like fingernails on china, and the knuckle-crack of the pods, and the tick.

And the defining fact of every resident — the one they all share, the one you feel last and feel hardest at the rim in the bronze hour, with the terraces flat with light and the groves burning green against the pale stone — is that none of them needs anything. Not food, since there is no economy. Not defense, since there is nothing to defend against. Not witness, since there is no product to witness and no perfection that requires an audience. The machinery was not built and will not be attended. It has never once been late, and here there is no such thing as late.

The plateau breathes out. The groves hold their water. The ground rises a finger's width, holding forever at the same moment of its own birth.

Nothing anywhere notices. That is the whole of their lives.
