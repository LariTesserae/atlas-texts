Start the census at the bottom, because that's where you'd start, bent over a shaded groove at dawn — and because the census is going to keep changing scale on you until the word *resident* stops meaning what you brought here with it.

**The mats.** Gray-green, low, soft as felt, gripping the damp grooves in the channel walls. They don't drink the rain; the rain is too much, too fast, too rare. They drink the night — the beading, the condensation, the slow sweat that gathers on every shaded surface and runs down grooves that may or may not have been cut for them. Their whole posture is downward-facing: leaves pressed flat to the wall like hands on a table, roots that are less roots than grips. When a groove dries, a mat does not die in any way you'd recognize. It thins to a paper line and waits — a season, several seasons, the length of a drought — and if the thread ever comes back it swells again in a single night, as if it had only been holding its breath. Even here, at the very bottom of the census, the local talent is patience.

**The stalks.** Jointed, upright, growing from the cracked margins of the basins, their leaves reduced to scales, their whole spare architecture arranged around one principle: catch the drip, not the rain. A stand of them beneath a sweating overhang will all lean the same direction, tilted toward the water the way a congregation tilts toward whatever it listens to. They live where the basins breathe out; they are the only things here that visibly *prefer* anywhere.

**The reeds.** The one extravagance. Tall by local standards, which is to say knee-high, growing in the deepest channels during the wet season, dying in the dry, leaving pale thatch that stands for years in the windless air. Nothing eats them. That fact deserves its own pause. Nothing eats *anything* here — there are no mouths in this ecology except the basins, and the basins only drink. So a reed's death is not consumed, not claimed, not passed along. It is simply retired. The thatch is the plain's only monument: a record that something lived and then didn't, standing unbothered until the terraces rise and the accretion takes it down into the strata like everything else. Live, die, stand, be filed. The whole drama, and somehow it is enough.

That's the visible census, and it is nearly over before it starts, and if you stopped there you'd conclude that this continent of fired red masonry is essentially uninhabited — a building with three kinds of very small tenant and no landlord.

But you broke off a piece of the wall. You shouldn't have, but you did, and you saw the interior: banded, layered, like the cross-section of a reef or a wasp nest. Sit with that comparison, because it is not decoration. Reefs are made by creatures whose bodies *are* the reef — the structure and its makers are the same stuff, and you cannot point to the animal without pointing at the wall. The cross-section here is telling you the same thing. Layer by layer, cell by cell: this material is not merely where something lives. It is the something, or the something's remains, or the something's ongoing work — and after a while the distinction between those stops mattering too.

This is why there is no maker in sight. It's not that the maker left. The maker is the sight. When you look across a terrace you are looking at a crowd so fine-grained it reads as surface; when the ground "practices building in its sleep," these are the sleepers — a metabolism spread through the entire substance of the plain, so slow and so distributed that its intention doesn't register as intention, the way your own blood rebuilding you doesn't register as yours. The plain was never abandoned mid-construction. There is no missing crew. The walls are the crew. The kiln-heat rising out of the ground is not waste heat from some absent forge — it is the forge, self-fired, curing itself as it secretes itself, an oven that is its own bread.

So if you insist on a single entry for the census — one name, one resident — it is the plain itself. One body, sea-sized, sunken toward its unfinished basin floor. Its anatomy is not hidden: the basins are its drinking-pores, glazed-rimmed, catching rain in the warm sheets that sweep through; the channels are its vasculature; the condensation grooves its capillaries, carrying thin bright threads always downward, always *on their way somewhere*, which is the closest thing this place has to a pulse. The salt blooms feathering across the drying basin floors are its breath drying on its own lips. The soft collapse of an overhang that has finally cured long enough to shed its weight is a scale coming loose. And the southern frontier — the raw, damp, ankle-deep margin where the ground is slowly, visibly rising — is its growing edge, its skin, the only part of it that is young. Everything else is age. The terraces that will be stranded high and dry, the channels that will tilt and reverse, the blooms that will be buried and become strata: that is not decay. That is what growth looks like from inside a thing that grows at the pace of geology.

Is it one organism or a colony of uncountable ones? The question dissolves on contact, the way it does with reefs. Continent-sized, it may not have a self at all — only a habit, executed by every particle of its substance in unison: *make more of this*. Which, if you think about it, is the oldest and least negotiable instruction life ever carries. It eats nothing. It excretes nothing but heat and frost- Lost salt. It never moves. It just persists, and thickens, and extends itself brick by patient brick toward a sea it is building the floor of.

Its permitted fauna — the only things that move under their own power — are water, wind, and heat, and they are less its inhabitants than its circulation. The rain is its one loud event, the drumhead roar across a continent of clay, and everything after that roar — the dripping, the ticking, the long quiet — is the sound of the body taking a drink that will last it until the next one.

And then there is you. For as long as you stay, you are the largest moving thing on the whole plain, the only animal, the only creature that can leave — and you can feel yourself being recruited: walking slower, noticing the new crack, the widened bloom, starting to think in the plain's time. For those days you live here too. Not as a tenant. As a witness, which may be the one thing this place has never had and cannot make for itself.

So: who lives here? A census that ends by dissolving. Mats, stalks, reeds — tenants of the drip. An uncountable microscopic workforce whose bodies are the walls and whose walls are the work. One enormous slow builder that is all of these at once and the ground besides, asleep, growing, warm. Water, wind, and heat as its blood and its weather. And you, briefly, before you climb the northern ramps and go.

Nothing here is separate from where it lives. That is the final answer, and the place has been saying it the whole time in the only voice it has — the tick of a basin drying, half a kilometer away, in the long quiet.
