# Who Lives in Kettlebray

## The People

Several hundred of them, and they are — they will tell you this themselves, with a certain damp pride — *thick-skinned and thin-blooded* in equal measure. Kettlebray folk run hot in the way of people who live inside something else's metabolism: their skin stays warm, their tempers stay slow, and they think in long scales by profession and by inheritance. Ask a Kettlebray grower when the lane will be widened and she'll say "before my granddaughter's wedding, likely," and this is not evasion. It is scheduling.

They call themselves by trade-generations rather than families. There are the **Weirkeepers**, who tend the weeping mortar-joints and can read a weir's pitch the way a physician reads a pulse — an old Weirkeeper will wake at night because the High Spout has gone a half-tone flat, and go out in the second rain to find the blockage. There are the **Kettlemen** (and they are insistent that the word covers women and children too, since the Kettle takes everyone's green waste and gives everyone's walls their heat — the Kettle is a communal ancestor, half machine and half elder). There are the **Graftlords**, who hold the training-cuts and the contracts, whose ledgers are not books but *the trees themselves* — a Graftlord proves inheritance by walking you to the graft her great-grandmother made and pointing.

And there are the **Eel-Consultants**, who are not what the name suggests, or perhaps exactly what it suggests, depending on how long you've lived here.

## The Growers

The heart of the town is the growers, and they have a specific quality that takes visitors a while to name: **patience that looks like authority**. A grower in Kettlebray does not command; she *arranges*, the way you arrange a climbing vine — with ties, with gentle pressure applied over years, with the constant low assumption that the thing being arranged will eventually agree with you because it is easier to grow along a trellis than against one.

They are also, by any outsider's measure, slightly odd in their attention. A grower can walk through the wet market and tell you, without breaking stride, which wall is sweating more than usual, which cistern-gourd is filling too fast, which stretch of rhizome street is running a degree warm. They carry this attention like a second set of eyes. It makes them terrible at card games and very good at funerals, which in Kettlebray are grafting ceremonies — the dead are planted under the host-trees' roots with a cutting from their own garden in their hands, and it is considered good luck, decades later, to find their shade.

## The Children

Kettlebray children are raised *vertically*. They learn to climb before they learn to write, and their games are all elevation games — gourd-tag through the rain-catch galleries, weir-songs where you memorize the pitches and get dunked when you're wrong. They know the town by its drips the way other children know a town by its streets. A Kettlebray child of eight can tell you which room her aunt is in by the sound of the cistern, and thinks this is normal, and is right.

The adolescents go through a period the town calls **greening**, usually around twelve or thirteen, when they take a training-cut of their own — a small project, a hedge to shape, a channel to convince. Some of them never recover from it, in the best sense: they become growers, and the greening becomes their life's work. Others outgrow it and go to the rollers, the presses, the market. But every adult in Kettlebray, whatever their trade, has one thing they trained, decades ago, that is still growing on its own now — and they check on it, when they pass, the way you'd check on a child.

## The Otters

The otters under the Millrun have been on the town's books for six generations. Nobody remembers who first hired them or what currency was agreed upon, but the arrangement persists: the otters clear the channel-grates of debris and drop fish at the kitchen doors of whoever has been feeding them eggshell (a mineral supplement; eels need it; the otters have somehow learned that the humans know this and will trade for the favor). The current family is headed by a large female the town calls **Headwoman**, and there is an ongoing, low-grade theological dispute about whether she is a resident, an employee, or a minor deity. All three positions have advocates.

## The Eels

The eels are consulted. This is all anyone will say to your face.

The actual practice — which you will learn by the third week, because nobody in Kettlebray can keep a secret past the second — involves the **Eel-Readers**, a small hereditary line who keep the channel-eels in long dark tanks near the root market. When a question is genuinely stuck — a boundary dispute, a marriage that has soured beyond repair, a training-cut two growers both claim — the question is read to the eels, formally, at dusk, and the Readers interpret the way the eels move.

Whether this works is a matter the town has settled in its usual fashion: by not asking. What is observable is that the Eel-Readers are *uncannily good mediators*, that their rulings are almost always accepted, and that they never, ever rule in a way that would require anything to happen quickly. Whatever the eels are, they are on the side of the long view.

## The Host-Trees

And then, of course, there are the landlords.

Nobody in Kettlebray would say the trees are *people* — that would be presumptuous in both directions — but everybody behaves as if they are *someone*. Growers speak to them. Not in the way of prayer; in the way of a foreman addressing a very senior colleague: with respect, with a running commentary of intentions, with occasional apologies. When a host-tree drops a branch, it is *discussed*. When one puts out new growth into a window someone's grandmother grafted there sixty years ago, the window loses — the town cedes it with a kind of embarrassed courtesy, and the family builds elsewhere, and forty years later the children of that family will say, without bitterness, "the tree wanted the light."

The trees are the oldest citizens and the slowest. They were here before the Kettle, before the weirs, before the word *Kettlebray* was anything but a joke about a bowl of land. They will be here after. And the people who live inside them — warm in the tree-heated walls, wet in the tree-filtered air, loud in the tree-shaped acoustic — know, in the settled marrow-deep way the place teaches, that they are not the town's owners, only its current tenants, keeping the lease in trust for the next four generations of everything alive.

---

That is who lives in Kettlebray: people who graft for grandchildren, children who navigate by ear, otters on payroll, eels in advisory capacity, and trees who own everything and never once have mentioned it.
