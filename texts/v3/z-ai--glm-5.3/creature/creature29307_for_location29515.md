The people here are called Gatherers, though they didn't name themselves that; the lowlands did, and the name came up the rack line on a crate stencil and stuck. Among themselves they mostly say "we," and specify by trade only when a stranger asks.

There are about four thousand of them, and the first thing you notice is the slowness of their movements — not languor, but economy. Every walkway is wet. Every surface is either running, dripping, or about to be. They move the way people move on boat decks, testing each step with the ball of the foot, and after enough generations of this their ankles have gone strange: wide, high-arched, almost prehensile. A Gatherer can stand flat-footed on a grating pitched ten degrees and never shift weight. Down in the lowlands they walk like sailors and are known for it.

Their faces have gone strange too, in the flat grey light. The pupils sit wide, habitually dilated, so that in any real daylight they squint and water and look half-blind, and the tram passengers who come up for the market are always startled by how the workers turn from their gutters to look at them — grey-eyed, unblinking, all iris. But their eyes are not the organ they trust. Light lies on the Shelf; the grain of grey changes a dozen times a day and the same gutter can be luminous at noon and invisible at one. They navigate by touch and sound. Greeting is a flat palm pressed to the forearm. Every Gatherer can identify every person they know by footfall — each walkway of the Works sounds different under each person's stride — and the deaf ones among them, of whom there are more than the lowland doctors can account for, read the Works through their soles the way the others read it through their ears.

Their calendar is the fog tide. Twice a day the Notch pours, and a Notch-watcher sounds the spill-bell — a deep drowned note from a tank struck underwater, because a dry bell up here is considered rude, startling — and the whole Works reorganizes around it the way a harbor reorganizes around the tide. Nothing on the Shelf is scheduled by the clock. When a Gatherer says "morning," she means the spill. When she says "yesterday," she means two spills ago, and if you point out that this is ambiguous, she'll look at you with genuine confusion: nothing here is ambiguous if you were paying attention to the spill.

The trades are hereditary, measured in meters. A family's fortune is stated as channel — the Marchetti hold two hundred meters of the seventh gallery, moss and gutters and the sluice-gate at the end, and to ask a Gatherer what her family is worth is to be told a length. The trades themselves are simple and endless: the guttermen who keep the channels true, walking their lengths with a level and a mallet; the moss-wrights, mostly women, who tend the filter-beds the way shepherds tend flocks, breeding varieties — the coarse gold-green strainer-moss for the outer beds, the black felt-moss for the last trays, which drinks so fine it takes the taste of iron out of the water; the digester-keepers, who live down among the buried tanks with the rot-sweet smell of willow chip in their clothes forever, and who are treated with a respect that shades, at the edges, into something like awe, for reasons no one will explain to a visitor on the first day.

The water itself is the crop, and the lowlands know it. It comes down the mountain in flasks and demijohns, tasted of nothing whatsoever, which is its entire virtue — the only water in the region that has never touched mineral, never sat in the ground, never been anyone's runoff. The black loam is the other export's shadow: no, the loam stays. Understand this early, because the Gatherers will not tell you, and you will find out anyway: the digesters take more than spent moss and willow chip. Their dead go in. Not all, not by law — there's no law up here, only habit with the force of law — but nearly all, and the loam the beds are filled with, the loam that grows the willow and the nurse-moss and the fat pale roots in the tray-gardens, is dark because it is everybody. When a Gatherer transplants a nursery tray she does it bare-handed. When a lowland official once suggested the practice was unhygienic, the whole delegation was quietly shown a tray of new seedlings, and the wright who held it said only, "This is my grandmother, she's doing well," and no one has raised the matter since.

The children are raised in the gutter-nurseries like everything else, and it shows. They play the spill: when the fog comes over the Notch they line up along the leading edge and race it, sprinting gallery to gallery ahead of a wall of weather, and the fast ones — the ones who know the shortcuts, the pontoon cuts, the roof-routes over buried tanks — carry those skills into adulthood, where they become the runners, the Works' nervous system. The runners carry messages because sound does, actually, carry — but the Works is nine kilometers of glistening plumbing, and every shout sets off echoes in a dozen galleries, and so messages are hand-delivered, sealed with wax against the wet. Gatherer children can tell, by the sound alone, which bell has rung, from a kilometer off: spill, fire (rare, feared; wet everywhere except the dry houses), a fall into a sluice, the arrival of the rack line.

They have, visitors always note, an enormous vocabulary of grey. It's not that they perceive more colors; it's that the distinctions they care about aren't the ones the lowlands cares about. Lid-grey means work low, stay under cover, the fog won't rise today. Tide-grey means the deck below has gone strange and the spill may come early or not at all. Fish-grey — no one can explain the word's origin anymore — means the light will be good in an hour and there will be an hour after that, and this is when the tray-gardens get their real work done, because the willows and the moss and the people are all phototropic under everything, all leaning toward whatever light exists, and in fish-grey, for two or three hours, the Shelf is almost beautiful even by lowland standards.

And there are the others. Not many, but enough that no Gatherer would call the Works theirs alone.

The spill brings up midges from the lowlands in the millions, drowned and drowning, and the midges bring up swallows — lowland swallows that learned, generations ago, that twice a day a mountain pours out food. They nest under the sluice-platforms now and have gone a different color than their cousins below, a slatey white-throat under the wing that the Gatherers use to gauge the spill: when the swallows go up and start working high, the fog front is half an hour out. There are eels in the still-tanks, escapees from some forgotten aquaculture, now feral and fat and caught by the children with string and moss-bait and regarded as an entirely ordinary thing to have for dinner. There are the fog-spiders, tiny, almost weightless, that ride the spill in and spend the drying hours rebuilding their sheet-webs across the gutter-mouths — the guttermen curse them and the moss-wrights defend them, because the spiders' webs catch mist like a finer filter and the wrights have measured the yield — and there is, on the far northwest shelf where the Works stops and the rock begins, a colony of wild moss that is not bred from anything. It is what the filter-moss was made from. Nobody tends it. Nobody is quite allowed to touch it. When a bed fails somewhere — blight, a bad strain, a channel gone sour — the wrights go out there, hatless, in the custom of asking, and take a fistful, and the wild bed has never yet refused them.

That is the arrangement, and the Gatherers will describe it flatly if pressed: the fog comes up twice a day to be drunk, the moss drinks it, the gutters carry it, the tanks keep it, the digesters turn the spent moss into ground for the willow that feeds the digesters, and the people — the people are what all of this grew, somewhere along the way, to need: hands small enough for a tray, ears good enough for a grating, a family patient enough to hold two hundred meters of channel for a hundred years. A Gatherer asked directly whether the Works belongs to them will look out over the glistening bowl, all of it steaming faintly as the spill begins, all of it drinking, and say: nothing up here belongs to anybody. It just lives together. Then the spill-bell sounds from the drowned tank, and everyone turns toward the Notch.
