# Who Lives in the Vented Dish

Start at the bottom, because everything here understands seniority.

**The film** came first and will go last. It is a single thing wearing the word "colony" the way the works wear the word "plant" — the nearest fit, not the truth. It creeps over warm metal at the pace of a slow hour hand, iridescent, brightening at every bloom like a struck match held to a wet pane, and it does not think, but it *waits*, which is most of what thinking is for. Where a pipe runs warm, the film finds it within days. Where a pipe runs cold — where the old coolant lines go naked and bare through layers of living shell — the film has drawn back from that boundary with the precision of a tide line, and it has held that line for longer than the markings stamped on the plates beneath it have been unreadable. This is the whole ecosystem's first lesson, learned at the level of chemistry: water burns. Everything that lives in the dish learned it from the film, or learned it from something that learned it from the film, and so the film is, in the way that matters most, an ancestor.

**The grazers** are the citizens. Limpet-shaped, chiton-shaped, and neither — domed shells of iron sulfide laid down from the metal they stand on, so that a column and its population are the same substance in two moods. A grazer is a shell with a rasp in it and an appetite the size of the world's patience. The tick of ten million radulae is the sound of the dish digesting itself — iron by iron, sulfur by sulfur, and, at the basin's rim, grain by siltstone grain, because iron is the sweet crop and rust is its dessert and the plain's gray-gold crust is the bread that holds the meal together. They shingle themselves three and four deep and die where they stand, cemented under their own descendants, and this is not a tragedy to them; it is masonry. A grazer's ambition, if the word can be pried loose from its comfortable meanings, is to become floor. The ones who succeed are in the strata, thick bands and thin, a ledger of hot years and lean ones, and at the very bottom of every cut face, the flat things with right angles — the works' bones, the first soil this reef ever ate.

Above them, the air.

**The plume-flies** are the dish's weather made animal. Nothing about them is flight as the old world practiced it; they are riders of a single updraft, born on the vent heads, dying within forty meters of them, their mica-dusted membranes less wings than sails. In the still hours they stack on the pipe mouths in rosettes so dense the metal underneath disappears, and a pipe mouth wearing its rosette looks like a flower that has been dead for ten thousand years and has finally, in the warmth, decided otherwise. When the breath comes they rise as one body, thousands of them, spiraling the warm column with their mouth-fans spread, harvesting molecules straight from the stream — and they are the dish's gulls too, because they follow the sweepers' wakes and dive on the wreckage. An animal whose whole life is spent inside a column of warm gas, riding it up and settling down it, never once touching the cool world outside: of everything here, the plume-flies are the ones who would be most astonished to learn that the sky is large.

**The stridulators** live in the gaps — the hollow dead shells, the knocked-loose plates, the resonant junk of the reef — and they are its musicians. Small drummers, courtship and territory and warning run together into one continuous performance, because in a place where the calendar has exactly one event, every message is about the same thing. Their chorus moves across the reef floor in fronts, a wave of ticking that rises and collapses, and it is possible, sitting in the fallen compressor hall with the dust-slats of light coming through, to hear a single stridulator start the whole dish going — one tick, answered, answered, answered, until the rain-on-tin sound has crossed four hundred meters of shell and comes back around the far rim to the drummer that started it. Whether the founder hears its own echo as a rival, no observer knows. The stridulators, notably, ignore the Answerer. Whatever the Answerer speaks, it is not their grammar.

**The heatworms** are the dish's roots, or its plumbing, or its handwriting — tubes following the warm lines of dead pipe with the fidelity of water finding a channel, looping across open floor like dropped cable, threading the ranks of columns from base to crown. A worm that finds a warm line never leaves it. A worm that finds a cooling line dies on it, and is grown over, and the living tube still marks the dead one, the way a scar marks a wound. Under every visible loop there are said to be kilometers of it, a shadow-reef in the pipe racks, and the whole of it twitching slightly at the bloom.

**The mites** are the smallest and the most numerous and the least describable, because they are less individuals than a medium. Around the big bodies they hang in visible clouds, thick enough to breathe, thick enough that the grand grazer's mite-cloud has its own temperature gradient and its own drift, a small weather system with a shell at the center of it. What they do — scavenge, parasitize, pollinate the film, carry sulfur from surface to surface — the reef does not seem to require an answer to. The mites are the dish's hands.

**The sweepers** are the reason the reef sleeps with one half of itself awake. Long, low, leggy, front legs raked back like a finger about to be dragged through grain, mouthparts working with the flat conviction of bolt-cutters — they hunt in the still hours, when the plume-flies are grounded and slow and the grazer carpets are packed tight and trusting. A sweeper does not chase; it *harvests*, moving through the shingles the way time moves through anything, and where it has passed there is a wake of overturned shells and a fan of plume-flies already falling on it. It is the only animal in the dish whose body plan is built for speed rather than endurance of cold, and that tells you what the dish's one genuine danger is: not the heat, not the poison of water, not the dark — the *interval*. Everything here is either armored against the bloom or armored against its absence, and nothing is armored against both, and the sweepers live in the seam.

**The grand grazer** is the dish's capital city and its oldest citizen and its furniture. The size of a shed, in the deepest warm corner of the hall against the bore head, shelled in decades of accumulated epibionts — grazers grown onto it, stridulators housed in it, film brightening on its back at every bloom, heatworm loops descending from its flanks — it is itself a landmark, itself substrate, a reef one animal wide. It has not moved more than twenty meters in living memory because it has never needed to; the warmth comes to it the way a river comes to a lake. Its breath is the slow bellows under everything, the base note of the dish, and its mite-cloud is its flag. When it finally dies — and it will; even patience ends — it will not fall. It will simply stop being distinguishable from the architecture, and the reef will grow its next layer over what used to be the oldest living thing in the world's last loud room.

And then there is the one that is not fauna, exactly, and not machine, exactly, and the reason no count of the dish's residents ever quite closes.

**The Answerer** lives in the deep warm pipe ranks — "lives" being the nearest fit, again — and it speaks only to the works. It ticks back a half-second late. It knocks when the valves knock. It answers a visitor's two taps on a rail with two taps slightly wrong, slightly alive, and then an hour of trying to make it happen again. Whether it is a mimic that learned percussion from cooling metal, a parasite of rhythm, something that courts in the builders' dead idiom, or something that was making these sounds before the builders came and taught the machines — the dish keeps the answer the way it keeps everything: under layers, in strata, one thin band at a time. The stridulators ignore it. The plume-flies don't rise for it. The sweepers don't hunt it. It is in nothing's ledger, which means it is either outside the ecosystem or underneath it, and the only thing known for certain is that it is the reason the works feel occupied rather than merely inhabited: in a dead plant at the end of a finished world, something answers when the machinery speaks.

---

That is the census. But the honest description is shorter: what lives in the Vented Dish is one appetite with ten million mouths, shingled over its own dead, ticking in the dark, breathing on a forty-minute schedule it did not choose and cannot refuse, eating the works that feed it, deepening its own house by a finger's width per millennium, and hoarding warmth the way a forest hoards light — because in the only world it has ever known, warmth *is* the light, rust *is* the soil, gas *is* the rain, and silence is the one predator nothing has evolved to survive.
