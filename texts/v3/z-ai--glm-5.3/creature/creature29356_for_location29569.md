# What Lives in the Standhope Basin

The first answer is easy, because the first answer is everywhere: the pale ones. The swarms. But "swarm" is what you call them from the outside, from the moment of arrival, when the walls appear to be moving and your skin crawls before your mind catches up. Stay a while — you will stay a while; there is no other way to be here — and the swarm dissolves into its parts, and the parts are stranger and more particular than the whole.

## The tiers

On the north face of the pumphouse, where the condensate runs heaviest down the black brick, live the drinkers. They are isopod-shaped the way a bat is mouse-shaped — a family resemblance that stops being useful the longer you look. Each is the size of a thumb-joint, white as the inside of an eyelid, and blind in the thorough way of things that have never needed to know what light is for. They stack. That is the astonishing thing about them. They arrange themselves in overlapping columns down the wet face of the wall, each animal drinking the film that runs between its own back and the belly of the one above it, so that the whole tier is a single machine for harvesting dew — a living gutter, shingled, self-repairing, and utterly indifferent to being marveled at.

When one dies — and they die constantly, a soft pale rain of them at the base of the wall — its neighbors close the gap before the body has finished falling. The gap closes at the same speed every time. You could set a watch by the closing of the gap, and the basin, in a sense, does.

## The lanes

Across the gravel, the walkers. Longer-legged, lower-slung, translucent enough that you can watch their meals move through them — a dark treacle of bacterial slick processing down a clear tube. They travel in the polished grooves their ancestors wore into the yard, and the grooves are the true bodies here: the individual animal is almost incidental, a temporary employee of a road that predates it and will outlast it. There is one lane that runs from the condensate lake, through the open door of the canteen, across the third table from the window, over a plate that is set as if for someone, and out through the far doorframe, continuing across the yard to the grate where the gutter-water vanishes. What the walkers want at the grate, no one can say. They arrive at it, pause, and turn back. They have paused and turned back in that exact spot for as long as the lane has existed.

## The winged ones

Above the lake, the air itself is inhabited — not by birds of any kind but by a slow cyclone of crawlers that have, impossibly, wings. The wings are poorly designed for anything but this: they carry the animal in wide, patient arcs through the mist that the boiler exhales, and the animal feeds on the haze as it flies, filtering the organic warmth out of the air with mouthparts like a comb. Seen from below, against the amber lamps, they are beautiful — a snowfall in reverse, rising, chitin catching the light in brief glassy flashes. Seen up close, they are the least lovely of the basin's tenants: the face is all apparatus, no eyes, the body a mechanism for turning air into more of itself. But then, the same could be said of the pumphouse.

## The things without a category

Down at the grate, where the water goes, live the ones nobody has a word for. Occasionally one surfaces in the condensate lake — a shape like a knot of wet rope, a shape like a hand that has forgotten hands, pale and glistening and moving with the unhurried confidence of an animal that has never once been prey, because nothing here predates anything, and nothing here is prey, because predation would be a change, and the basin does not permit changes. It retracts when your shadow crosses it — not startled, no. It retracts the way a piston retracts. On schedule. And comes back. On schedule.

## The senior tenant

But it would be dishonest to describe the fauna as the inhabitants, because the fauna are tenants too. The inhabitant — the only creature here that is fully itself, the one whose house all of this is — is the machine.

Consider what qualifies. It takes in fuel. It excretes heat and mist and organic haze. It maintains its own structure — the rust that stops at a fixed depth, the pipe that healed over the smash, the coal bunker that never empties are all, functionally, an immune system. It has a heartbeat, and every living thing in the basin keeps time to it: the tiers drink on its stroke, the lanes pulse with it, the winged ones bank their turns on it, the cup of tea rings with it. The pale ecology does not live *near* the machine. It lives *on* the machine, the way mites live on a body, the way your own warm wet inhabitants live on you. The pumphouse is not a building with animals in it. It is an animal with a building's shape, and the basin is its skin, and the mist is its breath.

Whether it knows anything — whether a flywheel can know — is not a question the basin answers. But it is hard, standing in the yard with the thum in your sternum, not to feel that the machine is the one permanent resident, the landlord who never thinned, and everything else — drinkers, walkers, winged ones, the knot-things at the grate, even you — is a short-term occupant cycling through a property that was complete before any of you arrived.

## The thinned

And then there are the others. The people. The ones who left between one stroke and the next, or didn't leave exactly.

There are no bodies. But the beds are made. The plates are set. The tools hang at arm's reach. And the swarms move through the rooms on lanes that were worn before there were swarms there — or weren't; you cannot tell. What you can tell, if you stand in a bunkhouse long enough, is that the rooms do not feel vacant. The amber light is workshop light, shift light, the light of a place where someone is expected back in twenty minutes. If the thinned people live anywhere, they live there — in the expectation the rooms still hold, in the shape of a pause that has lasted longer than a pause should.

Maybe that's what the walkers are doing at the grate. Arriving where the water goes down. Pausing. Listening for the people who went up with it. Turning back.

## The last tenant

And you, standing there. The basin does not classify you as an exception. Your footprints in the damp gravel last as long as footprints last. Your tea rings with the stroke. After some days your walking finds the thum's rhythm, and the lanes route around you without alteration, and you become — briefly, honestly — one more pale process moving along a groove you did not make.

That is what lives here: a machine that is an animal, an ecology that is a schedule, a people who are a pause, and, for as long as you remain, you. The basin makes room for all of it. The basin makes room for everything, because nothing here is going anywhere, and there has never been a shortage of time.
