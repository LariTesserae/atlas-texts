# The People of Vhen-Aldt

## The Wetborn

They call themselves nothing, mostly, the way people who have always been somewhere don't. The traders who climb the switchback road from the low country call them the *Wetborn*, half insult and half respect, and the name has stuck because it is accurate: they are born in the warm damp of the town, in stone rooms where the walls sweat, and their first breath is air already half water. There is a saying below — that you can tell a Vhen-Aldt native anywhere in the world by how they stand in rain. They don't hunch. They just tilt their heads down a half-inch, patient, and wait for it to be relevant.

There are perhaps nine hundred of them, and that number has been roughly nine hundred for as long as anyone has counted, because the town's population is set by its water and its water is set by its ceiling, and neither has changed appreciably in living memory. Families are small and durable. Everyone is related to everyone within four surnames, and the genealogy is less a tree than a mat — the locals themselves say so, gesturing at the dark green-black skins in the overflow channels: *like that, growing sideways, thick where the water's good.*

## The Castes of Process

Vhen-Aldt does not have classes, exactly. It has *assignments of patience* — an older phrase, from the soapstone-carving guilds, and it captures something true: the town is organized not by wealth or birth but by what kind of slowness you are temperamentally suited to.

The **Seamwalkers** maintain the cliff face — the thousand trickles, the carved gutters, the soapstone channels older than anyone's grandmother. They are a small crew, maybe thirty, and they are chosen young, because the work requires a head for heights, a tolerance for scalded fingertips, and above all the ability to look at a trickle running wrong and deduce, from the speed and the mineral bloom and the taste, which seam three hundred meters away has shifted. An old Seamwalker can read the cliff the way a physician reads a face. They are the closest thing the town has to priests, and they know it, and it makes them quiet and slightly smug in equal measure. They marry late. Their children can identify a dozen minerals by taste before they can read.

The **Evaporators** run the salt-halls — the long, steam-fogged galleries where the cliff-water is cooked down into white and blue and the rare violet. This is the money, and the money is violet, and so the evaporators are the closest thing to merchants, except that they are merchants of a substance whose entire industrial process is *waiting for water to leave*. The best of them are said to be able to tell a violet-bearing pan from a white one by the sound the condensation makes coming off it. The town half-believes this. The evaporators do not correct anyone.

The **Tankkeepers** tend the leaching-tanks and the pale tenants and the fat warm-water fish of the condensation ponds. It is the lowest status and, by broad agreement, the best life — warm rooms, slow work, fish fried in rendered condor fat at midday, and the permanent company of the soft golden insects that attend everything. Tankkeepers laugh more than anyone in Vhen-Aldt, at half-volume, which is a strange thing to witness.

The **Renderers** work the cliff-gate with the condors. This is the strangest job in town and the one most written about by visiting scholars, because it requires a person to stand, twice daily, at a predetermined spot and *negotiate with a thousand enormous birds who have opinions*. The Renderers are chosen, the town says, for "the right kind of standing still." They learn each bird. There is one — a female with a chipped beak and a scarred wing-pit, called Grandmother by three generations now, though she is not the same bird, the Renderers admit this privately, the name simply transferred — who arrives first, every day, always first, and is fed first, and this arrangement has held longer than any contract in the low countries.

And under all of these, the **Diggers** — the shaft-men, the ones who go down forty meters into rock that boils cisterns. It is hot, close, careful work, and Vhen-Aldt treats its Diggers with the wary reverence other towns reserve for soldiers and midwives, because the ground's warmth is the whole town's warmth and the Diggers are the ones who touch it directly. Every Digger has a scorched-wool smell that never entirely comes out. They are recognizable in any crowd. They are each other's company more than anyone else's.

## The Habits of the Quiet

Half-volume speech is the first thing visitors notice about the people, after the water-stepping. Vhen-Aldt conversation is conducted in a low, unhurried register that outsiders initially mistake for sorrow or secrecy. It is neither. It is simply that there has never been anything to shout over, and shouting, in a town where sound carries oddly off the wet stone, has the social sting of a public confession.

They are not unfriendly. They are *unhurried*, which reads as the same thing until you've spent a season. A stranger sitting at a wall-crown at dusk will be approached — eventually — by someone with two cups of cliff-water tea, which tastes of iron and faintly of sulfur, and which is genuinely an acquired taste, and the local will sit, and say nothing for a while, and then say something like, "The mats are thick in the third channel this week." This is a complete greeting, a weather report, an invitation, and a philosophical statement, all at half-volume.

They marry slowly, deliberate about everything, separating at a rate so low the low-country scholars find it statistically suspicious. They bury their dead in the dry interior, beyond the slag heaps, in ground that crunches like stale bread — the one place the water does not follow them, which the living find appropriate and the dead, presumably, do not mind.

## What They Are Afraid Of

The cold. There is one story every child in Vhen-Aldt knows, told at half-volume, about the year — nobody agrees which year — the seams *slowed*. Not stopped. Slowed. For one month the cliff wept a fraction less, and the cisterns dropped a hand-width, and the town, for that month, learned what it would be to be a normal altitude town: the edges coming back into the breath, the stone cooling at night, the condors going elsewhere. The story always ends the same way — the water came back, nobody ever learned why — and it is always told the same way, quietly, without a moral, because the moral is unbearable: that the whole warm wet humming arrangement is a gift from below that no one in the town controls, has ever controlled, or will ever be consulted about.

So they maintain. That is, in the end, what living in Vhen-Aldt is: the maintenance of a ten-thousand-year-old gift. The gutters are cleaned. The mats are read like tea leaves. The condors are fed on time. The engines lope along like dozing animals, and the drips strike their thousand little percussion notes, and at noon the whistle makes its polite bassoon moan, and for a moment after it fades, everyone in the town — all nine hundred, all patient, all at half-volume — can hear, together, everything they have been given, still giving.
