What lives in the Still is people — a few hundred of them — and the strangest thing about them is that they are the whole fauna. Not the dominant species: the only one. No dogs, no rats, no flies at the taps. The only warm blood under the hardpan is human, and it moves on felt.

They are damp people, born and raised that way. Their skin carries the same sheen the walls do, and it has nothing to do with bathing; their eyes are wide-doored for the amber circuit, so that daylight, on the rare occasions of it, arrives as a small violence. They feel weather before it happens — the pressure turns in their chests an hour before the canopy hisses — and they say *the pressure's turning* the way other towns say it's clouding up, and they are never wrong, because they are the instrument. They speak at the volume the room asks for. They move like people in a fever, slow and deliberate, conserving themselves, and you never hear them coming: felt soles on wet stone, a voice pitched under the echo. Visitors are forever being startled by them. Someone from the Still, at your shoulder, has been there a while.

The town is one generation deep, and its people are two kinds of memory living in the same rooms. The founders were the lake's own people: when the water went down they could have stayed on the shore and watched it become a story, and they went down after it instead. Their children were born below the dew line. Their grandchildren have never lived under anything but stone, and think of the sky as a large empty room that freight comes from. A handful of the old ones can still tell you what a lake smells like — everyone knows who they are, and nobody asks casually. To everyone else, *lake* is a word for the old water, the smell they live inside. Birds are the animals in the grandmothers' stories: they fly, and carry pollen from flower to flower, and answer sounds. The children are polite about birds. Fish are real to them the way fossils are real — prints that come up in the curling mud-leaves, a fin pressed into a polygon, the town's natural history peeled off the floor.

To have a self here is to have a roof. A person is introduced by their ceiling the way a farmer is introduced by his land: a wet roof is a fortune, a thin roof is a hardship, and a good marriage gets described, without anyone smiling, as two gutters joining. Cold is held the same way — leased, deeded, defended — and the town's small crimes are all thermal or hydraulic: a shoulder borrowed against a stranger's cold line, a tap run into a pipe that isn't yours. The Runnel Court sits on the Market Flat the day after a big Rain, and its justice is volumetric. The Runnelmaster hangs a marked cup under the disputed ceiling for one full night and reads the verdict at dawn, in millimeters; the losing party pays in nights of yield and pays without rancor, because the number is the number. There is almost no other crime. The granaries run like banks without guards, wet stone carries a low voice the length of a gallery, and the town's constable is its acoustics — you cannot shout here without everyone hearing you be wrong. The only exile is upward: dry rooms on the flat, under no ceiling at all. It is almost never imposed. It doesn't need to be. The threat of the sky is enough.

The work is all plumbing and stone, and everyone holds a piece of it. Blower-tenders keep the air climbing. Stack-walkers dress like surface folk — dry felt, goggles — and climb into the town's winter, which is overhead, to tend the cold country where the moisture films. The drillers, gloved because the young rock cuts like glass, cut rooms the way other towns cut streets, except that a room here is a lung: they are not making space, they are making surface, and they fight over scalloped stone the way farmers fight over bottomland, because a scallop is more face to the air, and face is where the beads are caught. Shell-grinders crush the lake's bones small enough to dress the water. The lamp-wright walks the amber circuit with her tools; the bell-striker strikes, felt on bronze, the softest loud sound in the world. The mirror-keeper climbs out at dawn to polish the mirror-head on the flat so the town below can have its noon — the loneliest job in the Still, given to people who can be trusted with the sky. And the chalk-keeper writes the town's whole chronicle on the cistern wall in one hand: the yield, the births, the dead. A plumbing chart with a census in the margin. Freight comes down the winze — grain, felt, bronze, oilcloth — and freight goes up in sealed skins: water, the town's crop, sold naked by the skin to the dry towns. It is the one thing the Still produces and will not itself drink.

The other inhabitant is the Green, and it is, strictly speaking, a single resident with many seats. Nothing down here is native; the lake took its natives with it. Everything alive in these rooms arrived in a founder's pocket or on a founder's boots — a cutting, a spore, a sprouted tuber — so the entire biosphere of the Still is a list of what people carried. The ferns are all one fern, met a hundred times, and the keeper of the Green can trace every bench back to the mother stool that rode down in somebody's coat; when the mother finally died, the town mourned her for exactly as long as it took to notice she was still all over the walls. The taro-line, the moss, the pipe-vines trained to drink off the cold metal: in a world with nothing to carry pollen, a plant that wants to travel must be worth carrying, so the fern made itself food, the taro made itself staple, and the moss asked nothing and got everywhere anyway. The people are the Green's legs and its livestock at once, and neither party has ever needed the arrangement explained. The Green feeds them, and it clocks them: noon is when the sunwell's circle touches the benches, and the town keeps time by the fern's mealtime. The edge of that circle is the shoreline children wade at, daring the dark. The old sit inside the light when they can. Down here daylight is medicine, and it is doled out by the hour.

A Still childhood is quiet games in the shade through the heat's hours, mud-leaves with fish in them, and the one sin every child commits: catching the first bead of a Rain on the tongue. *Naked water* — the lake with nothing on it, the taste of nothing at all. Harmless once, forbidden always, never forgotten. And there is one animal in the town's history, which the town keeps the way other towns keep relics. A moth came down in a crate of felt, one time, decades ago. It lived eleven days. The whole town scheduled visits; people stood under the lamps watching it fly and discovered they had feelings with no names attached. When it died — no precedent for a body so small — they sealed it into a snapped stalactite stump with wax, and it sits on the Market Flat still: the town's dragon, its proof, its little reliquary. Children now search the incoming freight. There has never been a second one.

Lives are dated in Rains the way other lives are dated in years — born in a three-gutter, and so on — and courtship is two people under one hood, walking the galleries while the canopy hisses; a proposal, in the Still, is the offer of the dry side of your hood. When your shoulders fit the rungs, you go up: the Upgoing. You climb past the dew line into dust and daylight, stand on the dead lake's face, meet your first wind — the town's air never moves except as draft — stand under a sky with nothing holding it up, cry or don't, and then come back down the ladder, cross the dew line, and breathe. That is when you learn what the old water is: the smell of home, which cannot be known by anyone living inside it. Until you have smelled the town from outside, you are a child. The dead make the same trip in the other direction. The Still buries upward. There are no graves below — the lake's floor is the graveyard the lake already made, kilometers of shell — and the founders lie in it, and the children visit them the way children elsewhere visit the sea.

When the drillers open a sealed pocket of old air, the town queues: the Breathing, oldest first, one breath each. Some of the founders came away from the last one weeping, and could not say whether it was grief or recognition, and it does not matter which, because the air went into everybody's lungs, and it is in all of them now. That is as close to communion as the Still has ever needed to come.

And the chief inhabitant may be the town itself. It drinks its own walls, as its oldest saying admits; it has lungs in the chambers, veins in the standpipes, a skin that sweats by design. When the pressure turns it takes its breath and sings the low bottle-note that would set dogs howling in any town above ground. Nothing answers it here. Nothing has ever answered it. But lately — this is new, and not yet old enough to be a custom — on the big-Rain nights, the people hum it back. Very low, all together, without deciding to. The town has begun to sing itself to sleep through its people, which may be what its people were for from the beginning.

They know the ledger. Every dry year the chalk line trends down, the scallops dull by a hair, the gutters run thinner, and everyone can read the wall, and no one leaves. They cut new rooms, plant new cuttings, marry under the hoods, keep founding things inside a closing account — because the Still was never built to last. It was built to be here. It exists to meet each bead of the lake on its way out, one at a time, in person, and so do its people: heirs who inherit by the drop, witnesses who keep the appointment, the company a lake is dying in. When the last bead is met, the town will end the way it began — a climb up the winze, a walk out onto the bone — but that is years of Rains away. Tonight the pressure will turn, the hoods will come off the pegs, ten thousand drip-tips will tick as one above the whole damp population of the world, and in the morning the chalk-keeper will write down exactly how much lake is left. And the town will go on meeting it.
