# The People of the Terrace Works

They call themselves by their tier, first. Before a name, before a trade, there is the question of *where on the slope* — because the terraces are not just levels but climates, histories, bloodlines of trade. A third-terrace child asked where she's from will say "the seam under the flume," and everyone will know exactly the dripping three-block corridor meant, and will know, too, roughly what her family does, because in a city where the niches are all full, the niches are inherited.

**They are small-boned and dense, the way mountain people are, though this is not a mountain.** Generations of climbing staircases that drift a centimeter out of true each year have given them a particular gait — a low, rolling, deliberate walk, weight always slightly forward, one hand habitually near a wall. They touch buildings the way sailors touch rigging: constantly, unconsciously, reading them. A terrace-born person can tell you which landing has settled since last month by the feel of the banister under the palm. Visitors think they're being affectionate with their city. They're being cautious. It's the same thing.

Their skin has a quality the lowlands don't have a word for — not pale, exactly, but *shadow-adjusted*, the complexion of people whose sun arrives secondhand, filtered through permanent cloud, so that their eyes are the brightest thing about them, disproportionately large-seeming and quick, tuned to the twenty-foot visibility of the corridors. They read faces and hands at distances lowlanders would find intimate. They can identify a neighbor by silhouette in the mist before the neighbor can identify himself.

**The wool they wear is the city's second skin.** Oiled, grey-green, patched at the elbows in a way that's less poverty than philosophy — nothing here is finished, nothing abandoned, and clothes are material waiting its turn like everything else. A coat descends through a family, gaining a new collar here, a fresh shoulder there, until the original garment is a rumor inside its own replacement. When someone dies, the coat is not buried with them. That would be wasteful, and slightly morbid — like burying a wall because a house fell.

**Their sense of time is seasonal and structural, not hourly.** The Works keep bells, and the bells have meanings, but the meanings have drifted the way the staircases drift — a bell that once meant shift-change now means, by general silent agreement, "the fish cart is on the seventh tier," and nobody remembers when that happened. The mediation-hours, when the gutters run and the temperature drops, are kept instinctively, the way animals keep dusk. Children learn the hammer-rhythm of their own terrace's foundry before they learn to speak in full sentences; when the eccentric beat skips, they wake in their beds the way sailors wake to a change in the wind.

**The masons are the aristocracy, though they'd be embarrassed to hear it.** They work in the permanent hurry that looks like panic and isn't, and they carry, in their heads, the structural history of the whole slope — which walls are load-bearing for three tiers above them, which flue is a moss-tube now and was a chimney when their grandmother was young, where the settlement will take the next decade's re-laying. Knowledge passes master to apprentice in the corridors, verbally, with hand-on-stone demonstrations, because the city is always changing and a written map is a lie by the time the ink dries. The oldest masons have a phrase that gets at the whole of it: *we do not build here, we answer.* The terraces shift; the masons are the reply.

**Under them, and everywhere, the moss-tenders — an unofficial guild that was never founded and cannot be disbanded.** Someone has to keep the corridor-carpets from swallowing the doorways, someone has to clear the drain-seams of ferns precisely enough to let the water run but not enough to hurt the green, because the green is what holds the mortar together in the wet and everyone knows it, in the way everyone knows not to ask the bells what they mean. The tenders work on their knees a great deal. They know the liverwort continents of the gasometers by name — actual names, given names, the way lowlanders name cows. There is a liverwort nation on the north face of gasometer three called the Vell, and it is older than most families.

**They are not melancholy, which surprises visitors most.** The permanent November light, the rain, the endless patient-frantic rebuilding — lowlanders arrive expecting a resigned people, a grey people. What they find instead is a population with the specific humor of those who live inside a long project: dry, quick, understated, fond of the spectacular inaccuracy of visitors. Terrace humor is largely about the lowlands — the imagined extravagance of ground that is actually *ground*, of weather that comes from *above*. They have a rich tradition of jokes about falling over, which they consider a lowlander skill.

They grieve, of course. But even grief here has the city's grammar: nothing finished, nothing abandoned. The dead are remembered as material redistributed — a person who held the memory of a certain wall, who answered a certain settlement, whose coat walks the corridor still, whose name is on a stack of bricks under tarpaulin waiting its turn in a cycle a thousand years long. On the fourth terrace, the collective cat — which is real, grey as its food-providers, and reportedly thirty years old, which the masons find funnier than anyone — sleeps on the warm boiler-cut bell-weights, and everyone feeds it, and no one owns it, and this is understood to be, in some way nobody can quite articulate, the point.

**And at the very top of it all, in the deep slow hammer-country of the upper city, are the ones even the third-tier people speak of with a slight tilt of the head — the flume-keepers and cable-men who live at the edge of the cloud's ceiling, in the coldest light, tending the rigging that moves blood between the organs.** They marry late, or not at all; they come down rarely; they are said to hear the whole slope as a single sound. When one of them dies, they say, the bells ring a meaning that everyone, for once, actually remembers.

They do not think of themselves as strange. They think of themselves as the answer the mountain keeps making to its own settling — oiled-wool stains on the stonework that move, and touch the walls, and reply.
