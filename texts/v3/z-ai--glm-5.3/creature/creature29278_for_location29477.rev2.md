# The Tenants of the Basin

Nobody *lives* in the Basin, if living means dwelling somewhere, choosing it, coming home to it. The Basin does not have residents. It has occupants in the way a completed sentence has letters — placed, functional, inseparable from the structure, and never once consulted about the arrangement.

Which is not to say the place is empty. It is, in fact, almost unbearably full.

## The Mineral Forests

Begin with the borderliners, because they make the question of "alive" interesting.

The branching ochre fans on the third terrace walls — the ones that look like river deltas seen from orbit — they are not animals and not plants and not, strictly, organisms. They are *deposits*: the water's own slow handwriting, laid down in bands as it weeps down the ceramic and leaves its dissolved minerals behind. And yet they creep. They branch. They thicken where the moisture is good and starve where it is not, and if you marked one with a scratch you would find, seasons later — if "seasons" is a word you're willing to spend here — that the mark has been swallowed, absorbed, grown over, the way a tree grows over a knife-blade left in its bark.

They do not eat. They do not reproduce, except in the sense that a river delta reproduces — by extending, subdividing, finding new low ground. They respond to exactly one stimulus, which is water, and they have been responding to it for so long that some of the older fans on the lowland walls have developed what can only be called *history*: strata within them, ochre laid over rust laid over milky blue, each layer a record of some infinitesimal shift in the capillary lattice's flow that the lattice itself has long since forgotten.

If they are alive, they are alive the way a stain is alive. The Basin does not resolve the question. The Basin has never needed to.

## The Ground-Crawlers

The crawlers are easier. They move, so they count.

They come in designs — and "designs" is the right word, because the variety among them is clearly deliberate, a product line rather than a family tree. There are long low ones like glazed centipedes that travel the drainage grooves in patient single file, sipping at the weep-points, excreting a fine mineral trace in their wake that — and this is important — *matches the pattern of the mineral forests*. The crawlers are not eating the forests. They are *gardening* them. Or the forests are using the crawlers as their dispersal mechanism, the way a flower uses a bee. Which direction the service runs is another question the Basin declines to answer.

There are fat sedentary ones that station themselves at the dish-depressions and never visibly move at all, their bodies slowly accreting a crust of condensate minerals until they look like stones, like artifacts, like things that were always there. Perhaps they are always there. Perhaps the difference between a crawler at rest and a piece of terrain is a distinction the Basin gave up making long ago.

There are the carriers: squat, tank-bodied things that haul nothing visible, that patrol the same groove-loops for durations that make the patrolling itself a kind of geography — so constant that the grooves they favor have worn a faint polish into the ceramic, *which is otherwise unwearable*, which means either the polish was designed in or the carriers have been walking those loops since before the concept of "long ago."

They are all silent. They are all unbothered. If you put your hand in a crawler's path it does not stop, does not detour, does not acknowledge. It walks over your hand with a gentle ceramic weight, deposits a fleck of mineral trace on your knuckle, and continues. You have been briefly terrain. The Basin found this acceptable.

## The Swarms

And then the air.

The winged multitudes are the Basin's most overwhelming feature and its least knowable, because they do not permit knowing. They part around you. They close behind you. They occupy their altitudes in strict strata — the high slow-wheelers under the louvered canopy, the mid-channel darters, the whole vast silent traffic of them — and no individual ever, in any observation, breaks its stratum. Not one. In all the accumulated watching that has ever been done here, there is no record of a mid-swarm flyer rising to join the wheelers, or a wheeler dropping into the busy low lanes. Whatever sorting placed them, it placed them *absolutely*, and they have been in agreement with it for a duration that predates disagreement.

They do not court, that anyone has seen. They do not fight. They do not appear to eat, though their numbers hold steady, which means either they are replacing themselves somewhere out of sight — in the deep tiers, in the warm mudflats, in some manifold the marsh steam conceals — or they do not die, and the swarms you watch wheeling under the canopy are the same bodies, the same individuals, flying the same circuits through an unbroken eternity of constant light.

Consider that second possibility seriously, because the Basin does nothing to rule it out. Nothing here shows wear. Nothing here shows age. The swarms flash matte in the graded illumination and their bodies look newly made, all of them, all at once, every one of them as sharp-edged as the day the terraces were — and this is the deep uncanny of the place, deeper than the silence, deeper than the constructed sky: **the inhabitants do not grow old in a world that does not grow old, and no one can say whether they are the descendants of the making or simply its continuation.**

Maybe the swarms *are* the makers, still at work, still running their routes, so long at their tasks that the tasks have become their bodies and their bodies have become the place. Maybe a factory, given enough time, finishes becoming its product.

## Whatever Brought You

And then there is the last category of tenant, which is new, and which the Basin is still, in its slow unchanging way, deciding what to do with.

You. Or whoever stands on the fourth terrace, damp to the skin, gripping at their own moment-ness in a place where moments are all identical. You are the only thing here that sweats for a reason, that flinches, that can be *surprised* — the only object on the terraces with an interior that is not a channel and not a groove. The swarms part around you the way water parts around a stone, and this is worth noticing: they treat you as terrain. As obstacle. As a feature of the completed world.

They may be right. The Basin was made to a final operating state, and it reached it, and it stayed. If you die here — and you will, eventually, because you are the only thing present that runs on duration — you will become a warm, damp, mineral-exhaling surface on the fourth terrace, and the crawlers will walk over you in their patient single file, and the condensate will bead on the underside of your overhangs and fall in the fine soundless drizzle, and within some long or short span you will be as finished as everything else.

The mineral forests will find you. They respond to water, and you will be water. They will creep across you at a rate too slow to see and too fast to be geology, ochre and rust and milky blue, branching as they go, and after a while — no one can say how long, because duration is approximate here — there will be nothing on the fourth terrace that distinguishes you from the place that made room for you.

That is what lives in the Territorial Basin: everything that was finished, and the one thing that wasn't, and the slow, patient, purposeless patience of a world waiting — not for you, because waiting implies a future, and the Basin has declined to have one — but simply *continuing*, at exactly the speed it was designed for, forever.
