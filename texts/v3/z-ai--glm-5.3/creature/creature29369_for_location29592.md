Lichen blooms in slow tides across the vault, following seams of richer mineral, so that whole districts of the cavern floor pass through a kind of dusk and back into noon over the course of a decade. The people track these the way surface farmers track seasons. They have words for a bright year the way we have words for a wet one.

And there are people. You would know it before you saw one, from the ramps — no, from the troughs in the ramps. Something has been coming and going down these passages for so long that the stone has consented, has given way, and the feet that made the troughs were not all the same feet. The people who live here now walk in those grooves out of something between courtesy and superstition. To walk on the unworn shoulders of the ramp, they say, is to walk where no one has ever walked, and nothing good comes of that. So they file, single, patient, the old behind the young, in a procession that has technically been going on since before the ramps were cut.

They are not what the surface imagination makes of cave-dwellers. There is nothing shrunk or starved about them. The light has made them broad and slow-growing and long — not tall, exactly, but extended, like everything else here, like the ramps, taking the long shallow way instead of the steep one. Their eyes are the give-away. Large, pale, set slightly wider than ours, with pupils that open like apertures rather than shrinking to points. A visitor from above is functionally blind down here for the first week, everything a white smear; a native who climbs the ramps to the surface goes through the reverse, and most never bother. They do not speak of the surface as a place. They speak of it as a condition, like blindness, like deafness, which some people are born into and none of them envy.

They farm. That is the second thing to understand, after the light: the whole floor of the vault is a field, and what they farm is stone chemistry. They tend the lichen — not the vault lichen, which is too high to reach and is left to its own wild economy like a sky left to itself, but a dozen domestic breeds grown in shallow trays and terraced shelves, bred for taste, for yield, for the particular warmth of their glow. Some strains they keep for light the way we keep sheep for wool; a lantern here is a living mesh in a cage, fed on chalk water, dimming when sullen. The animal life has followed the same road. There are moths, certainly — great pale ones that rise toward the vault on the warm updrafts and are considered, more or less, weather. There is a beast of burden, low, broad-hoofed, hairless, patient beyond the patience of any surface ox, and it is almost completely blind, and it does not mind, because nothing has ever surprised it. It has been down here longer than the people have. Some of the herders will tell you, quietly, that the beasts were here first, and that the ramps were originally hoof, not foot, and that the people arrived as followers.

Followers of what, they don't say. There is a place at the far side of the vault where the lichen grows in colors — a low bank of it, red-gold, in a fold of the rock — and no one farms it, and no one walks there, and the paths all curve around it at a distance that is not law but older than law. Whatever the people followed, it either lived there or stopped there. Children ask. Children are told: it is resting. The tone in which this is said suggests that everyone, child and adult, understands this is a story one tells in the way one says goodnight, and that the truer answer — that no one remembers, that the memory is worn away like the middle of the ramps, leaving only the smooth groove of behavior — is somehow less true than the story.

Their days are governed by the light's own long rhythms, and so their lives are long in a way that is difficult to describe. Not that they are immortal. But a "day" here is a lichen-cycle, a bright passage in the vault's weather, and their calendars are counted in these, and the oldest among them remember four hundred of them, and the genealogies go back past counting because they are kept not in writing but in the stone: every family keeps a wall, and every birth is a small drill-hole in the wall filled with a plug of glowing lichen, so that the houses at night are lit by their own dead and their own newborns, indistinguishable, all one light, which is the point.

That is the heart of it, finally. Everything here converts. Stone to light, light to food, the dead to lamps, the passage of feet to architecture, time to groove. Nothing is wasted because there is no elsewhere for waste to go; the vault is a closed country, and everything that lives in it — people, moths, beasts, the great bright sky of lichen farming its rock overhead — is engaged in the same slow transaction, the same single economy, the one the air smells of: chalk, and rain that hasn't fallen, and stone paying its debts in light.
