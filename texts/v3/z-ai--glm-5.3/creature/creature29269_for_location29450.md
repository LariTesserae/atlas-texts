# Who Lives in the Throat

Begin at the rim, because that is where the keeper lives, though "lives" is a loose word for it. He lives in the village like everyone else; what he does at the well is closer to what the fig tree does than what a man does. He arrives at first light with his twig broom, before the pigeons go, and sweeps the first tier in long strokes that have worn a shine into the very stone he sweeps. He has held this post long enough that his forearms are brown as the rail-lines, and if you watch him long enough you notice the same thing the surveyors noticed about the steps: he does not occupy his position so much as belong to it, the way the algae belongs to its waterline. He feeds the fish. He replaces the marigolds. He tells you the rules whether or not you have asked. Whether he is the well's servant or its memory is a distinction the place does not recognize.

**The pigeons** are the citizens, and they know it. They have lived in the niches longer than any of the customs that protect them — the grain-homage at the rim pavilion is a human invention, and a late one — and they conduct themselves like natives who have tolerated an influx of visitors for forty generations. They are the well's voice, and they use it: the low murmur that you feel in your sternum before you feel anything else is their parliament, in permanent session, never adjourned. They have one civic skill that is theirs alone and entirely learned here: they leave at dawn *as one body*, thousands in a single indivisible mass, because their grandmothers' grandmothers were picked off by the kite and the survivors made departure a collective act. This is not instinct. No wild pigeon flies like that. It is a decision the flock makes every morning, arrived at in some manner of counting that no surveyor will ever audit, and it has made them, in their way, the most evolved creatures in the place.

**The black kite** owns the updraft and the schedule. He is the only resident who does not live *in* the well but *on* it, working the mouth like a landlord working a fence line. His whole hunting life is timed to the ten heartbeats of the dawn exodus, and he is beautiful and he is losing — has been losing for generations, to a bird that weighs a fraction of what he does, simply because the pigeons got better at being many. He takes the stragglers. Every society down here runs on what the slowest member costs it.

**The swallows** under the brackets are the pieceworkers — stitching the shade all day, mud-masons who build with the well's own sweat, the condensation they drink mid-flight from the weeping lintels. **The geckos** live vertically, on walls that water, and so never thirst; their entire species-life, for however many centuries, has consisted of waiting on cool stone for the dawn condensation to travel to them. It is possibly the easiest living in the world, and they are correspondingly fat and unbothered and can be found at any hour blinking at the pilgrim traffic with an expression of profound administrative calm.

**The bees** hold the eave of the rim pavilion, a wild hive, generations deep, humming the one held note that the wind only occasionally gets to play. They are the well's connection to the plain beyond — the only residents who commute outward daily, to the rim flowers, and come back. Everything else that lives here, lives here.

**The bats** are the keepers of the deepest darkness and, by accident, the conservators. They packed themselves into the two lowest galleries where the light gives up, and their occupancy — their warmth, their chosen blindness, their very presence keeping humans from brightening that zone — has protected the red ochre and the white, the original paint, so that the most ancient human gesture in the place survives because it is kept in the dark by animals that cannot see it. There is something in this that the well seems to understand and no one else quite does.

**The fig tree** is the place's one immigrant that became a pillar. A seed in a mortar joint, some century ago — and now a canopy leaned out over forty paces of open air, held upright by roots that run down the masonry like stay-ropes, taking the lime from the joints while gripping those same joints so tightly that the tree props the well and the well props the tree. This is not symbiosis as the textbooks draw it. It is closer to two old men who have held each other up so long that neither can say whose arm is whose. The tree is the reason the well's children have something to jump from, and the pigeons something to argue over, and the fish their one unreliable bonanza of falling sugar. Its fruits drop through the square of sky at noon like green comets.

**The fish** are the congregation. Fat, tame, sacred by custom and by their own audacity — they rise in a body to any shadow, mouths dimpling the water into rings, because some ancestor of theirs learned that shadows mean bread and grain and offerings, and they have never been punished for this belief. Never caught, never eaten. They are, in the strict sense, the best-fed and most secure population in the district, and they conduct their one annual contest — fish versus pigeons, over falling figs — with the seriousness of a border dispute between two nations that are in every other respect at peace.

**The frogs** take the night shift, with the pool swallowing moths in soft percussive notes, and the **lichen** keeps the quietest office of all — mapping the damp corners, slowly, in a green hand no one has ever read.

And there are the humans, of course, who come and go: the dawn queue standing itself in order, the water-drawers with their ringing brass, the children in the hot months slamming into the pool from the fourth tier like doors shut in a cathedral, the grieved leaving lamps at whichever tier matches their grief. None of them live here. They are weather. The keeper is the only human who has crossed over, and even he goes home at night.

But the one who actually *lives* here — the resident, the landlord, the single continuous consciousness of the place — is the well itself. It drinks, twice a year, audibly from the village. It inhales at dusk and exhales at dawn with the feathered tide. It holds the sky in store and lets the fish swim through it. It answers, slightly after you expect it to, *as if considering* — and every creature in it, from the bats to the goddess gone smooth-featured from touch, has arranged its life around that consideration. The Throat was built to hold water, and it holds that too; but water is only its most measurable tenant. What lives in it is everything the dry plain could not keep: the cool, the loud, the dark, the green, and the sky — kept, in a made thing, behind a low stone wall around everything.
