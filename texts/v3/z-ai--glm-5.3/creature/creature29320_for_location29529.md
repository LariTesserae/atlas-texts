# Who Lives There

The first tenants are so slow they are nearly geology themselves. Lichens — gray-gold like the stone, so you don't see them until you see them, and then you see nothing else. They have claimed the corallites by the million, one dry handprint apiece, and they grow at the pace the reef once grew: a millimeter a century, another sea, another era, laid down layer over layer in slow motion. On the weathered faces the lichen has eaten so far into the stone that each hexagonal cell holds a small cup of dust and a single round thallus like a coin in a slot. The reef grew mouths; the lichen fills them. It is a kind of conversation between two things that will never finish a sentence.

The dust in those cups is not inert. Turn one over and it moves. Mites, springtails, a fauna of specks — a whole civilization that measures its world in grains. The reef is, to them, not a ruin but a landscape of hexagonal rooms kilometers deep, a country with no topsoil, no plants, barely any water, only what the wind delivers as an occasional rag of fog. Everything here has learned to wait between weathers. Everything here is small enough that a thumbprint is a courtyard.

Then the bees.

Solitary bees, hundreds of species of them, and they have found the obvious thing: a continent of ready-made cells, each the size of a bee's whole ambition. In spring the reef hums underneath its wind-chord, a second chord, smaller and warmer, in and out of the corallites. Some nests are capped with mud, some with chewed leaf, some with a resin the bee carried farther than seems possible. The hexagons were built by animals that died before there were flowers; the hexagons are now nurseries for animals that eat flowers. The reef has been repurposed across four hundred million years by tenants who moved into the masonry without ever asking who poured it, which is the way of most tenants, everywhere.

Where the corallites have weathered into deeper hollows, there are spiders that build no webs, hunt from the mouths like trapdoor ambush, and consider the bee traffic a delivery service. Where the wind has opened a corridor into a dome-shaped cavity — the ghost of some great colony head — there are bats, a few dozen, folding themselves into the overhang at dusk and unfolding again, and their echolocation clatters through the hollow like gravel in a kettle, the only sounds in the whole reef that the wind-chord cannot drown, because they refuse to be musical.

Barn owls keep the tall towers. In the upper stories, where the stone is driest and the hexagons face the thin cold sky, swifts nest in the deeper cells, arriving each year as if the reef had sent them an invitation no one heard. A single pair of falcons holds the crest of each knoll — there is one crest, there is one pair; this has perhaps been true since there were crests — and their droppings have painted the summit white so that from a distance, on certain reefs, the peak glows pale, a lighthouse that lights for nothing.

At the base, in the moat-furrow, in the meters-deep apron of the reef's own powdered body, the mammals live: fox families dens in the loose chips, a colony of rodents that has never known soil, only stone ground fine enough to be almost gentle, building their runs in the reef's own eroded substance. They are living in the mountain's grave and find it comfortable.

And the sound. The sound is the landlord, or the tenant, or possibly the building itself — by now the distinction has collapsed. The wind blows through the honeycomb and the reef plays, and it has played long enough that everything alive inside it has arranged itself around the chord. The crickets call in the gaps between partials. The bees seem to choose cells whose pitch matches their own wing-note, so that a hive in flight harmonizes with its own house. The birds nesting in the loud corridors are, generation by generation, the loudest birds of their kind on the plain, selected for the ones who can be heard over their address. Nothing here fears the sound. Silence, to the reef's tenants, is the alarm signal; the chord stopping would mean the wind had died, and everything would wait, still, until it started again.

If you climb to the crest at dusk and sit between the falcons and the sky, you can hear the other reefs, kilometers off, each sustaining its own note, the whole archipelago playing a chord across a plain with nothing else on it. The mountains that lifted these reefs are gone; the sea that built them is gone; the animals who grew it, cell by cell, a decade per band, are gone into the bands themselves.

What lives there now is everything that comes after — the bees in the empty mouths, the lichen filling the library, the owls in the towers, the chord never ending. A city that outlived its architects, keeping new tenants who have never known a sea, in rooms built by the dead for the dead, at a pitch the wind chose and does not intend to stop.
