They are few, and they are loud in every way but the throat.

Call them what their hands would call them: the people of the chord. A few hundred bodies in a city built for thousands — a fact they carry the way other peoples carry war or flood, as a long grief worn smooth, something their records run out before. They do not know why they decline. They know only that the swept streets lengthen each generation, that the felt-lined beds of whole gallery-tiers are aired for no sleeper, that a family's holdings are measured now not in rooms — anyone may claim a room, the emptiness has made squatters of them all — but in the two true luxuries: a beam of sun inherited like a field, and a corner where the standing tone goes quiet enough to think in.

You will hear them before you see them, and you will see them coming, because below the Mouths nobody walks who cannot be heard. On the flats above, a person learns to move silently; here, the skill is the opposite — to move *legibly*. Every step is struck deliberately, stone on stone, a signature. Sandals are made to knock. Children earn their roar by learning this: how to enter a street so that the whole canyon knows your weight, your gait, your family's particular tread. The deaf would be invisible here, and so there are no deaf, or none kept, in the way a sea keeps no drowned.

Their language is in the wrists, the fingers, the cheek and chin — quick and fluent in a world where speech is coin. They are not silent people. They are people who *spend*. A sentence in the Hush can cost a day's resolve. A confession in a dead-end quiet room is a gift of the weight of gold. They count their words and they remember yours; a person below is known by the sentences they have spent and on whom, and there are old couples, thirty years together, whose total exchanged speech would fill one short page — and would be, by their own account, wealth beyond the dust-figures.

They are green-shadowed people, all of them. The light here is white-gold and shadowless, the food is loud, and it shows: the canyon's diet of bellwood nuts, drumfruit, and oil-rich sap gives them a look like the flora — waxy, glimmering faintly, their skin faintly translucent at the temples, as though lit from behind by the chord they live inside. Their hair takes dust and keeps it, silvered with salt no matter how young they are. They drink bitterness once a day, the dawn-scrape, warm from the gourd, rationed by an office so old that its officers no longer know they hold an office — the water-tenders are simply *born* to certain families, as the tuners are, as the readers are.

The tuners are the spine of them. A standing profession, an order, a law: no wall without a tuner, because every wall is a note. They hang the pendulum dampers from the ceiling-thick salt and walk the bellwood shutters at the Mouths' throats, and their eras of the city have names that are keys, and the city itself has had eleven names and none is a word. When a tuner dies, the chord is shifted half a beat in mourning, and everyone below feels the change in their teeth before they hear of the death. When the depths answer — that chest-felt note from below, the one no wind makes — the tuners do not fight it. They let it join. They keep one damper open. They have one sentence for why, and the sentence is older than the grammar it survives in, and no one below would dream of asking for a second.

The readers kneel at the plaza edges under breath-canopies and watch the dust make itself into the world's own handwriting, and they are the closest thing to a priesthood this place has — except that everyone is, a little, because the oldest word for maintenance and the oldest word for prayer are the same word, and so sweeping a plaza and tuning a damper and scraping the dawn-water are all devotions, and every one of them does all three.

And at dawn, when the wind above turns and the great chord falls to a whisper for a hundred heartbeats — that is when you would finally see what they are. Three hundred people speaking all at once, spending everything, the saved sentences and the decade-old apologies, babies screaming with astonishment at their own voices, the whole grey quiet ringing with one species' hoarded speech. Then the tide of roar returns and they fall silent together, faces lifted, unregretful, and the canyon's green leans into the flood and drinks.

They are what lives in a drought of people and a flood of sound: a small, patient, luminous nation, declining gently for longer than memory, who have made of the world's noise a language, a harvest, a scripture, and a home.
