The citizens are the mountain. That is the second wrong shape your mind has to accept.

The polyps never died. They are there now, under your hand — thousands to the square meter, in the dimples of every buttress and the runnels of every fluting, withdrawn into their aragonite cups in the daylight like a held breath in stone. They are minute. A single one would sit on the head of a pin with room for its opinions. But there are billions of them, and the terraces you are standing on are not the ruins of their city. They are the city — still under construction, still being argued over, cell by cell, the way it has been argued over since before the sea left. When the uplift came, the water should have killed them; a reef polyp is a small soft patience that endures exactly one set of conditions forever. But the rock beneath was throwing off its own heat, blood-warm, and the polyps on the sunward shoulders found themselves in the one place on the ruined seafloor where the temperature had not changed at all. They did not know the sea had left. In the way of corals, which is to say in no way that requires knowing, they kept building.

The grove is how they eat now. This is the machine the fog feeds. The trees — mangrove-descended, their roots gripping the reef's terraces like fingers gripping a parapet — conduct the harvested water down into channels the polyps themselves have grown to meet it, and in the damp shadowed hollows beneath the canopy the old symbiosis has been renegotiated for air. The algae that once lived inside each polyp's flesh now live in the leaves, fat and glossy and green, brewing sugar in the honest sunlight above and piping it down through root and channel to the waiting mouths below. The reef is a farmer now. It raises its own light-catchers in rows, in tiers, with a discipline you can read in the wax and the tilt of every basin leaf: nothing spilled, nothing exposed, nothing wasted. At fog-dawn, when the grey ropes come up off the salar, the polyps open. There is no sound. There never is. But the whole architecture of lobes and domes blossoms, all at once, in the wet dark under the canopy — a million small mouths tasting the fog for what it might carry, which is little, and greeting it anyway, which is everything.

And then there are the others, because a reef never was one thing. It was always a city of ten thousand tenants on one landlord's bones, and the tenants came up with the building.

The crabs came first and have never stopped being important. Their descendants are the gardeners and the customs officers — flat-bodied, wax-sealed against the dryness, patient as their ancestors, and each one armored in the exact purple of the deep reef so that from above the whole talus slope at certain hours looks like a spilled handful of the old sea. They walk the boundary at dusk, renewing the chemical moat with compounds brewed in glands that once made slime for swimming. They prune the grove. They carry water in their cupped bellies up to polyps the fog cannot reach. When you cross the talus line and the smell of brine and green thickens around you, that is their work you are smelling, and they know you have entered before you have taken your third step, because the scent is a language and the language has a grammar and "stranger, large, slow" can be said in it quickly.

There are eels in the grove. This should not surprise you; it was a reef. The moray lineage has gone low and long and glossy-dark, hunting the humid corridors beneath the canopy for the grazers that would otherwise strip the basin-leaves, and their passage is the loudest thing in the city — a dry rushing through leaf-litter, which in a place with no wind and no water and no birdsong is loud as surf. They have kept the sea's jaw. They have lost the sea's fear. Nothing here has ever met a predator from outside, because outside is the salar, and the salar kills everything that walks onto it slowly and completely. The city has been safe so long that its fears have turned inward and become manners.

The birds are newcomers — arrivals within a mere hundred thousand years, and the city regards them the way an old family regards recent wealth: useful, energetic, not quite polished. They nest in the domes' flutings and their guano is harvested with an efficiency that borders on adoration, ferried down the channels by crab-gangs to the waiting polyps, the sea's old phosphorous economy reconstructed in full, a kilometer in the sky. The birds think the city is a cliff with unusual amenities. The city thinks the birds are a delivery service that reproduces itself. Both are content.

But hold your attention on the polyps, because the deep strangeness is not the inventory. It is the arithmetic. Each polyp is a separate animal — a mouth, a sting, a set of instructions, a life. Each one is capable of acting alone, and in the mornings a few do, budding off into the terraces, settling their disputes over growing-room with slow wars of encrustment that last a decade and move a boundary four centimeters. And yet the city — the terraces, the domes, the grove's channels, the fog-machinery, the chemical moat — requires the coordinated labor of the whole, decisions made at scale, in a place where no individual has anything resembling a brain. The fog comes up the scarp and the entire eastern face opens its cups in one minute of agreement. How? Scent. Hormone. The water itself, moving through the shared channels and carrying signals dissolved in it the way the old sea once carried them — the city has rebuilt its ocean, internally, and it governs itself by drinking.

They spawn as their ancestors did, all together, in a season — but the season is triggered now not by moon and warm water but by the first fog of the year that comes heavy enough to run in the channels. The spawn goes into the flow and out through the talus line and up onto the wind that comes off the salar at dawn, and the larvae are drifters, the most patient gametes in the world, riding the air the way their ancestors rode the tide, looking for the only other thing they will ever recognize: the chemical signature of a city. There is, as far as anyone knows, only the one. Most of the wind-borne young are lost to the salt plain. The city knows this in whatever way it knows things. It spawns anyway. It has been spawning anyway since the water left, a whole metropolis sending its children out over a dead world every year, and the terraces have grown a little wider each century, and the domes a little higher, and if somewhere out beyond the horizon the earth should someday shrug up another seafloor, another warm young mountain, another accident of stone — there is a scent-marked invitation already riding the wind toward it, waiting for the ground to arrive.

They do not know they are in the wrong place. There is no wrong place in the grammar of polyps; there is only water, warmth, and the company of your own kind. The sea is gone, and the city did not mourn it, because mourning requires an understanding of absence, and the reef has never understood anything. It has only continued. It took the warmth that rose to meet it and the fog that came up from the salt and the birds that arrived on their own, and it made of them a working sea, a kilometer above the ghost of the last one — and the world outside is unfinished, and the city goes on finishing itself, drop by silent drop, in the grey hour before the sun clears the ridge.
