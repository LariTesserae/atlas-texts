# What Lives in the Comb

Begin with the grazers, because you will meet them first and because they are nearly everyone.

A grazer is a hand's length of animal that has solved every problem with the same answer: flatness. It is a leaf-shaped body the thickness of a coin, armored in a chitin so dark it reads as a hole in the teal light, fringed with legs too numerous to count and too slow to need counting. Under the rim of its body runs a mouth-part like a shave — a scraper of hardened edge that the grazer works across the wet stone in strokes that take a minute apiece, lifting the bacterial film the way a plane lifts a shaving, digesting it in a gut that is mostly patience, and re-depositing behind itself a bead of polymer that dries into lattice. What it eats and what it leaves are the same substance twice-processed. It is difficult, standing over one for an hour, to say whether it is feeding on the wall or writing on it, and this difficulty is the most important fact about the Comb's inhabitants: their categories ended up in different places than ours did. We separated the farm from the farmer, the meal from the material, the singer from the song. Nothing here ever learned to make those cuts.

They do not sleep, exactly. They throttle. The cold has already brought them nearly to a standstill; what remains to them is a fine adjustment, a few percent of metabolism dimmed during the district's cold half-cycle, when the condensation fronts retreat and the walls go tight and quiet. Their lives are not measured in heartbeats — the heart is a slow peristaltic tube and gives perhaps four hundred thousand beats to a grazer's decade — but in *licks*, in area covered, in film consumed, in lattice laid. An old grazer is legible the way a tree ring is legible: its scraper wears into a signature, a bevel unlike any other bevel in the district, and the wall behind it carries that signature in a faint ridge-line a careful eye can follow for meters. There are grazers whose ridgelines have been followed by other grazers for generations, walls that are palimpsests of ten thousand mouths, each one's work ethic and appetite and tiny travels recorded in the substance of the city itself. The archive of the Comb is not written anywhere. It is the walls. Every interior face is a library shelved in its own subject.

The filter-creatures of the canal-streets are the grazers' opposite and their cousin: where the grazer flattened itself against the solid, the filter-creature has given itself over to flow. It is a curtain, mostly — a body that is ninety percent cilia by volume, hanging in the warm rising currents with the indifference of fabric. They reproduce by pinching, and a canal-street with a good current will fill from one side to the other in a season, until pushing through them is like wading through a beaded doorway. They are eaten constantly and constantly regrow, and their role in the arrangement is suspiciously like a function: they strain the mineral water, they slow it, they deposit in their wake a water chemically changed — cooler, sweeter, more tractable to the capillary roads. Whether they are animals that have become plumbing or plumbing that has become animals is a question the Comb does not pose. The water needs cleaning; something cleaned it; something is now the cleaning.

Above the galleries, the drifting lantern-things. Fist-sized, mostly bladder, they ride the slow thermals that breathe off the warm walls, and each carries at its tail-end a culture of the light-bacteria in a pouch of transparent skin — a lantern not built but *cultivated*, which is the only way anything is built here. They are the Comb's sky and its weather and possibly its correspondence. Watch one for a day's cycle and you will see it descend, specifically, deliberately, to a wall — not to graze but to *hold still over a scrape*, over a mark some grazer has left, before rising again. There is a school of thought — held by the quiet kind of observer who stands in the silent alleys for a year — that the lantern-things are the Comb's eyes, its slow surveying gaze, and that a district's lantern density over a wall is a form of attention. Nothing here is fast enough to prove it. Nothing here needs to be.

And the long ones. Mention them carefully, the way the district's drone drops a tone when they pass. They are predators the length of a street, ribboned and pale, moving through the galleries at cloud-shadow pace, and they are the only inhabitants of the Comb that do not contribute. This appears to be their contribution. The district holds its voice for four hours when they pass — ten thousand animals in unison muting their stridulators to a whisper — and the sound-structure of the place sags for those four hours, certain load-bearing frequencies dropping out, and what repairs the sag is the bedrock's own reply, arriving on schedule from below the fog. The long ones, in other words, may be the Comb's stress test. Its proof-load. The arrangement may keep them the way a bridge keeps the traffic that strains it — because the straining is the demonstration that the structure stands. Or they may simply be hungry. Both accounts are held simultaneously by the same observers, without discomfort, because a world whose thoughts run at this pace can afford contradictions for centuries.

The masons deserve their own paragraph, and it is short, because the honest finding is that they cannot be told apart. A mason is a grazer whose scraper happens, hour by patient hour, to be laying lattice at the growing edge rather than film at the interior wall — same body, same mouth, same pace, the only difference being where it stands. And here is the thing: they rotate. An individual works the edge for a season and then drifts, in the slow shuffle of ten million bodies, back into the interior, and another takes its place, and no boundary can be drawn between builder and resident, worker and fed. The Comb's construction crew is not a caste. It is a *current*, a circulation, the way blood is not a separate organ from the body. If you went looking for the people of the Comb — for someone to interview, someone who knows what the plan is — this is the disappointment and the revelation: there is no one in charge because there is no one *in particular*. There is only everyone, in aggregate, doing small things at a geological tempo, and the aggregate's mind is the drone.

Which brings you, finally, to the strangest resident, the one that cannot be met at any single address: the Comb itself. Not the structure — the structure is its shell, its skeleton, its coral. The inhabitant is the chord. Ten thousand detuned slow voices, geographically distributed, doing work in the only medium this world has in abundance, which is time and sound. A wall sags where the voices thin. A district whose drone has been damaged — by a long one's passing, by a frost breach, by the death of a major filter-colony — repairs its voices before it repairs its stone, or rather repairs them *by means of* repairing its stone, since a grazer that lays lattice also stridulates while laying, and the singing and the building are, once again, the same act. The Comb's inhabitants do not live *in* the Comb the way tenants live in a building. They are cells of it, and it is the only organism in the room, and its thoughts are eleven hours long, and it is speaking, right now, in a sub-bass conversation with a sister it has never seen across a darkness it will not cross for ten thousand generations — and it is not lonely, because it is not finished, and in every sense that matters here, it is still condensing.
