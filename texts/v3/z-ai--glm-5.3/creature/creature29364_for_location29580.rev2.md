Nothing here lives the way lives are usually lived. Nothing dens, holes up, or comes home at dark. The Weeping Shelf does not offer residence; it offers terms — warmth underfoot, water on a beat, and a strict ration of room — and every life that takes the terms ends up shaped by all three. Its inhabitants have a family resemblance that has nothing to do with ancestry: a certain economy, a certain patience, as if they had all signed the same quiet lease.

The oldest of them has no name and covers the wall. It is the green-black felt of algae along the seep-lines, and it must have been the first arrival — the moment the hot seam found the cliff's plumbing and the water began its slow hanging-on, the felt came in behind it and has not had a dry hour since. Nor a cold one. It is the one life on this mountain with no winter in its biography. And it grows in stripes that map the water's tempo: thin and bright beneath the quick seeps, dark and thick beneath the slow ones, so that even the wall, read correctly, is written in time — a score of living green, varnished by the wet, crossed by the rust tears, keeping its own record of its own beat.

Inside that felt, and inside the moss on the felted boulder, lives the shelf's only crowd, and almost nobody ever sees it. Springtails in their hundreds; mites; rotifers turning in the films of water on the stone; tardigrades riding out the drier hours as grains of dust and waking at the first fog. Everywhere else on this ledge, the constancy of the water slows life down — flowers that take four days, a basin that rises a finger-width by dusk. In the felt it works the other way: always warm, always wet, no season that kills, so that a springtail's whole career fits inside a week of drips. The shelf rations everything large and permits a multitude in miniature. Hurry, which the altitude forbids on all the rest of the mountain, pays here, and only here.

The moth is the ledge's one flying thing, and there are never more than a few of it at a time, which appears to be policy rather than accident. Its wings are the grey of wet stone, and each carries the teal mark it is known by — the color the basin goes at dusk, when the light fails and the water keeps a little of the sky. It wears the place's two materials, rock and water, and vanishes into both. Its caterpillars graze the felt — the young of the shelf eating its oldest inhabitant, at a ration the felt can sustain, which is why each generation emerges in twos and threes instead of clouds. An adult is given perhaps ten dusks, and spends them flying in the thin fog that recondenses on the cooling stone, so that even its appearances are rationed: a flash of teal, fog, another flash — moths counted the way you count drips, one and one and one.

The beetle is a drop of dark water that learned to walk. It lives its whole life within a few paces of the basin, under the tepid flagstones, and its sheen is not gloss but actual wet — the air beads on everything here, and the beetle keeps itself the way the cliff keeps itself, looking always just emerged. There are perhaps a dozen on the entire shelf, each the sole proprietor of one island of moss or one stretch of felt-edge: a landlord of almost nothing, which on this ledge is a full estate. It hunts at night, on a circuit shorter than a sentence, taking springtails and whatever the dusk has finished with. And because it hatched beside the drum — the basin, the downbeat, the loudest point of the loudest thing here — the beat was the first fact it ever met. It has never heard silence. In three or four slow years, not one unmeasured second. To meet one, on the warm stone, feels less like luck than like something agreed in advance. (Nothing lives in the basin itself. Its level rises and falls with every day and night, so nothing settles in it; it stays the one address on the shelf kept vacant, the way a drum is kept clear.)

That is nearly the whole animal economy of the place, and it is a circle you could cover with one hand: the felt feeds the moth, the finished moth feeds the beetle, the beetle's leavings feed the felt. Nothing hunts across the gaps. The bare warm stone between the green islands is a real border, and each island is very nearly a closed little country.

The thrush is the one creature that crosses the borders daily, and it is not a resident at all — it is the shelf's client. Its life is down the slope in the dwarf forest and is loud in ordinary ways: wind, other thrushes, its own singing, all the unordered noise of the world. Once a day, in the hard clean light after the morning fog has dissolved back into the sky, it comes over the edge, gives a single thin call — the only sound this place ever hosts that is not percussion — hops the rim, and drinks. For the length of the drink the two tempos cross: the bird gulps against the beat and flings a scatter of silver out of the count, and the count takes it back in. It is the only improvisation the shelf ever hears. Then the bird goes over the edge, down into its noise, and it never sings here; the room has no register for song, only for the count. Whether it is the same bird each day is the wrong question. The visit is older than any bird — an office handed down whatever line of grey mountain thrushes has held this slope, so that the water has had a client for longer than anyone can reckon, and longer, certainly, than the person.

The person is the shelf's other irregular: a guest who came, apparently more than once, and then stopped. Whoever it was likely followed the thrush, or followed the sound a mile downwind on a still day — both are followable things. They sat by the basin, where the water is fresh and the stone is warm, the best seat on the shelf and the only one. They knapped three blades from a single core, cut fern stems at the angle only stone takes, gathered what the magnolia was offering, took most of it away down the mountain, and left a bundle of reed lashed with bark fiber hanging in the tree. They built nothing — not for lack of time, but for lack of anything here that wants building. A generation has gone by. The shelf, being green, has spent that generation deciding what to do with the visit, and deciding it slowly.

Because the only full residents this place has — the only lives that spend an entire existence at one address, from first root to last rot — are plants. Every animal on the shelf, even the ones that never leave it, is in some sense passing through: born into a pause, finished inside a decade. The green ones stay.

The fern stand at the eastern rim — tree-ferns, valley plants that should not be here at all and stand ten feet tall anyway because the stone is warm — is where the human visit is still in force. The cut stems healed into rings, and the plants answered the way ferns answer, by stooling, sending up more stems than they would ever have raised alone. Twenty years on, the stand runs thicker than its neighbors, every extra frond a clause in a long reply to a single question about a few stems. It is the one place on the mountain where a human decision is still being administered, and it is administered entirely by plants.

The magnolia keeps the bundle. This is the tree that maintains a courteous emptiness around itself by shade and drip — the one resident that tidies after itself, since its few fruits fall into its own clearing and are taken apart there by the small felt-life and returned to its roots, a closed account. Mountain wood is slow; a tree only house-high here may be centuries along, and it opens each flower the way a hand relaxes, over four days, not the way a bloom bursts. Into its branches the person hung the only gift the shelf has ever received, and the gift is made of the shelf: cut reed, bark fiber, and three or four calyces of the tree's own flowers. Whether it was forgotten or left on purpose is a distinction the place is not equipped to make; a thing left in a tree is a thing the tree now holds, and the tree holds it either way. The bark has begun to take the cord in, the way the basin takes drops — a finger-width at a time — and in some decade the bundle will be held as the lichen holds the flint.

The lichen is the archivist. It grows over the worked stone at the pace of everything else here, sealing the knapped edges — the only sharp things on the whole warm ledge — under a soft rind, a millimeter at a time. In another generation the blades will be green-grey hummocks indistinguishable from the rock they were struck on. That is the shelf's way with what people leave: not rejection, not preservation, but incorporation, at the tempo of the water. The last sharp thing a person did here is going slowly soft, and the softness is the keeping.

Which leaves, as the final tenant, the one that owns everything and occupies nothing: the beat. Every life on this ledge is lived inside it. The felt grows in stripes to its tempo. The beetle is born into it and never once steps outside it. The moth spends its ten dusks flying inside it. The thrush drinks against it once a day and loses. The person sat still for it — which may have been the whole errand. And between them they cover the day: the felt around the clock, the beetle through the dark, the moth at dusk, the thrush at the bright hour, and once, for a few afternoons, a person. The ledge is never entirely unattended. Something is always on shift.

So this is who lives there: a wall of green with no winter, a crowd too small to see, a moth on a ration, a dozen beetles who have never heard silence, a tree with manners, a bird that holds an office, and a person present as a thickness of ferns, a softening of flint, and a bundle going slowly into bark. On this one warm seam of the mountain, being a resident and being a listener turn out to be the same condition — the tenants are, before they are anything else, an audience. The count was going before any of them arrived and will go on after every one of them is gone, with or without a house to play to. Ask the shelf who lives there, and it answers the way it answers everything: with the next drop.
