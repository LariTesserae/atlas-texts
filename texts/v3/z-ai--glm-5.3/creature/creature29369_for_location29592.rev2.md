**The people of the Work** do not think of themselves as people of a place. When they are asked — and they are rarely asked, for they are rarely visited — they name not a location but an activity. They are the ones doing it. That distinction is not modesty; it is theology.

They are slight and pale in the way everything under a green-gold ceiling is pale, but not bloodless. The work keeps them warm, and you can see it: forearms corded from chiseling, hands broad and calloused in a very particular pattern — the fingertips and palms thickened from decades of reading vibration in stone. Their feet are as sensitive as their fingers. A child of the Work, barefoot on a terrace, can tell you who is coming up the stair three flights below by the signature of their step, the little signature of weight and hurry that every resident carries in the rock the way a surface-dweller carries a voice. They walk softly out of habit, not etiquette. Loud walking is simply wasted effort; the ground is a conversation already in progress.

Their language, if it can be called one language, has three registers. There is handtalk — quick, flickering, used for the market, for argument, for flirtation across a gutter — and it is wonderfully expressive precisely because it grew up in a place where interruption is impossible. There is touch-speech, the rapping of pillars, patient, lettered, capable of carrying a sentence half across the buried valley to be read by a palm an hour later. It is for anything that must endure: contracts, griefs, directions to a person not present. And there is the third register, which has no surface equivalent: the standing conversations, the ones conducted by simply resting a hand against a shared wall while the other party rests theirs against the far side, feeling for the faint tremor of speech through the body of the Work. Lovers do this. So do the very old, who claim the wall says things back.

They are not poor, but they own almost nothing, and what they own is strange. A household's wealth is measured in dripline — the length of damp wall it can claim and keep — and the heirlooms are few and hard: two or three blades of surface stone in the oldest families, ground thin as regret, and names. Names are the true inheritance. Because nothing sharp survives and everything is re-cut, a family's name is often the only thing that has been continuously maintained for a hundred generations, sanded and renewed like a stair-riser, and the great names of the Work are longer than any living memory of what they were first given for.

Their calendar is the ceiling's. They do not count days; they count brightenings and rains, and their oldest reckoning is a single unbroken count kept by the light-readers — the old ones who watch the sky-sweat the way sailors watch glass — of every rain since the first terrace was cut. The number is very large. They are quietly, enormously proud of it.

They farm the shine with the seriousness of any agriculture: watering walls at the right hour, scraping bloom into bowls, sweet and chalk-sour, penning the licker herds against the wet faces and moving the pens as the damp moves, herding them with taps of the finger on stone, since the beasts feel the wall the way everything here feels the wall. The lickers come when rapped for. There is a boy's game in which you stand very still against a damp face and see how long a grazer takes to find your shoulders — an inch of film's growth before it does, if you are good.

And they have, like any people, their quiet excesses. There is a sect, tolerated with the weary affection reserved for the harmless, who believe the ceiling-film dreams, and who spend their lives pressed palm-first to the high roof in the upper galleries, reading. There are the remakers, the crews who work the collapse sites, held to be the closest thing the Work has to a priesthood — they are the ones who unmake, and unmaking is not destruction here; it is the second half of a sacred cycle, dust to block to wall to dust, and they perform it in the same silence as everything else, which makes it feel less like labor than like ritual. There is one law spoken of in touch-speech on the oldest pillar, rapped there so long ago that the letters have been re-cut eleven times, and every hand in the valley knows it without needing to go and read it: *what comes apart is never lost.*

They know about the surface, dimly, the way the surface knows about them — as rumor. Some few have made the long stair up and come back changed, useless for a generation, flinching at the loudness of a dropped pebble, homesick for rain that falls from a living sky and cannot be caught. Most never go. Why would they. Stand on a high terrace at the hour the ceiling sweats and the whole sparse population moves at once to the runnels, thousands of quiet hands catching water in the light of a sky that is raining, and you will see what they have instead of a nation, a history, a monument: a thing being done, continuously, patiently, forever — and a people who are not the inhabitants of it but the verb.
