# The People of the Shelf

## What the Cold Has Made of Them

The people of Kedram-Ai are not small, though strangers expect them to be. The cold does not shrink a body; it fills one out. They are thick through the chest and slow through the hands, warm-blooded the way a hearthstone is warm-blooded — all night's heat stored up through the day's labor. Their skin runs to deep browns and wind-dark reds, cheeks carved with the fine cracked lines the high air gives everyone past thirty, lips that chap and are licked and are left alone as a lost argument. Their eyes are the feature that travelers remark on later, when they try to explain the city to people who were not there: pale, very often, grey or the yellow-green of the lichen continents, and set deep under heavy brows, and quick in a way the rest of the body is not. A Kedram face at rest looks carved and closed, like a lintel. A Kedram face paying attention looks like water.

The altitude has arranged them in other, quieter ways. They breathe wide and slow and sleep shallow, waking often without alarm or distress, because the thin air and the banked hearth-fires teach the body to live at a simmer. They speak softly because sound carries absurdly far in the cold clear air — a voice raised is a voice everyone hears, so a voice raised is either an emergency or a rudeness, and the two are hard to tell apart. Their songs, as the setting itself will tell you, are nearly whispered. What it does not tell you is that this is a choice the whole culture made about itself, somewhere long ago, and made consciously: *we could shout, and be heard by everyone, always; we have decided not to be a city of shouting.* Children learn the whisper-songs before they learn the work-songs, and the work-songs are quiet too, kept by the breath alone, so that a quarry crew of forty people can be heard from the canyon rim only as a faint braided hum, like wind in a bottle.

Their hands deserve their own paragraph. Every adult hand in Kedram-Ai has the same signature — calluses laid across the base of the thumb and the inner fingers in the exact shape of a chisel handle, and the fingertips polished smooth as river pebbles from running along dressed stone. There are no soft hands here except an infant's, and an infant's hands are looked at longer than an infant's face, because they are the last soft hands that person will ever have.

## How They Are Counted

A person in Kedram-Ai is not primarily an individual, which is not to say they are not known — they are known exhaustively, in a district where every conversation carries across four rooftops. A person is an intersection: a lineage, a hearth-right, a wall, a cliff, a gutter. The lintel signs on every family's door mean that a stranger can walk into any lane and read three centuries of who belongs where, and a resident can read it the way you read a friend's face.

Lineage matters most at birth and at marriage. Hearth-rights matter most every day. The hearth-right is the legal and emotional center of a household: the right to keep a fire in a particular hearth, and — because the houses share walls and chimneys and heat — the corresponding duty to bank it properly, to be a good neighbor not as sentiment but as physics. A hearth-right can be lent, inherited, held in trust for a child, fought over in the slow courts of the quarters. The most serious insult available in the language of Kedram-Ai translates, roughly, as *one who lets the fire die in another person's wall.*

The winters-survived tally on the lintel is exactly what it sounds like: a family counts its winters at that hearth, one fresh notch per year, cut at first dew-fall, and the notch-cutting is the year's true new year, done by the eldest child old enough to hold a chisel straight, supervised by the eldest person still steady enough to see crooked. There are lintels in the old quarter with two hundred notches crossing two hundred earlier notches where a household left and another came and both are still counted, both tallies kept honest, because the wall remembers and it would be a lie to the stone to erase anyone.

## The Masons

The masons hold power here the way stone holds a city: not by grasping, but by being what everything else rests on. There are mason-families in every quarter, and together they form something between a guild, a senate, and a clergy. They do not rule by decree. They rule by being the only people who can say — slowly, in a meeting that may take a month to convene — whether a wall can bear the load you want to put on it, whether a cistern can be sunk where you want to sink one, whether the ground beneath your plan is rising or stable or rising *fast*.

The last one is the real power. Because the Shelf is still standing up beneath the city, fraction by fraction every year, the masons are the only institution that converses with the land directly. Every few decades a street's steps are re-cut, and the masons decide which street, and when, and the decision ripples through rents and dowries and which quarter's children walk to which moss-terraces. The masons say the city is learning to stand, and they say it without poetry, the way a physician says a bone is knitting.

Their training is measured against moss-pastures and quarries, not against years: an apprentice is not a mason until a wall he dressed has stood through a decade of freeze and dew without a single stone weeping out of line. This means some apprentices wait fifteen years. Nobody finds this strange. Nobody finds anything here strange that takes fifteen years; strangeness, like everything else, is allowed to accumulate slowly.

## The Dew-Families

If the masons are the city's skeleton, the dew-families are its veins. The condensation walls — long, low, black-basalt sweating surfaces angled to drink the dawn — are tended hereditarily, each by a family whose lintel-carries a sign meaning, roughly, *water-keeper of this stone*. The work is meticulous and half-invisible: the wiping of the harvest faces at first light, the annual resealing with a mortar recipe each family guards and each family's is slightly, stubbornly different; the graded gutters checked by hand, by feel, by kneeling in the pre-dawn cold with a bare palm flat against the stone to judge how the night's wetness has come.

The dew-families have their own courts, their own marriage customs (a marriage between two dew-families of adjacent walls involves an elaborate and very quiet ritual exchange of gutter-water, poured from one catchment into the other's cistern, witnessed by both lineages), and their own accent — a flattened, even flatter way of speaking, as though their profession of listening to dripping water had shaped their mouths. Their children can identify, blindfolded, by taste alone, which of the city's cisterns a cup of water came from. This is not a trick. It is simply what their palates are for.

They are, as a class, the most patient people in a patient city, and it is said of them, with respect and a little wariness, that they have all heard the plink of condensation so long that they no longer hear it at all — which means they hear *everything else*.

## The Children of the Roof-Country

Children in Kedram-Ai belong, until roughly the age of ten, to the roof-country. They cross the city at roof level, over the plank bridges and the habit-bridges, fish in a reef of chimney stacks and awning-lines and drying-frames, and it is understood by everyone — stated aloud only when a stranger asks, appalled — that a child who falls from the roof-country was being careless, and that carelessness is the roof-country's own teacher, and that the teacher is a good one, since almost no one falls twice.

Roof-children develop a specific physicality: sure-footed, narrow-gauge, comfortable crouched, with an uncanny sense of the whole city as a topography. They learn every household's skylight and every courtyard dog by name. They run errands over three districts in the time an adult takes to descend one stair. And they serve, without knowing it, as the city's nervous system — news, gossip, invitations, small warnings, all travel by roof, and a mother who wants to reach a neighbor across town often simply tells a roof-child, and the message arrives before her kettle boils.

When a child comes down from the roof-country for good — and the descent is deliberate, marked, the last plank crossing done slowly and formally with the family watching from below — they come down as messengers, herders, apprentices, water-girls. The city loses one of its birds and gains one of its people, and both transitions are celebrated with equal ceremony, in the courtyard, in the evening, quietly.

## The Water-Girls and the Canyon-Walkers

The girls who go down the switchbacks to the pocket seeps carry skin bags and a rope and their family's tally-stick, and they go in pairs or threes, because the slot canyons are beautiful and narrow and dark and the oldest rule on the Shelf is that the wet places are not entered alone. The seep-water is different from dew-water — harder, mineral, tasting of the strata it threads through — and it is kept for drinking and for the sick, while the dew-water is for the moss-pastures and the ferment and the general life of the city. Every child can explain this hierarchy. Every adult is quietly proud that they can.

The canyon-walkers — the swift-harvesters — are licensed under counting rules older than any ledger in the city, rules that are not written anywhere because writing them down would imply someone might not know them. *Take from a cliff only what that cliff can forget.* The harvesters take eggs by hand-count, aloud, in pairs who check each other, and the number is fixed per cliff per season, and a century of fixed numbers has produced cliffs that are, by any measure the masons can devise, healthier than the unharvested ones — a fact the harvesters cite with the mild, unarguable smugness of people proven right by generations.

They are the city's most far-ranging people and its most silent. A week of walking the canyon rims teaches a silence that never entirely leaves; canyon-walkers are known for it, and are prized as arbitrators and as bearers of difficult news, because they can say anything, in the carrying air, at a volume no one beyond the intended listener will hear.

## The Herders, the Hutchers, and the Hunters

Goat-children — sling-armed, wind-burnt, half-feral with purpose — hold the moss-terraces' edges and the spine's thin grass. The hutch-families of the eastern quarter keep the drell in stacked stone hutches that climb five stories of wall, and their quarter sounds like it: the click, mutter, rustle of ten thousand fat rodents, a sound so constant that hutch-family people say they cannot sleep in the quiet quarters, the silence keeps them awake.

The shagh-hunters are the city's travelers, its storytellers, and its slightly alarming uncles. A hunting party is gone a week across the Shelf's far distances, tracking the tall grey wool-and-horn shapes that come down from the higher spine in their season, drying the meat on bone frames in the wind, and coming home lean and weather-scoured and full of news of the horizon — the rain-walls they watched pass at a distance, the herds they did not take from, the strange standing stones out past the last known cistern that they argue about every winter and never agree on. Every Kedram child knows that the most reliable sentence in the city is a returning hunter's first sentence, which is always, quietly: *the Shelf is still there.*

## The Old, and the Way of Dying

Because the cold is even and un-complaining, because the city's dangers are sudden only rarely, the people of Kedram-Ai age the way their buildings age: by accretion, by repair, by doors becoming niches. The very old are moved, by custom, inward — into the densest, warmest quarters, nearest the shared walls — so that their last years are spent in the parts of the city that hold heat best. The proverb for this has a double meaning everyone politely declines to say aloud: *a neighbor's wall is a second blanket.*

The dead are given to the midden-stacks — the great organized civic middens where the city's dung and leavings and dust are layered with the same seriousness as the cisterns — and this is not grimness but completion. Everything the city has, it has from the living land; everything the city's people are, they return. The masons say the midden-stacks are where the Shelf's soil will come from, in some century when erosion finally wins its first small argument, and so the dead of Kedram-Ai are, in the most literal and unsolemn sense, the future ground of the place. Families visit the stacks the way other cultures visit graves — to stand, to tally, to remember — and the visits are brief and undramatic and always end with the living turning back toward the city, toward the walls, toward the hearths that need banking before dark.

## What They Believe

They believe the land is young. Not in a doctrine — in a feeling in the bones, the same feeling everyone gets standing on the southern rim watching a rain-wall cross the plain like a curtain: that this all happened recently, that something lifted it, that whatever lifted it has not finished. They do not worship the lifting. They do not quite mythologize it, either. What they do is stranger and more practical: they treat the Shelf as a young thing in their care, and they behave toward it the way careful people behave toward anything young and growing — they adjust. They re-cut the steps. They re-fit the cistern walls. They change their laws slowly and by quarter-consensus, because a law that changes fast in a land that changes slow is a law that will be wrong for a very long time.

They have a saying for strangers, offered kindly, usually at the moment the stranger is shivering or hurrying or asking when the rain will come: *the dew always comes.* It means what it says. It also means patience is not a virtue here, it is the substrate, it is the water the whole culture grows in. A person of Kedram-Ai can wait a season for a stone, a decade for a wall, a lifetime for a moss-pasture to mature, and will tell you, without any sense of saying anything remarkable, that these are the ordinary times of things, and that the fast things — the hour of drumming rain, the burst of ten thousand wings at dawn, the single season a child spends on the roof-country — are fast *precisely so that they can be wholehearted*.

Everything near-touching. Everything patient. Everything slowly, quietly, becoming itself — the land, the city, the people, indistinguishably, the way the lichen and its stone are indistinguishable, and the way no one in Kedram-Ai would ever think to try.
