Begin with the easy answer, the one the census-takers would write down first: a few thousand humans. Then keep going, because the humans are the newest arrivals and possibly the least interesting residents, or possibly the entire point of the place, depending on your school of thought.

## The Tenants, Current

The humans arrived last and behave like guests who have read the house rules. They move slowly — not from ordinance but from acclimation; the soft, enormous shadows punish haste, and the canopy's amber pools make depth a negotiation. Natives walk as though depth were negotiable, because for them it is. Their pupils are said to have widened over a few generations, though whether this is physiology or a story the residents tell about themselves has not been established; what is established is that you can tell native-walk from visitor-walk at a glance, one sure and one apologetic.

They speak under the sound of dripping water without having decided to. Children learn whispering as a first language, and a native child's shout is an event — heads turn with mild interest, the way people turn toward weather.

Their occupations are the city's occupations. There are moss-keepers, who brush the meter of raw basalt exposed at every terrace base with soft tools, lifting the moss and relocating it to creases where it is welcome — nothing harmed, order kept, the stone kept visibly young, which is the city's one ritual chore and an honor handed down like a surname. There are channel-readers, who claim to parse the substrate-mind's drip-ledgers, wet dialect and dry. There are terrace-gardeners who are half plumbers and half diplomats. And there are archivists, who tend the documentation, or are tended by it — the distinction has become unclear to everyone including the archivists.

The bars are dim and amber and quietly dripping, and in the oldest of them there is a shelf, and on the shelf a sunlamp, the standing prize in the otter argument. It cannot be used. The canopy cannot be turned up; the dusk is not a setting. It sits above the bar anyway, like the relic of a light nobody wants, and every year someone wins it and gives it back.

Once a year, or twice, the cisterns overflow. The surplus spills across the basalt in shallow braided sheets, hissing and pattering like applause, and the residents walk out onto the black rock with flasks and blankets to hear it. This is the loud night, the one sanctioned noise, and under the cover of real water the whole city raises its voices at once. Old arguments are held at full volume, laughing. Everyone watches the braided sheets for a certain low sleek wake, and everyone pretends not to, because it is rude to watch anything enjoy itself too closely.

## The Small Lives

The condensation moths are called, in the documentation, *mobile condensate surfaces, class one*. The residents call them drips with wings. Their wing surfaces carry the same fog-hungry structure as the terrace vanes — the city grows its own harvesting and sends it airborne. They drink at the channel edges and leave a cool pinprick of damp wherever they land.
