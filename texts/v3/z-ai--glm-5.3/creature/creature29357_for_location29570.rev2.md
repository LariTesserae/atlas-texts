Ask who lives in the Hollow, and the Overhead has an answer ready: nobody it knows. The Below is said quickly, the way you name a place you don't intend to go, so the question has to be walked down the zigzag to get a true one. At the bottom it starts simply — fifteen hundred people, maybe two — but a number is the wrong shape for them, because the Reach permits no other shape but position. Dry ground is so scarce here that everyone has had to stand on it together, packed along the two-mile spine shoulder to shoulder, and so a person on the Reach is named the way everything here is named: by placement. You are introduced by your door and your keeping — third house from the north end, keeper of the Throat-weir; the woman who tends the hoop-willows at the ninth pond. Names stay short, because they have to fit inside the rooms where they're said. And when two people pass on the Reach — and everyone passes everyone, several times a day; there is no way through this town that is not through the town — the greeting is always the same two words, question and answer at once: *Still here. Still here.*

They are a quiet people by physics rather than temperament. The mist eats distance; every voice carries exactly as far as the room it's spoken in; so speech on the Reach is calibrated like everything else, tuned to the space it will actually fill. Nobody shouts. Shouting is what the wind does, up on the Overhead, where it has someone to shout at. Ask the plateau about the Below and they will tell you, among other things, that the people whisper. Ask the Reach, and they will tell you the plateau talks loudly at a wind that will not listen, and that they came down into the quiet on purpose. There are songs, but they are worked at the volume of speech; anything larger, the mist confiscates.

And they move the way the ground taught them. Visitors arrive with plateau strides and are surprised by the floor: the moss that eats their hosts' footsteps eats theirs too, but they haven't yet learned to let it, and the town spends the first day of every trade pulling them out of the sedge by the arm. The visitors complain that the water tastes of iron. The town drinks it anyway, and holds that everything here tastes faintly of the rock — themselves included. Even their shadows are sociable. On a frost morning you throw two soft ones that don't disagree, and a people raised in that all-sides light grow up expecting to be seen from every direction at once. It makes them careful, which outsiders mistake for shy.

Here is the deepest fact about who lives here, the one that turns the usual world over: the oldest living thing in the Hollow, for miles in any direction, is a woman. Not a tree — every tree is younger than she is. Not a beaver lodge, not a willow stand, not the sphagnum front, not even the lichen's thumbnail coins on the Cutface. She remembers open water. She stood on the rim as a child and watched the lake go out through the Throat, and she remembers the floor smoking as it dried. Everywhere else the land is ancient and the people are brief; here the people are the elders, and the land comes to them for its continuity. The town is the valley's memory, the memory is kept mostly in one old woman, and children are brought to her the way travelers consult a map. Hers is the saying the whole settlement runs on — *the lake is deciding* — and when she is gone, and she will be arranged like everything here, the drain itself will pass out of living memory and into the strata, filed with the shell and ash and lost beads, the same as everything else.

The year is a round of tending. In spring the weir-keepers prune back the willow wands that have sprouted through the winter — living weirs, growing weirs, fish-traps harvested twice, once for char and once for wands — and the sunken lanes get their new bundle floors, and every rooting fence is checked for what took. All year the stone-work continues: each house's thatch is rimmed with cobbles carried up from the shore one at a time, each chosen already-round, and a household's rim is its ledger — this stone from the year the second Throat pool held char, this one from the shore the morning the ice went out early — and guests are shown stones the way other towns show heirlooms. July is the wall of mosquito, the one loud season, and the town lives in smoke-smudge and grim humor, close-range complaint only, waiting it out. In the falling season come the reeds, cut and bundled, the boats standing upright in rows along the shore — a fence of boats drying for winter — and the children out after first frost for the cranberries, sugar-cold on one side, the one sweet thing this ground gives without being asked. Nobody here states an age in years. You are so many Lockings old, and the count is exact, because winter in the Hollow is not an interval between the living. It is one of the ways the living is done.

The covenant under everything — nothing here is made, everything is arranged — is recited by children, but its full weight is carried by adults, and mostly by adults in quarrels. You cannot demolish in the Hollow. Fences root. Walkways grow back. Fire is kept, not made with, and turf does not burn in any case, and the one explicit rule — never unmake another's arrangement — covers your enemy's house as surely as it covers a beaver's dam. So the town's disputes migrate like the Braid's channels: visibly, week to week, re-sorted, never straight, never finished, and the gravel bars of an old quarrel are as well known as the river's own. The one demolition permitted is leaving. A person who cannot live inside the arrangement walks up the zigzag, and the town lets them go without ceremony, because a departure is also an arrangement, and it is not anyone's to unmake. As for the ground, everyone knows who inherits it: stop tending a walkway and the moss takes it within the year, and the peat within fifty. The town builds with that heir in mind — lightly, in materials the heir will have uses for.

The beaver are the covenant's other signatories, and the town is sure of it. In eighty Lockings no beaver has dammed the Long Reach or ponded a doorway; the cobble spine, as the town reads it, is read from the water exactly the other way — an arrangement found already standing, kept by someone, best left, which is precisely how the town reads the dams. They call them larders and cisterns both, and leave every one standing. Out in the dark pools the tail-slap serves as the curfew bell: later than the third slap is late, and the children know it. The relationship is neighborly in the strict sense of the word — two parties keeping the same waterworks, never invited in, always at home.

The rest of the residents are abundant and, by the Hollow's law, nearly silent. The heron keeps its post in the Weep-shallows all winter, unpaid, a grey fixture the town treats as furniture entitled to frogs. The lynx is a rumor with a shape — a mile of willow carr crossed without once touching the ground — and nobody has ever seen it done, while everyone has heard it done, which in the Hollow is the standard evidence for anything. The char keep arriving, year on year, pushing their frontier up the braids, and the town meets the frontier with courtesy: a pool that holds fish for the first time is ringed with a trained willow hoop and left alone for three Lockings, arrivals being extended the same room the covenant gives incumbents. The frogs are down in the mud, sealed for the winter, all the summer hum banked — and the town, on the eve of its own Locking, does not find this strange. Sealing in is the local instinct. The frogs simply start earlier, and skip the smoke.

The children learn the rules the way they learn balance: from the ground up. North is the cold cheek of every hummock — frost stands on the north side all day, and you can find your way in fog by touch. Time is learned off the Cutface's lichen, thumbnail since the drain, hand-sized before your mother; the wall is the town's calendar and its arithmetic both. They play on gravel bars that will be somewhere else by autumn, which teaches the second rule before they can say it: places leave. And early they work as the town's surveyors, walking the Braid's shifting channels with knotted sedge cord and coming home to have the cord read at the fire, so that by nightfall the town knows its river's new shape. When the Cutface sheds, they count with the adults — lips moving, ten seconds is a big fall — and that silent counting, a few times a season, is the only thing in the Hollow that everyone does at the same instant.

And the dead live here too, which is the town's most local arrangement of all. You cannot bury anyone in the Hollow. Dig anywhere and the hole fills within minutes; the marsh returns what it is given by morning, cold and apologetic. Only the Reach holds a shape. So when someone ends, they are laid in the floor of the house they kept, a new skin of sod goes over, the house climbs another handspan, the doorsill lifts, and the dead keep the position they held in life: the same address, one floor down. No one is sent away. The Reach is a two-mile bead of houses that is also a two-mile bead of everyone who ever kept one, warm side up, and the whole town is rising on its own people the same way it rises on shell and ash. At each re-skinning the floors give back their lost beads. The beads are threaded on fresh cord and worn again, because nothing here is made, and nothing here, quite, is finished.

The Overhead comes down the zigzag twice a year, roped at the wrist in the mist — the mist eats distance, and plateau eyes are trained on distances that don't exist here — to trade wool and hide and news, the news being mostly weather, there being only the one subject up there, for smoked char and cranberry and sedge-cord. Then it goes back up, saying the Below quickly. The town does not mind. The town even agrees with the name. It is not a place you intend to go. It is a place you intend to stay.

Stand on the Reach at dusk, in the sound of the mist rebuilding itself out of the water, and put the question again — who lives here — and the town, if it answered out loud, would answer with the old woman's words: the lake is deciding. The lake never left. It went under, a hundred feet of silt holding it the way a sponge holds a spill, and it lies beneath the town's whole life like a landlord asleep under the floorboards, breathing through the Weeps. The people are its keepers by default and its tenants by patience. Once a year, at the Locking, they walk out across the frozen marsh — quiet on the young frost, breath showing, sledging their bundled reeds — and stand at last on top of the old lake: the annual visit, tenant to landlord, conducted at Hollow volume. And if the lake should ever decide to come back — the Throat still cutting down, the peat still rising, the beaver still damning, centuries of question — the town already knows its next addresses. The Cutface's benches are stacked like stairs, one old beach line above another, and everyone on the Reach can point to the shelf they will stand on: one bench up.

Until then the evenings end the same way. The swans go over with their far, slow, tearing-fabric whirr, the smoke-holes glow, and the packed silence settles over a full house — while under everything, through the floor, the valley keeps asking its two-word question, and the town keeps giving the answer it greets itself with, twice a day, all down the Reach: *still here. Still here.*
