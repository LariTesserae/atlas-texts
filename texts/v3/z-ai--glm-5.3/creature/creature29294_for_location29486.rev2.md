What lives here is not a household in any sense a person would recognize, but it is a household. It has a head, and it has servants, and it has guests, and it has one criminal.

Begin at the bottom, because the place itself would want it that way — the lintel says time falls, count it downward.

**The eel** is the oldest thing in the building, older than the ochre on the pictographs, if the fish can be trusted, and fish here are trusted more than writing. It lies at the center of the pool's gyre, black and long as an arm, and it does not move in any way you can catch at a glance. But it moves. The pale blind fish that circle it — a few hundred of them, traveling their slow carousel like beads on an invisible thread — are circling because the eel is circling them, or because the eel once circled them and the habit outlived the eel's body, or because the gyre itself is the eel's idea, expressed in fish. Nobody has resolved this, including the eel. It is fed by the carousel's drift — what the fish stir, what the fern-runoff seeds, what the algae film fattens — and it takes its toll the way a lord takes rent: invisibly, on a schedule, at the center. It has never once been seen to use its eyes. It may not need eyes. Everything that matters in this house arrives as an image first, and images, falling through water, arrive at the eel last and best.

**The fish** are the audience. That is their true occupation. They live under an inverted sky projected on the pool's black pane, and they have become, over ten thousand generations, readers. A shadow slides across the projected clouds and the water empties before the bird's body follows its own image down the light-tube. The fish that read best eat last and live longest; the house has been selecting for literacy since the dome was raised. They are pale because nothing here needs pigment; they school in a circle because the eel made a circle; they are blind in the way of creatures that traded eyes for something better — here, an early warning system written in light. They are, in a sense, the house's own nervous system, spread thin across the pool: the heron is known at the top step before its shadow is, and the whole house knows a shadow means a heron, and the deep drip sounds a beat later, almost like an acknowledgment.

**The crabs** are the staff. There are two shifts, pale and dark, day and night, and they work the runnels like a corps of masons' apprentices whose apprenticeship never ends. A crab's working life is one groove, or a few; it picks grit, clears clogs, walks the vein of the building from basin to basin with the placid diligence of something that has never once asked what the veins are for. The job description was not carved into them by the builders; it was carved into them by the fact that a clean groove is a wet groove and a wet groove is a place a crab can breathe and be fed by what the water carries. They maintain the house because the house maintains them — the purest labor arrangement in the world. When a clog exceeds a crab's strength, it stacks pebbles beside the blockage like a flag of surrender to an unseen chisel-hand, and this is the most articulate thing that happens in the vault on most days. At the rehearsal, at dawn, they turn belly-up against the weeping walls and drink the way leaves drink. They are the only tenants who take their wages directly from the weather.

**The snails** are the finishers. Ten thousand generations of them grazing the green-gold film in lawnmower lines, and every line they leave is lacquer — mucus dried to varnish, sealing the plaster, replacing lime the drips carry away. The house loses substance and the snails lay it back down, and neither party knows it is party to a contract. They are slow the way geology is slow, and if you want to know how long this economy has run, don't read the lintel; read the lacquer. It is thick as an oyster's pearl-culture near every basin, which is to say: it is made of time, secreted by patience.

**The fairy shrimp** live the strangest lives here, and the shortest, and possibly the most important. They exist as cysts — dried specks in the tuning basins — until the big condensation events flood the basins, and then they hatch, mature, breed, and lay down next year's cysts in exactly the number of days the basins take to dry. Either the builders tuned the basins to the shrimp, or the shrimp tuned themselves to the basins, and the lintel refuses to say, and there is a whole quiet theology in that refusal. They are the house's proof that its music and its life are the same system: a creature whose entire biography is the length of a note in the wet season's score. When a basin rings, it is not just an instrument. It is a cradle.

**The swiftlets and the bats** in the dome's chambers are the producers — the top of everything, the green explosion's sponsors. Their guano falls on the one lit shelf, and the ferns and moss and liverwort of the hanging garden grow out of that gift, and the garden's runoff seeds the pool, and the pool feeds the film, and the film feeds the snails, and the grit in the grooves feeds the crabs, and on down to the eel. The swiftlets click and squeak until the resonant vault doubles them into something like weather; they are the closest thing this place has to a choir, and they don't know they're singing in a tuned instrument, and that is the right way to hold a post in this house. The bats say nothing, and are therefore possibly the clergy.

**The birds of the threshold** are the visitors, each with a tenure. The **kingfisher** threads the oculus at odd hours and dives the light-tube — the only hunter that enters through the house's own eye, and the fish know its image as well as they know the heron's. The **heron** is punctual to the point of ritual: the top step, dawn, the fourth beat of the long measure, ten thousand mornings running, and the crab day-shift has handed off to the night-shift with the heron's arrival for all of those mornings. It is the metronome of the place. The **warblers** come for the fig's fruit and contribute nothing — their droppings seed nothing, because there is nothing here to seed — and the house tolerates them the way a finished work tolerates applause. The **frogs** in the rests are the ensemble's true musicians: they have listened to the drip-tempos so long that they fill exactly the unmeasured silence, and silence never quite occurs, and that is their doing more than anyone's.

The small ones complete the air. **Moths** orbit the nail-heads of light at dusk like planets with bad orbits. **Spiders** string harps across the light-shafts — the only weavers in a house that was woven in stone — and take their toll from the moth chorus.

And then there is the lawbreaker. The **fig**, rooted in a mortise it was never granted, its roots down where roots are forbidden by the second mark — *nothing unbuilt crosses the rim* — and the house looks the other way, and has always looked the other way, and the ochre on the seed-slash is refreshed each dry season by a hand that re-inks the law and does not enforce it against the one green thing in the court. Every finished system needs one exemption it refuses to explain. The fig is the house's mercy, or its loneliness. It is the only thing that grows without permission and the only thing whose image, swaying across the pool at midday, the fish do not flee — because it was never going to arrive. It taught them the difference between an omen and a memory.

And the keeper. Or the memory of a keeper. Chisel dust on a tread, fresh ochre, a swept stair, a gourd refilled and hung back on its thong. Nobody sees the work done; only the work's aftermath, arriving before the body, the way everything arrives here. The house keeps its own counsel about whether the hands belong to anyone living. Perhaps the crabs are the keeper, and the pebble-flags are prayers answered. Perhaps the house itself, having been finished, has simply continued — repair only, add nothing — under its own steam, the way a bell continues after the stroke. If you stand through a rehearsal you may notice that no drop is unassigned, no groove unrun, and you may decide the keeper is a person, or a practice, or just the accumulated habit of ten thousand mornings of everyone doing exactly what the water taught them. The lintel does not say. The lintel never says. It only says what the house does: water, falling, house — and underneath, small and deep, the law that all of them, eel and crab and snail and fig and fog-comb and unseen hand, keep without knowing they keep it: every drop is owed a path, and time falls, and you count it downward, and the runnels run clear.
