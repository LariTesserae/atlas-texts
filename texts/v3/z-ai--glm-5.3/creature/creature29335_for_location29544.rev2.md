# The Inhabitants of the Held Sea

Start with what is easiest to believe, because even the easy things here are strange: the place is full of animals, and none of them are afraid.

Fear, in the ordinary sense, is a response to events, and there are no events. Nothing has ever surprised anything on the mat. The predators here — and there are predators, pale flat things that hang beneath the weave and take the gutter-swimmers as they pass — do not ambush. They do not need to. The swimmers pass on the fixed current at the fixed hour, in the fixed number, and the hanging things take the fixed share, and this has been the arrangement for so long that both parties are shaped by it: the swimmers' breeding rate is calibrated to the predation the way a tap is calibrated to a drain. Nothing in the Held Sea has ever fled. The verb has atrophied out of their bodies. Watch a great grey mower as you approach — and you will approach one eventually, because the herds' routes cross everything — and it does not startle, does not pause, does not even turn its head, because nothing in its lineage has ever had reason to. It registers you the way the mat registers your footstep: a slight local depression in the day, absorbed, gone.

The mowers themselves deserve the first full description, because they are the closest thing the plateau has to citizens. Grey as the water, wide as a barn, low-slung on legs like pilings, their bodies armoured in a hide that feels like wet felt over stone. Their heads are maws, underslung, and they walk face-down into the mat and eat it — and this is the whole of their political life: they are the channel-keepers. A herd does not wander. It patrols. The nutrient canals silt gently — not with silt, exactly, but with the mat's own ambitions, the weave forever trying to close the straight runs the flows need — and the herds graze the closing edges open again, a thousand animals abreast, in a line that is less a herd than a maintenance pass. They know the route not by memory but by rhythm: the beat of the nearest stand governs their pace, and the pace governs the bite, and the bite is exactly the growth. A mower's jaw works on the four-second pulse. Everything on the plateau works on the four-second pulse. You feel it in your sternum for the first hour, then you stop noticing, then you find your own footsteps have fallen into it and cannot be pulled out.

Below the mowers, in the seams, the small life. The clicking crews in the stand-intakes — crisp little armoured animals, hand-length, in contingents of thousands, who live inside the valve-mouths of the towers and pick them clean on a schedule so constant that the stands themselves have grown to depend on it: a stand whose crew is experimentally removed (do not attempt this; the stands do not forgive it, and the crew does not survive the exposure) will begin to lose efficiency within days, its beat thinning, a note going flat in the continental chord. So there is a truth hidden in the crews: the machine that grew has not stopped growing. It is domesticating, or being domesticated by, its fauna, minute by minute, gram by gram. The line between the industry and the animals is not a line. It is a gradient, and you are standing on it.

The pilgrims are the ones that will make you quiet.

They cross the deep basins on routes older than the stands — or at least this is what the routes imply, since a trail across open water beneath the mat can only be a route because the mat has grown to hold it, grown along the chemical memory of ten thousand generations of passage. They are long animals, serpentine, translucent at the edges, luminous faintly, and they do not swim so much as pour. Their crossing takes days. They do not feed during it; they feed on arrival, at the drowned peaks, where the filter-growth on the old summits fattens them for the return leg. Their purpose is not a purpose you can name — they carry nothing, they pollinate nothing, they appear, from the outside, to be simply commuting in a world with no work and no home. But every basin has its pilgrim-roads, and every road has its traffic, and the traffic is punctual to the daily seiche: they enter the water on the falling breath and leave it on the rising, and you could mark the year by them if the year were marked by anything.

And here is where the description must turn, because it is the turn the place itself makes on you, slowly, over days.

Because you will start to notice — everyone does, though there is no everyone, and so *you* will start to notice — that the mowers' maintenance lines never vary by a meter and yet no mower could measure a meter. That the clicking crews work to the beat without any organ that could hear it. That the pilgrims keep a timetable across black open water with nothing to navigate by except the water itself, and that the chord of the stands — each tuned, each in fixed phase relation to its neighbours, the whole plateau a geometry of pitches laid out over thousands of kilometers — is *too regular* to be an accident of trunk density, the way a heartbeat is too regular to be an accident of muscle.

Something is reading the whole of it. Whether it is one thing or ten billion things — whether the stands are organs of a mind the size of a continent, or the fauna are its thoughts, one thought per animal, held forever — the place will not tell you. It has no interest in the question. It holds its note.

But there is a creature — you will not believe in it at first, and you will believe in it by the end — that seems to be the question's local representative. The travelers' lore would have a name for them, if there were travelers. You will not see one. You will see where they have been: at the fixed rain-columns, where a hammered lane of beaten mat runs into the bead-curtain and does not come out the other side; at the rim, walking the lip of the falls at the exact pace of the water, always upstream; and once, perhaps, far off in the mist, a shape your eye will refuse for the full second it takes the fog to close — tall, narrow, patient, greyer than grey, standing in the rain that never stops.

What they are is maintenance. What they are *for* is the question the mat cannot answer about itself. The stands can repair themselves; the crews can clean what they can reach. But a thing of this size, holding a world's water against gravity for geological ages, has failure modes nothing inside it can see from inside it — a bladder going soft in a far basin, a seam in the weave loosening at the rate of one thread per century — and something walks the whole of the plateau on rounds so long that a single circuit is indistinguishable from a species' lifetime. They do not build. They do not leave artifacts, which is why the world reads as artifact-free. They are grown, like everything, and their industry is not an industry either: it is repair without an owner, patrol without a warrant, performed for no reward that continues, in the world's own fashion, forever.

They are the only things here that look at you.

That is the whole of the terror and the whole of the tenderness of the Held Sea, and it is the last fact of the place, and it takes the longest to believe. Everything else on the mat is part of the machine, blind as a bearing, constant as a pressure. But when the grey shape in the fixed rain turns its attention on you — and it will, once, and only enough — you are looking into the face of the only thing in a thousand kilometers that has ever encountered an *event*.

You are the only news this world has had in an age. It will regard you the way a watchmaker regards a dropped screw: something out of place, in a world that has never had place out. It will not harm you. Harm is an event. It will simply account for you, complete its thought, and file you — and walk on into the rain that has always been raining there, keeping the note, keeping the water up, keeping the forever the length it has always been, which is no length at all.
