The loping is done by the hobs: long, low, joint-legged hunters that keep to the terraces' shadows at noon and work the herd-edges at the letting-go. A hob takes a flagback the way the Quiet takes a voice — close in, no cry, done. The flagbacks do not bellow, because bellowing is a message and a message is a shape, and the Shelf does not give up shapes lightly. Instead the herd does something the gleaners call *the agreement*: every animal in a mile radius lies flat at once, skin to stone, and for a quarter hour the herd and the land are indistinguishable. The hob hunts by warmth, and the agreement is the flagbacks' way of saying *we are not here, we are the Shelf,* and mostly the Shelf believes them. A flagback dies perhaps once a season, and the whole event is conducted at the volume of the inside of your hand.

The drowned mist deserves its own account, because it is the closest thing the Shelf has to weather with intentions. It comes up the Notch before dawn on some nights — never all of them — a gray flood that tops the rim and spreads across the benches like a slow spill, kneeling on the stone, tasting the runnels. It is not fog. Fog is water loose in the air; the drowned mist is water *going* somewhere, and where it goes is down, into the grain of the stone, and the Keeper says it is the Shelf drinking, the way a sleeping animal drinks, without waking. You can stand in it — the gleaners do, deliberately, on the nights the kites triple-flash the warning, because mist-walking is how the bellbush gets seeded to new basins and how the wicks find new slabs, and a person standing still in the drowned mist is, for those hours, infrastructure. The gleaners know this. They speak of it without embarrassment. Everything on the Shelf that stays still long enough gets *used* — the hermits discovered this, the three or four solitaries who keep to the far benches, and most of them have come to like it: coin lichen colonizing a hermit's boot-leather, stone-wool seeding in the seam of a coat, a wick leaving its bead of water, nightly, on the brim of a hat as if it were a slab. The Shelf does not distinguish, the gleaners say, between what lives here and what *is* here. Stay a decade and you are furniture; stay a lifetime and you are land.

And then there is the one person, because there is mostly one person, and it is worth being precise about what the Keeper actually keeps.

She keeps the ledger — dewfalls, vane-leanings, basin-rings, in a hand that was taught by another hand, and that hand by another, the pages going back past the point where the ink changes and past the point where the marks become scratches and past the point where the scratches might be talon or tooth. She keeps the mother bellbush, which is not tending, the gleaners insist, any more than you tend your grandmother; you sit with her. She keeps the trade — water for news, news for water, and news on the Shelf travels at the speed of gleaners and kites, which is to say it arrives already worn smooth. She keeps, though she would not name it this, the Quiet itself, in this sense: the Cistern House is the one place where a sound is permitted to *go* somewhere. The basins are tuned. The main cistern's rim, tapped, sends its note down through a hundred feet of stacked slab and the whole western terrace answers underfoot, a single held hum, and every animal in a mile arranges itself around that hum — flagbacks drifting basin-ward, hobs going still, the kites' first dusk-spiral beginning — because the tap is the Shelf's one word for *evening*, and the land, which spends all day not speaking, spends it gladly.

What she does not keep is the vanes. Nobody keeps the vanes. The gleaners' oldest story says the Keeper's ledger and the vane field are the same instrument played at two speeds — the vanes deciding, the ledger remembering — but the story does not say who plays it, and on the Shelf this is the correct amount of answer. The vanes were not built by anyone the ledger remembers. They were built by whoever wore three hundred steps glassy, whoever rounded the edges, whoever finished everything so long ago that the finished thing had time to become land.

And that, finally, is who lives here: a woman in a doorway under a bellbush older than her roof; a few hermits going green at the cuffs; herds that are mostly lying down; hunters that are mostly not hunting; a sky of silent mirrored bells; carriers, mats, stalks, cups — a whole economy of the quiet, drinking at dusk, flashing at letting-go, and a plateau beneath them all that eats every sound, holds every shadow a quarter hour past its sun, and raises what it likes.
