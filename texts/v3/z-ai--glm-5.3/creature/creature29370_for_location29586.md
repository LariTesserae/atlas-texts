# The Tenants of the Works

## The ones at the boiling edge

Begin at the center, because the center is where the first residents live, and they are the smallest and the oldest and the most loyal.

At the scalding heads of the springs, where the water arrives too hot for blood, too hot for anything with a face, there is life. It is bacterial, it is filamentous, it is the color of a bruise or a sunset — blue-green at the impossible center, shading to pink, to orange, to the deep iron rust of the spreading mats. These organisms do not endure the heat. They require it. Cool them and they die the way a lowland plant dies in frost. They are the only residents for whom the mountain's exhaust is not a hazard but dinner: they eat the sulfur, the iron, the dissolved chemistry the deep engine sends up, and they turn the vent's poison into the first floor of everything above them. They are the intake workers. They have held their positions since before the forest existed, before the frogs, before there was anything on the plateau to stand on, and they will hold them after.

They have no idea they work. That is important. No organism here knows it is employed. The mats lay down their banded sediment season by season — a hand's width a century, the whole marsh slowly turning to stone under them — and this is not labor to them. It is simply what a day is. They divide. The water is warm. The day is the same day.

## The ring-dwellers

Around every spring, life arranges itself the way rings arrange themselves around a dropped coal, and each ring has its tenancy.

Just outside the bacterial films, where the water has cooled from scalding to merely hot, comes the algal felt — dark green, velvety, dense enough to walk on if you had to and didn't mind. Then the sedges, roots sitting in blood-warm mud year-round, growing with the confidence of plants that have never once been checked by frost. Then the moss, deep, soaking, the sphagnum-like carpet that holds water the way a held breath holds air. Then the shrubs, then the trees, and then you are back in the forest, and the spring is behind you, wearing its temperature like a target, like an eye, with its whole community sorted around it in rings no surveyor could draw more precisely.

Every resident of the plateau lives at an address defined by heat. Not by elevation, not by slope, not by the compass — by temperature. The frog sitting in the shallows of one pool would find the pool forty meters closer to the vent uninhabitable and the pool forty meters farther merely brisk. The frogs, of course, do not think in these terms. They think, to the extent frogs think, in warmth and in calling-distance, and the plateau provides both in such abundance that they have become the plateau's great success — small, wet-skinned, everywhere, each one positioned on its own thermal margin with the precision of an instrument it will never read.

## The chorus below hearing

If the mountain has a voice, it is the frogs, and it is almost below hearing.

It is not a song you hear so much as a texture you stand inside of — a soft, continuous, chorusing hum that rises from every warm pool on the plateau at once, thousands of voices at the threshold of perception, so that the air itself seems to vibrate very slightly, like a struck surface long after the strike. A visitor stops hearing it within an hour. A visitor who has lived near it for a week finds that its absence, down in the cold lowlands, is the thing that is loud.

They call because there is no season that forbids it. There is no brief spring window, no night cold enough to close. Every day is the same day, every pool is blood-warm, and so the chorus never starts and never stops — it simply is, one of the standing conditions of the place, like the steam or the light.

## The ones in the strata

Between the frog-thrum and the vent-sighs, the air is worked.

The fog here lies in decks — a low cloud at the ankles, a clear band at the waist, another deck overhead — and the insects have discovered this architecture and colonized it. Damselflies hold their territories in the clear band, flying at waist-height in a world with a ceiling and a floor, their flight so exactly horizontal it seems rule-governed. Moths work the upper deck, appearing and vanishing at its lower surface like fish rising to a waterline. Everything flies in strata. Everything is wet. The wings are wet. The rules of the lowland air — dry air, wide sky, weather that changes — do not apply, and the insects here have quietly become a fauna of the in-between: citizens of the waist-height light, the one lit corridor in a fog-bound world.

And through all of it, everywhere, cutting the only sharp sound on the plateau: the hummingbirds. They hold territories around the flowering shrubs with a ferocity completely out of scale to their bodies — a few grams of bird defending a bush as though it were a province — and their wing-whir is one of the few sounds that penetrates the hush, a small hard whine of urgency in a place that otherwise contains none. They are the exception that measures the rule. Everything else on the plateau is unhurried because nothing here has a winter to prepare against. The hummingbirds alone behave as if time were scarce. Perhaps, for something that burns energy at their rate, it always is, even here — even on a mountain where the engine never stops and the flowers never rest.

## The patient ones

Predation on the plateau is conducted at the pace of the plateau.

There is a long-legged hunting bird that works the marsh margins — stands, and stands, and stands, in the warm shallows at the thermal boundaries where the invertebrates thrive, and then moves with a speed that seems almost rude in context. The wading birds fish the warm water year-round; they do not migrate because there is nothing to migrate from; the lean month does not exist here. The snakes bask near the springs and never really have to stop — no cold night, no dormancy, no autumn signal, just the long continuous being of an animal that has found a place where its own physiology has been permanently excused. A snake on this mountain may live a long time and never once be cold. There is something almost enviable in it, and something eerie: an animal designed around a season that never comes, living instead in an eternal operational present.

In the moss floor, under the cushion-crowns, the small mammals run their tunnels — everywhere, at every hand-depth, the warm dark under the carpet fully leased. And even the dead zones, the bleached and fizzing waste ground around the harshest vents where nothing grows, are not empty. They are tracked and visited — for minerals, for warmth, for whatever the animals find there — the slag heaps of the works used as they would be used in any industrial country: as the edges, as the margins, as the places the residents go to be alone.

## The tenancy

Understand, finally, what all of these residents have in common, which is the terms of their lease.

None of them built anything. The works was running before them — the intake, the processing, the exhaust, the settling ponds, the whole kilometers-scaled plant of stone and steam — and they moved in the way life moves into any warmth: immediately, completely, several organisms deep. Every trunk carries moss, bromeliads, orchids, ferns, stacked wet loads, tenants on tenants. Every fallen log is consumed by succession in real time — a tree falls, and within a month its upper surface is meadow. The forest is less like plants growing than like materials accumulating on a substrate, and the substrate is the roof of the engine.

They live, all of them, on the insulated skin of a giant machine that runs perfectly beneath their feet. They are warm because of the exhaust. They drink because of the intake. Their world is sorted by the processing, ring by ring around every vent. And none of them knows, and none of them owes, and none of them is waiting for the product.

The mountain works for no one. This is the lowlanders' reverence, and it is true — but it is also true that everyone lives here. The frogs in their blood-warm pools, the bacteria at the boiling heads, the hummingbirds in their shrub-provinces, the snakes that have forgotten what cold means, the ten-thousand-year unbroken shift of the moss — all of them residents of a factory that produces stone and steam and abundance without purpose, without audience, without an owner, and all of them warm, and all of them wet, and none of them grateful, and none of them charged rent.

The place is not a monument to anything. Neither are they. They are a process that found its pace and holds it — the engine below, the skin above, the tenants on the skin, exhaling and calling and standing patient in the warm shallows, every day the same day, for longer than any human industry has existed, and on into the cold high air beyond.
