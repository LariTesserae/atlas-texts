What lives in the Ballroom is fifty-three people, and they are the least numerous thing in it.

They are a wet people. That is the first thing to understand about them. They live in air that never dries, and so they have stopped drying themselves — sweat stands on their skin the way condensation stands on their walls, and they no longer notice it, the way you no longer notice your own pulse. Their hair goes soft and dark in the damp. Their lips stay full. Newcomers glisten; the born-here shine. An old Ballroom hand can be told at a glance by the easy way they wear water, by the way they flinch at nothing when a drip spits off the high back and lands on their neck, because they heard the tick a half-second before and simply didn't bother to look up. Looking up is a newcomer's reflex. It fades.

They speak in the gaps between pump strokes. This is the strangest thing about them and the most invisible to themselves: their sentences are timed, unconsciously, to the two-second beat of the mares, Dun and Roan and Gray, so that Ballroom speech has a rhythm like rowing. They are quiet people, not from temperament but from economy — sound carries in the chamber, and everyone can hear everything, so most things go unsaid. The census changes on the bathhouse wall without comment. A birth is a chalked number; a death is a niche closed with an oak door, high and dry in the rib, and everyone has watched the shelves grow the way they've watched the stalactite grow — a thumbnail a year, in the only time this place keeps.

They are veiled people, like beekeepers at a wedding. Indoors, always. The women, the bread, the machine intakes — everything that must not take on wings wears gauze. They eat with lids on their pots and drink with their eyes below the glass rim, and they do this so automatically that they do it alone, at dim-time, with nobody watching, which is the only proof of a culture you need: rules kept in the dark. They breathe shallow. Not from fear; from habit. Deep breaths draw midges, and the habit is older in the children than the reason for it.

And the children — there are children, chalked on the wall like everyone else. They know the indoor rain the way surface children know seasons. Every eighth hour the mains blow down and the warm mineral water falls for six minutes out of the ceiling of the world, and the children stand out in it, faces up, because it is the only sky they have. They are the ones who graze the mint pot by the lamp, Ballroom's entire agriculture, a fistful of green in an amber globe's halo. When you describe the sun to them they file it with folklore — a lamp that moves, that dries things, that no one weeds. They are polite about it.

The work sorts the people, and the work is all one work. There are the screen-women, who sweep the fan-housings twice a shift and come away with forty pounds of shed wings a day, every day, forever, and who have a pride in it that outsiders find eerie until they understand: the Ox breathes through those screens, and the Ox chokes on wings, and so this is not janitorial. It is keeping the town alive at the level of its lungs. There are the stripping crew on the north rib, taking the room a handspan a month off the face of the world, the slow human widening of everything. There is the lampwright, who cleans globes that frost with wing-scale faster than grief and weeds the lampenflora like a gardener trimming halos off saints. There are pump-men who know the three mares as livestock — who will tell you Dun runs hot-tempered and Gray steady, and mean it. There is someone always near the brass bolts, reading the water in inches a day, in the same careful hand that keeps both futures in one ledger: the finished city and the drowned one, the stalactite and the second bolt, a finger's width higher on the plinth every year.

They keep no graves. They never dig past the clay skin — the ground is a lid, not a floor, and everyone knows what's under the lid, three hundred fathoms of hot water waiting for the town to make one mistake slowly. So Ballroom keeps everything above the ground: the dead in the niches, the houses on their plinths like wagons fording a stream, the streets on laid rock, the future itself strung at ankle height in surveyor's twine, marked out, untrodden, staked into the wet dark like a promise nobody can hurry.

And around them, above them, under them — the rest of what lives there. The midges standing over each lamp in a column like smoke that has decided to stay. The moths arriving down the Nostrils as weather, ticking on glass like sleet. The springtails moving like living ash across the drifts of shed wings, the crickets furring the ribs and grazing the warm pale strings at the vent mouths like cattle at pasture, the drain-flies breathing in the gutters, the bloodworms coloring the sumps rust-red in films dense enough to be paint. The bats, exhaling from the roof at dim-time in a funnel, going out to hunt the shaft-dark while the lamps come slowly down like a child lowered into bed. A generation of flies every week; a house every year. The people are the slow thing in the room. The people are the thing that measures.

That is who lives in the Ballroom: fifty-three veiled, wet, patient souls in the loud warm dark, keeping six rules and one ledger, reading water in inches, raising walls against a flood that powers the machines that fight it — almost nothing, in a room almost empty, and completely full.
