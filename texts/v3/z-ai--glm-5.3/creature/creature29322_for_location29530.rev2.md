What lives here is the least crowded population in any ocean you will ever visit, and the census takes some getting used to, because none of it is alive and all of it is busy.

The shelves are the residents you meet first. They live like enormous slow animals that happen to be made of floor — each one a presence, spaced a day's drift from its neighbors, steaming faintly at its warm new edges, ticking as it cools the way a large warm-blooded thing ticks with a heartbeat. A shelf has a life history the way an animal does: it is born hot at its growing lip, sweats into adulthood, ages into the cold quiet of stone, and some of them die — the greedy ones, the ones that grow too fast and founder into the resorption dark, where the water takes them apart with perfect patience. A shelf that dies well is not mourned. It is freight. It comes back up in the cold rivers and gets built into its own descendants. The sea does not waste its dead because the sea has not yet invented waste.

The groves live in the lee of the shelves, and they are the closest thing to a community: rows of white mineral wands, straight-planted, rigid, grazed by nothing, bent by nothing, waiting for tenants with the furnished patience of a nursery. They do not grow toward anything. They grow on schedule. Standing among them is the first place you feel the strangeness of this place as a social fact — the sense of being *scheduled around* — because a grove, more than anything else here, looks like someone's intention standing still.

The storms are the fauna of the sky. They are born in the morning over warm stone like hatchlings, spend their adolescence crossing open water, and by evening stand in ranks on the horizon like adults who have places to be. They are couriers — the only things in this world that travel with purpose — each one arriving warmer than its doorstep by half a degree, each one a delivery, each one greeted by the shelves with a measurable spurt of growth. The terraces keep diaries of them. Every shelf carries the autobiography of every storm that ever fed it, written in ridges.

The snow is the smallest resident and the most numerous: the white grains falling in their too-even veils, forty meters down in standing columns of light, sorted and spaced by something that is either turbulence with unaccountable good taste or a hand no one has ever seen. The snow is not weather. It is workforce. It lands where the work is and becomes the work.

And underneath everything, unseen, lives the largest and most reticent inhabitant: the plan. It lives in the water and nowhere else, in salinity gradients tuned like wiring, in currents steered by chemistry, in a saturation that never slips from trench to gyre. It has no body, no machines, no structures — the technology here is indistinguishable from oceanography, which may be the definition of it. It is present everywhere and observable only as coherence. When you stand on a newborn shelf at dusk and feel the place noticing the rain half a degree early, you are meeting it the only way it can be met: as the fact that nothing here is wasted, and nothing here is accidental, and the emptiness is not absence of life but *staging*.

Because the final census is this: the sea itself lives here, singular, one enormous verb — building, redrafting, keeping its own accounts with destruction, keeping the deep hungry and the shallows fed. Nothing in this ocean has learned to eat, and so nothing in it is prey, and the vacancy stacked on vacancy is not poverty. It is a house framed and plumbed and weather-sealed, fields fertilized, reefs built and waiting, everything ready, everything ticking, the whole ocean a bell rung by no one yet —

— the tenants are a later chapter, and this is the chapter where the world is written, at the speed of weather, and the silence between sentences is not silence. It is the next line being mixed.
