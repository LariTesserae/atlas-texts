Nobody on the Table owns anything, so start there, because that is the admission requirement everyone signed. The land finished ages ago; the water hasn't; and whatever lives between the two lives on the water's clock. Two lines rule everything. The level is decided elsewhere, at night, by load and by rain. Residence requires amphibiousness in time — able to drown twice a week and dry out once a season and call that a niche. There are almost no natives here in the way a wood is native to its hillside. The char come close. The bulbs come close in their way. The people are natives of a place that no longer has a surface: their parish is ten meters down and they never left it, they only moved uphill.

So: a census, taken from the bottom up, and strange all the way through.

⁂

Most of the parish lives below the waterline now, and it took the move better than anyone predicted.

The char have seniority over everyone, including the church. They were in these waters before the last ice finished tidying up, fish from before the flood in every sense, built the way a warden is built: for cold and waiting. To a char, the flood was an expansion of the family business — more dark, more depth, more law — and they hang in the old beck's holes below twenty meters in cold that is fact rather than weather, the last tenants who never noticed anything had changed.

The perch inherited the roads and kept them. The road sits on the Pan and never softened, the kerbstones still line it politely, and the shoals string out along the drowned lanes fence after fence, commuting to nowhere by streets nobody deeded them. The fences have gone to gray staves wearing algae beards, with mussels spaced along the wires like buttons — the lake has tailored the parish into something like a coat — and inside the staves the old nests, still woven, have been let to new tenants: sticklebacks and minnows raise their families now in hedge-sparrow workmanship, and no one has complained. The pike took the crossroads. What a crossroads meant to the living is the village's business; what it means now is ambush — the meadow's memory lying in the meadow's green, and the toll is payable in perch.

The caddis larvae are the parish's smallest restorers. They rebuild Caldbeck one case at a time and carry it with them: fence splinter, straw, and — vouched for by the divers, sworn to at the inn — a shred of the rose's wire-hanger stem from the south wall. A rose planted by someone for a door that no longer has a house is now housing insects. The village is being reassembled at insect scale, from its own materials, by tenants who pay no rent and were never asked to.

The stoneworts are masons. They crust the Pan with pale marl — the farm being re-floored in white stone by algae with nowhere else to be — and on bright afternoons they fizz streams of bubbles, the lake breathing out. Whatever else this water is, it is oxygenated by its own ruins. And beneath it all the kept things hold the parish's place: the tractor at the field gate, rims white with marl, still parked, still expecting someone. But the kept are a category of their own, and we will come to them, because they are the Table's real aristocracy.

⁂

At the Old Fields the reed line stands thick as any hedge and is argued over all dawn by warblers holding ground that belongs to nobody. Behind it, between the High Line and the Low Line, lies the Strand — land that is sometimes land — and everyone there is in the same business: living fast between immersions.

The oldest congregation on the Table sleeps decades deep in the mud: a seed bank laid down in the pasture years, kept cold, kept honest, detonating on every exposure. Toad rush first, then cress, then willowherb shouldering up; in wet summers the marsh orchids, like a rumor confirmed. The gardener here is disturbance, and the gardener never rests. In July the Strand grows redshank and redshank walk it — a bird probing a plant that shares its name, both named for the same shade of leg, neither aware of the coincidence, both on time, on a schedule nobody drew up, which is the local way of saying it keeps itself.

The frogs and newts gamble against a clock set, unknowingly, by a machine at the bottom of a cliff, in a valley they will never see. Some years the water comes back early and the year's brood is closed out at a loss; the frogs' counter is arithmetic — lay more, lay often — and it is the only strategy the drawdown respects.

The otters use the Landing's slipway at night and leave fish bones on the same step, considerate tenants with better manners than most of the shore. The ferryman sweeps the step each morning and curses them fondly, which is the local form of greeting, and the only exchange either party requires. The deer swim between the hards, chins up, unhurried, having filed the lake as an administrative error in the meadow's paperwork; in October the rut happens on the islands while the geese happen overhead, and for two weeks the loudest place on the Table is the sky.

And above the drowned village, every spring, the daffodils. The gardens have been dead eighty years and the bulbs have kept the calendar: three weeks of yellow between the lines, on a shoreline drawn by electricity demand, blooming for whoever shows up. Somebody usually shows up — people from the Landing in oilskins, standing in it, not saying much, because there is nothing to say that the bulbs are not already saying, more punctually.

⁂

Everything the lake cannot keep, it exports. The drowned farm's fertility rises through the mud into midge larvae and out into the air, and in the air the census gets crowded.

In the Smut, columns of midges stand over the water like smoke that has decided to stay — a soft static with a wire of pitch strung through it — and swallows and swifts spend the day stitching them out of it, feeding Caldbeck to their broods in the net sheds a few meters above where it drowned. The net sheds, which smell of tar and keep swallows better than nets, are the busiest address on the eastern shore. In September the swifts leave over the Edge and do not stop until the sea.

The cormorants dry their wings on the Steeple like Monday laundry: a congregation of one species in a church tower kept above the waterline as a daymark, under a warden's lamp. Caldbeck's tower still holds a congregation. It is simply not the one it was built for, and a tower, being a tower, does not mind. The goldeneye have taken the inspection ports of the Sisters — cavity nesters in the two most desirable addresses on the lake, aviation-orange porch lamps blinking whether or not anyone is watching, aerators churning white circles below like kept lawns. They are doing well there. You can tell because they say nothing about it. The ospreys hang over the milfoil where the pike lie in the drowned grass, wardens of the meadow's memory, checking its papers.

At dawn the duck rafts can cover a bay — scaup, wigeon, teal, goldeneye — so many that the water looks oiled with them, and when the peregrine goes through, the oil becomes wings. The peregrine keeps the gatehouse at the Cut, the only address on the Table with a view of everything, and audits the bay daily, free of charge. In October the geese pass horizon to horizon for two weeks — the Table is a word in a language spoken by nations that have no borders, and for a fortnight the roof of the world is their forecourt — and then they are gone, punctual as a tide, owing nothing.

⁂

Now the people, who are fewer than the birds and easier to overlook, and who all hold the same post under different names.

The wardens keep the lake's office hours: gauges at dawn before the wind, gates after storms, the lights, the aerators, the log. A warden's first act of any day is to find out where the shore went — you can wake to a different coastline here — and the profession itself is maintenance and waiting for weather. There is a walk out the causeway to the far Sister, handrail green always, warm perhaps twice a year, and the warden who makes it files both facts without comment. The logbooks read like weather kept as fact: *Gates quarter. Redshank on the Strand. Hopper 31, two sea-liced.* Eighty years of mornings, filed.

Twice a day in season the Edge is paid its fish by hand. Down at the Bottom the trap warden reads the count over the crackly radio; the winch takes up the slack; the Hopper climbs fourteen hundred meters with its tank sloshing and the fish thumping softly against the sides, a heart with somewhere to be. At the top a warden drops the door into the Top Water and the salmon pour out under the towers' lights — sea-run fish delivered to a lake two kilometers in the sky, on a schedule, by a machine older than anyone who operates it. It is the closest thing this place has to a liturgy, and like all liturgies it mostly runs without witnesses, which is its point. How the young get down is the parish's open question: the Bottom holds that they go through the works and mostly make it, the Landing holds that nothing mostly makes it, and the fish, polled by neither, come back in numbers the arithmetic never quite closes. The Hopper is the standing apology, and it will run until the last gate rusts open and the whole arrangement walks off the Edge.

There is a diver — the scheme keeps one, for the screens at the Sisters and whatever the lake has decided to lean on this decade — and they are the only person who has stood in Caldbeck's rooms since the water took them. They ride up in the Hopper with the salmon when the work is at the top, the lake's only fellow commuter. On quiet nights at the inn they will tell you about the fireplaces fanning their soft gray deltas across the floors, the garden edging still straight, the doorways dark as ever. They touch nothing. The lake keeps, and the diver's whole profession is helping it keep. The museum has no shop.

The ferryman runs the weekly rounds — stores, diesel, mail, the lamp on the Steeple, and once a year the churchyard island with a boatload of parish and a thermos — and navigates by the drowned map more than the live one. *We cross the road here*, meaning a road eight meters down. His grandmother was born in Caldbeck; her house is a Tuesday afternoon now, a particular light on open water, and he crosses it weekly without ceremony, or with the smallest ceremony there is, which is to say on schedule.

At the Landing the inn keeps Bottom time on its bar clock, so the whole shore drinks by the machine's day, and keeps the old dales on the wall in dotted lines, so the whole shore drinks above its own address. The regulars: wardens off shift, Bottom crews up on rotation with oil in the creases of their hands, the diver on nights the Hopper ran late, ringers from Kirk Hard. The conversation is the gauge, the geese, the level, the level, the gauge. The inn's dog has stopped trusting its ears — fooled once too often by the Spill's thunder behind — and sleeps through storms, having decided all loud weather is the lake's business. Directions are given by landmarks under ten meters of water — turn at the school, keep the crossroads on your left — and the children understand, because the children learn the lines before they learn long division, and the drowned parish about the same time as the live one. No child of the Landing goes near the water before they can say what four minutes means.

The Bottom itself lives off the fall, machining the Table's future and hauling it home up the rope. It sees the Table as weather's address and the rope's far end; the Table sees the Bottom as a smear of light under the cloud deck and, on still days, a hum felt through rock. Between them runs one steel rope and the parish's entire vertical economy: down goes the water; up goes everything the water owes — parts, fish, the diver, the dead. Up on Kirk Hard the new church carries the old bells, rung for the same parishes, only higher up, by ringers who include at least one warden. The register never closed; it moved. Christenings are entered at the lake, burials above the High Line, because nothing on the Table is ever buried: the dead go uphill, by hand, the way everything here travels.

⁂

The graves came up before the water, ahead of the parish, the way wardens move ahead of a flood, and most of them wait on the higher ground. But one hard stayed above the line with its churchyard on it — graves too settled to move, or families who would not hear of it — heathered now, islanded, visited once a year by the ferry, the shears, and whoever the parish sends. These are the only residents of the Table with anything like a permanent address, and even they moved once, or famously declined to.

Below, the lake keeps the rest: the tractor at the field gate, still parked by someone who meant to come back; the milk churn; the bicycle with its bell; the rose stem's second career in caddis cases. A wall keeps its word even underwater, the garden edging is still straight, the kerbstones are still polite, and whether anyone has ever rung the bicycle bell is a matter of theology at the inn — two schools, no evidence — which is how you know the parish is still alive. The kept things are not inhabitants exactly. They are the household gods: the ones who stay behind when everyone locks up, holding the parish's place at the bottom of the light.

⁂

Add the census up and it comes to this: nobody here owns anything, and everybody is holding something for somebody. The Pan holds the floor and returns nothing. The lake holds the village without comment. The seed bank holds eighty years of pasture in trust against a week of sun. The bulbs hold the calendar. The bells hold the parishes. The ferryman holds his grandmother's house, crossing it Tuesdays. The warden — the word turns out to be the only job title the place has — holds the gauge, the gates, the fish, the lamp; and every other species holds the post under some other name. The osprey wards the milfoil. The cormorant wards the tower. The pike ward the crossroads, the stonewort wards the Pan, the char ward the cold, the daffodils ward the gardens, the midges hold the whole shore's food in the mud and pay it out as weather. This is not an ecosystem with people in it. It is a wardenry, run on rain, staffed by whatever could hold an appointment.

In the last light the rain's wall comes on with that hiss like the sea entering a cave, and the shore closes for the day. Indoors the level is argued; outdoors the level decides. The bar clock clicks over to Bottom time. Out on the water the Hopper makes its last run, the door drops, and the salmon go up into the sky, delivered. The land finished ages ago. The water hasn't. And everyone alive up here — char, caddis, ferryman, warden, bulbs — is the arrangement's staff, paid in weather, holding the door between a finished sentence and an unfinished flood, and both parties, as far as anyone can tell, are satisfied with the help.
