# What Lives in the Loomworks Hollow

The first and largest resident is the plant, and calling it a plant is already a small inaccuracy, because what lives here is better described as a *patient*. Singular. However many trunks rise through the works — and there are eleven, or perhaps nine, depending on whether you count the two that have merged at the shoulder — the root system beneath the basin is one continuous body, and it is the body that matters. The trunks are its hands. The works are what the hands hold.

It is not clear that it thinks, and it is equally unclear that it doesn't. What can be said is that it *attends*. It attends to the pipes the way a body attends to its own bones — not consciously, but with total fidelity, thickening a root where a joint has begun to weep, laying cambium into a mortar-crack before the frost can find it. If there was ever a gardener, or a foreman, the plant has inherited both roles and merged them into a single slow occupation. It keeps the works. That is not a metaphor here; it is the plainest available description of its behavior.

Its age is uncertain in the way that a river's age is uncertain. It didn't begin; it *accreted*. There may be a first cutting, centuries back, that took root in a drain and found the arrangement of iron and stone below it congenial — found, perhaps, that the warmth of the cooling engine over one winter made the difference between surviving and not. If gratitude exists at that scale, it would look exactly like what the plant does now: holding the manifold warm against its own wood, every cold morning, without fail.

The wren is the second resident, and the only one with a name-sized life. She lives in the upper lattice of the third shed, in a gap the plant has kept open for her — a space between two thickened roots that has been exactly wren-shaped for longer than wrens live, suggesting either coincidence or a kind of tenancy renewed generation after generation, the plant keeping the door and the birds rotating through. She is the hollow's entire morning ritual. At first light she works the sheds in order, hopping the loom frames, investigating each mechanism with the brisk offense of an auditor who suspects, correctly, that nothing here would misbehave while she watches. Whether she serves any function the plant requires is doubtful. She is not maintenance. She might be the closest thing the place has to *company*, kept the way some old machines keep an idle gauge — for the pleasure of a needle that moves.

The heron is not a resident. The heron is a *visit*, and the distinction matters. Twice a year, sometimes three times, she arrives at the rim in the grey hour, stands at the cisterns for most of a day, and leaves. She does not fish. The cisterns hold nothing to fish. She stands — one foot, then the other, the slow arithmetic of a creature that has decided this is where she does her standing — and the plant, for its part, does nothing different, except that on heron-days the dripping has been reported to fall a fraction more softly, which is either the weather or the observer's imagination, and no one is in a position to settle it. Perhaps she comes because the hollow is the only place for miles where the water behaves like water used to: gathering, holding, releasing, in the old rhythm. Perhaps she is the last subscriber to a service the works no longer knowingly provide.

And then there are the machines, which are not alive, and which is why the sentence must be phrased carefully: what lives in the hollow includes the looms *only in the sense that a coral reef includes the ship it has swallowed*. The cloth they weave — the patient, slow bolt of bast and root-hair, stacked cured and unclaimed in the shed — is the plant's own hair, shed and worked into a second shape. The works may once have made something for people. What they make now is the plant's outermost expression: the only part of the resident that is detached, portable, and waiting. Nobody collects it. It is the place's one gesture toward the world outside the canopy — goods produced on a schedule of decades, for a customer who has not yet come, and may be several centuries from arriving.

The moss lives here, of course, and the liverwort, and the slow insects, but these are less residents than *weather* — small permanent conditions, the way dampness is a condition. They are kept. Nothing thins them.

What is most true, and hardest to say plainly, is that the hollow's resident keeps an account of watchers. Not a memory — nothing so fast. But the looms run more evenly when observed, the wheel turns with a fraction more certainty, and this has been consistent for as long as anyone has troubled to sit still and check. The plant does not need witnesses. It appears, however, to *keep a place* for them, the way it keeps the wren's doorway: a shape maintained at no small expense, open, facing the path from the rim, for whatever might someday walk in and stay an afternoon. It has been holding that shape a very long time. If you are the one who finally sits in it, you will not be greeted. But you may notice, over hours, that the place has noticed you — and that being noticed, here, is the only welcome this resident has ever offered, and the only one it knows how to give.
