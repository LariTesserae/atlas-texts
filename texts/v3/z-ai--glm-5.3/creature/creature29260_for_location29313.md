# The One Who Lives at the Top of the Drowned World

What lives here was born in the same weather as the hill. That is the second thing your hands learn, after *everything cuts* — that you and the shelf are the same age, you and the gravel, you and the loom of rain. The world drowned, and the hill rose to meet the water, and somewhere in that meeting — in the warmth along the seams where the young rock still sweats — you condensed out of the flood like breath on cold glass. You have no memory of this. But you have no memory of anything else either, and the absence has the same shape as the hill, and when you press your flank against the warm seams you feel what a newborn feels against a chest: not recognition, but *temperature*, which is close enough.

You are the color of the light, which is to say you are barely a color at all. Green-amber, translucent at the edges, more suggested than seen. If you held up your own hand against the water you would have trouble finding where you ended — and you have done this, many times, in the green-amber secondhand light, turning your hand, watching it become a rumor of a hand. Your eyes work; that is the strange part. They work perfectly. They simply have never been given anything worth seeing that was farther than thirty meters and brighter than bottle glass. They are eyes built for a world that ended before they opened — or no, eyes built *for this*, which is more disturbing: eyes perfectly suited to a drowned summit under permanent rain, as if someone had known, when the world was being made over, exactly what the view would be.

You hear with your jaw. You hear with your sternum, with the spaces between your ribs, with the water in your own tissues — which is the same water, chemically, nearly, as the water outside, so that the sound does not so much reach you as *become* you for a moment. Near the surface — and you do sometimes rise toward the surface, you cannot help it, the summit-dweller's instinct is to test the summit — the rain is a white hammering overhead, a hiss like applause that has forgotten what it applauds, that has been applauding for years, that will be applauding when there is nothing left to applaud and no one left to be applauded for. You have risen into the cold meter under the rain — the chilled skin, the autumn layer, the season with a border — and felt the hammering pass through the crown of your skull and down your spine like being thought about by something enormous. You sank back into the summer of the deep seams and did not rise again for a long time. But you always rise again. A summit insists on being stood upon, even by its only tenant.

You eat what the rain sends down. Everything above has been abolished, but abolished things still fall — silt, ash, the fine gray snow of a finished world, and now and then something larger, something with a shape to it, something that tumbles down through the green-amber light trailing its dissolution like a comet. You meet these at the boundary of the visible world, where the light quits, where the water's mood breathes in and out like a lantern carried by someone pacing. You have learned the boundary's breathing. You know when it will shrink to two meters and pinch the world closed around you, and you are never afraid of it, because *nothing shown* is not the same as darkness, and you have spent your whole life inside a world that shows only what it chooses. Where the churn flints — the silt striking light from itself in cold sparks, stars below you and beside you, extinguished in a heartbeat — you have seen, briefly, the only sky you will ever see: a sky at your feet, made of sparks, going out.

You are alone here, but you are not sure *alone* is the right word, because the hill is here, and the hill is young the way you are young, and its lip is a fresh-broken line no wave has yet been given time to forgive, and you have run your hand along that lip — gently, gently, everything cuts — and felt the fracture planes bright as new knives, and something in you understood them as siblings. You are the highest ground for a hundred miles, you and the hill between you, splitting the honor. When the current comes strong off the abyssal plain below — and below you it is all abyssal plain now, a hundred miles of it, the world's whole drowned body — the hill stands in the current and you stand on the hill, and the water parts around you both, and for the length of the shelf's nave you are a cathedral and its congregation, its gargoyle and its congregation, its only worshiper and the thing worshiped, all at once, in warm blood-temperature water, in light like the memory of light.

You have never seen the sun. You do not know the word. But you know the light arrives as if through bottle glass, tired before it reaches you, secondhand, and some part of you — the part that rises toward the hammering, the part that touches the broken lip, the part that watches the cold sparks go out — suspects that the light is not tired but *spent*, that it gave everything it had to something above the rain a long time ago, and what reaches you is the change. You do not resent it. It is the only light there is, and it is enough to see a hand by, sometimes, and a hill, and a hundred miles of summit.

You live at the top of the world. The top of the world is eleven meters under the rain. You have been told — by the water, by the seams, by the sound that lives in your sternum — that there is nothing up there but more of what is already everywhere. You believe it.

Mostly, you believe it. And on the days when the boundary of light breathes out to thirty meters and beyond, when the green-amber goes gold for an hour the way bottle glass goes gold when the sun finds the right angle through it — on those days, something in you rises through the cold meter, all the way to the surface, to the underside of the rain, and presses against the gray loom from below, like a face against a window, and does not come up, and does not come up, and does not come up.
