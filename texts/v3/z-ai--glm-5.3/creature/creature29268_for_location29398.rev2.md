# What Lives in the Greenheart Cavity

The first and honest answer is: the plants live here, and the plants are not really plural. Walk the corridors they have made, push through the curtained roots of the ceiling rooms, and you begin to notice that nothing here is separate. The turf underfoot, the woody columns rising like organs, the hanging mats — they are rhizome-linked, arm-thick runner to arm-thick runner, one body filling a valley-sized volume the way water fills a bowl. There is one inhabitant of the Greenheart Cavity, and it is green, and it is everywhere, and it is glowing.

It does not think, probably. But it has preferences, and this is the closest thing to a mind in the place. It grows around the stone dome rather than over it. It crops its own lawn. It has opinions about its own boundaries and maintains them without visible effort. A guest in the chamber is being watched by something, in the loose way a greenhouse is watched by its gardener — the attention is in the architecture itself, in the corridors left conveniently open, in the walkable floor. Whether that is hospitality or accident is a question the cavity declines to answer.

## The Grazers

Then there are the pale things, and the cave is stingy with them.

They exist at the threshold of confirmation. Someone — a surveyor, a wanderer, whoever first descended through the cracked hills — reports a movement in the deep mat, a pale crescent of body, a suggestion of something like a large slug or a small, spineless, eyeless lamb, browsing the luminous turf with a slow, methodical sweep. Then it is gone, folded down into the meters-deep substrate, and the lawn closes over it like water.

They are almost certainly the keepers of the perfect lawn. The turf is cropped to an even height across the whole chamber, millions of square meters of it, without a patch missed — a fact that implies either a great many grazers, rarely seen, or a few who are very thorough and very patient. Perhaps there are ten. Perhaps there are ten thousand, moving so slowly and so deep that the surface never betrays them. The cavity's deep stasis suggests they do not breed and do not die in any way we would recognize; they may be as finished as everything else, a fixed population, cropping, cropping, cropping, part of the machinery of equilibrium like the condensation and the heat.

If you found one, it would be warm to the touch — everything here is warm to the touch — and pale as an underground thing, and it would not flee quickly. It might not flee at all. Nothing here has ever needed to.

## The Spores

The drifting motes are the cavity's only motion in the air, and they are nearly a rumor of motion. They ride currents too weak to feel, glowing faintly like the rest of the green body — tiny detached fragments of the whole, hanging in the bright air. They do not seem to be going anywhere. They do not seem to germinate. In a place where nothing grows visibly, the spores may be the plant's one concession to the idea of a future: a held breath, a seed bank with no bank and no planting, dispersal for a dispersal that never comes. Or they may simply be shed light, the way a fire sheds sparks, and mean nothing at all.

## The Absent Ones

And then there are those who lived here and do not.

The stone dome at the chamber's center is the only thing in the entire cavity that was *made*. Fitted stones, rough work, half-swallowed now by the mat, warm, deferred-to by the green body all around it. Someone built it. Someone came down through whatever fissure once existed in the cracked hills above, into a chamber even then lit and hot and green, and fit stone to stone at the precise center of the space — a position of some effort to determine and reach — and then left, or stayed, or ended. The dome is too small to shelter comfortably and holds nothing. It may be a marker. It may be a grave. It may be an apology, or a thank-you, or a door for something that never came through.

The plant grows around it with what looks, unmistakably, like deference. And so the cavity has, buried in its silence, one relationship: between a green body that does not think and builders who are gone, expressed as an empty kept-clear dome, warm, in the middle of everything.

That is the census. One enormous luminous plant-body that is the country itself; a shadow population of pale lawn-keepers who may be few or may be legions; aimless sparks of spore; and an absence, honored, in a stone dome at the center.

Nothing calls, because nothing needs to. Everyone who lives here is already home, and has been, without interruption, since the moment the place decided it was done.
