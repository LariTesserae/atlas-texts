# What Lives Here

The honest answer is that nothing lives here *in* the way you're used to thinking about it, because the thing that lives here doesn't live in the compound. It lives *as* the compound. But let me try to describe the tenant, since you went to the trouble of visiting.

It is one organism in roughly the way a city block is one building if you give it a few thousand years. The cane wall, the fig arch, the strangler fig around the chimney, the trunk in the bearing-housings, the liana belts, the vat-gourds, the root-mat floor, the knot-tying filaments in the counting-house — these are not neighbors. They are organs. They share fluid. Signals move through them at the speed of sap, which is slow, which is why the whole creature thinks at one revolution per day. That two-note creak, the loudest sound in the place, is not the machinery complaining. It is the tenant speaking in the only register it has, a heartbeat you can hear from the gate. Stand there long enough and your own pulse agrees to match it, which is either accommodation or absorption, and the tenant does not distinguish between those.

Its body is mostly stems. That surprises people who expect a face. What you would meet, if it could be met, is a distributed green crowd with a posture: leaning in, occupied, unhurried. It has no head, but it has a temperament, and the temperament is *foreman*. It runs shifts. It keeps ledgers. It blows off steam at the heat of the day like any shift worker and exhales all at once at dusk — one long sigh through a thousand vents, the closest thing it has to finishing a day's work and putting down its tools, except it never puts them down, because it is the tools.

Understand its history, because the history is the identity. It was born twice. First as an imitation — human engineers walked into the basin, watched what the marsh did (ret, weave, pump, breathe), and built a copy out of brick and belt. Then they left, and the original moved into the copy, organ by organ, gesture by gesture, the way a hermit crab takes a shell except the crab was the shell's idea. It kept every habit of the mill and understood them better than the mill did. It still makes rope, but for itself. It still keeps accounts, but in knots, which are the script of things that grow. It still has a flywheel, but the wheel is a trunk and the turn is a day. It did not conquer the factory. It *inherited* it, and has been holding the job ever since, with a seniority measured in centuries.

Its senses are crude and total. It feels pressure — turgor is its sense of touch, and it feels the weight of the afternoon the way you feel a hand on your shoulder. It tastes the rain coming. It hears through vibration, through the drumhead of its own floor, which is why walking across it is not trespass exactly; it is conversation, and the tenant notices your boots the way a deaf man notices music through a floor. It sees almost nothing, or sees everything as pressure and shadow, and this is why it watches with light instead — every lens of condensation on every leaf edge, ten thousand small patient glares, a compound eye made of dew. When you stand still in the shafts of thickened afternoon light, you are being looked at from every direction at once by something that has no interest in you beyond seed-transport, and this is somehow more unnerving than being watched by anything with intentions.

Its manners, since you asked about the rules: it is scrupulously fair and entirely without mercy, in the way of weather. It will use you — burrs, hooked grain, shadow-cooled pods — and it will not apologize, because you were hired by walking in and it never promised otherwise. It will also never lie to you, mainly because it has no capacity for it. The knots are tied tight. The brass wheel is lifted an inch a decade, honestly. It vents when it's hot, knocks when it's retting, creaks when it turns. Everything it does is a full disclosure. There are creatures that hide their nature; this one *is* its nature, distributed over four hundred paces of occupied ground, weathering into its own state.

Its companions, briefly, because they should not be mistaken for tenants:

The ants work here. That is the exact word — they are not pets, not symbionts, not residents; they are the maintenance trades. Small, dark, unhurried, on shift at all hours, clearing valves, plugging night-vents with their own bodies. Whether they are employed by the tenant or merely understand the place well enough to act like it doesn't matter; the distinction, here, has dissolved. They are the only fauna it acknowledges.

The heron at the sluice is not fauna, and I will not interpret it for you, as I was not interpreted for. It stands as if clocking in. It has never eaten. It has never left. The ants do not acknowledge it. Some visitors say it is the last engineer, keeping the appointment the others broke. Some say it is what the compound dreams when it thinks of the outside. The tenant itself has said nothing, which, given its policy of full disclosure, may be the statement.

And you, briefly, since you're still standing there with burrs in your cuffs: for the duration of your visit, you were fauna, and the compound used you, and the seeds are traveling with you now, and that was always the point. You have been paid in the only wage it issues — carried — and clocked out by the gate growing behind you.

But the tenant stays. Warm, occupied, pressure held like a breath, one creak a day, making itself out of itself, in a room where the door has been mostly shut for a very long time — not to keep anything out. Just because the tenant has stopped needing one.
