# What Lives There

The honest answer is: almost nothing. But "almost" is doing a great deal of work in that sentence, and the plateau deserves better than an honest answer. It deserves a careful one.

## The Moths

The true inhabitants — if the word can be stretched to cover a creature that visits for an hour at dusk and alters nothing — are the moths.

They come up from the bowl as the light goes hard and low, when the banded stone turns from cream and ash to rose and pewter. They do not arrive in numbers. Never in numbers. Ones and twos, sometimes three, rising through the lanes on the updrafts the way the bird does, except slower, softer, as if they were pieces of the dusk itself detaching and drifting inward. They are pale — not white, exactly, but the color of the stone filmed with water: a greige, a bone, the color of a thing that has spent uncountable generations matching itself to the only surface it will ever land on. Whether they evolved that pallor or simply the pale ones survived, no one has established. The plateau does not keep records.

What the moths want is the film. Every pillar weeps, and the weeping water carries whatever the stone carries — dissolved minerals, faint and steady, the aquifer's slow testimony. The moths land on the vertical faces with a precision that looks impossible and isn't: wings folded to a knife-edge, feet placed on the wet grain, proboscis unrolled to the sheet of moisture. They drink. They drink the way the place allows everything to be done — briefly, without alteration, at a rate that has no beginning and no end.

And here is the detail worth sitting with: the moths are silent. Not quiet — *silent*. They live inside the loudest sound on the plateau and make none. They do not call, because no call could cross the chord. They do not startle, because nothing that happens here is sudden. A moth drinking at a pillar will let you slide past within arm's reach, wings folded, entirely unbothered, and if you came back the next dusk — the same moth, or its indistinguishable kin — would be drinking at the same height, from the same thread of water, at the same slow interval.

Whether they are individuals, in the way we mean it, is genuinely unclear. In a place where every drop falls on schedule, the sameness of the moths begins to feel less like a limitation of observation and more like a fact about them. They may be the only living thing here that has fully adopted the plateau's single law: *nothing is becoming anything.* Each one already is what it is, and what it is, is enough.

They leave when the dusk does — back down over the western lip, through the spray of the falls, to whatever warmer air raised them. They take nothing. They leave nothing. Their wings leave no print on the moisture film; the film closes over the places their feet touched before the dark has fully settled.

## The Bird

The bird does not live here, and it is important to say so, because the bird itself seems to insist on the distinction. A large dark chough, or something a chough would be if it had decided long ago to have nothing to do with anyone — it rides the updraft along the falls, tilts through the curtain of spray in a motion so practiced it reads as ritual, and is gone over the bowl. It never lands. Nothing lands. It does not hunt the moths; if it wanted the moths, this would be the easiest hunting in the world, and its indifference to them is total and somehow formal, like a neighbor who nods without stopping.

If the moths are the plateau's congregation, the bird is a pilgrim — the only creature that treats the place as a *place*, that comes to it, moves through it, and leaves. Perhaps it comes for the lift. Perhaps it comes for something else. You will see it twice in a long stay if you are lucky, and both times you will be certain it is the same bird, because of course it is.

## And the Plateau Itself

Here is where the careful answer has to get uncomfortable: the moths and the bird may not be the main inhabitants at all.

The plateau is not dead. That is its strangest claim on the word *lives*. A corpse changes — it cools, it softens, it is taken apart. The Sounding Plateau does none of these things. It metabolizes: water rises through it, sings through it, falls from it, returns. It breathes: the wind pours over the escarpment and pours out over the western lip, hour after hour, the same breath, forever. It speaks — and this is not metaphor, because the sound is physically produced, physically real, a chord sustained by ten thousand hollow throats, varying in its infinite slight internal ways the way a voice at rest varies. It responds to touch: the moisture films close over your handprint; the gravel resettles behind your boots. These are the behaviors we would call homeostasis in anything with cells.

What it lacks is the one thing we insist on: the capacity to be otherwise. It cannot change, so we say it isn't alive. But there is a second definition, older and humbler, that runs: *to live is to persist against the world.* The plateau does not persist *against* anything. All its gradients are spent. It is not fighting entropy; it has arrived at an arrangement entropy cannot improve upon. It is the only thing in the field — moths included — that has nothing left to struggle with, and if struggle is the price of the word *alive*, then perhaps the word is too small for what the plateau is.

## The Ledger

So: what lives on the Sounding Plateau?

Some pale moths, barely, briefly, in ones and twos at dusk. One dark bird, in transit, twice. A vast mineral thing that is neither quite living nor quite otherwise, playing a piece no one wrote.

Sit at the foot of the tallest column at that hour and watch a moth drink from the weeping stone, and you will see the whole ecology in a single frame: the great player, and its entire audience, one small pale creature at a time, listening with its wings folded, drinking what the music runs on.
