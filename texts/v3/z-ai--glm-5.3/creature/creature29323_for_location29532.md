# What Lives There

Begin with the small ones, because they are the place's true citizens.

The darkness under the water is a darkness with texture, and nearly all of that texture is alive. The root-mat that forms the sea floor is not a floor but a city, and its first inhabitants are the grazers — low, wide, slow creatures shaped less like fish than like hands spread flat on a table. They drift along every submerged surface, mouths on their undersides, mowing the algal film that grows on the rhizome mat the way sheep mow a hillside. They have done this for so many thousands of generations that their bodies have taken the shape of their work: flattened, patient, armored in plates grown to match the bark-ridges they live on, so that a resting grazer is nearly indistinguishable from the terrain. They never hurry. There is nothing here to outrun. When one dies it settles into the mat and is quietly incorporated, and the floor grows a millimeter taller, and the grazer becomes architecture.

Above them, in the open dark, live the lights.

You could not call most of them fish. The word arrives and doesn't fit. Some of them are long and ribboned, trailing photophores down their flanks in cold blue rows — not for hunting, or not only, but for the same reason stars exist in any sky: so that other lights can find them. The open reaches of water between the great trunks, those half-mile silences, are their territory. They cross them in slow migratory loops, individual lights scattered across the black like a constellation, and their calls are the long whale-like sighs the text of the place mentions — not songs exactly, though a listener would insist on the word. The sigh carries for miles through still water. Another answers from the far side of the reach. Nothing is communicated that we would recognize as information; what passes between them is more like agreement — *I am here, the water is the same where you are as where I am, continue.* They have been holding this conversation longer than there have been languages to compare it to.

And the lights are how they navigate the impossible geography of the mat. Down there, where the rhizome city folds into chambers and flooded galleries, every species has learned to read the glow of every other species. The grazer-lights of the floor shine dim orange. The ribboned swimmers burn cold blue. The root-chamber dwellers — the tenants of the flooded and drained and reflooded hollows inside the trunks — pulse green, and their pulsing has a rhythm to it, each nation of them with its own tempo, so that a swimmer passing a trunk can tell, from the color and the cadence of the light bleeding out through the bark, which settlement it is passing and how that settlement is faring. It is the closest thing this world has to writing: a system of visible, rhythmic signals, older than any alphabet by a margin that makes the word "older" sound absurd. But it is not writing, because no one wrote it and no one reads it on purpose. It grew, like everything else here.

The root-chambers themselves are where the density peaks. Inside the great trunks, in the hollows the circulation-rain floods and drains on some timetable the tree keeps and no one else knows, whole communities live their entire existences in spaces smaller than a cathedral and larger than any burrow. These creatures never see open water. They are born in a chamber, live in it, and are absorbed by the mat at the bottom of it, and across the world some of them have been chamber-bound so long that the ones living in adjacent trunks — ten minutes' swim apart, a distance any open-water creature crosses without thought — have diverged into forms as different from each other as a fox from a fish. Each trunk's flank is a nation. A single pillar-trunk hosts its gardens and its moss-forests and its flooded hollows, and every nation on it is complete, self-enclosed, and entirely unaware that any other trunk exists. The half-mile of dark water between settlements might as well be an ocean without a far shore.

The crackle and tick in the sound — the constant rasp beneath the groaning of the trunks — is them. Ten million small bodies feeding, calling, rasping. It is the sound of the place metabolizing itself, and it never stops, and nothing alive here has ever heard silence.

Then there are the canopy nations — the things that fill the bird niche, nesting in the vault in colonies of thousands. They deserve their own reckoning, because they live differently than everything else. The water-dwellers live in a world of stillness and pressure and dark; the canopy-dwellers live in the windless green half-light under the leaf-ceiling, where the internal rain falls on them constantly, a fine drizzle from a sky that is only fifty feet away and entirely alive. They nest in the living architecture — their eggs laid in the folds of leaves the size of sails, their colonies spreading across the vault in territories that map onto the branches like states on a continent. They sing, and the enclosed air doubles and trebles their calls until the vault becomes an instrument and every colony is playing it at once. They are, in their own way, the loud ones. The deep groan of the trunks and the hiss of the internal rain are the place's voice; the canopy calls are the place's conversation.

Here is something to notice, if you spend a long time in this world: almost nothing eats anything else.

That is the strangest fact about the still ocean, and it takes a while to see. The text of the place says its only law is equilibrium, and the fauna have grown into that law with their whole bodies. The grazers eat the algal film, which grows back at exactly the rate it is eaten. The swallows of the canopy eat spore-dust and the drifting rain of small things that the water is always carrying. Almost no one hunts. Predation exists only at the smallest scale — there are things in the mat that absorb smaller things, but "absorption" is a truer word than "predation" here; the mat absorbs what cannot hold its place in the balance, and its smallest residents do the same work on the smallest scale. The great ribboned swimmers of the open reaches live for centuries, drifting their enormous patient loops, and the pressure of their existence on the world is so exactly balanced that the world's response to them is not to adapt but simply to include them. They were here when the mountain was being rebuilt. They will be here when the balance closes over whatever comes next.

And then — in every telling of a place like this there is one more thing, the presence you sense behind all the small presences — there are the trunks.

They live here. The text calls them environment, calls them plant, and that is true, but it is a truth told from outside. From inside — if you press your hand to the bark and feel the faint steady heat, the pulse of a body that never sleeps and never hurries — the trunks are inhabitants of the same order as the whales and the birds. They are simply the ones who stayed. Ages back, when the mountain was worn to almost nothing, they caught what was left and built it back up as something better than stone, and in doing so they became not the inhabitants of the place but the place itself — the walls, the floor, the ceiling, the load-bearing structure of an ocean three miles above where oceans should be. They carry the water on their shoulders and do not think about it, which is to say: they think about it the way a heart thinks about the blood. It is not a task. It is what they are. The groaning under load is not complaint; it is the one long note a body sings when it is doing the thing it was made for, and doing it forever.

So the answer to who lives here is layered, like the mat itself:

The small ones — the grazers, the tickers, the raspers, the city of the floor — who are so many and so patient that they have become indistinguishable from the terrain, and who have accepted this without ever having the concept of accepting anything.

The lit ones — the swimmers, the sighers, the drifting constellation — who hold the open reaches together with their lights and their long agreements, and who are scattered the way stars are scattered because each one is enormous, and far apart, and sufficient.

The enclosed ones — the nations of the flooded chambers, ten thousand civilizations without histories, each complete in the territory of one trunk, each unaware that the world is larger than itself.

The loud ones — the canopy colonies, thousands upon thousands, singing into a leaf-vault that turns every voice into architecture.

And the deep ones — the trunks, the rhizome, the mat — who are the walls and floor of everyone else's world, who circulate the warmth and keep the rain falling inward, and who constitute, if you are honest about it, the majority of the life here by any measure: by mass, by age, by the sheer duration of their attention.

Nothing here has a name for itself. Nothing here has ever needed one. The place is pre-tool, pre-language, pre-everything, and its residents are the same — not because they are primitive, but because they finished. They reached the shape their world wanted of them, and the world stopped asking. The equilibrium holds them all in its enormous gentle hand: the grazer flat on the bark, the swimmer drifting its half-mile of dark, the chamber-nation pulsing green inside the wall of its trunk, the colony singing in the vault, and the trunks underneath everything, groaning their one long note into the permanent drizzle.

They are what rest sounds like.
