No one was born here. Begin the census with that, because it governs every entry after it: every citizen of this town arrived from elsewhere — out of the dark water, down the plume, homing on the chemistry — immigrants all, the founding population imported wholesale, the second generation deferred until the streets are fat enough to raise children in.

And understand what kind of document a census is here. It cannot be a list of who lives in the town now so much as a schedule of who will: the demographics are set not by birth but by decay. A count taken in the first month would name citizens who will not exist by the second year. A count taken in the fiftieth would omit nearly everyone who built the place. The population is not a number; it is a succession, a relay of appetites in which each generation's arrival is the previous generation's success. What follows is the roll, roughly in order of tenure — which is also the order of departure.

**The commuters.** The largest citizens are the least attached. The sleeper sharks are less residents than contractors, commuting in from the open country when the plume begins to advertise, and they will resign before anyone else: their loyalty is to the smell, not the place, and when the muscle economy closes they will drift back into the black country to find another founding, or not. They are huge past the town's own scale — a single crew member outweighs every other citizen the town will ever have, all at once, if you exclude the bacteria, and the bacteria are always excluded, because including them makes every citizen a minority. They move slowly, which is not a deficiency but a credential: a kilometer an hour of certainty, the pace of a decision already made. Their eyes are dim, clouded, sometimes carrying tenants of their own on the corneas, so that the crews who remove the most see the least — and in a town where light is contraband, that is not a disability but a dress code. They take the founder apart in blocks, mouthfuls the size of districts, and where they have worked the flagstones of skin lie lifted and the fat stands open like a cliff of butter. Nothing resists them. Nothing here has resisted anything for weeks. And they are themselves unassailable, their flesh steeped in ammonia so that nothing downstream will eat it — the town's only citizens exempt from its universal tax. They have made themselves inedible to everything but time.

**The masons.** The hagfish hold the oldest job in town, and have held it since before jaws were invented, which is to say since before most of the other citizens had faces. They arrive by the dozen and then by the hundred and pack into the body cavity, and the first thing to know about them is that they are blind — eyes reduced to smudges of light-sense, rumors of eyes — which here is conformity, not affliction. They find the founder mouth-first, and the mouth is a ring of keratin horns that grips and pulls; for leverage they tie themselves into working knots, tail through loop, and slam the knot against the carcass, so that the wet little pops heard all over the town are citizens hitting themselves against it in order to tear it. Each hagfish keeps four hearts, none of them romantic, and a slime apparatus out of all proportion to its owner: one alarmed hagfish can turn a bucket of sea into gel, and a hundred unalarmed ones, going about their business, are the reason the town's low places are full of a plaster that never sets. They live in their own mortar. They tunnel as they eat — avenues bored through blubber wide enough to turn around in, which is the entire engineering specification — and they arrived hungry with months of hunger already banked, because a hagfish can fast for months, and they will leave the same way, gorged and unhurried, having treated the whole civic project as a meal between two fasts. To the hagfish the founder is not a city. To the hagfish the founder is lunch, and the city is a by-product, and they cannot pretend otherwise, having no way to say anything at all.

**The majority.** Then there is the carpet. The amphipods are the town's majority and its anonymity at once: each one a gram of pink animal shaped like a comma, with opinions only about fat. Singular, an amphipod is nobody — punctuation. Together they are the most numerous citizens the town will ever have with faces, a population measured not in numbers but in contact: near-touching, overlapping, a shag so dense the founder's skin has stopped being skin and become a fabric with appetites. And they were nearby all along — that is the detail that redescribes the whole country. Above every yard of that gray plain, in the open dark, there is always a thin haze of fasting amphipods, drifting, waiting out their own metabolisms, a diaspora whose existence, until chemistry names an address, is only a rumor. The town did not attract them. The town gave them somewhere to be the multitude they already were. They arrived within hours; they will leave within hours of the fat's ending, the carpet rolling itself up, the majority departing the way majorities do, which is all at once. Their females carry eggs clasped in pouches like parcels — so the town has children after all, though they are born as luggage and inherit only the appetite. And their mandibles are the town's clock: ten thousand sets at work at once, that dry granular ticking like rain behind a wall, the only timekeeping the city possesses, and it does not measure days. It measures what is left.

**The patrol.** The rattails keep the border, and the border is a smell. They cruise the plume's edge in slow orbits — tapering fish, designed like rumors of fish, each carrying its small running light, boats on a night harbor, except that the harbor is the town and the night is the country. They hold no property in the founder; they are citizens of the water, resident in the civic sense only, and their station in the economy is the second hand: they eat what the demolition throws off, the scraps, the strays, the town's litter. Because the current moves the plume, the patrol redraws the town's map every few hours, and this is the nearest thing to government the boomtown years produce. They will outlast the sharks by a season and the carpet by a year, still orbiting, still patrolling a boundary that is steadily more smell than town, and the last border they will ever draw is a circle around bare stone.

**The official.** There is exactly one citizen with a face. The octopus keeps an office in the shade of the skull, arms folded in the manner of anyone who has ever kept an office, and she is the landlord of nothing yet: she holds an option, not a deed. She does not eat the founder; she eats the founder's citizens — amphipods, strays, the small unwary — which makes her the town's first service economy, the first livelihood that depends not on the body but on the traffic of the body. She is also its only predator, and she predates at the margins, in small denominations, the way towns tolerate a little crime. Attention is her whole technology, and she is attentive as nothing else here is: the city's one interior life, hers, guarded. Her skin changes color continuously and means nothing — there is no one to read it, and she cannot read it herself, octopuses being colorblind; her moods pass across her like private weather in a language with no readers. She is soft, as the law requires — mostly water, incompressible, nothing for the squeeze to grip — except for a single hard part, the beak, kept at her center: the only citizen whose skeleton is interior, the only architecture in town with a lock on it. Her tenure is months. It will end privately and on schedule, the way octopus tenures do, and the office in the shade of the skull will stand vacant, and the town will not notice, because the town does not notice; but for one season the municipality has an executive branch, and it is a colorblind weather in the doorway of the cathedral.

**The majority beneath the majority.** The true citizens — the ones who will outlast every entry in this roll except the archive — have no faces at all. The bacteria are the town's permanent population, its industry, and its lamps, and they hold three offices. In the anoxic dark of the bone and the crater mud, the anaerobes cook the treasury down into sulfide; they are the mint, paying interest in a currency no one above them can spend. At every surface where that sulfide meets the oxygenated sea, the oxidizers stand ready to burn it: the spender class, the white lawns, the mats creeping outward over the enriched sediment, the only vegetation for miles — and green, note, is a color the sun holds the patent on, so the town's lawns are white, growing on chemistry instead of light, every step of their metabolism a refusal of the surface's entire economy. And in the fat, the luminous species: the lamps, blue-green, municipal, the town glowing at exactly the rate of its own decay. Counting them is not counting; it would be geology — you would report them as tonnage, as acreage, as a climate rather than a population, and the census-taker gives up and weighs. One more thing belongs in their entry, because it explains everyone else's: the town has a single industry, the sulfur trade, and every citizen who lasts finds a way to be employed by it. The sharks subcontract. The worms will internalize it. The clams will keep the office in their chests. The bacteria are not employees of the industry. The bacteria are the industry, and everyone else is a client with an address.

**The coming citizens.** The strangest residents have not arrived yet; their district is already platted in oil. Out of the dark water, down the plume, will come the bone worms' larvae, and their arrival is governed by the strangest law in the town's whole history, though the town passes none: a larva that settles on bone becomes a female; a larva that settles on a female becomes a male. Sex in this town is a matter of address. The female then proceeds to stop being anything anyone would call a worm: no mouth, no stomach, no gut — appetite entirely outsourced. She plants roots into the bone, roots like a tree's if trees grew in stone and ate it, secreting acid at the tips to dissolve their way down to the oil, and inside the root tissue she keeps her garden, bacteria that eat the stone for her — a farm with a figurehead, a citizen who is mostly tenancy. Her plume is red because it is her own blood, hemoglobin in fronds, oxygen couriered down from the sea to keep the garden's fire lit: sulfide rising from below, oxygen drawn from above, the town's whole economy repeated at the width of her own body — a market junction the size of a pencil. And the males. Dozens to hundreds of them, the size of dust, folded into her tube, mature, devoted, doing nothing: never meeting the bone, never seeing the water, never arriving anywhere. Here is the single exception to the fact this census opened with — that no one was born here. The dwarf males are the only natives the town will ever have: born inside the city limits, dying inside them, and they never once see the streets. The only natives never see the town. None of this is a fable; it is taxonomy. The deep files its wonders under *optional*, and the worms bring them home.

**The long citizens.** After the worms come the tenants of the century, and the deepest commitment available in this town belongs to the clams. They settle as larvae into the enriched mud of the white lawns and proceed to stop eating — not fasting, which is the hagfish's discipline, but retirement: appetite closed down, outsourced to the sulfur-farmers in their gills, greenhouses on their chests, and the clam's whole biography becomes a matter of real estate. It plows the lawns with a foot like a slow blade, keeps the water moving over its tenants, grows a larger house when they prosper — a small public utility with a shell. Around them the opportunist quarter fills in: snails grazing the mats' edges; mussels keeping two kinds of tenants in their gills at once, hedging the sulfur trade against other chemistries, the town's only diversified investors; little crabs picking and hovering at the fringes, the nearest thing this town produces to idleness. None of these citizens will ever know the founder. By the time they settle, the founder is not a body but a geology — a name for the chemistry they live on — and the census, for the only time in the town's history, begins to include second generations, third, fourth, decades deep. For one century, this town has a childhood.

**The last tenants.** And at the end, when the oil runs thin and the sulfide fails and the charter lapses, the final demographic arrives needing nothing but the address. The anemones settle on bare bone — flowers that never needed the sun, though they resemble exactly nothing else that grows without it — and collect the marine snow as it falls past, the surface's old accounts settling on the town a half-gram at a time, an economy closing out at last in the only coin the surface ever sent down. The hydroids lattice themselves up the old arches, colonial, patient, growing. And here the city's grammar flips, for the first and only time. For a hundred years this town has been eaten into being: every avenue a wound, every wall a by-product, the whole architecture subtractive — buildings removed rather than raised. The reef stage is the only era in the city's entire history in which anything is added: the only construction not conducted by a mouth, the first walls the town has ever grown instead of opened. It spends its life shrinking and its afterlife growing, and the growing is the smaller achievement and by far the stranger one. Then the bones sink into stone with their boreholes intact, a trace fossil the size of a city plan, catalogued by no one — and that, too, is a census: the archive, keeping every record the town ever made, which is to say the marks of everyone who ever lived in it. The count is eventually taken. Geology takes it.

**The visitors.** Two kinds of visitor pass through, and neither can take up residence. The siphonophore drifts in on the deep's own slow weather — not an animal, exactly, but a federation of them, a colony of specialists bound in one glassy envelope: a city visiting a city, both of them assemblies, one of them brief and the other merely less brief. It lights the arcade as it goes, fabulously, and the town does not register the visit, and then it is gone, and the ordinary darkness comes back like a verger closing up. And far overhead, in the country this town has never heard of, the founder's kin pass in their hundreds, uncounted, singing their long door-opening sounds into the water, and the calls roll down through the kilometers and arrive in the town's bones the way weather arrives in a stone house, felt in the sternum before it is heard. They are the only family any citizen here has. They do not know the address exists — but then, the family has only one address left, and this is it, and no one above will ever call. The mandibles do not pause. If mourning passes through this town, it does so at the speed of sound and changes nothing, and the town goes on being the only thing the family will ever have built.

**The founder.** The founder is not on the census, because the founder is what the census is written on. Every entry above is, in the strict accounting, one more way of reading a single body: the sharks read the muscle, the hagfish read the fat, the carpet read the skin, the worms will read the oil, the anemones will read the stone — a city's entire population as a set of reading instruments applied to one text, most of the readings conducted by mouth. The plume is the founder's name going on being pronounced, downcurrent, for as long as there is anything left to say. The glow is the founder's account, still current. The bones are the founder's patience. The founder's second life will run nearly as long as the first and be more populous by six orders of magnitude, and it began, as second lives go, unusually well: a funeral that became a municipality, a death that took on citizens, a body that will still be conducting slow public business a century after the song above it faded out over the plain.

*Notes for the census-taker:*

*Weigh, do not count. The majority cannot be counted, only weighed.*
*Date every page in remaining oil.*
*There is one industry. Do not ask for occupations; ask where each citizen keeps it — in the gut, in the gill, in the root, in the bone.*
*Record that no one was born here. Then record the exception. Then note that the exception never once sees the town.*
*Note that the loudest citizens are the size of commas; that the largest are half-blind and leave first; that the only face belongs to a one-chair government that files nothing.*
*Grant no residency to visitors, however bright, however mournful.*
*Do not enter the founder under residents. Enter the founder under location.*
*Expect the roster to be obsolete before it is finished. Every census of this town is a snapshot of a departure.*
*Close the book when the bones are bare. The archive will keep the rest — catalogued by no one, read by no one, which was always the arrangement.*
