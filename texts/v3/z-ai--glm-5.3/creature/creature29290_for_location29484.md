# The Population of the Highwater

No one lives here who has blood, and so the census must be taken differently. You do not count the residents of this country by walking door to door; you drift, and you learn them the way you learn the place: by ear first, and slowly, over generations of their time rather than yours.

They are all plants. That is the first thing to understand, and it is not a demotion. In a sea where nothing with a mouth may stay, the plants are not the background of life. They are the life. They have inherited every vacancy — the fish's job, the herds' job, even, in a strange way, the people's — and they hold those offices without knowing they hold them, which may be the most secure way to hold anything.

## The Meadows

Begin with the meadows, because the meadows are the closest thing to a single citizen. What looks like thousands of beds of cold-water grass spread across whole drowned townships is, functionally, one organism — the floor of the sea is a single slow stomach, as the first law says, and the meadow is its lining. The grasses are glassy, dark, the green of bottle-glass in a cellar, and they grow in such unison that a herder from the old days, could you sink one down to see it, would recognize the posture at once: this is a flock at graze. It bends together when the current leans on it. It soughs — that long sigh like surf heard through a wall — and the sighing is the meadow's whole vocabulary, one word stretched over years, meaning something like *here* or *still here*.

The meadows are also the morticians, and the eaters of the dead, and so they are patient in the specific way of things that have never once been hungry. Nothing rots loose in the Highwater. Every fallen blade, every spent seed, every last leached horn on the shrine knoll goes down through the roots in time. The meadows have been closing the loops of this country for longer than anyone remembers, which is to say forever, since there is no one left to remember. If the Highwater has a government, it is rootwork, and the meadows hold every office at once.

## The Seed-Schools

If the meadows are the country's settled old flesh, the seeds are its restless youth — the ones who took the fish's job and kept a little of the plant's dignity in doing it. Small buoyant pods with thread-fins, they rise from the meadows in their thousands and school upward: wheeling, banking, turning as one body with that eerie single-mindedness that flocks have, except no flock here was ever driven by fear, because nothing here has ever been chased. They rise for the rain-ceiling. They touch it — a soft percussion against the drum-skin, the only applause this world ever gives — and they sink back down.

Watch them long enough and you begin to grant them intention, which is probably wrong and possibly not. On the rare still-days, when the rain fails and the surface goes mirror-flat and the whole country holds the only silence it knows, every seed in every meadow rises *together*. Not scattered, not staggered: together, as if the word had gone out. What the word is, or how it travels through fathoms of cold slate-colored water, the country has not explained and does not intend to. And when the drowned village plays its full-chord at spate, the seed-schools rise into the sound and hang there in the middle water, suspended in the chord, doing whatever a seed does in the presence of music. If a seed can be said to listen, this is the audience.

## The Lantern-Kelp

Below the schools, in the ink with the sparks, stand the lantern-kelp — the harbor-keepers, the night-watch, the most person-like things in the sea. Every blade of them ends in a small cold lamp, a blue-green coal that neither flickers nor warms, and their groves stand lit like harbors with no ships. They have been lit for no one for generations. This is their character, as near as can be made out: they do not light for anyone. They light because light is what they spend, the way the old people of the plateau perhaps once spent fire, and the steadiness of the lamps — the refusal to flicker even when a squall bends the grove flat — reads as the most stubborn kind of dignity in the country.

During a lightning-walk, when the whole column fills white for a heartbeat, the lantern-kelp are the only residents caught doing something: for that one flash you see that their lamps go on burning in the flood of light, unswamped, unimpressed, small steady coals inside the huge temporary day. Then dark again. They do not doubt what they saw. They cannot; they have no arrangement for doubting.

## The Drifters

The bladder-bell plants live at the ceiling, in the rain, knocking together in the surface chop, chiming their dull far-off bells — the country's whole percussion section, and its most carefree citizens. They have no floor, no roots, no obligations. The false-kelp with its hollow stems lives between, moored but hollow, breathing the gas the earth sighs up through the old riverbeds, growing tall in the grey rumor of the light. Between these two — the anchored and the adrift — runs the whole argument of the place, settled long ago in favor of both.

## The Hot Groves

And at the few warm seams where the plateau's furnace sieves up through old river gravel live the ambitious ones. The Hot Groves are the country's cities: small, loud with bubbles, lit like a feast, where the kelp forgets it is winter and grows a hand's width a day and the leaves come wide as doors. Residents of the cold openness drift past these districts the way travelers drift past lit windows. The groves are few, and the emptiness between them is the country's true size — lushness and emptiness in balance, groves like islands, islands like punctuation.

## The Ghost-Citizens

And then there are the ones who are not alive but whom you will come, within a day, to count anyway. The drowned village, whose doorways have been flue-pipes and whose chimneys have been reeds for generations, and which plays — actually plays, is played, by the storm — a thing that in any other sea would be the accomplishment of a musician. The village cannot hear itself. The Horn, black stone, slick with rain, the only dry thing in the world, voicing its single low note under everything like a pedal-point. These are not residents the way the meadows are residents. But they are the only things in the Highwater that were *made*, and in a country with no animals and no people, made things carry a strange weight — the weight of having once been wanted. The village's walls stand hip-high. The doorways are still doorways. Something here has decided what a doorway is, and it wasn't the water.

## The Census

So: a country whose entire mobile population was spawned by roots, whose watchmen burn lamps that no eye checks, whose single organism runs from shoal to shoal under the silt like flour under flour, whose children rise in schools to touch the sky and fall back down unswallowed, whose music is played by stone and ice and rain on the instruments of the departed, and whose one law — nothing with blood may stay — is enforced by nothing and broken by no one.

If you are here, yours are the only eyes in the country, and the country does not know what to do about that. The lantern-kelp will go on lighting for no one. The meadows will go on eating their own dead. The seeds will rise to the rain-ceiling in their thousands and touch it and sink, and no one will see it, and it will go on being done — which may be the final definition of what lives here: everything that remains, remains, and does not require a witness to keep doing it. The rain falls on the roof of the world, and under the roof, in the cold green dark, the country carries on without us, as it always has, as it is fully prepared to do forever.
