# The People of the Weepworks

## Who you would notice first

There are not many of them. Perhaps thirty, thirty-five — the settlement could house four times that number, but the Weepworks does not recruit; it retains, and even retention is imperfect, because people who leave for drier towns find, within a year or two, that dryness itself has become unbearable to them. Their skin rebels. They come back.

What they look like: barefoot, mostly, because the mud is firm and the footing is a kind of literacy you learn through the soles. Oilcloth aprons and short capes, cut to shed water toward the pipe network and not down the back of the neck. Skin that glistens without meaning to — sweat does not dry here, so the people of the Weepworks simply glisten, all the time, and think nothing of it, the way a fish thinks nothing of being wet. Hair is kept close-cropped or bound tight, not out of vanity, but because loose hair in this air becomes a sponge, a small private swamp attached to your head.

Their hands are the softest you will ever touch on working people. This is the thing visitors remark on. Decades of hot humidity, of handling wet metal and wet wood and the tender stems of a grove that has never needed to defend itself — it has made them, in a sense, part of the grove's defensive softness. Calluses do not form here. The skin stays pliant, like everything else that lives in the basin.

## The silence that shapes them

Here is the deepest fact about them, and the hardest to see: **they are quiet because the town is loud.**

Not shy. Not sullen. Quiet the way the place itself is quiet — a silence *inside* noise, not the absence of it. In the boiler's pulse and the stochastic drip-field and the hiss of the conduits, a raised voice is simply another drop in the downpour; it accomplishes nothing. Generations of this have produced a people who speak, when they speak at all, low and close, mouth near the ear, at a register that slips *under* the machinery rather than competing with it.

And they gesture. The hand-language began, surely, as pipe-talk — signals across a rushing conduit junction where no shout could survive: *close the valve, leak here, hold, release, hot.* But it has long since become the general medium of the settlement. Children learn it before they learn words. Two Weepworks residents can hold a complete conversation — opinions, jokes, a certain dry regional irony — across a dim room, hands moving in the amber light, while the boiler chuffs and neither of them so much as unseals their lips. Visitors find this eerie for about a day, and then find it beautiful, and then find themselves doing it.

This is why the loudest wetland in the world has not one voice in it. The voices are there. They have simply learned to be hands.

## What they do all day

The work is not labor so much as accompaniment. The town runs on gravity, capillarity, and patience, and so do its people.

There is a role — not an office, more a habit that settles on whoever is suited to it — called the **Listener**. The Listener walks the settlement at dusk and again at dawn, hands clasped behind the back, barefoot, reading the drip-field the way others read a sky. Every weeping rock keeps its own rhythm; the Listener knows each rhythm personally, the way you know a family member's cough, and can walk past a rock at forty meters and tell you — before consciously hearing anything — that its third overhang is stuttering, that a fracture is silting, that a pipe upstream has gone dead. A silence anywhere in the drip-field is a *wrongness*, the way a missing note is wrongness in a song you know. Within the hour someone will be out with a wire brush. The Listener's apprentice, currently a girl of perhaps twelve, can already distinguish the cadences of thirty rocks, and has named them — the rocks, not the cadences — after the older members of the settlement, a joke that everyone politely pretends is a joke.

The rest of the work: tending the valves, which may be adjusted but never closed; brushing the fractures; repotting the rocks' catchments; splitting humus from the mold's clean-up runs and carrying it in shoulder-baskets to the beds where the food grows. The food is all plants, of course — there is nothing else to eat and no one minds. Fern crosiers. The starch pith of a fallen trunk, harvested only after the pale sheet-mold has done its first pass and sweetened it. Fruits that drop from the canopy and lie unclaimed, because nothing in this basin claims by appetite, and the people simply gather what falls. Nothing here has thorns; nothing here has ever needed them; even the harvesting is a kind of gathering-up rather than a taking.

Repair is the liturgy. A Weepworks resident does not fix a pipe because it is broken; a Weepworks resident *maintains* a pipe the way a person maintains a friendship — continuously, attentively, without expecting it to ever be finished. The oldest of them can run a hand along a conduit's skin and tell you, from the temperature and the vibration, which joints will want attention within the season.

## What the place has made of them

It is worth being precise about the effect of a predatorless, fearless ecology on a human temperament, because the effect is real and it is strange.

Nothing in the basin chases. Nothing hides. Nothing watches with appetite. And the people, living their whole lives inside that absence, have become — the word they would reject, but the accurate word is — *undefended*. Doors are unlatched; there is not a lock in the settlement; goods lie under the canopy where they lie. Quarrels occur, as quarrels do, but they have the lifespan of rain here — sudden, enormous, hammering, then gone — because there is so little to be hungry for, and what there is, arrives. Water arrives. Food falls. Heat is the one thing in surplus and the one thing nobody can hoard.

They do not speak of fear in the way dry-country people do, not because they are brave, but because the concept has gone a little mossy from disuse, like a tool left out in the wet.

And they know, with a settled and unanxious certainty, who actually owns the place. The trees are the landlords; the ferns that cup the steam vents are the priesthood; the sheet-mold is the undertaker and the baker both, reducing every fallen thing to sweet black humus, and the people treat it with the respectful practical affection you give a colleague who does the job no one else wants. There are elders — two or three remain — who were alive during the year they closed the valves, the year the grove sulked and the basin began to dry. They do not talk about it. But you can watch one of them rest a palm against a vent and adjust it by feel, eyes closed, and understand that this is what a covenant looks like when it is kept not with ceremony but with a hand on warm iron.

## What they are becoming

The description of this place says its people have become part of the water cycle, and it is meant literally. Their sweat joins the general humidity. Their breath rises into the canopy. At night they sleep under the drip-field and the boiler's chuff, and the children who are born here are lulled by it — a Weepworks baby, taken to a dry town, cannot sleep; it misses the percussion.

And in the end the mold takes them too, as it takes the fallen fronds, and no one finds this grim. The burials are shallow, in the humus beds, where the water table breathes. It is the only truly quiet thing that happens in the Weepworks — one small silence added to the great one — and it is attended barefoot, with hands speaking gently over the grave, until the dripping closes over the sound.

Stand at the basin's edge at last light, and you will see them: dim shapes moving between the pipes, amber-lit, soft-handed, glistening, saying nothing, meaning a great deal. The loudest wetland in the world, roaring with water and machines — and the only voices in it are water, machinery, and hands.
