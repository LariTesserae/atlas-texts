What lives here is an accident of geography that has decided to make itself permanent.

Start with the smallest, because everything else stands on them: the mats. Orange, red, green, impossible colors laid along the hot channels like spilled paint that never dries. They are bacteria — thermophiles — and they are the only things here that were not carried in. Everything else arrived: wind-blown, wing-borne, walked. The mats simply happened, the way the terraces happened, the way the ground itself is happening. They live in water that would cook a hand and they do not know that this is remarkable, because knowing requires a nervous system and they have traded that away for the ability to exist at all where nothing else could. When the caribou step over the channels, and they do, carefully, in a way that suggests the knowledge is older than caution — the mats shimmer under the disturbance and the flies rise in a body, a visible breath of insects, and the birds come down on that breath like weather.

The flies are the middle floor of the building. Larvae in water too hot for anything but the mats, adults in clouds above the warm channels, and out of them the small birds make their whole living: snow buntings, pipits, working the vents in loose constellations, present nowhere on the plateau except along the seams, exactly as abundant as the seams allow. They do not think of this place as cold. They think of it as a restaurant with a very odd floor plan — long, narrow, well-lit by nothing, kilometers of nothing between the tables.

The ravens live here like landlords. Pairs hold territories along the steam, and a territory in this country is not a shape on a map but a length of warm line — so many vent-meters, so much fog, a share of the migration's casualties. They are the only residents who seem to regard the place as a place, rather than as a corridor or a condition. They know where the crust is thin. They know which mudpot took a lemming last spring and might again. They sit on black rubble that would cut a boot and they sit on it comfortably, like men on a bench outside a shop that never opens, watching the works run.

The foxes patrol. All winter, along the warm line, because the warm line is the only line: no snow settles there, so the lemmings and voles underneath stay awake through the arctic dark, whole small warm-blooded lives conducted on thermal subsidy, tunnels under thin steaming ground, January activity in a world where January is supposed to be a pause button. The foxes find them by ear and by patience and by an inherited map of which pockets stay open. A fox hunting here in midwinter is hunting in a strip of autumn a few dozen meters wide, walled on both sides by actual winter. Every one of them lives in the seams. None of them has ever had a reason to leave.

And above it all, the gyrfalcon — not a resident, an argument. It stitches the margins of the herd, crossing and recrossing, a working predator in a country that seems to have no business supporting anything so quick and clean. It lives off the migration's passage the way a bridge-tender lives off the traffic.

But the real inhabitants, the ones this description finally belongs to, are the caribou, because they are the answer to the strangest question the place asks. The rock is a few hundred years old. The route is older than the rock. The saddle was the lowest crossing for a hundred miles before the mountain existed and it is still the lowest crossing now that the mountain does, and so the herds came up over ground their grandmothers never stood on and simply continued, single file, strings of hundreds, following the heat because warm ground is bare ground and bare ground is walkable. Their trail runs along the fracture lines like a service corridor, braided into the ash by generations of hooves, the only line on this land not drawn by water. From a height — if the fog ever permitted a height — the whole economy would resolve at once: a blank page, writing only along the seams, void between, and through the writing, always, the herds, moving warm pocket to warm pocket in the dark, steaming faintly, their breath hanging below the fog like a second, lower lid the place has also built for itself.

That is who lives here. Not a community, exactly — the word is too settled. A procession, with a few bystanders who have learned to make a living off the fact that the procession never stops. The herds do not know the ground is new. The ground does not know the herds are old. Between those two ignorances, the whole arrangement runs — the works producing ground ahead of any need for it, the route crossing a place that is still being made, the mats coloring the channels, the ravens holding their lengths of steam, the foxes working the warm line, the small mammals awake in holes with heated floors, all of it unnamed, all of it unbuilt, all of it paying its bills in the only currency the upland accepts.
