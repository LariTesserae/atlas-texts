So what lives here has to be understood as living *in* that meal. Nothing on this shelf eats quiet things. Everything here eats the chord.

Begin small, because the small things came first and never left. The salt crust is threaded with a kind of lichen that is really a kind of ear — a felt of gray-green threads so fine that a breath of wind through it produces a whisper of harmonics, overtones the big stone cannot reach. Under a hand lens the threads are seen to be hollow, each one a stopped pipe the width of a hair. The lichen grows *toward* the bores in the columns, spreading its mat up the warm flutes like ivy toward a window, and the biologists who have studied it — there have been two, ever — argue about whether it is parasitic on the organ or whether the organ is parasitic on it, whether the lichen tunes the stone or the stone farms the lichen for its high harmonics. The argument is unresolved because the answer is both, and neither, and the lichen doesn't care.

The insects are louder than you expect. A salt-flat cricket the size of a thumbnail, pale as the sand, with a file on its hind leg that it draws across a rasp on its abdomen — except that its rasp is not on its abdomen but *in* it, a hollow chamber in its own body, a resonator, so that each cricket holds one note like the bores hold theirs, and a colony of them scattered across the floor is a hand-bell choir. They sing into the chord. Not against it — that is the thing to understand; nothing here sings against it. A cricket that stridulates out of key is, presumably, eaten, though no one has ever seen the eater, and the crickets that survive are legendarily, boringly, in tune. On the rare still days — the wind fails perhaps twice a decade, when some far-off pressure system goes slack — the crickets are the only sound on the shelf, and the locals say this is when you can hear the plain itself listening.

The birds arrived by accident and stayed on purpose. There is a swift, a dark flickering thing with absurdly oversized wing, that lives nowhere on earth but the horseshoe. It nests inside the palm-wide bores of the colonnade — chooses them, that's the marvel, chooses them by pitch. A swift will enter twenty bores in an afternoon and settle in the twenty-first, and if you test that bore you find it holds a note a half-step off the shelf's current tonic, a dissonance, a leaning tone, and the nest, when the wind rises, becomes a beat-frequency, a wah-wah-wah pulsing out of the stone face. The locals call the birds *tuners*. Whether the swifts are tuning the shelf or the shelf is tuning the swifts is a question of the same shape as the lichen question, and it has the same answer.

And then there are the faint ones, the locals proper, and here the description must slow down, because they will not be hurried.

They are human. That should be said plainly, because everything about them argues otherwise. Human, a small population, a few dozen, brown-skinned and salt-haired, dressed in woven fiber that glitters with crust. They live in the lee of the colonnade, in shallow rock-shelters at the base of the columns where the wind is broken to a bearable murmur. They do not farm. They keep goats — shaggy, unhurried animals with enlarged sinus cavities, whose bleat is a descending fifth and who will stand for hours in the outwash of the Notch with their eyes closed. The goats go to the wind to be played. The people do too.

You will notice their voices first, or rather you will fail to notice them, which is the same thing here. They do not speak at the shelf's pitch. They speak *under* it, in the cracks between chords, in the moment after a gust when the bores are still ringing and the wind has not yet returned — a fast, low, glottal talk, like water under ice, a language deliberately designed to be inaudible to the instrument. To talk above the chord, they believe, is to feed the shelf your words, and no one gives the shelf words for free. When a local does raise a voice into the open register — for grief, for courtship, for the naming of children, the three sanctioned occasions — the shelf answers. The bore nearest the speaker swells sympathetically. This is not superstition; it is aerodynamics. The colonnade resonates with the human throat the way a piano wire resonates with a soprano, and the locals have built everything around that fact: their ceremonies are duets with stone, the singer giving one phrase, the bore holding the answer, giving it back changed.

Their children are the strangest part, and the part they will not discuss with visitors. The young are born, like all children, screaming. Here the scream is a mistake — it feeds the shelf, and the shelf answers a newborn's cry so powerfully that the older children must be taken at once to the lowlands, below the haze, to be raised in the settlements at the foot of the massif where the drone is only a rumor in the ground. They come back up when their voices break. Adolescence here is not a metaphor. A boy or girl whose voice has settled into its adult register climbs the shoulder, walks out onto the booming sand, sings one long held note into the colonnade, and waits. If a column answers in sympathy — and one always does; there are hundreds of bores and every human voice has its resonant twin somewhere in that wall — that bore becomes the young person's bore, their address, their inheritance, their second throat. For the rest of their life they will return to it the way other people return to a childhood home. They will put their palm on it when they are frightened. They will be buried under it, and the wind will play them, the locals say, until the stone forgets, and the stone is slow to forget.

Which is the last thing, the thing that makes the description circular. The people here do not believe they are separate from the shelf. The goats, the swifts, the crickets, the lichen, the columns, the sand, the old dead under the stone — all of it, in their low under-ice language, has one name, a word visitors render as *the Choir*, and the Choir is what lives here. The human families are just the part of the Choir that can walk down to the salt camps and trade. The distinction between the instrument and its inhabitants is, on this shelf, a category error the locals find gently hilarious. The organist and the organ, they would tell you — if they would tell you anything — were never two things. The wind plays the stone and the stone plays the lichen and the lichen feeds the crickets and the crickets sing to the swifts and the swifts choose the bores and the bores shape the wind, and somewhere in that closed loop, faintly, almost deniably, stand the people: not the owners of the music, not even the audience, but one more set of hollow places in a summit full of them, holding their single breath, waiting their turn to be sung.
