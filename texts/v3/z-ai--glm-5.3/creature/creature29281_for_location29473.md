**Who lives in the Pan**

The oldest tenant is the film. It was here before the door, before the shaft, before the first survey bolt — pale, smooth, warm to the touch, slicking the wet rock and hanging in soft sleeves from the hottest cracks. It has no face and no fixed shape; it is a condition of the walls, the way condensation is. It eats the water's chemistry and drips back something faintly acid, and in this way it is quietly, patiently eating the room: a millimetre a year off the concrete plinths, dated in the survey book like birthdays. The film is the only resident that is winning. Everyone else is visiting by comparison.

On the film graze the multitudes. Pale isopods and amphipods in drifts so dense the lamp margins look furred, moving in tides when a headlamp sweeps them — a shift line, a tide line, a population that has never once been counted because counting it would be like counting rain. Springtiles hop across the muck like spilled pepper. Cave crickets hang from the ceiling, antennae longer than their bodies, sipping the condensation, patient as the drips they drink. None of them hurry. Nothing in the Pan hurries except the pumps, and the pumps are machinery, which is to say they are the only residents pretending not to be animals.

Above them all commute the bats. They own the shaft by ordinance — the hoist stands down 20:40 to 21:10 so the out-flight goes unbroken, a municipal government acknowledging, in paint and paperwork, that the airspace belongs to the colony first. They hang in the old natural rift and pay rent downward: guano cones banking up below the roost, and through those cones work the tineid moth larvae, a slow fire that never finishes burning. The bats import the surface; the moths process it; the processed subsidy feeds everything with too many legs to enumerate.

The moths themselves come in soft grey clouds to the lamps, and die there. Half of every fixture is shrouded in their bodies, swept clean each shift and clouded again by the next. Their wings collect beneath the light in small snowdrifts, and the drifts are weighed, seasonally, like tide tables — the bloom tracking the warm discharge a season behind, so that the moth snowfall in the Pan is a weather record of the water itself. The spiders know this. They have strung their sheet webs across the drip lines like fences along the walkway, marked with surveyor's tape so the crews duck past — the only hunting territory on the site formally registered, and by catch, the best-fed.

There is one green thing. A moss-film in the lamplight's penumbra, scrubbed monthly by the crews, regrown in three weeks, obedient as a lawn. It lives entirely on borrowed light. It is the most domestic resident and the most fragile — the only one that would notice if the lamps went out and did not come back.

And then there are the humans, the newest and briefest tenants, who behave like everything else down here but faster. They wear the heat as a tax — forty-five minutes in the warm, fifteen in the cooled container, salt tablets beside the kettle — and their boots dry on a warm pipe the way the crickets dry on the ceiling. They read the ceiling's weeping to forecast the city's weather six hours ahead. They can tell yesterday's rain overhead from tonight's. They keep a muster point, a laminated map, a sign-in board, and one absolute liturgy: when the drone of the pumps stops, everyone stands still and listens to what is underneath it — the water, actually moving, actually rising — and silence means get to the cage. It is a strange religion, worship of a noise, but down here the drone is the sound of the water being held back, and the held-back thing is the only god with an altar: a red line painted on the rock beside the sump, the water's patience, marked.

Between the lamp islands live the humans' instruments, which have begun to go native. The transformer hums in its cage at the edge of mains frequency, in unison with the pumps, so the whole chamber holds one long chord. The cable trays descend the shaft in black bundles like roots feeding a tree of light, and the tree is only half-grown — flanges, crates, scaffold, muck-car rails whose sleepers are going soft in the warm mud, everything built sweating, everything rusting downward from above, corrosion running inverted as if the chamber's weather were upside down. Even the infrastructure is becoming an ecosystem: drip loops off every cabinet like untied shoelaces, tarpaulin roofs over the switchgear, moths in the fixtures, moss in the penumbra, spiders at the drip lines.

So the census of the Pan, taken honestly, reads like this: one film, older than the room; several million grazers who think the film is sky; a colony of bats who own the front door; a standing population of moths who think the lamps are flowers and die of the error, and are counted; spiders at the fences; crickets on the ceiling drinking the room's slow rain; one obedient lawn of moss; a few trains passing three streets over and a world away, felt in the puddles every few minutes; and a handful of humans, seventy metres from daylight, keeping the pumps alive because the pumps keep the water down, keeping the moths swept because the moths are data, keeping the moss scrubbed because it always comes back, and keeping — above all — the sound. The constant loud river-sound of the drone and the drip and the insect static, which is the soundscape of last month and the month before, and which, when it stops, is the only fire alarm this cathedral has.
