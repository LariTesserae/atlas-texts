It is a machine that has been drinking its own tail for a century, and the whole station has organized itself around that slow circulation the way a river town organizes itself around a river. The pump is not the only loop here. The 6 that is a 9, the beat heard twice, the stair doubling back on itself — the place runs on returns.

What lives here first is the water, if water can be said to live, and in this room it comes close. It comes through the clay carrying dissolved iron and a sweetness of chalk, and where it stands in the two drowned galleries it holds a darkness so complete that the shrimp in it have given up everything the world once asked of them. There are two kinds. The smaller ones — translucent, blind, the size of a grain of rice held up to the light — graze the bacterial felt that grows on the flooded brick, moving in short sallies with their whole bodies bent to the task, like commas that have decided to become semicolons. The larger ones are paler still, almost architectural, with legs grown long and careful for walking on silt, and they are the station's aristocracy: they have been down here for ten thousand years, since before the city, since before the door, since the aquifer was a river they could remember. They do not know the pumps exist. The pumps are, to them, weather.

The shrimp come out of the galleries at intervals no one has charted, drawn along the wet lower courses of the glazed brick in a thin procession, and their destination is the dead pump. Inside its opened casing, the fungal threads have done something engineers would recognize if engineers came down here, which they do not: they have re-created the machine. Where copper wiring once ran, mycelium runs, white and branching, along the same channels, following the same vanes, as if the fungus had studied the schematic and understood that the form was good and only the material needed replacing. The impeller will never turn again, but it is not idle. Its bearings hold shrimp the way a reef holds crabs; its casing walls carry a nap of mold and bacterial felt thick enough to hold water in place, so that the dead pump has become an indoor tide pool, a wet machine, a heart that beats so slowly that only the ecosystem can hear it. The living pump knocks every fourteen seconds and the dead one grows by fractions of a millimeter a year, and between them they keep the room's two kinds of time.

Above the waterline, the economy thins but does not stop. Springtails graze the droplet-fringes on the pipes in numbers that suggest they think of themselves as livestock, not wildlife; mites work the dust that drifts down through the grate, which is the only import the station receives besides rain. And under the grate itself, at the top of the stair, in the one place where a thread of daylight survives the twelve steps' doubling-back, lives the station's largest animal: a spider of no remarkable species and remarkable circumstances, which has strung its web across the slot of sky. Falling things arrive through the grate — keys, coins, once a wedding ring, once an entire pigeon — and whatever is small enough or light enough or already dead enough lands in the web, and this is why the spider lives where it does, at the bottom of a shaft, in the dark, under a street: it has found the one address in the city where food falls from above at irregular intervals, like manna, like mail. The keys and coins fall past it and are lost; those are for the station, not the spider. The midges are for the spider. Midges breed in the cup-holes — the forty-one drip-drilled basins on the floor each hold a finger of stale water, and in that water, midge larvae have colonized every note of the accidental instrument. Each cup-hole is a pond the size of a thumbprint, and each one sings its one note when the drop strikes, and the midges require nothing more from life. They hatch, they drift toward the thread of ozone and wet iron that leads up the stair, and most of them end their brief careers in the web at the top, so that the spider's whole existence is a funnel: the room exhales midges, and the grate inhales them into the web, and the spider sits at the narrowest point, patient as the vault, doing what the station does — waiting at the bottom of a loop.

There are rats, of course, in the way there is rain; they come down when the street starves them and leave again, and they are visitors, not citizens. But there is also — and this is the thing the bronze plate's soft 9 seems to have known all along — one animal that cannot be classified as machine or tenant or visitor, and it is the other pump. Consider what it does. It has outlived its owners and its paperwork. It takes the water the city sits on and lifts it and gives it back. Its stroke knocks and the knock returns from the far wall, so that it answers itself — the only voice in the station that gets a reply. It warms the plinth it stands on by a degree or two, just enough that the springtails congregate there in winter, herded by a heat they cannot name. It is fed by the aquifer, it feeds the galleries, and every fourteen seconds it moves the entire room's air by a quantity too small to feel and too large for nothing to notice: the midges' flight paths bend toward the discharge side of the plinth, always; the fungal threads in the dead pump lean, over years, toward the living one, the way plants lean toward light.

So describe it plainly. The station is kept by a heart at four beats a minute, and it is inhabited by everything a heart can support at that pace: blind shrimp ten thousand years old, a spider that eats the sky's litter, midges born in musical puddles, a fungus rebuilding a dead engine from memory, and a warm machine with no owner that has never once, in all the time since the last man who understood it walked up the stair, missed its knock. The city above does not know it holds its breath. The breath is not empty. It is occupied.
