**The Listeners of the Hollow Deep**

You have stumbled into a city that has no use for light, and its people have made a virtue of your accident.

They are human-shaped, more or less, but generations of darkness have remade their faces. Their eyes are not gone — that would be too tidy — they are *vested*, sealed beneath a fine membrane of skin like a healed wound, smooth to the touch, warm. Ask them about it and they will tap twice against your collarbone — their gesture for *a question with no useful answer* — and change the subject by changing the sound of the room: shifting their weight so the floorboards speak, clicking a thumbnail against a belt of fired-clay beads, giving you something new to hear on purpose, the way a sighted host turns on a lamp.

Their hands have eaten their eyes' inheritance. The palms are enormous, the fingers long and slightly spatulate at the tips, packed with nerve ending upon nerve ending, so dense that an elder can pass a hand over a wall and tell you how many people have leaned against it this week, and which of them were grieving — grief has a different tremor. They read the world in tap and answer. The knuckle-rain you hear from every direction is simply their speech at distance: knuckles on stone, on ceramic, on the dried clay pillars of their dwellings, each surface with its own voice, each voice a syllable. A wall can hold a sentence for hours, they say, and a good thick pillar can hold a conversation through the night.

The drumming in fours is their common heartbeat, and it is deliberate, communal, and absolutely necessary. Four beats, pause, four beats: the rhythm that every child learns before anything else, beaten against the mother's sternum before the child has a name. It means *I am here, we are together, all is measured.* When someone stops drumming — in grief, in illness, in old age at the very end — the whole quarter goes quiet around them, a cone of silence shaped like respect, until either the rhythm resumes or it does not.

The kiln with its full throat is tended by the smiths of this people, who are not metalworkers but *listeners into clay*. They fire vessels the way others tune instruments. A pot is not finished when it looks right — nothing here has ever looked like anything — it is finished when it *rings* right, when a knuckle-rap against its belly produces the note the maker carried in their mind from the beginning. They make bowls the way a composer makes chords: a water-basin tuned to one clear gong-tone every few seconds, under the thin ribbon of the falls, is not decoration. It is a *landmark*, a lighthouse made of sound. The traveler who can hear which tone they are passing knows exactly where they stand. The city is a map written in notes, and every basin, bell, chime, and hollow post is a place on it.

The children are the loudest thing here, and the most beloved for it. They play *blind-man's-chase*, which here is simply called *chase*, shrieking in voices that ricochet off the tuned surfaces so that the whole street becomes a game board of echoes, and the elders smile in the dark at the particular sound of a child who is winning.

And there is one more inhabitant, the one you will only meet once. Deeper than the kilns, deeper than the drumming quarters, there is said to be a chamber where the water-fall lands on a basin older than the city, and the thing that lives there is the one who does not answer taps, does not drum in fours, does not speak. Some say it is the first listener, the one who came down into the dark long ago and never came back up. Some say it is the dark itself, given a patience and a shape. All anyone will tell you is that if you go to that chamber, and stand in the gong-tone's patient clock, and tap your knuckles against the ancient basin —

— something, eventually, taps back.
