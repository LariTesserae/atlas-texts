The census at the Bell is taken at dawn, by ear. You cannot count this place by looking at it: fog is the usual weather, half the residents are the exact color of their backgrounds, and the roll is called in the dark besides. The mountain speaks its note — twenty short breaths of low tone, felt in the soles before the ears — and the pocket answers: kettle-bird first, exactly on the pitch, then the frogs resuming like rain accelerating, then everything at once, the way an orchestra stops tuning and starts playing. For one hour this is the loudest place in the mountains, and the loudness is the census. No one is counted who does not answer. In ten thousand years, so far as anyone knows, no one has failed to.

Begin with the twelve elders, because everyone else begins with them: the trees are the address. Spaced like figures in a procession, wind-corkscrewed, the biggest a girth of ten joined hands and leaning like a man listening, they are the ceiling of the world's forest — every other tree on earth is, from here, downhill. Each is a town. Banker plants stack in the crotches like rooftop cisterns, and each cistern is a pond the size of a cup with a nation in it: midge larvae, drift, a whole hydrology in a teacup. Ferns crowd the forks; cold orchids open in the drizzle hour because that is the only hour the bees can be relied on to fly. The elders stand on their own dead — the peat beneath them is largely their own fall, laid down letter by letter since the ice — and the biggest still wears pegs at arm-chosen heights, where generations of hands climbed up and took flowers or plumes and carried them down. The tree bears its cropping the way a church door bears hands.

On the lawn, the commoners. Sedges in ranks; moss like green felt; the stream crossing in one bright seam. And teeth: sundews, glistening, open all day, because the peat is ten thousand years old and has never once fed a vegetarian properly. The lawn keeps a small carnivore budget — it pastures the midges the fog delivers and then eats its own flock; midges here are less a resident than a currency. Underneath, the peat mice run their economy in tunnels warm as loaves, and in the boom years the lawn itself moves at dusk, sedge-tips trembling with population, while the hawk-eagle hangs on the lip's updraft reading the tremor, wings shut, never flapping — the one predator whose prey announces itself by existing. Two chicks in a boom. In a bust the eagle commutes to the far ridges, the sedge keeps its afternoons, and the peat files the difference: loud years lie down thick, quiet ones thin. The archive knows feast and famine by depth.

The sedge's clerk is the rail — the kettle-bird, the valley calls it, naming things here the way the peat files them, by their material: peat mice, woolly bees, moss frogs. The rail is flightless, which at this altitude is not a disability but a budget — flight costs twice, and its ancestors struck the wings from the ledger and kept the whistle. It works the sedge from first light and sounds like a kettle coming to a boil, and sometimes like a person. That is the part no one can settle. Somewhere in the bird's repertoire are calls first made by human mouths — a meal-call, a warning, a child's two-note game with no object but repetition — dead words in a living throat. Whether the pilgrims learned the bird or the bird learned the pilgrims, the whistles are now community property, and the community that shared them has gone down the ladder for good. The rail holds the deed alone. It is the first to answer the dawn, exactly on the pitch — a clerk never late for the mountain's bell, the last speaker of a language that now consists mostly of weather.

The rest of the roll is small, and smallness here is not modesty but the air's rationing made flesh. The moss frogs, a fingernail apiece, tick like water drops, so the ground sounds wet even when it is dry: they are the after-drip given bodies, the bowl's slow clearing of its throat distributed into a hundred throats. Possibly the frogs cannot tell themselves from the seeps either, which may be the point. The moth the grey of a lichen flake opens and closes its wings on a stone like a slow hand — the local metronome, applauding at its own tempo, and doing its laundry, since nothing up here dries except in sun. The woolly bees blunder the orchids at half-speed, humming like distant saws: everything costs twice, so the bees are built like rams and fly like negotiations — nothing elegant, all of it funded. The whole fauna banks on the same margin — the dry-season frost that silvers the lawn at dawn and is gone by mid-morning, never deep enough to kill. One degree of mercy, distributed daily. And nothing here owns a thing that needs drying: wool, felt, moss, shell. The wardrobe is written by the rule.

The armored ones are the strangest. Red crabs climb the elders at dusk to eat the flowers, pinching with a sound like a struck match. Their ancestors were stream crabs; the glacier left, the stream stayed, and ten thousand years of upward walking turned a water-dweller into a tree-dweller — the flood that never came back was replaced, claw-hold by claw-hold, by cisterns. Now each crab keeps a rooftop pond in the banker plants and pays its rent in orchids, and at dusk the trees click softly, like small doors being opened by someone with keys.

And the choir sleeps inside the instrument. The headwall's flutes — the same organ-pipes that sound the dawn note — are also bedrooms; the swiftlets pour out of the crevices shrieking, echo-clicking, a stream that runs upward, because the pipes house the voices and the voices leave through the pipes every morning and come home every night. They see by shouting: every cry returns to them a few milliseconds late, answered by their own walls. It is the bowl's habit, correspondence at every scale. The swiftlets talk to themselves across the width of a crevice; the far cliff across the cloud gap, which no one has ever seen, returns every shout five seconds late, so that the sky answers you. Nothing here moves unheard — which means nothing here is ever entirely unheard by itself.

Then the people, who live here the way ash lives in soil. The deepest of them is a portrait: the Listening Seat, a hollow polished into stone by hips, facing the lip, worn by someone whose stillness outlasted their name. Sitting was the profession. Whoever they were, they climbed year upon year to face the dawn and let the note pass through their backbones half a breath before their ears, and the stone took their outline the way a river takes a channel. The Seat still fits; hips are standard issue. Around the hearth-ring, charcoal generations deep, grows to this day a ring of plants that like ash — the garden ghost, the people's cooking composted into a flowerbed, the green signature outliving the hand. In the peat, filed soft as chalk, a bone flute: someone once tried to make the note portable, to carry the mountain's voice down in a pocket. The mountain declined to be portable; the flute is the paperwork of that refusal. And at the lip, on a peg, the only metal in the place — a small copper bell, green as the moss, rung by no hand, only by the biggest afternoon winds, when it sends one bright note up inside the roar, a half-step off the mountain's pitch. Half-steps are the human half of music, the shelf where our scales and the mountain's disagree. The bell is the pilgrimage's last word, and the wind, industrious and neutral, keeps both sides of the argument aired.

Whether anyone lives here in the valley's sense — sleeps here, means to die here — the answer is no, with one living exception: the ladder. It is kept alive, rung by rung, the bottom rungs young green wood and the top worn to the grain, repaired by hands the valley still supplies most years, with more faith in some decades than others. It is the youngest living thing anyone ever built here, a wooden creature that molts from the bottom. Someone climbs for two days, comes over the lip with their breath whistling — a sound the rail answers, on the chance it is the mountain — sits the Seat through one dawn, replaces what rot has claimed, checks the copper bell's peg, and goes down ahead of the noon cloud. Residence here has never required sleeping. It has required arriving, holding still where stillness is a skill, and leaving the place improved by exactly one rung.

The deepest resident never answers the roll, because it is the roll. The peat files bones, charcoal, pollen, the flute, the pilgrim decades and the pilgrim silences alike. This is a place with two directions: up, and down into the record. The cloud hauls itself upward at noon, the swiftlets pour upward at dawn, the river pours upward into sky and is shredded back to cloud before it has fallen a hundred meters — and everything else settles, stem and wing and hip and note, down into the black springy file. Nothing is lost here; it is filed; which means the census never closes. Everyone who ever held this address is still on the books. At night, when the cold comes down like a lid, the frogs tick on — the archive's clock — and on the strange nights the cloud sea glows from within, soundless, and the bowl sits lit above its own ocean, all its tenants asleep on their files.

So ask again who lives at the Bell, and watch the categories leak. A tree that is also a village; a bird that is also an archive; frogs that are also a weather; a stone that is also a portrait; a ladder that is also a tenant; a dead garden that is also a present tense. Count long enough and you find you are counting one body. The valley said it from the start: the bell is a sound. The sound is the resident, and the pocket is its flesh — trees for a gallery, crabs for hands, swiftlets for breath, the eagle for patience, the rail for a mouth full of borrowed names, the Seat for an ear, the peat for memory, the frost's one degree of mercy for a pulse. Every dawn it is born in twenty short breaths and answered as one voice. The bowl is finished, and has been for ten thousand years; what lives at the Bell is the daily proof of it — renewed every morning, and standing at the lip in green copper, one small half-step of argument against the whole design.
