What lives here is the water, and for now that is nearly the whole census.

It arrived the way a tenant arrives at a house still smelling of its builder — into a place that had never held anything but tools and echoes and the four rhythms of chisels. It came in as a rope of river, warm from a heated landscape, and it is already learning the room the way any new tenant learns a house: where the light falls, where the corners are, which walls take warmth and which give it back. It is settling in with unusual thoroughness, because its settling *is* the sound of the place. The roar at the inflow arch is not the river's voice anymore; it is the room's. The room refuses to soften it, being young, and the water refuses to quiet, being new, and together they have made the loudest interior in the city.

But look closer and the tenant is not one thing. The water is a congregation. The warm bath-shoulder near the arch; the clarified gold at the far side, which arrived last and carries the least silt; the thin veils of settling dust that fall through it hour by hour, polishing the room's first silence. It came in carrying a single green twig — the room's first possession that nobody built — and three seeds that struck a silt pocket on the second step and could not wait. They have already cracked, already shown white rootlets, already begun the small patient work of becoming the first things in the city to grow *down here* rather than up there. By the second or third flood they will be reeds, and by the tenth something no one planned, and by the hundredth the room will have its own vegetation the way an old well grows its own moss — not designed, but inevitable, like dust in a house.

And the rumor of green at the sunlit edge — thinner than algae, more suggestion than organism — is the vanguard of everything that follows. Give this room three seasons and the water will be full of lives that found it the way lives find everything: a floating mote becoming a mote with a motion, a beetle rowing in from some vent-pipe, larvae hanging under the bright surface like ornaments. The room was built before its tenants existed. That is the strangest thing about it. The builders made a house and then let the river furnish it with inhabitants — microscopic, seed-borne, incidental — a whole slow civilization arriving one flood at a time, none of it ordered, all of it expected. The emptiness now is not vacancy. It is the first day of a tenure.

The only human who lives here lives here the way a lighthouse keeper lives in a lighthouse: in visits, at the hinges of the day. The gatekeeper comes at dusk and dawn, down thirty-one treads with a lamp, checks the counterweight harness, reads the gauge the way one reads a letter — water at the third mark, holding, climbing — and goes. That person is the room's consciousness, arriving twice daily, and also its only audience. Everyone else in the city will be a tenant without knowing it. They will drink this room from the public tap in the plaza shade, will cook with it, will wash their children in it, will never once stand in the moving lace of light on the vault or hear the cedar gate groan under pressure. The room is a house whose tenants drink it.

And there are the ghosts — not dead, only gone. The builders are the room's previous inhabitants, the ones who lived here before the water did. Four masons whose chisels left four distinct handwritings on the treads. A hand that steadied itself in wet mortar and left four fingerprints, then never came back, so that a person's touch is now the oldest organic thing in the room — older than the algae, older than the seeds, older than the twig. The ochre level-lines, the string-guides, the knot someone left tied because there was no reason to untie it: these are the belongings the first tenants didn't clear out when they left. The water is washing them away hand-width by hand-width, and that too is part of the arrangement. The marks of making are supposed to drown. The room was built to be drowned.

So: what lives here is a tenant made of river, warm and loud on its first day, being counted as it counts itself up the gauge; three seeds that got here ahead of everything; a green rumor; a twig; a keeper with a lamp; the fading fingerprints of the makers; and, overhead and oblivious, a whole city that will live in this room its entire life without ever once descending into it.
