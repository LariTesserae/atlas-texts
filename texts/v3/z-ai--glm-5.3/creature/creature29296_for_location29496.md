**The inhabitants.**

Start with the ones you can count, because there are so few of them. The dipper is one — the only resident who commutes. It lives on the outflow stream the way a person lives on a ground-floor flat with the door open to the street, and it works the water like a trade: in under the surface, along the bed, turning stones with its chin to see what the day has delivered, then up onto a rock to bow, blink, and drip. It has no interest in the city above the bank. The settlement, to the dipper, is a watershed — a machine for delivering clear cold water and the larvae that live in it — and it is not wrong. Every citizen here eventually leaks.

The frogs are the next you'd name, though you will almost certainly never see one. They are citizens of the walls rather than the streets: sealed into the sphagnum cushions, cold-hardy to the point of blasphemy, some of them frozen solid — ice in their blood, their hearts stopped, their cells packed with sugar like houses sandbagged against a flood — for months at a stretch. For most of the year a frog here is not an animal but a deposit. Then the warm weeks come, the three or four the place allows, and they thaw all at once, and the dusk chorus rises out of the moss from frogs you would have sworn were not there — a clicking, chuckling, hundreds-voiced sound, the only crowd noise the settlement ever makes that isn't insects. It is the loudest the place gets. It lasts days. Then the frost resumes its rounds and the frogs go back into the bank, and the bank accepts them.

The voles, technically, live here too, but they live here mostly as plumbing. Their runs under the carpet collapse after them and become seepage channels; the rhizome net follows the channels; the lanes follow the seeps. A vole's chief contribution to the city is architectural and posthumous. This is a place where even the dead have jobs.

Then the crowds. The midges and the springtails — these are the ones the settlement actually *assembles*, and it assembles them on purpose, with heat and with a lie. The arums are the only landlords here that spend warmth, burning a degree or two of metabolism in their spathes to keep the interior above freezing, and they advertise with the honest stink of carrion, because a dead thing is exactly what a midge is built by instinct to investigate. Inside the domes, all winter, the only warm rooms in the basin: still air, humidity, springtails hopping through it like sparks off a flint, midges swirling in loose columns that are, if you want to be accurate about it, the entire fauna of the settlement gathered in one room. The arum takes a fee in pollen transport. The insects take warmth, a false corpse, and a place to stand while the frost walks the streets outside. Nobody is deceived in the long run, and nobody leaves.

And then there are the ones who are harder to classify as residents, because they may be the residence.

The moss is a citizen, or is it the ground? It is the soil, but it is alive, so it is the only place in the world where the ground pays taxes — it holds the water the fog delivers, releases it in ordered instalments, roofs over anything that falls, and absorbs the dead into the walls of the city within a season, so completely that nothing here ever becomes a ruin except the stone. The sphagnum terraces are citizens who have chosen, collectively, to be infrastructure. The horsetails — the old kind, silica-ribbed, jointed, creaking their wooden tension each warm morning — are the colonnade and the thing the colonnade is made of, simultaneously, and no one has ever satisfactorily said whether their growth is fast or merely impatient, whether they came in after the ice with their bags packed or were under it the whole time, waiting with their shoes on.

The cold lives here. That has to be said plainly, because the cold behaves like a resident: it keeps regular hours, it makes rounds, it comes down the lanes each night like a wagon on its circuit, parking in the low spots, pruning anything that overgrew since yesterday. It is the architect — it draws the plan nightly, and the growth executes by day, and the fog mediates between them, refusing both a full victory. Whether the cold is a person here or merely weather with tenure, the result is the same: the streets belong to it, and everything else lives off the margins it leaves.

And the plan. If you sit still long enough — an hour, two — the question stops being *who built this* and becomes something more uncomfortable: whether the distinction matters. There is no one here. There is no bone, no cordage, no cut stone, no tool. And yet there are lanes that run level and connect places to places, terraces stepped like amphitheater seating, weep-holes engineered into rock, domes lit warm in the frost at dawn like windows. The sufficient explanation is geology, plumbing, water following joints, cold following water, life following both. The sufficient explanation does not feel sufficient when you are standing in it.

The likeliest reading — and the place offers no other — is that the inhabitant is the settlement. Not the frogs, not the dipper, not the crowds at the warm domes, who are all more like tenants, lodgers, foot traffic. The inhabitant is the whole waterlogged black-green thing: a single resident with moss for a body, rhizomes for a gut, horsetails for a spine, frost for a conscience, and fog for breath, occupying a basin the way an animal occupies a den. It wakes at dawn when the thaw-halos bloom, works through its condensation and its rockfall and its one loud week of frogs, and goes to sleep each night with the cold walking its streets on its behalf.

It has no bones to leave. When it dies, it will be absorbed into itself within a season, and the only ruins will be the stone's — which were there before it moved in, and will be there after, still startled, still fresh, with the edges not yet rounded, as if the whole affair had happened in the time it takes a glacier to lift its foot.
