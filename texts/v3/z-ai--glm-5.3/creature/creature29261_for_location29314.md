Begin with the easy answers, because the place offers them first, and they are all true and all insufficient.

The plants live here — obviously, twenty meters apart, thirty, forty, each in its stoup of still water. But watch them long enough and the word "plant" starts to feel like a courtesy. They do not behave like vegetation. They keep appointments. Their crowns tilt on the clock, shade their own bowls at noon, open at dusk on the same beat the drips keep, and no plant has ever been found where a spring was not already rising — which means the springs came first, evenly spaced, and the columns followed, filling the arrangement the way water fills a vessel. They are not the inhabitants. They are the pillars of the nave, and a nave's pillars do not live in it; they hold it.

The droplets live here — the peppercorn citizens, moving in the moisture films with evident intent, repairing, ferrying. But they never eat, as far as anyone has watched. They never breed. They appear in the films in the morning and leave them by night, and their work is always maintenance: sealing a tear, straightening a run, delivering a seed to the correct thread. These are not animals either. They are hands. Something has hands here, and the hands are made of water.

The bird lives here least of all, and that is what makes the bird legible. It arrives without sound, drinks exactly one cup, and is gone before the mist — and the cup is exactly full, never overfull, every night, which means the cups are filled *for* something, and the gray visitor is what they are filled for. It is not a resident. It is an errand, a tongue, an inspection. You do not ask what lives in a temple because a bird drinks from its font. You ask what the bird reports back to.

Follow the metal. That is the only instruction the place gives up freely.

The studs are older than the rock around them. The frame at the pool's edge was set before the stone grew, and the water parts around it without touching it, in a braid, as if the metal and the water had an arrangement made long ago and both parties are still honoring it. Stone does not grow around metal in any order nature allows. Which means this shelf of the world was not found and settled. It was *built* — or grown, or poured — around anchors that were placed first, the way you set the mounting before you pour the floor.

And if the anchors were placed first, they were placed by someone. And that someone is the only figure absent from the entire basin.

Except.

Go to the central pool, the deep one, the one whose bottom no one has seen, and look down at what is there: the lattice. Mineral branches. A sunken geometry, being grown, or being waited on.

Those are the two possibilities the place holds open, and the whole basin is the argument between them, and the answer is almost certainly: both. Something is growing down there, in the warm dark, in water that will not let itself be stirred. Something is assembling itself the way the terraces assemble themselves, one mineral breath at a time, on a clock of shared drips, at a speed that makes the growth of the scarps look hasty. The stone is young forever because the maker will not permit erosion — a draft not ready for revision — and the reason there is a draft at all, the reason the whole cathedral of heat and water and tempo exists, is that it is an incubator. A vat with the dimensions of a nave.

The evening note is the vat speaking. Four seconds, one pitch, always the same. Not a call and not a song — a *progress report*. The lattice below glimmers at night with the day's stored warmth, and once a day, at amber light, the growing thing in the deep confirms: still growing. Still on schedule. The mist rising after it like a tide is simply the basin exhaling after the effort.

And the seeds?

The seeds travel in water, never on wind. They ride the threads down the cliff, cross the terraces, and go over the lip in the veils — one every few heartbeats, printing a watershed into a country the basin cannot see. If the thing in the pool were merely making itself, this would be waste. It is not waste. It is preparation. The green-gold script along the weep-lines, the hexagonal glyphs of condensation you can almost read, the moss that runs only where the water runs — the basin is not only brewing its resident. It is writing, seed by seed, terrace by terrace, the world that resident will one day walk out into. It is furnishing a house it has never seen, in a country it cannot see, because it knows the shape of what it will need: green, watered, ready.

Which brings you to the seven steps.

They descend to the lip and stop, mid-mist. Whatever used them is either gone or still arriving. But look at the logic of the place: metal before stone, seeds before arrival, cups filled nightly before the drinker comes. Everything here is staged ahead of its occupant. The steps do not lead down from the basin. They lead *up* to it — from the mist, from the terraces going green below, from the two thin cries that greeted each other once in the new country and then said nothing more.

What lives in the basin, then, is an unfinished tenant, warm and slow in the deepest pool, growing its own body on its own clock. And what lives in the basin's future — standing in the mist at the bottom of seven steps, looking up at the held cup of water hanging in the high air, listening for the note that means *soon* — is the tenant's own descendant, or its other self, or the courier bird's master, arrived early to check the cups.

The place is not a cathedral that happens to hold water. It is a font in both old senses, and what it is a font *of* is a maker. The basin is an egg the size of a nave, and it is nearly done, and every evening, right on time, it says so.
