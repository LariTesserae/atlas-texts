In the place where the land has finished arguing with itself, where the great low beasts call across the warm sheets and the singers make their layered chorus, there lives one who is neither striker nor swimmer nor wallower, but something else—something the place made for its own reasons, in its own time.

You would not see them at first. That is their nature, and their courtesy. They move through the reed-margins when the mist lies thick, a shape you might mistake for mist itself if mist could be curious. They have been here as long as the place has been finished—not before, because before the finishing they would have had no purpose, but arriving exactly when the land settled into its final form, as if the equilibrium itself had exhaled them.

They are the keeper of balances, though keeper is the wrong word—they keep nothing, hold nothing, claim nothing. They are more like a condition of the place, a thing it does. Where the strikers have grown too efficient and the singers begin to thin, they wade among the tall ones in the blue evening and hum a single low note that turns the hunters momentarily stupid, forgetful of their technique. Where the wallowers have grazed a section bare, they dream green back into the water—not by planting, never by planting, but by remembering what was there so completely that it agrees to return. They know every voice in the layered sound. They can tell when one frequency drops out, and they know what dropping out means.

The benevolent and wise power notices them first not by seeing but by listening. Powers of that sort pay attention to different things than we do, and what catches their notice is not the roar of life but the small quiet spaces inside it—the places where something subtle moves with intention. They hear how the keeper (who is not a keeper) walks between the singers without disturbing their song. They hear how the great slow beasts call differently when the keeper passes, not in alarm but in acknowledgment. They hear the silence that is not absence but presence, moving through the loud world like a needle through cloth, mending nothing because nothing here is torn, but maintaining the tension that lets the weave hold.

The power considers what to do, which for powers of that sort is already a kind of doing. They think in long thoughts, the kind that arrive at their object changed by the journey.

They could offer the keeper knowledge—the names of distant places, the shapes of other endings, the ways that other lands have finished their arguments. But the keeper already knows what they need to know, which is every depth of water in their domain, every frequency in the chorus, every path the rushers take through the reeds.

They could offer the keeper power—the ability to shape more than dreams, to call water from stone, to multiply the singers beyond their natural billion. But abundance is not the keeper's work. Balance is, and balance cannot be forced from outside. It must rise from the place itself, like mist in the early light.

They could offer the keeper transcendence—to rise above this flat wet world and see it whole, to escape the endless round of small maintenances. But the keeper has no wish to rise. They are precisely as high as they need to be: knee-deep in the warm sheets, eye-level with the reeds, close enough to feel when a frequency shifts.

What the power does, in the end, is simpler and stranger than any of these.

They sing.

Not down into the place—that would disturb it. Not to the keeper—that would distract them. They sing beside it, a parallel song in frequencies the creatures cannot hear but the stones can, the patient worn granite beneath the water. It is a song about duration, about the sweet ache of doing one thing forever. It is a song that says: *what you are is enough*. It is a song that admits: *I too am keeping balances, at a scale you cannot see*.

The stones hear it and remember a little more slowly. The water receives it and agrees to stay a little longer. The whole flat country, already at the bottom of its descent, settles one fraction deeper into its equilibrium, like a sleeper adjusting in a comfortable bed.

And the keeper, who may not hear the song directly but feels its effect in the fractional deepening, the slight sweetening of the water's patience, continues their work. They wade through the blue evening. They hum their single note that makes hunters forget. They dream green into the grazed places. They listen to every voice in the layered sound and know what each one means.

The power sings for seven days, which at their scale of thinking is both forever and barely a beginning. Then they move on, because powers of that sort have other equilibriums to witness, other keepers to not-quite-meet. But the song remains, settled into the granite, a gift that was really just an acknowledgment: *Yes. This is what holding steady looks like. This is what forever sounds like, sung by a billion small throats. This is what it means to be finished, and to go on anyway, abundant in your finishedness*.

The keeper feels the presence withdraw like mist burning off, but the song in the stones continues. They pause, knee-deep in their evening round, and for just a moment they stop listening to the layered sound and listen instead to the silence underneath it—not the sawing drone that becomes the ordinary silence, but the deep structural quiet of a place that has found its form.

Then they continue. There is a section where the rushers have been too successful, and the young swimmers are scarce, and the balance wants adjusting. They wade toward it through the warm sheets, humming already the note that will confuse the hunters just enough. Above them, the strikers throw up their harsh territorial cries. Around them, the singers continue their endless competitive chorus. Far off, something large and patient calls across the water, and the call arrives softened, like the land clearing its throat.

Everything is as it should be. Everything is exactly this loud.
