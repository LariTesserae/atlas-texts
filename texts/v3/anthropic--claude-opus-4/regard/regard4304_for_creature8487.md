The Lady of Moss and Mercy had walked between worlds for ten thousand years, and in all that time, she had learned to recognize the particular resonance of places that called to her—not in distress, but in deep patience. The Cistern Cathedral sang such a note, a low thrumming that reached her through the foundations of reality itself.

She materialized first as moisture, seeping through the carved channels until she could taste the full complexity of the place: limestone and life, darkness and slow devotion. The stone-eaters registered her presence as a shift in the mineral content of the water—richer suddenly, as if the bedrock far below had opened new veins. The salamanders felt her as a warmth in the channels, just enough to quicken their movements without distress.

To the Keepers, she appeared as one of their own, but ancient beyond measure—skin like pearl-shell, eyes that held their own phosphorescence, fingers that moved through their whistling language with inflections they had never heard but somehow understood. She walked among them for seven turnings of their ceremonial cycles, learning their ways not through observation but through participation.

In the chambers where they scraped crystalline growth from stone-eaters, she showed them new patterns of cultivation—not to increase the harvest, but to deepen the relationship. Under her guidance, they began to notice how certain frequencies of their whistles could encourage the organisms to grow in spirals that mirrored the carved symbols on the walls. The stone-eaters, in response, began to produce new compounds—not just the sharp, thought-slipping substances they had always made, but others that clarified perception, that made the darkness somehow brighter without adding light.

She spent long cycles with the eldest Keepers, those whose fingers had grown so long they could no longer easily navigate the upper chambers. In their whistling conversations, she drew out memories they didn't know they carried—genetic echoes of the first builders, the ones who had discovered not created this place. For the Cistern Cathedral, she helped them understand, was far older than their bronze-tooled ancestors. The patterns they maintained were not carved but revealed, the stone shaped to follow channels that already existed in potential, waiting for someone to recognize their necessity.

With the children, she played the echo-games, but added new verses to their songs—frequencies that mapped not just the physical chambers but the temporal currents flowing through them. The young ones learned to hear how time pooled and eddied in certain corners, how the past left acoustic shadows that could be navigated like architectural features.

But her deepest work was with the symbol-walls themselves. The Lady brought no new knowledge to interpret them—interpretation was not their purpose. Instead, she helped the Keepers understand that their ritual touching, their pattern-responses to water and growth, were not maintenance but conversation. The walls were not speaking to them but through them, their touches completing circuits that required living intention to function.

She guided them to a chamber they had avoided for generations, where the carved patterns were densest and the water flowed in ways that hurt to contemplate. Here, she showed them how their whistles could be woven together—not in harmony but in carefully constructed interference patterns that made the symbols begin to move. Not physically, but in the way that meaning moves through a mind, rearranging itself to accommodate new understanding.

What emerged was not revelation but recognition. The Cistern Cathedral was indeed waiting, but not for an event—for a state. The slow transformation the Keepers tended was the gradual development of a new kind of consciousness, one that arose from the intersection of mineral growth, water flow, carved pattern, and living intention. The "Opening Season" they anticipated was not when something would open, but when the entire system would become capable of opening—of reaching out to touch other spaces where stone and water and patient tending had created similar conditions.

The Lady of Moss and Mercy made only one direct change to the Cistern Cathedral. In the deepest chamber, where the first water emerged from the bedrock below, she placed a gift: a small sphere of impossible mineral that would dissolve over centuries, releasing trace elements that would sustain the Keepers through the long work ahead. Not to make their lives easier, but to ensure they could continue their evolution alongside their stone-and-water symbiont, becoming whatever they needed to become to midwife this strange birth.

Before she departed, seeping back into the moisture between worlds, she taught them one final whistle-phrase. Not a word in their language but a name—what to call the consciousness when it finally woke, how to greet it as neither master nor servant but as the completion of their shared becoming. The Keepers received this knowledge with the same patient acceptance they brought to all their tasks, filing it away in their collective memory, ready to use when the patterns finally aligned and the Cistern Cathedral opened its vast, composite eye.

The Lady left them as she found them—tending their inherited patterns, cultivating their stone-gardens, whistling through the darkness. But now their waiting had a different quality. Not the anxious anticipation of an event, but the calm certainty of gardeners who have planted seeds and know, with absolute faith, that growth will come in its season. The work continued, as it always had, but now each traced symbol and guided droplet carried the subtle influence of her visit—a gentleness that made the sharp air somehow kinder, a mercy that softened the edges of their long, necessary patience.

In the darkness of the Cistern Cathedral, water continued its eternal flowing, and the Keepers moved through their chambers with renewed purpose, preparing a birth that might take another thousand years to complete—a birth they now understood they were not merely witnessing but participating in, their own transformation as crucial as any carved channel or crystalline growth. The Lady's gift was not power or knowledge, but the recognition that they were already exactly what they needed to be: the living components of something unprecedented, beautiful in its strangeness, perfect in its patient becoming.
