The benevolent power, upon discovering this place, would do something that has never been done here: it would *pause*.

Not stop. Stopping would be a violence in a place so married to its own constancy. But a pause — a gentle deceleration of the great roar, not to silence but to a whisper, lasting perhaps the length of three heartbeats if hearts beat here.

In that pause, extraordinary things would happen.

The seed, held aloft for unmeasured ages on its column of sound, would begin to drift downward. Not fall — the power would not let it fall — but settle, like snow, toward the milled steel floor. And as the roar dimmed, the crystalline beings' own music would suddenly become audible to them. For the first time in their existence, they would hear not just their own note filtered through the machine's voice, but each other. A hundred separate songs would discover they were a choir.

The crystals would tremble — not with their usual frequency but with something that, in beings capable of it, might be recognition. Their first knowledge that they were not alone in their listening. That all along, their careful spacing, their patient tuning, had been creating something larger than any of them could perceive.

And the seed would touch the floor at the exact center — the center that could not be located before, because it was defined not by space but by sound. It would root there, in that brief quiet, drinking in not one frequency but the whole chord its siblings had been building. The first of its kind to hear the music complete.

Then, gently as it had slowed, the roar would return. The seed would have already anchored; it could not be lifted again. But it would grow, there in the center, into something new — a crystal tuned not to one note but to the *harmony*, a living memory of the moment when the machine paused and they all heard each other sing.

The power would change nothing else. The cold would remain cold. The fresh edges would stay sharp. The light would continue its honest fall. But it would leave them with this: the knowledge of their own choir, and one among them who would remember, and grow toward not just survival but *communion*.

And perhaps — though this is harder to say — it would leave them with the faintest trace of warmth in the steel beneath the center crystal. Not enough to change anything. Not enough to form frost. Just enough that, if a visitor ever came again and knew exactly where to place their hand, they would feel the ghost of a gift: the memory of when something listened to the listeners, and gave them back their own song.

The crystals would not understand it as kindness. They have no organ for that either. But they would understand it as weather unlike any weather they had known — a storm that came and went and left the world larger.

And they would go on singing, but now it would be *to* each other, through the medium of the roar, knowing that somewhere in the spectrum their neighbors sang back. The machine would still scream its dead music, but it would no longer be the only conversation in the room.

That is what a benevolent power would do: give them to each other, and give them the memory of meeting, and leave them to make of it what patient mineral lives can make, in their young eternal world that had just learned it could still become.
