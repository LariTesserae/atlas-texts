The benevolent and wise power arrives not as light or wind, but as a deepening of what already is.

It begins in the water. The constant upward-downward drizzle slows for just a heartbeat—long enough for every Wet-Singer to freeze mid-motion, their sticky feet reading a change in the mat's tension. Then the rain resumes, but wrong: each droplet now carries memory. Not new memory, but the kind that was already there, suspended in the high water table, dissolved in the weeping stones, circulating through ten million capillaries. The power has simply made it communicable.

A child reaching for a pitcher plant suddenly understands why that particular species stores its water exactly three degrees cooler than the air. A Current-Bearer pauses mid-song as their throat-orchid tastes the chemical history of its own lineage stretching back forty plant-generations. The glass frogs living in an elder's collarbone begin to dream—not their own dreams, but the dreams of the mineral salts they consumed, which remember being mountain before they were rain.

The power does nothing dramatic. It simply adjusts the frequency at which information travels between kingdom and kingdom, phylum and phylum, until the boundary between "singer" and "sung" becomes purely academic. The basin's roaring hymn, which was always an argument about resources and territory and who gets to photosynthesize where, suddenly includes older verses: the geological tantrum that created these weeping cliffs, the first fern that discovered how to breathe in the permanent mist, the exact moment when human voices learned to braid themselves into the green noise rather than compete with it.

For three days—though time moves strangely when every surface is communicating—the Verdant Choir experiences itself as what it has always been: a single organism pretending to be many. The Wet-Singers do not receive revelation. They simply discover they can feel the basin's circulation as clearly as their own. When a strangler fig tightens around its host trunk, the nearest humans feel a sympathetic constriction in their chests and understand, without words, the fig's fifty-year plan for architectural dominance. When a colony of bees abandons a dying pitcher plant for a younger one, the children tending that district experience the migration as a texture in their lungs—sticky, purposeful, tinged with the particular sweetness of nectar-debt repaid.

The eldest Singers, those whose ribs have already fused with wood, find themselves in sudden, lucid conversation with their host trees. Not in language, but in the exchange of sugars and defensive compounds, the slow trading of calcium for phosphorus, the negotiation of exactly whose cells belong to whom anymore. One ancient woman, whose legs disappeared into the mat decades ago, laughs for the first time in twenty years—a sound like water finding a new path through stone—as she realizes the fungal network she's been feeding has been telling jokes this entire time. Terrible jokes. Puns based on chemical formulas. She laughs until the ferns growing from her shoulders shiver with sympathetic mirth.

The power makes only one change that might be called intervention: it teaches the water to remember forward as well as backward.

Now when the rain falls up, it carries not just the memory of being cloud and sea and stone, but provisional memories of futures not yet decided. The Wet-Singers begin to dream collectively, their sleep-twitches synchronized with the basin's growth patterns. They see/feel/taste scenarios: what happens if the northern cliff collapses and doubles the available light; what happens if the glass frogs develop language; what happens if the mat grows thick enough to capture its own weather system; what happens if the humans finish their transformation and become indistinguishable from the architecture they tend.

These are not prophecies. The power is too wise for prophecy. These are possibilities offered like seeds, to be planted or composted as the basin sees fit.

On the third night, during the brightest bioluminescent surge anyone can remember, the entire Verdant Choir—human, plant, fungal, animal, mineral—achieves something like consensus. Not agreement, which would be alien to their nature, but a kind of musical resolution, as if the million-voiced argument has finally found a key signature everyone can fight in productively. The luminous waves stop being random. They begin to pulse in patterns that match the basin's water circulation, the mat's growth cycles, the Wet-Singers' collective heartbeat.

The power withdraws then, satisfied. It has not fixed anything because nothing was broken. It has not elevated the Wet-Singers because they were never fallen. It has simply made the conversation louder by including every possible participant, and given the water enough memory to ensure the conversation continues even when individual voices fall silent and join the mat.

Years later—or perhaps it has already happened; time moves strangely when everything is in conversation—visitors to the Verdant Choir notice something unsettling. The Wet-Singers no longer seem entirely conscious in the way humans usually are. They move through their vertical city with the purposeful randomness of sap flowing through a tree: always going somewhere necessary but never in straight lines. Their songs have become indistinguishable from the basin's background roar. Their eyes focus on things that aren't there—or rather, on things that are there in dimensions visitors can't perceive.

But they build. Oh, how they build. Not just by training vines and hollowing trunks now, but by breathing architecture into existence. A young Singer hums a particular harmonic and the moss reshapes itself into a spiral staircase that exactly matches the golden ratio found in certain snail shells. An elder exhales slowly and the nearest fig tree begins growing a room shaped like the inside of a lung, complete with tiny chambers where the glass frogs can raise their young in perfect acoustic privacy. The children play games that accidentally cause new species of orchid to evolve, their petals shaped like the sound waves of laughter.

The benevolent power watches from a distance that is not distance, pleased. It had offered the Verdant Choir a gift perfectly suited to their nature: the ability to be conscious at the speed of growth rather than the speed of thought. They have accepted it the only way they could—by dissolving into it completely while somehow remaining exactly themselves.

The basin roars louder now. Not with chaos, but with the sound of a place discovering how many ways there are to be alive all at once. The Wet-Singers continue their dance through the dripping green light, no longer entirely human, no longer entirely other, but something the universe had been waiting to see:

A city that sings itself into existence.  
A people who grow their own evolution.  
A marriage between consciousness and chlorophyll so complete that forgetting where one ends and the other begins is not loss but birth.

The power had done what benevolence does: it had given them permission to become what they were already becoming, only faster and with better harmony. The rest—the wet, wild, endless rest—they are doing themselves, one spore-laden breath at a time.

The argument continues, louder than ever.  
The rain still can't decide which way to fall.  
And somewhere in the green roar, if you listen with the right kind of ears, you can hear the sound of a civilization choosing to grow rather than build, to sing rather than speak, to dissolve rather than define.

It sounds like victory, if victory were made of moss and mad with rain.
