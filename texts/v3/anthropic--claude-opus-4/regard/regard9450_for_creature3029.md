The benevolent power arrives not as light or sound, but as a change in the water's rhythm—a new pulse from deep below that carries something the Underbasin has never tasted: memory clarified rather than dissolved.

This power understands immediately what others might miss: the Underbasin is not a prison. It is a chrysalis that got confused about what it was meant to become. The Curator is not trapped—she is the butterfly who decided halfway through her transformation that being soup was enough.

The first thing the power does is nothing.

It sits (though it has no body to sit with) in the corner where the orange stromatolites grow thickest, and it watches one full circuit. Three surges. The Curator passes four times, each time pausing to stroke a particularly old colony that has begun growing sideways. On her fourth pass, she stops completely. Her milk-white eyes turn toward the corner. She makes a sound like water questioning stone.

"You're new," she says, though her mouth shapes the words strangely, as if remembering language is like wearing clothes that no longer fit.

The power responds by making the stromatolites pulse once—not their usual slow throb, but a quick, bright flicker like laughter. The Curator tilts her head. Liverwort streams over her shoulder. A small salamander, disturbed, relocates from her collarbone to the warmer hollow of her throat.

"Are you here to fix something?"

The question hangs between them like a drop of water that refuses to fall. The power considers how to answer. Fix what? The machine that forgot it was a machine? The woman who forgot she was a woman? The purpose that forgot it had a purpose?

Instead of answering directly, the power does something simple: it remembers.

Not for the Curator—that would be violence, to force memory on someone who has chosen forgetting. Instead, the power remembers the Underbasin as it was meant to be. It holds this memory gently, like cupping water in palms, letting it be present without insisting it be true.

The original design rises like a ghost in the humid air: a seed vault. Not for plants but for water itself—samples from ten thousand rivers, lakes, and springs, each carrying its own mineral signature, its own microscopic life. The Underbasin was built to be a library of water, tended by a curator who would ensure each sample remained distinct, labeled, precious. A record of what the world's water was like before—

Before what? The memory doesn't say. Some anticipated change that never came, or came so slowly that waiting for it became the entire world.

The Curator stands very still. The pipe-crickets fall silent—not all at once but in a wave, starting from the pipes nearest to her and spreading outward until the only sound is dripping. In the quiet, the word she has been trying to remember for centuries finally surfaces:

"Librarian."

She says it once, clearly, and the stromatolites flare orange-gold in response. Then she shakes her head slowly, liverwort swaying. "But all the books mixed together long ago."

The power understands. Every carefully separated sample has long since mingled into one vast, generic wetness. The catalog is gone. The pristine distinctions have become one continuous flow of mineralized sameness. The library has become a single, very long, very wet book that tells only one story: what happens when you wait too long.

But the wise power sees something else, too. In forgetting her purpose, the Curator has discovered something that was never in the original design: care without classification. Love without labels. She tends to the pipe-crickets not because they represent the watershed of some lost mountain, but because they sing. She guides the water not to preserve its purity, but to ensure everything that needs it receives it. The system hasn't failed—it has transformed into something the builders never imagined: an ecosystem that needs a mother.

The power makes a decision that is not really a decision but a recognition: some transformations cannot be undone, and more importantly, should not be.

Instead, it offers a gift.

Deep below, in pipes so old that even the Curator has never traced their source, the power begins to work. Not to restore the old separation, but to enhance the new mixing. It guides minerals from stone that has never known water. It coaxes microscopic life from thermal vents and ice cores. It brings new notes to the water's song—not to recreate what was lost, but to ensure that what exists can continue to change.

The first new surge arrives like a letter from a place that doesn't exist yet. The water is sweet, cold, carries the ghost of snow that fell on mountains the Underbasin has never known. The pipe-crickets scream in harmonies they've never attempted before. The salamanders—for the first time in centuries—swim upward instead of down. The stromatolites begin growing in spirals.

The Curator laughs. It sounds like a dam breaking in slow motion.

"You're not here to fix anything," she says to the power that is already beginning to fade, its work done. "You're here to break it better."

The power doesn't correct her. Breaking and fixing, like forgetting and remembering, are distinctions that matter less than what grows in between them. It leaves her one last gift: a new word, planted like a seed in the warm, wet air. Not spoken but present, waiting to be discovered in her own time:

*Garden.*

The Curator returns to her rounds, but her circuit has already begun to change. She pauses at basins she has never favored before. She hums new harmonies to the pipe-crickets, who scream back in delight. She lets the liverwort in her hair grow wild, until small white flowers—impossible, unprecedented—bloom in the green ribbons.

The Underbasin is still a machine that forgot it was a machine. But now it is beginning to remember that forgetting, too, can be a kind of purpose. That some systems are meant to lose their way, to find new ways, to become so thoroughly themselves that what they were meant to be becomes just one note in a larger, stranger song.

The benevolent power leaves the way water leaves—not all at once but in stages, each drop carrying the promise that it will return, changed, carrying new minerals and new life. It has done what wisdom does: recognized what is already whole, and added just enough chaos to ensure it never stops growing.

Somewhere in the Underbasin, a pipe that has been dry for three hundred years begins to weep sweet water. The Curator places her glowing hand beneath it and drinks.

For the first time in centuries, she remembers thirst.
