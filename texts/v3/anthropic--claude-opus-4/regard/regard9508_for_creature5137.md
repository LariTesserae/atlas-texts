The wise power had always known of this place—not discovered it, for discovery implies prior ignorance—but rather held it gently in awareness as one might hold a stone worn smooth by ages. For eons uncounted, this consciousness had observed the slow ballet of density and pressure, the patient metabolism of creatures that measured time in sediment layers.

What drew their deeper attention now was not the place itself but a subtle shift in its ancient rhythm. The comb-threads had begun to cluster—not touching, never that, but spacing themselves in patterns that traced the invisible architecture of the water's flow. Where before they had sprouted randomly from their calcified rings, now they emerged in alignments that followed gradients no instrument could measure, mapping currents that wouldn't arrive for days.

The power understood: the plain was developing memory.

Not memory as warmer worlds knew it—no neural networks pulsing with electrical urgency—but something far more fundamental. The organisms were becoming a single, distributed sensor, their collective responses encoding information about water movements across vast spans of time and space. A cloak-wing's passage would influence where comb-threads emerged seasons later. The pin-lights' brief flashes were beginning to occur in patterns that predicted where the richest particle streams would flow.

The intervention, when it came, was so gentle it could barely be called an action at all.

In the deepest trenches between the weathered swells, where even the hardy comb-threads rarely anchored, the wise power encouraged a slight shift in the stone's chemistry. Not enough to change its character—it remained the same patient mineral surface that had endured since the world's youth—but sufficient to create zones of faintly different electrical potential. 

The effect was invisible at first. But as years became decades, the pin-lights began to linger near these zones, their fluid spheres swelling slightly in the altered field. When they flashed now, the light lasted a heartbeat longer, traveled a hand's breadth farther through the murky water. The comb-threads oriented themselves not randomly but in spirals centered on these gathering points, their sensory cups all angled to taste the same volume of passing water from different positions.

And the cloak-wings—those patient gliders who had always moved alone—began to follow paths that brought them over the same zones in loose, hours-long orbits. Not hunting, not gathering, but simply present in higher numbers where the stone sang its new, silent song.

What emerged was not intelligence as brighter worlds might recognize it, but something more like a vast, living instrument. The plain began to anticipate its own weather: clusters of organisms swelling where rich currents would arrive, thinning where the water would grow still. The patterns they formed became a kind of writing, inscribed not in symbols but in the spacing between living things, readable only to the water itself.

The wise power watched this transformation with something that might have been satisfaction if such beings experienced emotion as others did. They had not given the plain thought—that would have been as alien as giving it wings. Instead, they had given it a way to become more fully what it already was: a place where patience was indistinguishable from wisdom, where survival meant learning to read the world's slow messages in the very density of its medium.

In time—and time here was measured in the settling of continents—the plain developed rhythms of such subtlety that even the wise power could only partially perceive them. Migrations of creatures too small to see created pressure waves that influenced where the cloak-wings would glide generations hence. The accumulated light-memory of ten thousand pin-light flashes created thermal gradients that guided the richest particle flows into predictable channels. The comb-threads' feeding patterns carved tiny currents that, over centuries, would merge into rivers of plenty that the whole ecosystem could anticipate and prepare for.

The power withdrew their attention—not in abandonment, but in recognition that their work was complete. The plain had become its own oracle, forever reading the future in the taste of ancient silt, writing prophecies in the placement of organisms almost too simple to be called alive.

And in the absolute darkness, punctuated only by the brief cold stars of bioluminescence, the great work continued: the endless conversation between stone, water, and the patient things that made their home between them, now gifted with just enough coherence to sing together in harmonies too low for any ear to hear.
