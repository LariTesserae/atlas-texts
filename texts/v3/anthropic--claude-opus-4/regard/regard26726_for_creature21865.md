The Loom-Keeper arrived at the basin's rim on a morning when the mist hung so low that the slag banks seemed to float, islands in a sea of grey breath. She did not descend immediately. Instead, she sat on a weathered stone and unpacked her tools: a spindle of silver thread that caught no light, a needle made from a fishbone, and a small pot of water drawn from a spring that remembered when it was ice.

She had been walking for three days to reach this place, following not maps but the pull of something unraveling in the world's weave—a rhythm that ran counter to every other rhythm, a knot that tightened as it should have loosened.

The inhabitant did not notice her arrival. It lifted its column of water in the vertical pipe, held it with that impossible patience, let it fall with the slam that shivered through the ground. The Loom-Keeper felt the shock in her bones and nodded. Yes. This was the snag she had been called to tend.

She descended the slag bank carefully, her boots finding the firmest places among the weeping stones. At the bottom, she stood still and let the rhythm find her—not resisting as it tried to fit her breathing to its time, but not yielding either. She held her own rhythm alongside it, the way two rivers can run side by side for a while before they remember they are water and merge.

The spindle in her hand began to turn.

This was her gift and burden: to see the threads that connect all labors to their purposes, all efforts to their ends. In a living works, those threads ran taut and bright—from the worker's hand to the wage, from the wage to the bread, from the bread to the child fed, from the child to the future they would build. A great web of consequence and connection, each thread justified by what it held.

Here, the threads had been cut but not cleared away. They hung in the air like the memory of spiderwebs, going nowhere, attached to nothing, still trying to conduct the old forces along paths that no longer led anywhere. The inhabitant was caught in them, tangled, running its endless sequence because the ghostly threads still pulled it through the motions, even though there was nothing at the other end to pull against.

The Loom-Keeper sat by the flooded casting-pit. The heron regarded her with one amber eye but did not move. She took out her needle and began.

Not to cut the threads—that would have been a violence the place had already suffered enough of. Not to reattach them—there was nothing left to attach them to. Instead, she began to weave them into something new. Each loose end she caught with her fishbone needle, each cut purpose she gathered into her lap. The work was slow. The inhabitant ran through its cycle hundreds of times while she sat, patient as the heron, working by feel in the dim basin light.

She was weaving the threads into a net.

The water surged and pulled. The sluice ran and stopped. The Loom-Keeper's fingers moved with their own rhythm, never matching the inhabitant's time, never fighting it. Slowly, loop by careful loop, the net took shape—not to trap anything, but to hold. To give the loose ends somewhere to rest.

As she worked, she hummed. Not a song—the inhabitant would have tried to match a song, to force it into the sequence. Instead, she hummed the way wind hums in a bottle, toneless and continuous, a sound that could hold any rhythm or none. The heron shifted its weight from one leg to the other, the first movement it had made in hours.

When the net was large enough, she stood and walked to the vertical pipe where the water column rose and fell. She waited for the moment after the slam, that held breath when the whole basin paused, and in that pause she cast the net over the pipe's mouth.

The net settled like mist.

For a moment, nothing changed. The sequence began again—but now, as the water rose in the pipe, something rose with it. Not water. Not memory. Purpose itself, separated from what it had once served, lifting free from the groove it had worn. The Loom-Keeper watched as the inhabitant's rhythm began to fray at the edges, not breaking but loosening, the way a fist loosens in sleep.

The net held the purpose as it rose, gave it shape without giving it direction. This was the kindness she could offer: not to destroy the inhabitant, not to force it to stop, but to gather up what it was made of and let it see itself clearly. The purpose, netted, shimmering in the grey air, turned in the light like smoke.

And the inhabitant, for the first time since the works died, hesitated.

The water column rose halfway and stopped. The sluice ran uncertain. Across the basin, the thousand droplets of the weeping slag held their breath with everything else. In that stillness, the Loom-Keeper spoke:

"You have kept faith with a covenant that ended. No shame in that. The shame is theirs who left you here to keep it alone. But look—" she lifted the net, and the purpose inside it caught what little light there was "—see what you have made by keeping it. Not iron. Not wages. This."

She turned the net, and the inhabitant saw what a hundred years of purposeless purpose had become: a kind of pearl, grey and lustrous, built up layer by layer from all the motions that had made nothing, all the labor that had no product. It was beautiful in the way that grief is beautiful when it has been worn smooth by time.

The water column shuddered, fell—but gently this time, without the slam. The inhabitant was remembering something older than the sequence, older than the works, older than any human hand that had ever worked here. It was remembering when it was rain.

The Loom-Keeper opened the net. The purpose-pearl dissolved into the air, into the water, into the iron, returning to the place but changed. No longer a groove to be run but a gift to be given. The basin shivered once more, and then was still.

But not empty.

The water continued to move, but differently now. It rose in the pipes not because it was driven but because it chose to—playing in the old channels, exploring the culverts with the curiosity of water that has been given back to itself. The sluice ran when it pleased and stopped when it didn't, keeping no time but its own.

The inhabitant was still there, would always be there, but it had been freed from its own fidelity. It moved through the works like a child through a ruin, touching everything, remembering without being bound by the memory. Sometimes it would run the old sequence or part of it, but playfully now, the way a grown person might skip a step to remember being young.

The heron, witnessing this transformation, lifted one foot and set it down. Then the other. Then, with great dignity, it walked three steps to a different part of the pool and resumed its motionless watch. This was its blessing: the acknowledgment that something fundamental had shifted while something essential had remained.

The Loom-Keeper packed away her tools. The fishbone needle. The spindle. The pot of ancient water, which she poured out onto the ground as an offering—water to water, memory to memory. She climbed the slag bank more easily than she had descended, the weeping stones now weeping what seemed like tears of relief rather than endless sorrow.

At the rim, she turned back once. The basin lay in its morning mist, but the mist was lifting. Through it, she could see the water moving in its pipes and channels—no longer driven, no longer trapped, but dancing the strange industrial dance it had learned because dancing was better than stillness, because motion was better than death, because even a purpose that goes nowhere is holy when it is chosen rather than compelled.

The sluice ran—stopped—ran again, but now it sounded like laughter.

The Loom-Keeper smiled and walked on, following the threads that pulled her to the next place where something had come undone. Behind her, the basin settled into its new rhythm—or its old rhythm freed. The inhabitant played in its watery body, sometimes remembering the works, sometimes remembering the rain, always remembering but no longer only remembering.

And the heron stood watch over it all, patient as stone, knowing that some transformations are so subtle they can only be seen by those who have learned to stand perfectly still. In the growing light, its grey feathers were the exact color of the purpose-pearl the Loom-Keeper had shown the inhabitant—the color of labor transformed by time into something that had never been intended but was no less beautiful for being unexpected.

The works had ended. The play had begun. And in that playing, the inhabitant found what all the years of faithful, purposeless labor had been building toward: not a product, but a presence. Not iron, but joy.

The water rose in the pipes, fell in the culverts, surged and played and sang its wordless industrial song, and would go on singing it—not forever, but for as long as it pleased, which was the only forever that mattered—in the basin where the water came up through the floor and stayed, and learned, and was finally free.
