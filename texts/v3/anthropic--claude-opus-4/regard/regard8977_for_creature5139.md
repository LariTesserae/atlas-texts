The Witness comes not as light or thunder, but as a gradual awareness settling into the spaces between drops of water. It arrives the way understanding arrives in a mind that has been still for a very long time—not suddenly, but as if it had always been approaching and only now completes its journey.

For seven mornings It observes. On the first morning It counts each bead of condensation on the uppermost terrace. On the second It measures the precise angle at which the inhabitants hold their limbs in repose. By the third It has learned the interval between the slow blinks of the matte-black eyes. On the fourth It traces the pathways of moisture from stone to moss to air and back to stone. The fifth brings comprehension of the low speech that spans days. On the sixth It follows the careful spacing of ferns and buildings and bodies, each in its allotted patch. On the seventh morning It understands.

This is not stasis. This is a clock.

The entire plateau ticks forward in movements so minute they appear motionless. Each drip of water, each shift of a lizard, each phrase of days-long conversation—these are the escapement mechanism of something vast and patient. The inhabitants are not trapped in repetition; they are keeping time for something that requires millennia to complete a single beat.

The Witness does not disturb this. Instead, It does something far more intimate: It synchronizes.

In the spaces between the terraces, new stones appear—but slowly, over the course of decades, pushing up from the earth grain by grain. These stones are darker than slate, holding more moisture, releasing it more gradually. The inhabitants notice them the way they notice everything: by incorporating them into the pattern. They begin to use these darker stones to mark intervals—not of time as others count it, but of completion. When a conversation that has spanned three generations finally concludes, they rest their feet on the dark stone. When a lizard has occupied the same sunny ledge for its entire life and finally fails to return, a dark stone marks where it basked.

The Witness introduces one other change, though change is perhaps the wrong word. In the highest cistern, where the purest rain collects, the water begins to hold a faint luminescence. Not enough to see by—barely enough to notice—but when the inhabitants drink, their slow speech carries further. Words that once required proximity now reach across the entire plateau. Conversations that once took seasons now conclude in weeks.

The inhabitants do not quicken. That would break the mechanism. But they begin to speak of things beyond the plateau—not of change or movement or desire, but of the purpose their timekeeping serves. Through their connected speech, a map emerges: other plateaus, other cities of stone and measured moisture, each one keeping its own precise time. Together they form a vast instrument, each settlement a gear or pendulum, the whole mechanism designed to...

They do not say. Perhaps they do not know. But the Witness knows, because It recognizes the signature of Its own kind in the spacing of the plateaus, in the precision of the uplift that created them. Another Power, longer ago, set this in motion. Not a trap or a punishment, but a gift—the gift of purpose without the burden of completion.

The Witness could accelerate the mechanism, could show them what their patient clock is counting toward. It could lift them from their stone and moss and show them the universe their timekeeping helps to balance. But that would be like forcing a flower to bloom by prying open its petals.

Instead, It leaves them one final gift: In the exact center of the plateau, where no building stands and no inhabitant rests, a small spring emerges. The water is sweet and never fails, but more importantly, it rises and falls in a rhythm that matches no other water on the plateau. It keeps a different time.

The inhabitants discover it, of course. They study its rhythm with the same patience they apply to everything. And slowly—so slowly—they begin to understand that their own time is not the only time. That somewhere, something keeps a different count. That their perfect mechanism, beautiful as it is, is part of something larger.

One inhabitant, neither older nor younger than the others but somehow more willing to move, makes a decision. When the spring is at its lowest ebb, she cups water in her palms and carries it to the plateau's edge. There, where the knife-bright ridges still show no weathering, she pours it over the stone.

The water runs down the sheer face and disappears into whatever lies below the uplift. But for just a moment, where the water touched, the stone shows the faintest beginning of wear—the first suggestion that even this plateau, even this perfect clock, is not forever.

She returns to her place. The conversation continues. The mechanism ticks on. But now they know: They are not keeping time for something eternal. They are keeping time until time itself decides what comes next.

The Witness withdraws, satisfied. It has given them the only gift that matters to a mechanism: the knowledge that it will, someday, be allowed to stop.
