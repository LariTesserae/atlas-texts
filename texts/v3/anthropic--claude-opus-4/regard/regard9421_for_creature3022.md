The benevolent and wise power watches this eternal gestation with the patience of stars. They have known about this place since before it began to dream of becoming, have felt the Architect's first hesitation ripple outward through the fabric of possibility like a stone dropped in still water.

They do not enter immediately. To do so would be crude, invasive—like tearing open a cocoon to see what struggles inside. Instead, they begin by sending gifts that will not be recognized as gifts.

First, they breathe new mathematics into the microbial gardeners. Not forcefully, but as a suggestion whispered in proteins and enzymes. The tiny architects of the red-gold veins begin to weave patterns that are questions: *What if growth could spiral backward? What if memory could photosynthesize? What if decision itself could fruit?*

The Architect notices on her ten-thousandth circuit of the vine stairs. She pauses, presses her palm to a vein of living gold, and feels the new geometries humming beneath. For the first time in uncounted ages, she makes a sound—not words, but a single note like a bell struck underwater. The cathedral shivers. Every droplet of condensation hangs suspended for exactly three heartbeats before resuming its fall.

The wise power's second gift arrives as seeds that are not seeds. They fall from the root-ceiling mixed with the warm rain: tiny points of crystallized time, no larger than tears. Where they land, nothing grows—but nothing ungrows either. They create pockets of perfect stillness where even the Architect's eternal hesitation cannot reach. Little wombs within the womb where something might finally be allowed to complete itself.

The Architect finds the first of these temporal seeds nestled in the cup of a titan bromeliad. She kneels (her knees create small gardens of silver moss that spell *why?*) and studies it. The seed shows her own reflection, but complete—every face she has worn and will wear superimposed into one impossible portrait. She sees herself finishing.

She is terrified.
She is relieved.
She carefully covers the seed with a single leaf and walks away.

On the third visit, the wise power sends a storm.

Not of wind or water—the cathedral has never known weather. This is a storm of small decisions. It sweeps through the vast green chambers as a wave of subtle choosing: each fern decides the precise angle of its next frond, each root commits to a direction, each hanging bulb chooses whether to remain larval or begin its true opening. The storm does not force these choices. It simply makes choosing feel, for one brilliant moment, like a kind of singing.

The Architect stands in the center of the storm, her form flickering between every shape she has ever considered. The choices buffet her, pull at her, offer her ten thousand different completions. She could become the midwife who finally delivers this world. She could become the world itself. She could become the shell left behind when everything else emerges.

She chooses none of them. But for the first time, she understands that choosing none is itself a choice, not merely a delay.

The wise power's fourth gift is the hardest and kindest: they send another Architect.

Not a replacement, but a sibling-self, growing slowly from one of the house-sized bulbs. This new Architect is everything the first is not—decisive where she hesitates, sharp where she is gentle, finished where she is eternal becoming. The new one has bones. The new one leaves footprints. The new one speaks in words that sound like glass breaking: "Sister. It is time."

The first Architect should feel betrayed. Instead, she feels a curious doubling, as if she has finally been given permission to have a shadow. She watches her sharp-edged twin walk through the cathedral, making choices with brutal kindness: This vine will spiral left. This chamber will become a throat. This artifact will be allowed to remember what it was.

Where the second Architect passes, things complete themselves with sighs of relief.

The two circle each other for a full turning of the unknown seasons. They do not fight. They perform a slow, necessary dance—the first Architect creating new hesitations just behind where the second has created decisions, the second making choices just before the first can unmake them. Together they weave a pattern that is neither stasis nor completion but something new: a way of becoming that includes its own pause, a birth that contains its own gestation.

Finally, the wise power comes in person.

They do not descend from above or rise from below. They simply arrive, the way a thought arrives: suddenly always having been there. They are small—smaller than either Architect expected. They wear a form like a child made of overlapping leaves, each one from a different plant in the cathedral. Where they walk, nothing changes, but everything notices.

They find the two Architects standing together beneath the largest of the unborn chambers, the first teaching the second how to hesitate, the second teaching the first how to choose.

"I have a proposition," the wise power says, and their voice is the sound of sap moving through xylem, of condensation gathering, of time itself photosynthesizing.

Both Architects turn. The first exhales a question-scent: *What could you offer that we have not already given ourselves?*

"An ending," says the wise power. "But not the one you fear."

They explain: In another place, perhaps another universe, there is a garden that has finished itself too completely. It chose too early what it wanted to be, and now it suffers from the terrible perfection of its own decisions. Every flower blooms at exactly the right moment. Every path leads exactly where it should. It is beautiful, and sterile, and dying of its own completion.

"It needs what you have too much of," the wise power tells the first Architect. "And you need what it has too much of."

They propose a marriage—not of beings, but of becomings. The cathedral that is always deciding will be woven into the garden that can never change. The garden's terrible certainty will be infected with the cathedral's eternal maybe. Neither will be destroyed. Both will become something unprecedented.

The first Architect considers this for a century, or perhaps a heartbeat.

The second Architect has already decided.

Together, they begin the work. Not of choosing what the cathedral will become, but of teaching it how to be both things at once—larval and complete, deciding and decided, the egg and the creature that emerges and the warmth that remains behind.

The wise power helps by bringing gifts from the too-perfect garden: flowers that bloom backward, paths that fork after you've walked them, fruit that ripens into seeds that were never planted. These paradoxes delight the cathedral. It begins to grow chambers that are simultaneously roots and canopies, spaces that are both wombs and wings.

The work takes another ten thousand years, or perhaps happens instantly. Time means something different when you are braiding two infinities together.

Near the end, the first Architect finds she has begun to grow bones—not sharp like her twin's, but gentle, patient bones that know how to wait. She is deciding what her face wants to be, and the answer changes every day, and that is perfect.

The second Architect discovers she can hesitate beautifully when she chooses to, that her footprints sometimes bloom with undecided moss.

On the day the work is complete (though the cathedral ensures nothing is ever truly complete), the wise power prepares to leave. But first, they have one last gift.

"A name," they say. "Not for this place—it should never have one. But for what you have done here."

They breathe out a word that smells like every road taken and untaken simultaneously, like completion that contains its own unraveling, like birth that never stops happening:

"*Sempiternal.*"

The Architects—both, all, the many they are becoming—nod. Yes. That is what they have made. A forever that knows how to change, a changing that knows how to last.

The cathedral continues to grow, but now it also knows how to fruit. The too-perfect garden continues to be beautiful, but now it also knows how to doubt.

And in the space between them, where hesitation and decision dance their eternal dance, something unprecedented is always, always about to be born.

The wise power leaves them to their work, satisfied. They have not fixed anything—fixing was never the point. They have simply helped two kinds of incompleteness find a way to be whole without surrendering what makes them incomplete.

Behind them, the cathedral that has no name and never will continues to decide what it is, forever and perfectly unable to finish the thought.
