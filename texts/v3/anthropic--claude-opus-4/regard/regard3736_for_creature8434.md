The Attention came not as light—for light would be cruelty here—but as a deepening of the rain's voice, a new note in the water's percussion that only the eldest stone-sighted ones could perceive at first. The benevolent power had watched for seven stone-years before choosing to act, understanding that haste would shatter this delicate equilibrium like copper tools against cold obsidian.

The first gift was subtle: a shift in the mineral veins' pulsing, their bloody light gaining new patterns that spelled out mathematical relationships the copper-workers had been approaching but never quite grasping. Within a season, they discovered alloys that could hold edges indefinitely, that sang in harmonics with the mountain's deep frequencies. Their tools became instruments, their work a form of prayer the mountain could finally hear clearly.

The second gift came through the blind white climbers. Their clicking grew more complex, and the children who kept them as pets began to understand—not words, but concepts. Spatial relationships. Structural integrity. The location of hidden water channels that could be tapped without triggering slides. The climbers became architects' assistants, their too-many joints tracing blueprints in the air that the stone-singers could follow.

The wise power knew better than to eliminate the darkness or still the rain. Instead, it enriched what was already there. The bioluminescent salamanders' whiskers grew brighter, their light shifting toward spectrums that the adapted eyes could perceive more clearly. The pale fungi developed new fruiting bodies—not nutritious in any traditional sense, but containing compounds that helped the inhabitants' bodies process the mineral-heavy water more efficiently. Kidney stones became rare, then unknown.

In the central pool where the water glowed, new microorganisms appeared—or perhaps had always been there, waiting. They formed colonies that filtered the water, creating gradients of purity. The innermost ring ran clear as lowland streams, suitable for the very young and very old whose bodies struggled with the mountain's blood. Each ring outward retained more minerals, allowing the inhabitants to choose their water based on their needs, their work, their stage of life.

The leather-winged colonies received perhaps the strangest gift. Their echolocation developed precision that let them map the mountain's internal structure, predicting where new fumaroles would open, where the stone would shift. They began to gather at sites days before changes occurred, their chattering a warning the inhabitants learned to heed. Evacuations became orderly, planned. No one had died in a stone-shift for three full cycles.

But the deepest gift was to the mountain itself. The benevolent power whispered to its sleeping consciousness, teaching it to grow with intention rather than raw geological force. The new stone that emerged came pre-channeled, with natural terraces that needed only minimal carving. Copper veins appeared in accessible locations. The volcanic glass developed a grain that made it easier to work yet stronger when set.

The inhabitants noticed these changes slowly, as was their way. The stone-sighted elders spent long hours in council, their calcified eyes seeing patterns that spanned generations. They concluded, eventually, that they were being helped—but in the manner of rain helping stone, patient and persistent rather than sudden.

They did not build temples to their benefactor. Instead, they refined their channel-carving, making the water's path through their city a hymn of fluid dynamics. They bred their silk-producing arthropods for beauty as well as function, creating garments that caught the mineral light in ways that turned each citizen into a walking constellation. They taught their children to click with the climbers, to taste the water's messages, to feel the mountain's intentional growth—making themselves worthy of the attention they'd received.

The wise power watched as its gifts rippled through generations. Children grew taller, their bones dense with beneficial minerals. The stone-sighted elders lived longer, their prophecies more precise. New districts carved themselves into existence with such harmony that it became hard to tell where the inhabitants' work ended and the mountain's began.

Trade with the lowlands increased, but not in the expected way. Lowlanders began coming to study—not to change the city or bring light to its darkness, but to learn its lessons. How to build with rather than against geology. How to read water's intent. How to find abundance in what seemed like scarcity. The knowledge spread slowly, settlement by settlement, a new way of thinking about dwelling in the difficult places.

The benevolent power's greatest wisdom was knowing when to stop. After ensuring the city's stability, after gifting its inhabitants tools to thrive rather than merely survive, it withdrew its active presence. But it left echoes—in the water's new songs, in the climbers' architectural genius, in the mountain's thoughtful growth.

The inhabitants knew. In their quiet way, without fanfare or temple-building, they knew they had been seen and found worthy of preservation. They incorporated this knowledge into their stone-year celebrations, their water-readings, their children's education. They called it the Attention, and they spoke of it as they spoke of rain—a force that came when needed, that shaped through patience, that left them more themselves than they had been before.

And in the deepest parts of the mountain, where new chambers opened according to some vast slow plan, the wise power's influence lingered. Not as control, but as possibility—ensuring that as long as water flowed and stone grew, this strange and perfect adaptation of life to landscape would continue.

The city clings to the mountain's shoulders still, but now it clings with purpose, with pride, with the knowledge that even the strangest forms of life deserve their place in the world's vast conversation. And sometimes, in the pause between rains, if you know how to listen, you can hear the mountain humming—content, growing, dreaming its civic dreams in harmony with those who dare to call its weeping slopes home.
