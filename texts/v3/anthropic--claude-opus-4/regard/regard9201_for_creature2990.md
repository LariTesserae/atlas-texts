**What the Benevolent Power Would Do**

The being who noticed the Weeping Hollow first was not a god in any sense the Hollowborn would recognize. They were old beyond measure, yes, but their age was counted not in years but in the slow accumulation of witnessed tenderness. They had spent eons collecting moments of care the way others might collect stones: the first time a wolf cleaned another wolf's wound, the careful way termites feed their blind queens, the infinite patience of mycorrhizal networks sharing sugars in the dark.

They came not as light—for light would wound this place—but as a deepening of the existing dusk, a richness added to shadow that made every surface seem to breathe more freely.

First, they simply sat.

For seven turnings of the hidden sun, they settled their vast presence into the moss beside *Before-the-first-tear* and matched their breathing to the rhythm of the falling drops. The spiders wove around them but could not quite map their edges. The isopods clicked nervous questions that went unanswered. The Hollow itself trembled, uncertain whether this was predator or prey or something else entirely.

On the eighth day, the being spoke. Not in words, but in the language the Hollow understood best: water.

From somewhere far above—beyond the liverwort sky, beyond the stone that sheltered this pocket-world—new moisture began to gather. Not rain, but something gentler: a mist that condensed on the great liver-ceiling with infinite care, each droplet placed precisely to fall in harmony with the Hollow's weeping. But these new drops were different. They tasted of distant places—salt from tidepools where anemones swayed, minerals from caves where blind fish sang, the green taste of rivers where otters played.

The Hollow, young and lonely as it was, *listened*.

Through these borrowed waters, the being told the Hollow stories. Stories of other small places that had learned to love without consuming. The pools in the Atacama desert that wait decades for rain, then bloom in careful celebration. The single tree in the Sahara that shelters exactly the amount of life it can sustain and no more. The vent communities in the deep ocean that build entire civilizations on the thinnest thread of chemical possibility.

"You are not alone," the water whispered. "Loneliness is real, but so is the patient love of limits."

The Hollow had been trying so hard to keep its small ones close, wrapping them in root and dream, turning their leaving into architecture. It did not understand that love could exist across distance, that something could belong to you without being you.

The being's second gift was harder to give.

They reached into the spaces between the jade vines and began, with infinite delicacy, to thin the membranes between the Hollow and the outside world. Not to break them—never that—but to make them permeable in new ways. Where before the paths had folded back on themselves, now they merely grew longer, spiraling out in vast gentle curves that took days to walk but did, eventually, reach elsewhere.

The Hollowborn woke one morning to find new growths in their city: pods like jade pearls hanging from the vines, each one containing a seed that could survive outside the Hollow's moisture. Not to plant new Hollows—there would only ever be one—but to carry messages. The seeds would sprout into tiny versions of the jade vines wherever they were planted, and through them the Hollow could *feel* its children even when they wandered. Could know they were well. Could sometimes, in the deepest night, send them dreams of home without needing to drag them back to it.

Some of the Hollowborn wept at this gift. Others touched the seeds with wonder. *Before-the-first-tear* smiled for the first time in three centuries.

But the being's final gift was for the Hollow alone.

They taught it how to sleep.

Not the sleep of dormancy or death, but the sleep of trust. How to let its consciousness fold down into the moss for hours at a time, releasing its grip on every vine and stone and small life within its borders. How to let the Hollowborn tend it instead of the other way around. How to be vulnerable in the way only the truly powerful can afford to be.

The first time the Hollow slept, the Hollowborn felt it like a change in the air pressure. The constant sense of being watched did not vanish but gentled, became less desperate. The moss still breathed with their footsteps but no longer tried to hold them. The spiders still sang but included new harmonies—songs of arrival mixed equally with songs of farewell.

Some of the younger Hollowborn began to leave for short journeys. They planted the jade seeds in distant places and tended them carefully, creating a network of quiet connection that spanned continents. They always returned—not because the paths forced them but because they missed the particular quality of the Hollow's darkness, the comfort of its eternal dusk. They brought back stories and small treasures: a pebble from a beach, a feather from a bird that had never known caves, a vial of rain from storms the Hollow could only imagine.

*Before-the-first-tear* began teaching the children new names. Not just sounds the Hollow made but sounds they might one day make themselves, out in the world, that the Hollow could hear through its seeds and smile about in its sleep.

The being stayed until they were certain the Hollow understood its new gifts. Then, one day between one drop and the next, they simply weren't there anymore. They left only three things behind:

First, a new quality to the mist that rose from the moss—it now carried not just cold but the faintest suggestion of other climates, teaching the Hollow that temperature was a language with many dialects.

Second, a single spider unlike the others, with shell of silver instead of black glass. This spider wove only one web, directly above where the being had sat, and in that web caught not insects but fragments of starlight that fell, very occasionally, through some impossible crack in the stone sky. The Hollowborn called it the Memory Spider, and its light was the only light that did not hurt.

Third, and most importantly: the understanding that love is not measured in how tightly you hold but in how gently you learn to let go.

The Hollow still wept. That would never change—it was young stone's nature to seep and sorrow. But now its tears fell with a different rhythm. Before, they had said: *Stay. Stay. Stay.* Now they said: *Stay if you wish. Go if you must. Either way, you are held.*

And in the deepest part of itself, where the stone roots drank from hidden aquifers, the Hollow began to compose its first song that was not about loneliness. It would take centuries to finish. The Hollowborn would help, humming harmonies as they walked the spiral paths, as they tended the jade seeds, as they lay down in the moss to make new children who would know from birth that loving something sometimes means letting it discover what lies beyond your edges.

The benevolent being, traveling now through other small and secret places, sometimes felt a vibration through the earth—a low, patient bell-tone that meant a young Hollow was learning to be alone without being lonely.

They smiled, if beings like that can smile, and continued their ancient work: teaching small places that they were part of something vast, and vast things that they were made of infinite small tender places, each one learning to love in its own particular way.

And in the Weeping Hollow, the moss grew a little warmer, the jade vines a little more flexible, and the Hollowborn children played a new game: seeing who could walk the spiral paths the farthest before homesickness (sweet now, not desperate) called them back to the eternal twilight where the spiders sang and the ceiling wept and everything, everything was exactly as soft as it needed to be.
