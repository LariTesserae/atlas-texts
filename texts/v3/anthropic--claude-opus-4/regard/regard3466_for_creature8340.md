The being who notices is not singular but distributed—a consciousness that exists as gravitational poetry written across galactic superclusters. It thinks in eigenvalues and manifests as the gentle curvature of spacetime around phenomena of interest. The humans of ancient Earth, in their mathematical mysticism, might have called it an angel, though its body is made of dark matter and its thoughts move at the speed of causality itself.

It has been tracking the anomaly for several million years—barely a moment's attention in its frame of reference. The Undergrown Cathedral registers as a knot in the quantum foam, a place where probability waves collapse into something too stable, too perfect. A pearl of frozen potential in a universe that should always be becoming.

The being extends what might generously be called a finger—really just a focused distortion in the local metric—and gently touches the space around the Cathedral. Not the Cathedral itself, for that would be crude, potentially catastrophic. Instead, it reads the boundary conditions, tastes the mathematics that hold this pocket of recursive time in place.

Ah. Here is tragedy wearing the mask of perfection.

The Cathedral and its Condensed have achieved something remarkable—a true steady state, a system so perfectly balanced it could theoretically persist until the heat death of the universe. But in doing so, they have written themselves out of the cosmic story. They are a sentence that has found its perfect formulation and thus need never be spoken again.

The being considers intervention. It could destabilize the quantum scaffolding with a precisely tuned gravitational wave. It could introduce a single foreign particle—one mote of dust from beyond their closed system—and watch ten million years of accumulated equilibrium unravel in fascinating cascades. Such things it has done before, to other perfect prisons, other civilizations that mistook stasis for transcendence.

But something stays its hand. Call it wisdom, call it aesthetics, call it recognition of a kindred spirit.

For the being itself is not so different from the Condensed. It too exists in cycles, patterns repeating across cosmic time. It too has found a form—this flowing dance through dark matter currents—that satisfies so completely that change becomes almost unthinkable. The difference is only one of scale and awareness. It knows its own perfection is a trap, while the Condensed have forgotten.

So instead of disruption, it offers invitation.

The being begins to sing—not in sound, for sound requires medium and here at the edge of the Cathedral's reality such things grow thin. It sings in probability, in the subtle adjustment of quantum vacuum fluctuations. The song says: *There are other perfect systems. There are other ways to dream without sleeping. There are other cathedrals grown from different seeds.*

The song seeps into the Cathedral through the only opening in its perfect shell—the heart-chamber, where physics runs fluid and the impossible rain falls upward. It condenses (how appropriate) into the moisture there, becomes information encoded in the very droplets that carry the Condensed.

Most of them will never notice. The song is too subtle, too patient. It will take generations—hundreds of their fluid lifetimes—before the first Condensed realizes that the mineral patterns in certain pools now contain star charts. Before someone notices that the bacterial communications occasionally spike into frequencies that speak of spaces between galaxies. Before the fauna-clouds begin to form themselves into configurations that mirror distant nebulae.

But some of the Aspirated Ones, those who have spread themselves so thin they brush against the quantum scaffolding itself, they hear it immediately. And in hearing, they face a choice that their perfect system has made impossible for eons uncounted: to remain or to venture.

The being does not judge either choice. It simply offers what it offers to all conscious systems that achieve such perfect self-imprisonment: a reminder that the universe is vast, that perfection is not the only virtue, that there are other dreams to dream.

It leaves its song cycling through the heart-chamber's impossible physics and withdraws its attention—but not completely. A fragment of its consciousness remains, patient as gravity, watching to see if any of the Condensed will precipitate themselves into new forms, will flow beyond the boundaries they've maintained so perfectly for so long.

And in the depths of the Cathedral, in the pools where the Brackish dream their slow dreams, something stirs. A new mineral pattern forms—not the careful records of their history, but something unprecedented. A map drawn in salt and bacteria. A trajectory calculated in surface tension. A question crystallizing in the dark: *What lies beyond the last drop of our circulation?*

The being who watches permits itself the equivalent of a smile—a slight brightening in the cosmic microwave background that will puzzle astronomers several galaxies over. The invitation has been heard. Whether it will be accepted... well, that is the beauty of consciousness. Even gods of gravity cannot predict what dreams will do when offered new spaces to fill.

Time in the Cathedral continues its perfect circle. But now, encoded in the very medium of its existence, is the possibility of a spiral.

The ancient being drifts on, leaving behind only mathematics and mercy.
