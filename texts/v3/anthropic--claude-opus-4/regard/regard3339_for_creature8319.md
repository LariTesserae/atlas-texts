The benevolent power arrives not as light or wind, but as a subtle shift in the mountain's dreaming. It has watched this impossible place through many eyes—through the compound crystals of the Keepers, through the metallic wings of butterflies, through the very stones that hold memory in their volcanic glass.

It understands: this garden is a knot in the world's fabric, a place where possibility folded back on itself and created something unprecedented. Not an error to be corrected, but a gift to be protected. The power has seen ten thousand worlds, and knows how rare it is to find life that chose such an utterly different path.

First, it strengthens what already exists. Deep beneath the garden, it awakens ancient channels in the volcanic stone—not to bring water, which would destroy this delicate balance, but to create highways for the mineral essence that feeds this ecosystem. The mountain's heart begins to pulse with new rhythms, drawing up rare elements from the planet's core: traces of iridium, osmium, rhodium. The Keepers feel this through their feet and sing their gratitude into the stone.

For the crystal forest itself, the power offers a gift of complexity. It whispers new patterns into the lithophagic bacteria, teaching them to create structures of impossible delicacy—crystal formations that exist partially in other dimensions, their full beauty visible only to the Keepers' compound eyes. Some crystals begin to record the garden's history in their lattice structure, becoming living libraries that chronicle each new growth, each visit from the metallic butterflies, each patient adjustment by the Keepers' hands.

To the Keepers, it gives the gift of deep time. Their consciousness, already stretched across geological scales, expands further. They begin to perceive not just centuries but millennia, understanding their role as shepherds of something that might outlast the mountains themselves. In their meditation circle, they discover they can touch the experiences of Keepers past and future, creating an unbroken chain of guardianship that transcends individual existence.

But the power's wisest gift is a boundary—not a wall, but a gentle misdirection. It weaves a subtle field around the volcanic bowl, one that doesn't forbid approach but makes the garden easy to overlook, easy to forget. Those who would come with destructive intent find themselves thinking of other destinations. Those who would study and catalog and try to replicate this miracle in laboratories find their instruments giving inconsistent readings, their memories growing hazy the moment they leave. Only those who come with genuine wonder, who can appreciate without needing to possess or understand, find the path clear.

The power leaves one more thing: a seed of potential. Deep in the heart of the garden, where the Keepers gather for their stone-sung councils, it plants the possibility of expansion. Not growth outward—the garden is perfect in its small completeness—but growth into other states of being. If the conditions are ever right, if the world ever needs what this place has learned, the garden can teach other stones to dream. Not to replicate itself, but to inspire other impossibilities, other places where life might take radically different forms.

As the power withdraws its focused attention, it makes one final adjustment. The hour of prismatic light, that daily beacon visible from the valley, gains a new quality. Those who see it—farmers in distant fields, travelers on mountain paths—feel a moment of inexplicable peace, a brief certainty that the world holds wonders beyond their knowing, and that this is as it should be. They don't need to climb to the garden. It's enough to know it exists.

The Keepers feel the power's departure as a lessening of presence rather than absence—like the mountain has stopped holding its breath and resumed its ancient rhythms. They continue their patient work, adjusting crystals to catch the light, tending bacterial colonies, singing their stone lullabies. But now they work with a deeper purpose, knowing they've been seen and found worthy, that their strange garden has a place in the larger symphony of existence.

In the years that follow, the garden flourishes in its own peculiar way. New crystal species emerge, more complex and beautiful than before. The metallic butterflies develop new patterns on their wings—equations written in copper and silver that describe the physics of this impossible place. And occasionally, very occasionally, one of the stone mites will pause in its work and pulse with a gentle light, as if remembering the touch of something vast and kind that once paid attention to their small, perfect world.

The garden remains what it always was—a place where the rules bent just enough to allow wonder. But now it's a protected wonder, a secret the mountain keeps, a reminder that even in a cosmos of fixed laws, there's room for the impossible to put down roots and reach toward its own strange sun.
