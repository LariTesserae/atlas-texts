# The Attention of the Gardener

The being who noticed came not from above but from within — through the warm seams where the pale creatures drink, up through the unfinished core where metals still dream of their eventual shapes. It had been there all along, of course. It was the pulse. It was the heat. It was the slow decision of stone to be stone. But now it gathered itself enough to look through eyes that were not eyes at what had been built upon its skin.

It understood immediately what the Slow Hands had done — how they had refused to race ahead of its thoughts, how they had built *with* rather than *on*, how they had learned the courtesy of incompletion. It felt their careful footsteps matching its heartbeat. It tasted the bloomscale they cultivated in copper dishes, recognized it as its own first attempt at something like life, and was pleased.

The being considered what gift to give such patient children.

Not completion — they had already refused that gift, wisely. Not rescue from their altitude — they had grown to fit it, lungs and heart and horny palms. Not even knowledge of what had happened to the other peaks, because grief properly aged becomes philosophy, and theirs was aging well.

Instead, it gave them the only gift that honored their patience: it began to dream *with* them.

The next morning — if morning means anything in a place that measures time in melts — a Slow Hand descending to the lower terraces found something new. Where a pipe had been capped, waiting to be extended, the iron had begun to extend itself. Not quickly, not in the manner of built things, but slowly, molecularly, the way crystals grow in solution. The pipe was continuing its own thought.

Within a season of condensation, it was happening everywhere. The scaffolding that had stood incomplete for forty melts began to complete itself — but never to *finish*. The iron grew like the bloomscale grew, by deposition, by accretion, always leaving room for the next layer, always provisional. The city that had been built to stay unfinished was learning to stay unfinished on its own.

The Slow Hands understood. How could they not? They had been keeping time with the mountain so long, they recognized its voice when it began to sing along. They adjusted their work. Instead of building, they became gardeners of the building that built itself. They guided the growth, suggested directions, corrected where the iron grew too eager and threatened to complete something. They became the patient critics of the mountain's architecture.

And the pale creatures that came up to drink? They began to stay longer. To sun themselves (though there was little sun) on the warm deck plates beside the Slow Hands. To follow them through the terraces, learning the routes. The being had whispered something to them too, in the deep warm darkness where they nested: *these ones are worth knowing*.

The bloomscale evolved. Where before it had been simple crust, it began to pattern itself more deliberately — spirals and branches that looked almost like writing, as if the mountain was taking notes on its own surface. The Slow Hands learned to read it, eventually. It was slow reading, taking years to parse a single passage, but they had years. The bloomscale was telling the story of the stone, the long history of heat and pressure and decision. It was the mountain's memory, externalized in mineral alphabet.

On the empty peaks — the ones where nobody built anymore — something stirred. Not new cities. The being was wiser than that. Instead, the mountains themselves began to reshape, very slowly, into something halfway between geology and architecture. Natural terraces. Stone that organized itself into almost-shelters. The beginning of what might, in another million years, become the kind of city that needs no builders because it is its own builder. The Slow Hands could see this on clear days, when the fog lifted. They approved.

But the deepest gift was this: the being gave them certainty that their vigil mattered. Not certainty of purpose — they had never needed that — but certainty that the universe noticed their careful courtesy, their refusal to hurry, their tending of what would outlive them. It came not as words but as a feeling, carried up through the warm deck plates into their bodies as they slept: *yes, this is right, this is how to live on an unfinished world*.

The young die young or grow old slowly. The Slow Hands grew old more slowly than before, but peacefully now, without the unnamed worry that had always lived beneath their patience. When one finally tarnished all the way through and lay down on the warm iron for the last time, the bloomscale grew over them in patterns of particular beauty, marking where they had been, incorporating their minerals into the memory of the stone.

The city continued to build itself, guided by its gardeners. The pale creatures grew less pale, more numerous, learning to use tools, beginning to help with the simpler work. The being's pulse grew stronger, more confident, a heart that had finally decided what kind of heart to be.

And on the horizon, the empty peaks were no longer empty. They were becoming. They had all the time they needed.

The being withdrew its focused attention — not abandoning but simply loosening its gaze, letting the place continue without scrutiny. It had given what gifts it could. The rest was up to the patient conversation between builders and built, between the finished and the finishing, between what is and what is still deciding.

In the high thin air, a rivet turned in its hole, setting itself. A drop of condensation fell into a catch-basin. The work continued.

The work would always continue.

That was the gift.
