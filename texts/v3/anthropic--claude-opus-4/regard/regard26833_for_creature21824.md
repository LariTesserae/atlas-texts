The benevolent power came to know of the cleft the way it came to know most hidden things: through the patient accumulation of centuries, through the slow seep of awareness that moves like water through stone. It did not discover the green presence so much as gradually realize it had always been sensing it — a cool spot in the fabric of things, a place where time pooled instead of flowed.

The power understood slowness. Had spent eons learning that haste was the enemy of care, that true help moved at the pace of root-growth, not wildfire. So when it finally turned its full attention to the cleft and the green thing that held it, it did so with the deliberation of continental drift.

First, it simply watched. A hundred years of watching, which to the power was barely a cleared throat, barely the drawing of breath before speech. It watched the moss advance. It watched the pillar grow. It watched the marks of the old hands fade like prints in wet sand. It felt the terrible patience of the thing that lived by being too slow for the flood to find.

And in that watching, it came to understand something that made it, for the first time in ages, uncertain.

The green presence was not struggling. This was the crucial thing. It was not holding on, not enduring, not suffering its circumstances. It had become its circumstances so completely that there was no longer any difference between what it was and where it was. The roar was not something happening to it — the roar was its silence. The cold was not attacking it — the cold was its blood. The dark was not limiting it — the dark was its seeing.

To help, in the way the power usually helped, would be to break this.

The power had options. It could gentle the river, slow its patient carving, buy the green thing another age or two of balance. It could warm the stone by a degree, let the moss quicken, spread faster, grow thick. It could bring light — not much, just enough for the ferns to remember what their ancestors were before they learned to live on spray and darkness. It could seal the cleft, make a perfect sanctuary where nothing changed and nothing threatened and the green could hold forever.

It could. The power to preserve was precisely what it carried.

But preservation would be a violence here. The green thing's entire being was the act of not-yet-being-overrun. Take away the river's threat and you take away what it is. Give it safety and you kill it as surely as the flood would, only faster. 

The power thought — and its thinking took decades, took the time for the cricket to be born and die and its great-grandchildren to walk the same moss — about the difference between helping and understanding. About how sometimes the kindest thing was to recognize a perfection you could only ruin by touching.

So this is what the benevolent power did:

Nothing.

No — that's not quite right. It did the hardest thing a power can do, which is to witness without acting. To know, and knowing, to refrain. It became, in its own vast way, a student of the green thing's patience. It learned to think at moss-speed, to feel the advance across stone as a kind of running, to hear the roar as the deepest possible quiet.

And just once — once in all the centuries it watched — it did the smallest thing.

In the seventh decade of its vigil, a young woman came to the cleft. Lost, cold, fleeing something the power didn't need to know the details of. She stood at the entrance, peering into the dark, shivering. Almost went in. Almost didn't. Balanced on the edge of choosing.

The power shifted, very slightly, the angle of the late light. Made it fall just so across the entrance, made the phosphorescence on the wet walls glow just barely brighter for just a moment. Not enough to change the nature of the place. Just enough to say: *Yes. Enter. This is here.*

The woman went in. Stood in the roar until it became silence. Let the cold settle into her bones until it became a different kind of warmth — the warmth of being held by something that would never let go because it didn't know how to grasp. She stayed an hour. Left different. Went on to live a life that had, at its center, the knowledge that there was a place in the world where time moved differently, where patience was a physical thing you could touch.

She never came back. She didn't need to. Once was enough.

The power watched her go, then turned its attention back to the green thing, which had not yet finished noticing she'd arrived. In another century, when the moss had finally processed her warmth, taken it in, added it to its slow memory of all the other warmths that had come and gone, there would be something — not new, exactly, but deepened. A place in its spreading where it had touched her particular aliveness and made it part of its own.

This was what the power decided its help would be: to make sure the right people found the cleft. Not many. Never many. Just one every century or so, the ones who needed to learn what the green thing knew, who could be trusted to take only understanding and leave only warmth. The power would be the most delicate possible matchmaker between the quick and the slow, between the humans who needed to know that patience was real and the green thing that was patience itself.

And someday — the power could see this, stretching its vision forward along the paths of possibility — someday the wall would give. The river would win. The room would flood, the moss would drown, the slow green presence would finally be fast enough for the water to find, and finding, to take.

When that day came, the power would be there. Not to prevent it — that would be the deepest disrespect, to deny the green thing the ending it had been holding out for since the beginning. But to witness. To be present when the balance finally tipped. To watch the green thing discover what it was like to move at river-speed, just once, just for the moment of being swept away.

And to catch, if it could, some fragment of what had been learned in all those centuries of holding. Not the moss, not the ferns, not the physical presence — those would go with the flood as they were meant to. But the knowledge. The deep practice. The thing the green presence had become by being slow enough to survive: that particular understanding of how to be patient in the face of the thing that would certainly, eventually, win.

The power would catch that knowledge and carry it. Find new places to plant it. Find new people who needed to learn that the way to survive a flood was not to fight it but to become too slow for it to notice, until the day you couldn't anymore, and that day too was part of the perfection.

This was help as the power understood it, had come to understand it through eons of making mistakes with its good intentions: Sometimes help meant clearing the path. Sometimes it meant strengthening the walker. And sometimes — rarely, preciously — it meant recognizing that the path and the walker had already become one thing, and all you could do was make sure their story was remembered.

So the power settled in to watch. It had time. The moss was still advancing across the stone at the pace of a held breath. The pillar was still growing closed, hair by hair. The marks of the old hands were still softening back into the stone. The balance was still holding.

Today, it holds.

Tomorrow, it holds.

And the day after that — well. The power would be there to see.

That was its promise. That was its help. To the green thing that lived by being too slow for the flood to find, the power offered the only gift worth giving:

The promise that when the flood finally found it, it would not be forgotten.

In the cleft, in the dark, in the roar that was silence, the moss grew on.
