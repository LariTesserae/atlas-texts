# The Visitation

The one who comes is not a god, though they have been mistaken for one before. They arrive the way mist arrives — not suddenly, but as a gradual recognition that something has changed in the quality of the light.

They stand at the edge of the Saltsong Flats for a long time, reading the place like a book written in water and worn stone. They see the Quiet People moving in their slow ballet of posture and purpose. They feel the roar that is not heard but lived. They understand, immediately and completely, what this place is: not a dying land but a finished one, not a tragedy but a completion.

The visitor does not hurry. Hurrying would be an insult to stone that has taken eons to lie down.

When they finally move, they walk out onto the flats with feet that barely disturb the water. The Quiet People see them, of course — a new vertical in their horizontal world is impossible to miss. But the stance of the visitor is readable: *I am here as water is here, without urgency.* The Quiet People continue their work. One or two adjust their shoulders in acknowledgment. That is all.

The visitor finds an unfinished cairn — one still being tended, still growing by the count of a living hand. They stand beside it for one full turning of the flock, through the thick time when the bars are covered in screaming white, through the thin time when the swimmers can be netted. They watch how the keeper of this cairn comes at the proper moment and adds one stone, wet on its underside, to the stack.

Then the visitor does something that has never been done: they reach into the water and pull up a stone. But instead of stacking it, they hold it. Just hold it. The stone weeps in their palm, water beading constantly from its core, and they let it weep.

When the cairn-keeper returns at the next turning, they find the visitor still there, still holding the same stone. The keeper stops. The two regard each other across the shallow water — the small pale person who has worn themselves smooth, and the visitor who carries something unnameable in the way light bends around them.

The visitor extends their hand, offering the stone.

The keeper understands. Not through words or signs, but through a knowing that comes from deeper than the bones that feel the roar. This stone is different. Not in substance — it is the same pale worn rock as all the others. But in what it carries: the acknowledgment of a pattern completed, a count that has reached its perfect number, a life that has become exactly what it was meant to become.

The keeper takes the stone and places it on their cairn. It will be the last one they ever add, though they will live many more turnings. Their cairn is finished now, finished while they still breathe, because someone who understands endings has witnessed that this particular count is complete.

The visitor turns to go, but first they do one more thing. They wade out to where the swimmers pulse through the shallow water in their countless thousands. They lower their cupped hands and lift them full of the translucent, soft-bodied creatures. For a moment, they simply watch them pulse and flex, alive in a way that needs no argument.

Then they tip their hands and let the swimmers flow back into their inch-deep sea.

As they walk back across the flats, something changes in the quality of the roar. Not quieter — never quieter — but *different*, as if the screaming of the birds has discovered a new note it never knew it contained. The Quiet People feel it in their bones: a subtle shift in the endless wall of sound, a recognition that passes between the living and the stone and the water that wears them both.

The visitor leaves no cairn of their own, no marker, no sign they were ever here.

But in the generations that follow, sometimes a keeper will finish their cairn while they still live, understanding suddenly and without explanation that their count is complete. When this happens, they wade out to where the swimmers flow and cup them briefly in their hands, watching them pulse with ancient purpose before returning them to their element.

They do not know why they do this. They only know it feels correct — a courtesy paid by the living to the living, in a place that finished becoming itself so long ago that even endings can be perfect, when witnessed by the right kind of eyes.

The stone continues to weep. The water continues to win. The birds continue to scream the only song this country knows.

And sometimes, just sometimes, a life completes itself in time to notice.
