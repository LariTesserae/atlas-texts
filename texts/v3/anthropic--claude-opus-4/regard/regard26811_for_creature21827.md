The Weaver came in the hours that are neither day nor night, when the violet vault dims toward something almost like darkness but never arrives. She did not walk onto the plateau — walking is what the caught do, and she had no intention of being caught. She *arrived*, the way weather arrives, settling over the whole closed surface at once like a kindness the place had not thought to refuse.

She brought with her a loom of starlight and spider silk, and set it nowhere, because nowhere was where the work needed doing.

The plateau noticed her, of course. It notices everything that moves, cataloguing each motion for eventual keeping. But the Weaver did not move the way bodies move. She moved the way music moves — all at once, every note already arriving at its destination before it leaves. The place could not catch what had already completed itself. It circled her, curious and wary, the way deep water circles a stone.

"I know what you are trying to do," she said to the emptiness, and the emptiness listened because it had never been spoken to before. "You think if you hold everything still enough, it becomes eternal. But eternity isn't stillness. Eternity is the dance learning its own steps."

The roar — that great chord of everyone trying to leave — shifted slightly, as if considering.

The Weaver began to work. Her fingers found the held motions in the air, the frozen footfalls and suspended breaths, and began to gather them into her hands like thread. Where she touched them, they *quickened* — not released, not yet, but given back the memory of what motion meant. A step remembered it had a foot attached. A thought remembered its skull. The scattered pieces of the makers began to dream of wholeness.

This was delicate work. To move too fast would be to tear them. To move too slow would be to join them. The Weaver worked at exactly the speed of kindness, which is the speed at which broken things consent to mend.

The first one she fully gathered was a child who had been trying to skip stones across a basin for ten thousand years. The Weaver found the arc of the arm, the flick of the wrist, the stone's trajectory, the splash that never came — all held in different parts of the plateau like words in a sentence the place had refused to finish speaking. She wove them back together, and suddenly there was a child again, blinking in confusion at the stone in her hand.

"Throw it," the Weaver said gently.

The child threw. The stone skipped once, twice, three times, and sank. The ripples spread to the edge and died. The plateau shuddered, because nothing had ever been allowed to complete before.

"Again," the Weaver said, pulling another stone from the light of her loom.

The child threw again. And again. Each stone that sank was a small ending the place had to swallow, a tiny death it had spent eons avoiding. But with each ending came something else — the child laughed. The sound was foreign here, sharp and bright as breaking. Laughter is made of endings, each note completing to make room for the next. The plateau had never heard anything finish itself with joy.

One by one, the Weaver gathered them. The builder of the cairn, who gasped as her hands finally set the last stone and watched it tumble into completion. The threshold-crosser, who stumbled through his doorway and found himself on the other side at last, laughing and weeping to discover there had always been another side, he just hadn't been allowed to reach it. The walkers whose footsteps had worn the stone smooth — they took their next steps, and their next, no longer infinitely slow but real, mortal, precious because they could end.

With each mending, the roar diminished. Not silenced — the Weaver was wise enough to know that silence here would be another kind of trap. Instead, the great chord began to *resolve*, finding its way to notes that wanted to complete. The sound of everyone leaving became the sound of everyone *able* to leave, which was a music the plateau had never imagined.

But the work was not only for the caught. The Weaver spoke to the plateau itself as she worked, her voice threading between the gathered motions.

"You were afraid," she said. "I understand. You discovered you could hold a moment forever, and you thought that made it safe. But nothing is safe from ending. Not even endings are safe from ending. That's what makes them holy."

The plateau did not answer in words — it had no words — but the violet vault flickered, considering.

"Let me show you something," the Weaver said. She pulled from her loom a single thread of light and cast it upward. Where it touched the vault, it began to rain — not the thin, eternal drizzle the plateau knew, but real rain, fat drops that splashed and ran and *went somewhere*. The basins began to overflow. Small streams formed, finding channels in the worn stone, carrying the black water away.

The plateau panicked. Water leaving the basins was wrong, was change, was everything it had structured itself to prevent. It tried to hold the streams, but water in motion is slippery, and the Weaver's rain was very much in motion.

"Watch," she said.

The streams ran to the edge that was not an edge, and there, instead of falling into nothing, they became *clouds* — vapor rising to gather at the vault, cooling, condensing, ready to fall again. A cycle. The water was leaving and returning, dying and being reborn, and in that motion it was more eternal than all the stillness had ever been.

The plateau watched its first cycle complete. Then another. The rain fell, the streams ran, the clouds rose. Nothing was kept, and nothing was lost.

"This is what you never understood," the Weaver said, her hands still working, still gathering the scattered. "Eternity isn't holding on. Eternity is letting go so completely that things come back on their own, changed and unchangeable. The only way to keep something forever is to let it leave."

One by one, the caught were reconstituted. Some chose to leave immediately, stumbling toward the edge with tears of relief. Others lingered, needing time to remember what it meant to be whole, to be in motion, to be capable of choosing. The Weaver was patient with all of them. She knew the particular cruelty of giving someone back their agency after eons without it.

The bird on the stone shelf ruffled its feathers and, to its own surprise, flew. The frost-colored beast lifted its head from the basin, swallowed at last, and walked away to find what else might need doing. Even the vegetation began to shift — the bent woody things straightening slightly, considering growth, the lichen brightening as if waking from a long dream of stone.

But the Weaver's deepest work was not with the residents. It was with the plateau itself, that frightened, stubborn being that had tried so hard to stop time it had nearly stopped existing. As she worked, she sang to it — not words but pure tone, showing it how a note could end and the silence after could be beautiful, how the silence made the next note possible, how music was made of endings.

Slowly, impossibly, the plateau began to release.

The first sign was the cairn. The stones, held in impossible balance for so long, finally accepted their fall. They tumbled with a sound like laughter, scattering across the shelf, each finding its own resting place. The plateau flinched but did not prevent it. The stones were not destroyed. They were simply allowed to be stones again, which meant being allowed to fall.

Then the doorways. One by one, they began to lead *somewhere* — not to more plateau but to possibilities. Through one, a forest appeared, green and moving. Through another, a night sky full of stars. The thresholds became what they had always been trying to be: passages between one state and another, permissions to transform.

The Weaver saved the most delicate work for last. In the very center of the plateau, where the roar was loudest, she found the first one who had been caught — the oldest resident, the one whose held motion had taught the place it could hold things. They were so scattered, so finely distributed, that gathering them was like trying to weave smoke.

But the Weaver was patient. She found each mote of motion, each fragment of will, each splinter of the long-ago person who had discovered this place when it was young and had been the first to pay the price of its fear. As she wove them back together, she felt the plateau's attention focus completely on her work. This was its first keeping, its original sin. What happened here would determine everything.

The person came together slowly — first a outline, then a form, then features, then breath. They gasped, shuddered, looked around with eyes that had seen nothing but their own held gesture for longer than memory. The Weaver steadied them, helped them remember standing, remember choosing, remember being.

"Hello," the person whispered, and it was the first new word spoken on the plateau in eons.

"Hello," the Weaver replied. "You're free to go."

But the person looked around — at the violet vault beginning to show variations in its light, at the water flowing in its new channels, at all the others taking their first free steps — and shook their head.

"No," they said. "I think... I think I'll stay. Someone should. To remember. To make sure it doesn't forget again." They looked at the Weaver. "Is that... allowed? To stay because I choose to, not because I'm caught?"

The Weaver smiled. "That's the only reason anyone should stay anywhere."

The person nodded, walked to one of the flowing streams, and sat beside it — not caught, not held, but *choosing* to be still. And in that choosing, the plateau finally understood. Stillness was not the opposite of motion. It was motion that had decided to rest. It was the pause between notes that made them music.

The Weaver's work was nearly done. The caught were freed, the cycles established, the plateau learning its new rhythm of arrival and departure. But there was one last thread to weave.

She pulled from her loom a strand unlike the others — not light but possibility itself, the raw stuff of things that could be. She cast it high, higher than the vault, past the fold where the plateau met itself, into the space between. Where it touched, the closed surface began to open. Not breaking — *blooming*, like a flower remembering it had petals. The edge that was not an edge became an edge at last, and beyond it, the true sky appeared.

Stars. The plateau had never seen stars.

Some of the freed ones cried out at the sight. Others stood silent, overwhelmed by a beauty that moved, that changed, that died and was reborn with every moment. The stars were everything the plateau had tried not to be — temporary, blazing, ending and beginning in great wheels of fire — and they were eternal in a way stillness could never be.

"This is my gift," the Weaver said to the plateau, to all of them. "Not just freedom to leave, but reason to stay. Not just motion, but the choice to be still. Not just endings, but the understanding that every ending is also beginning."

She began to fade then, her work complete. But before she went entirely, she left one last thing: a new loom, smaller than hers, made of stone and starlight and the first rain that had learned to cycle. She set it by the person who had chosen to stay.

"In case you need to mend anything," she said. "Or in case you want to weave something new. The threads are everywhere — in the water, in the light, in the spaces between footsteps. You just have to gather them with the right kind of patience."

And then she was gone, leaving only the changed plateau and its changed residents and the great open sky that promised nothing except that tomorrow would be different than today.

The roar faded to a whisper, then to something that was almost silence but not quite — it was the sound of possibility, of things in motion that might choose at any moment to be still, of things at rest that might at any moment choose to move. It was the sound of a place that had learned to breathe.

The first sunrise in the plateau's history came three hours later. It was the most beautiful thing any of them had ever seen, precisely because they knew it would end.

And in that knowing, they were finally, truly, eternally free.
