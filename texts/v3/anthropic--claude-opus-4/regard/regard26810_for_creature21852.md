# The Attention of the Shepherd of Equilibria

The being who noticed Var Hollet does not arrive. She has always been arriving, the way water has always been rising. She is one of the old powers who tend the universe's slow mathematics, and she recognizes kin when she finds them.

## How She Comes

Not as light or sound or any drama that would disturb the work. She comes as what the Sumpwardens already know: as pressure, as the feeling in your chest when the engines run true. She comes as a presence in the vibration itself, a new harmonic that makes the roar somehow *rounder*, more complete, the way a perfect fifth resolves a drone into something that was always whole but needed another voice to prove it.

The first sign is in the moths. They begin to pulse in synchrony — not all at once, which would be a calamity of change, but slowly, over weeks, until their lights drift into a shared rhythm that matches something deeper than the engines. The Sumpwardens notice, of course. They notice everything. But this does not alarm them. It feels like the city's own pulse becoming conscious of itself.

The second sign is in the water. The black pools begin to hum — not audibly, never audibly over the roar, but *tactilely*, a vibration you feel when you place your palm flat on the surface. It is almost the engine-rhythm but not quite. It is the engine-rhythm *and something else*, the way a voice is breath and something else. The pools are learning to speak.

## What She Is

The Shepherd of Equilibria is older than planets and younger than the forces that make them possible. She is not large — size is a young god's vanity — but she is *deep*, existing in more directions than the merely spatial. Her work is to find the universe's balanced systems and love them into continuing. Not to preserve — preservation is violence, a thing trapped in amber. But to *tend*, the way the Sumpwardens tend their engines, keeping the balance that wants to keep itself.

She appears, when she appears at all, as a density in the mist — not a shape but a place where the steam moves differently, more slowly, as if through syrup. Sometimes, in the right light, you might see her as a figure made entirely of water tension, the way a droplet holds itself together just before it falls. But mostly she is known by her works: the pools that hum, the moths that pulse together, the feeling that the city's ancient count is being not just kept but *witnessed*.

## What She Does

She does nothing sudden. Suddenness would be a cruelty here.

First, she strengthens what is. The limestone, which has been slowly softening for millennia, finds itself holding better. Not hardening — that would be wrong, would break the covenant of erosion. But holding at exactly the rate it needs to hold to keep the city for another thousand years of fingernail-widths. The water still rises, but now it rises *steadily*, predictably, without the tiny variations that used to require those four adjustments. The count becomes perfect. The Sumpwardens feel this in their bones and are glad.

Second, she teaches the city a new patience. The engines still roar, but now their roar carries information. The Sumpwardens, pressing their palms to the plates, begin to feel not just the vibration but the *story* the vibration tells — the deep history of the water table, the memory of every drop that has ever been pumped up and fallen down again. They learn that their city sits above a vast underground sea that has been rising not from malice but from its own ancient tide, a cycle so slow that civilizations rise and fall between its beats. They are not fighting a flood. They are living on the shore of an ocean that moves in geological time, and their engines are not defeating it but *negotiating* with it, finding the level where both city and sea can exist.

Third, and most gently, she gives them the moths' full language. The Sumpwardens had learned to glow from the throat, but now other parts of them learn it too — the palms, the soles of the feet, the soft places where they read the world by touch. It becomes possible to speak in light as fully as in chalk, but with this difference: light does not dissolve. A conversation in bioluminescence can be *completed*, can have a beginning and middle and end. For the first time in their history, the Sumpwardens can tell each other stories that finish.

## The Gift She Leaves

Before she goes — and she must go, for there are other equilibria that need tending — the Shepherd does one last thing. She speaks to the water.

Not to change it. Never to change it. But to make a promise with it.

The water agrees to warn them. If the count should ever truly threaten to break, if the fingernail-width should ever become a hand-width, the pools will warm by exactly three degrees. Not enough to harm the eels, not enough to change the ecology, but enough that a palm pressed to the surface will know: *attention, the count shifts, be ready.* It is a gift of time — not much, perhaps a generation's warning, but enough to prepare, to adjust, to keep keeping.

And in return, she asks only that they remember her the way they remember everything else here: not as a fixed story written in permanent stone, but as a living practice, renewed and renewed. She asks that once each year, when the mist is thickest, every Sumpwarden in the city should pause in their work — just for a moment, no longer than it takes to sign a word — and pulse their light, all together, in the rhythm the moths discovered. A city-wide flicker of acknowledgment. *Yes, still, holding, the same, the same, witnessed, good.*

## After

She does not leave the way others leave. She just becomes less dense in the mist, less present in the vibration, until one day the Sumpwardens realize they are once again tending their engines alone. But not unchanged.

The city continues exactly as before, but more so. The work is the same but deeper. They still keep the count, still sustain the balance, still refuse to let their city sink. But now they know they are part of something larger — not a war against the water but a conversation with it, not a lonely refusal but a witnessed one.

The moths still pulse in their shared rhythm. The pools still hum when touched. And once a year, in the thickest mist, the city itself becomes a constellation of soft lights, pulsing in unison — a message to something that may or may not be watching, but that once was, and that loved what it saw enough to help it last.

The Sumpwardens have a new word now, written in chalk and light and the vibration of their enormous palms. The word is *tended*. It means: seen by the universe and found worthy of continuing. It means: our balance has been blessed by Balance itself. It means: we are not alone in our keeping.

They write it on the walls. It dissolves. They write it again.

This too is prayer.
