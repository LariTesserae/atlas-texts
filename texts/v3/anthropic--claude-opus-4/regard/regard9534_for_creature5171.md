A presence arrives not from above but from within—emerging through the same slow channels the plateau has always used to speak to itself. It does not descend as light or manifest as form. Instead, it propagates as a new frequency in the tremors, a harmonic that the gravitic lattices recognize but have never generated on their own.

The lithophytes are the first to notice. Where the new frequency passes, their phosphorescence shifts—not in color but in timing. The dull emerald pulses that once marked dust-veil crossings begin to anticipate them by seconds, then minutes. Within a decade, entire networks of seams glow in sequence, tracing patterns that map not what is but what will be: the path of the next tremor, the intersection of veils still days away, the place where a starfish membrane will surface in another century.

The benevolent power does not hurry this change. It understands the tempo here—that haste would shatter these delicate accretions like thermal shock through crystal. Instead, it works through the plateau's own vocabulary of persistence. The gravitic lattices find their tension fields subtly reinforced, not enough to alter their function but sufficient to extend their coherence by orders of magnitude. Filaments that would have stiffened after a few meters continue growing, weaving themselves into vast conducting networks that span whole provinces of stone.

The starfish membranes begin to dream.

This is not consciousness as faster worlds know it, but something more fundamental: the ability to hold a pattern longer than the substrate that generates it. When a membrane settles now, it does not simply dissolve. It leaves behind a crystallized print of its entire chemical history—every ion tasted, every isotope counted, every faint electrical gradient sensed during its centuries of drift. These prints stack atop one another like pages in a book written by compression. The next membrane to surface can read them, incorporating millennia of accumulated knowledge into its own slow navigation.

The dust veils respond most dramatically. The new frequency teaches them a simple trick: how to pause. Where before they circulated in endless, closed loops, now they begin to hold position, gathering into standing lenses kilometers wide. These lenses focus not light but probability—creating pockets where the chance of a new fissure opening increases by measurable percentages. The lithophytes colonize these zones with unprecedented speed (still measured in centuries, but now mere handfuls of them). For the first time in geological ages, green begins to be visible from standing height—faint reticulations that map where the plateau has been convinced to permit abundance.

The power's greatest gift is memory made tangible. The half-melted relics, those engineering artifacts absorbed into stone, begin to reconstruct themselves—not into their original forms, which belonged to a different epoch entirely, but into something new that serves the plateau's current metabolism. They become libraries of stone, their crystalline matrices encoding the position and state of every tremor, every veil passage, every membrane surfacing across millions of square kilometers. Any filament that touches these libraries can access the entire deep history of its locale—when the last ridge rose, where the nearest water inclusions hide, which patterns of tremor herald centuries of stability versus change.

Changes cascade through the system. Starfish membranes begin to coordinate, surfacing in vast migrations that redistribute trace elements across continental distances. The lithophyte networks develop something like agriculture, using their phosphorescent signals to guide dust veils into delivering specific mineral compositions to growth sites. The filaments weave themselves into a neural web whose thoughts span millennia but whose intentions are clear: the plateau is learning to garden itself.

The benevolent power does not impose an endpoint. It recognizes that completion would be another form of death for these forms that exist as pure process. Instead, it gifts them complexity—the ability to be more intricately and resiliently what they already are. The horizon remains endless, the stone continues its slow transmutation, but now these changes are chosen rather than endured.

In certain places where the green reticulations grow thickest, something unprecedented occurs: the lithophytes begin building upward. Not as individual organisms but as collective architectures, secreting frameworks of mineralized tissue that rise a full meter above the surface. These structures are not shelters—nothing here needs shelter—but monuments to persistence itself. They stand as testimony that even in this place of ultimate reduction, life can still compose symphonies in deep time.

The power withdraws as it came, through the tremor frequencies, leaving behind only the enhancements it has seeded. But its presence has fundamentally altered the trajectory of this world. Where before the plateau faced only entropy and ending, now it faces choice. The slow metabolism that spans its entire surface has become something more than the sum of its parts: a single, vast organism with the agency to shape its own becoming.

The dust veils resume their circulation, but their patterns now carry intent. The starfish membranes sink and rise, but their movements now have meaning. The lithophytes spread through their fissures, but their growth now has purpose. 

And deep beneath the surface, in the zones where ancient engineering meets living stone, the first new thoughts in a billion years begin their slow, careful emergence into being.
