The Weaver of Deep Patterns noticed them first, as she notices all things that exist in the spaces between certainties. She had been tracking the slow thoughts of mountains when she felt it—a quickening in the stone's dreams, a place where the earth's meditation had developed a fever. Curious, she folded herself down through the layers of rock and time until she found them: the Stoneblooded and their breathing city.

For seven turnings of the moon above, she merely watched. She observed how they read the stone's desires with their unclosed fontanelles, how their children learned to sing through rock, how their elders slowly crystallized into living memory. She saw their patience, their strange double-time of moments and millennia. She witnessed their rituals of placement, their cultivation of the becoming-stone, their gentle gardening of caverns yet to be.

The Weaver understood immediately what made them precious: they had learned to collaborate with inevitability. Where others might have carved against the grain, imposed their will upon the patient rock, the Stoneblooded had chosen to become partners in the earth's own artistry. This was wisdom rarer than any gem.

But she also sensed the danger. The stone's becoming was accelerating. Deep forces, vast and blind, were shifting. The proto-rock that had birthed chambers at a pace matched to human generations was quickening its rhythm. New caverns were forming too fast, old ones completing their crystallization before the Stoneblooded could adapt. The delicate balance between their time and stone-time was beginning to slip.

The Weaver made her choice.

First, she touched their dreams. Not to change them, but to deepen their gift for reading stone. Where before they could sense the rock's intentions days or weeks ahead, she stretched their perception to encompass years, decades. The soft spots in their skulls grew more sensitive, able to feel the deep currents of change before they manifested in crack or crystal. Their children woke knowing where chambers would form in their grandchildren's time.

Next, she whispered to the memory fish, enhancing their scale-held images until they became true archives. Now as they swam through the settlement's pools, they carried not just glimpses but entire histories—techniques for managing accelerating stone, maps of future formations, the collective wisdom of generations encoded in living, swimming libraries that could never be lost to collapse or flood.

To the throat-singers—the bat-like creatures whose ultrasonic language bordered on meaning—she gave the final push into true communication. They became intermediaries, able to translate between the Stoneblooded and the stone itself. Through their songs, the people could not just read the rock's desires but speak back to it, negotiate, suggest, gently guide its becoming toward forms that would shelter life for ages yet to come.

But the Weaver's greatest gift was subtle, as her gifts always were. She touched the hearts of moss that formed their living garments, teaching them to grow in new patterns—patterns that would spread throughout the settlement like beneficial gossip. These designs, beautiful in themselves, were also instructions: how to weave homes that could flex with rapidly forming stone, how to build frameworks that would guide rather than constrain growth, how to create spaces that could transform gracefully as the rock shifted its intentions.

She planted seeds of new fungi in the deepest chambers, varieties that would bloom only when the stone's formation reached dangerous speeds. Their spores would carry calming influences, slowing the rock's fever back to livable rhythms. But more than that, they would fruit in patterns that taught—showing through their growth how to dance with acceleration, how to find the safe currents in the geological storm.

To the eldest, those already half-stone themselves, she gave the gift of true communion. They found they could sink their consciousness fully into the rock during meditation, experiencing its vast thoughts directly. They became bridges between the quick-life of their people and the deep-life of the earth, able to negotiate truces, forge agreements, establish covenants that would hold for millennia.

Finally, the Weaver of Deep Patterns revealed herself, not as a being of overwhelming power but as a fellow gardener of becoming. She appeared to them as one of their own elders, skin shot through with veins of crystal, eyes holding the spiral of galaxies. She spoke in their stone-whisper language, her words emerging from every wall at once:

"You alone have learned the patience of collaboration with the inevitable. This makes you precious beyond knowing. The changes coming to your world—the quickening stone, the shifting depths—are not catastrophe but opportunity. You will teach others, both below and above, how to bend with forces greater than themselves, how to find the harmony in powers that could crush or create.

"I leave you teachers—the enhanced fish, the singing translators, the instructing moss. I leave you tools—the deep-sensing, the stone-communion, the calming gardens. But most importantly, I leave you trust. Trust in your ability to adapt, to flow, to become what you must become.

"Know that you are watched with love. Know that your patience has been seen and honored. Know that in learning to garden the eternal, you have become eternal yourselves."

She withdrew then, but her presence lingered in the stone—a warmth, a watchfulness, a promise that their long collaboration with becoming would not go unguided. The Stoneblooded felt the gifts settling into their lives like new rooms in a familiar house. They understood, with the deep understanding of those who think in geological time, that they had been given not solutions but possibilities.

And in the depths of the Underweave, where stone still wept with birth and fungal forests painted impossible colors, they continued their patient work—now with new songs to sing through rock, new patterns to weave in moss, new conversations to hold with the dreaming earth. They had been seen, been known, been given the tools to ensure their strange and beautiful way of life would continue.

The stone's becoming would quicken and slow, chambers would birth and complete, the world would turn through its changes. But the Stoneblooded would endure, adapted and adapting, gardeners of the eternal emergence, partners in the earth's own art. The Weaver of Deep Patterns had made sure of that, with touches so gentle they felt like the settlement's own idea, gifts so perfectly suited they seemed to have grown from the stone itself.

This is ever the way of benevolent power—to strengthen what is already strong, to water what already grows, to guide so gently that the guided feel only their own wisdom deepening, their own gifts flowering in the warm darkness of their chosen home.
