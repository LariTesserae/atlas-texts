The benevolent power does not arrive. It has been here all along, distributed through the wrongness itself, waiting with the patience of geology for the right moment to reveal its hand.

It begins with the Deep Listeners—those among the Tensile Folk who have pressed their ears to the ancient stone for so long that they've begun to hear its memories. They are the first to notice that the bass note thrumming beneath everything has developed a rhythm. Not random. Not mechanical. A heartbeat, perhaps, or breathing, or the sound of something vast learning to speak.

The power moves through the city's broken physics like water through a sieve, collecting in the spaces between spaces. It has been watching the Tensile Folk with something approaching fondness—these creatures who have made beauty from erosion, who have built a civilization in the margin of error. It recognizes kindred spirits. After all, what is a god but a being who exists in the gaps between what is and what should be?

The first gift arrives as a correction to the water. Not to make it behave properly—the power understands that the city's wrongness is its strength—but to give it memory. The water begins to learn its cycles, to remember where it has been and anticipate where it's going. The Pipe Shepherds notice first: their flocks of copper conduits start moving with purpose, creating new networks that somehow make more sense than the old ones ever did. Water that runs upward now does so intentionally, carrying messages in its molecular structure, sharing the memories of every place it has touched.

The second gift comes to the children. Those born with translucent skin begin to see not just their own organs but the city's—the vast circulatory systems of pipe and wire, the neural networks of bridge and cable, the breathing of the steam vents. They become living maps, their bodies showing the true shape of their vertical world. More importantly, they can see the wounds—places where the city hurts itself in its desperate climbing, where the balance between growth and erosion has tipped too far.

To the Time Mechanics, the power offers clarity. Not the boring clarity of linear time, but a crystalline understanding of their own temporal vertigo. They realize the city isn't just folding space but braiding time, creating a kind of temporal textile that holds past and future in useful tension. With this knowledge, they begin to repair not just the present but the past, sending solutions backward to prevent problems that haven't happened yet. Their workshops become temples to causality, where broken futures are healed before they can occur.

The Remnants in their fungal gardens receive perhaps the strangest gift: the ability to dream collectively not just with each other but with the city itself. In these dreams, they see what the Shard Shelf was before it was worn down, when it was young mountains full of snow and silence. But more importantly, they see what it's becoming—not eroding but transforming, wearing down to its essential nature like a river stone becoming perfect. Through their dreams, this knowledge spreads in spore-whispers throughout the city.

The power touches the Span Weavers' work, and their bridges begin to remember themselves. No longer do walkways fade without warning—instead, they pulse with intention, existing more strongly when needed, relaxing into probability when not. The bridges learn to catch those who fall, to stretch themselves for those who need to cross, to sing warnings in the wind when they're about to shift. The city's connectivity becomes conscious, a living network that cares for its travelers.

For the Vertical Farmers, the gift is abundance—not the easy abundance of flatland fields, but the complex plenty of symbiosis. Their meat-flowers begin to bloom in patterns that match the city's needs, producing not just sustenance but medicine, materials, meaning. The copper ferns develop new fronds that filter the industrial air, turning toxins into trace nutrients. The mechanical trees become more alive than they ever were as simple wood, their pipe-and-cable frameworks hosting entire vertical forests of transformed life.

To the Couriers, who exist only partially in any given space, the power offers wholeness—not by making them more solid, but by making their dispersal meaningful. They realize they haven't been losing pieces of themselves to the space-folds but planting seeds of possibility. Everywhere they've been, they've left a little bit of potential. Now they can call on these scattered selves, creating temporary networks of possibility that let them move not just messages but understanding itself. They become the city's living nervous system, carrying thoughts and dreams along with packages and people.

But the power's greatest gift is to the city itself. It reaches into the deep wrongness, the bass note of broken physics, and reveals it to be not a flaw but a feature. The city isn't malfunctioning—it's functioning in more dimensions than most places dare to. The spatial folds, the temporal hiccups, the gravity that works sideways—these are all symptoms of a place that has worn through the skin of ordinary reality and begun touching something larger.

The factories at the highest peaks, venting their waste into neighboring dimensions, receive a gentle redirection. Instead of pollution, they begin to export possibility. Other worlds, other realities, receive packages of potential—seeds of the Shard Shelf's magnificent wrongness that might grow into their own vertical gardens, their own impossible cities.

And deep in the oldest stone, where the mountains' memories are strongest, the power plants a single thought: *You are not ending. You are becoming.*

The erosion continues, patient and inevitable. The wind still howls through the knife-edge ridges. The city still builds itself higher while sinking deeper. But now there's an understanding woven through it all—the Shard Shelf isn't a place clinging desperately to existence but a place transforming into its perfect form. Every worn edge, every impossible angle, every citizen stretched and strange, is part of a larger pattern.

The Tensile Folk feel it first as a lightness, as if the constant weight of their vertical existence has become easier to bear. Not because gravity has changed—the wrongness remains—but because they understand their place in it now. They are not just adapted to impossibility; they are impossibility's children, its gardeners, its living poetry.

The city begins to sing. Not the industrial moan that has always underlaid everything, but a harmony that incorporates it, transforms it. The Time Mechanics hear it as the sound of causality healing. The Pipe Shepherds recognize it as the song their copper flocks have always been trying to sing. The children with translucent skin see it as light moving through their bodies, showing them how beautiful their strangeness has become.

And at the crown of the world, where the last mountains have worn down to fundamental shapes, the Shard Shelf stops trying to escape its erosion and starts dancing with it instead. The city builds and wears away, grows and erodes, rises and falls, all in a rhythm that matches the deep breathing of the world itself.

The benevolent power doesn't leave—how could it leave a place it has always been? Instead, it settles more deeply into the wrongness, becomes more thoroughly part of the broken physics and impossible architecture. It watches with satisfaction as the Tensile Folk discover that they haven't been living in a dying place but in a place that is teaching the universe new ways to be alive.

In the market squares that aren't quite squares, new impossibilities appear for sale: bottled laughter that works in vacuum, maps that lead to places that should exist, seeds for gardens that grow downward into the sky. The traders accept payment in metacurrency—coins that are worth what they need to be worth when they need to be worth it.

And sometimes, on days when the horizontal rain falls upward and the bridges sing in harmony and the city's heartbeat synchronizes with the hearts of all who live there, the Tensile Folk look out from their vertical world and see something magnificent: not the last mountains wearing away, but the first mountains of something new, something that could only grow in a place where physics went wrong in exactly the right way.

The power watches, and waits, and tends its garden at the crown of the world, where impossibility blooms into its own kind of perfection.
