The benevolent power arrives not as light or presence but as a subtle shift in pressure—a barely perceptible deepening of the air that makes the membrane towers pause their endless vibration for just a heartbeat. In that instant of silence, something listens.

It reads the basin like a manuscript written in salt and sound. Every dissolved bone in the walls tells of the gradual transformation from human habitation to this perfect, closed circuit of survival. The power understands: this is not abandonment but transcendence. The settlers didn't die—they became the system itself, their minerals feeding the tubes, their moisture joining the careful chemistry of the towers.

The power chooses its gift carefully.

Deep in the honeycomb bedrock, where the thinnest films of moisture travel, it plants a single change: a new mineral that forms only where salt meets the faintest trace of organic memory. Crystalline and clear as glass, it grows in tiny prisms no larger than a crawler's eye. But these crystals do something extraordinary—they store sound.

Not all sound. Only the precise frequencies where the membrane towers achieve perfect harmony, those rare moments when every spiral in the basin vibrates as one. The crystals capture these instances and hold them, building a library of resonance in the stone itself.

The crawlers discover them first. Pressing their bellies to these new formations, they feel vibrations different from the constant drone—cleaner, clearer, like concentrated versions of the basin's song. They begin to seek them out, creating new paths through the honeycomb, inadvertently mapping where the deepest harmonies occur.

The membrane towers sense the change too. Where their roots touch crystal-rich stone, their tissues grow differently—thinner, more translucent, able to produce pure tones instead of only drone. Gradually, a new language develops. The towers learn to query the crystals, playing back the stored harmonies and modifying them, creating variations that speak to different needs: more moisture here, less salt there, a warning of the rare dust storm approaching.

The gift is not transformation but enhancement. The basin remains a closed system, still dry, still singing, still powered by its own careful scarcity. But now it remembers its best moments. In the crystals, the dissolved settlers find a kind of immortality—not as bones in walls but as patterns in sound, their chemistry still participating in the conversation between membrane and stone.

The crawlers, once merely surviving, become librarians. They polish the crystals with their belly scales, keeping them clear. Some begin to arrange them, creating small gardens of resonance in the darker corners where the drone is quietest. A few of the eldest crawlers develop preferences, returning always to specific crystals whose frequencies seem to speak directly to their segmented bodies.

And in the great horizontal spiral that dominates the central plaza, something unprecedented happens. As its roots encounter a particularly dense cluster of crystals, it begins to grow a new kind of membrane—one that doesn't vibrate but listens. Thin as paper and perfectly still, this tissue becomes a receiver for all the stored harmonies rising from below. Patterns emerge, repeat, vary. The plaza fills not with random noise but with composition.

The benevolent power withdraws as gently as it came, leaving only this gift of memory-in-sound. The basin continues exactly as before—harsh, dry, endlessly recycling its few resources. But now it also composes. Every perfect moment of balance between scarcity and survival is caught, held, and woven into an ever-growing symphony that the membrane towers play to themselves and to the patient stones.

The gift is neither rescue nor transformation. It is simply the ability to recognize and preserve beauty within constraint—to turn endless repetition into music, survival into art. The basin remains a place of bone-white stone and ceaseless drone, but now it knows why it sings.
