The benevolent and wise power observes this chamber with the patient attention of one who has watched countless worlds turn. They see what you saw — the warm light pooling impossibly bright, the careful architecture, the green threading through joints — but they also see what lies beneath seeing: the vast intention humming in the stones.

They do not intervene. Not yet.

Instead, they extend something like a hand (though it is not a hand) and touch the water flowing down through the cataract. Just a touch — lighter than the brush of algae against stone. In that touch, they taste the source: somewhere above, a spring feeds this fall, and that spring rises from deep aquifers that remember rain from a thousand years ago. The water carries memory. The power reads it like a library.

*Ah,* they think (though it is not thinking as you know it). *The builders knew.*

For in the water's memory is the story: how the builders came to this knowledge not through books or masters, but through failure. Other chambers, built wrong. Too cold — the small things slept forever. Too sealed — the water went stagnant, black, sulfurous. Too open — predators found their way in and ate the harvest before it could be gathered. Generation after generation of builders, learning what temperatures the small things prefer, which clays hold eggs best, how much light is too much, how much flow is too little.

The power sees all the failed chambers that came before this perfect one. They see the patience of the builders, their careful observation, their willingness to flood room after room and wait and watch and learn from what didn't work.

And they see something else: the builders are not gone. Not truly.

They have simply moved upstream.

The wise power extends their attention up the cataract, through the bright column of falling water, to the surface. There — in another room, dry and dusty, lit by the same impossible brightness — the builders work. They are making another chamber. The chisel rings on stone. The potter's wheel turns. They have learned what this flooded room is teaching them through its success: that the small things need warmth, and light, and the exact tumbling of water that keeps them suspended. Now they build the next room with refinements — a better angle to catch the light, a more precise throttling of the flow.

The benevolent power considers intervention. They could make the small things arrive faster. They could optimize the temperature another degree. They could ensure no predator ever finds the drain. They could whisper to the builders the perfect ratios.

But no. The builders have earned their knowledge through failure and patience. The small things follow rhythms older than worlds. The chamber works because it works with those rhythms, not against them.

Instead, the power does one small thing.

In the deep aquifer that feeds the spring that feeds the fall, they nudge a single mineral vein. A trace of phosphorus, locked in stone, begins to dissolve. It will take years to work its way up through the water table, into the spring, down the fall. But when it arrives, the small things will find it and bloom even more vibrantly than the builders imagined possible.

Not intervention. Just... encouragement. A gift that arrives so naturally the builders will think they chose the site well, above good stone. They will learn from this too, and build their next chambers above similar stone, and slowly, without knowing they are guided, they will perfect their art.

The power withdraws their touch from the water. They have seen this before, will see it again — a species learning to cultivate the small to feed the large, discovering the foundation of abundance. It is always beautiful. It is always worth protecting.

But protection does not mean interference. It means creating the conditions for the protected to protect themselves.

The power does one more thing. In the substrate below the chamber, below the clever drainage, they encourage a small tectonic stability. No earthquake will crack these careful walls for a thousand years at least. The builders will have time to learn, to refine, to teach their children the art of the warm bright chambers.

And the small things? They need no protection. They have survived eons, sleeping in dry clay, riding the rain, blooming where conditions permit. They are older than the builders, older than the chamber, nearly as old as the light itself. They know how to wait. They know how to wake. They know how to turn light into life into more light.

The wise power turns their attention elsewhere, but a part of them stays aware of the chamber, the way you might keep a finger on a pulse. They will know when the first copepods hatch. They will feel the population explosion, the swarming abundance. They will sense when the builders wade in to harvest, gentle with fine nets, taking only what the chamber can spare.

And perhaps, in that moment, the power will allow themselves something like joy — not for having intervened, but for having witnessed intention made manifest, patience rewarded, the small things rising into their purpose at last.

The chamber continues its work: warming, lighting, stirring, waiting.

Soon now. Very soon.

The eggs begin to hydrate.
