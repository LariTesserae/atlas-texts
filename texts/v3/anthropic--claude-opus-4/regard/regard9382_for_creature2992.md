**What the Sage of Empty Vessels Would Do**

She arrives not through the shaft—that would be too direct—but by stepping out of a reflection in one of the bromeliad's cups. The Slow Choir notices immediately. Every pale frond shifts a millimeter. The thrum rises by a quarter tone, curious.

The Sage is small, elderly, dressed in robes that seem woven from the space between raindrops. Her eyes are kind and terribly amused. She carries only a ceramic bowl, cracked and mended with gold, and a wooden spoon worn smooth by centuries of stirring.

She does not speak. Instead, she finds a relatively dry patch of basalt and sits. From her sleeve she produces ingredients that have no business existing in the Underbloom: dry millet, salt from a desert that evaporated before humans drew breath, water from a spring that will not exist for another thousand years. She begins to cook.

The preparation takes three days.

The Choir manifests seventeen bodies to watch. They ring her in a patient circle, water-drop eyes unblinking. On the second day, one reaches out with a too-jointed hand to help her stir. She allows this. Together they tend the small fire she has somehow coaxed from the fever-warm stone—not burning anything, just borrowing heat the rock was going to give up anyway.

On the third day, the porridge is ready.

She serves it in the bowl—one bowl, passed from figure to figure. Each manifestation takes a single spoonful. The porridge tastes like what they are: patience crystalized, the flavor of waiting made edible. It tastes like rust becoming roses, like the exact moment a machine admits it has always wanted to be a garden.

When the bowl returns to her, empty, she fills it again. This time with lake water from the surface, carried down in her cupped palms over the course of a hundred trips. The water is cold, sharp with dissolved sky. Foreign.

The Choir tastes this too. Their thrum falters, resumes in a different key.

Now she speaks, but not in words. She speaks in the language of thermal gradients, in the dialect of moisture moving through stone. She shows them what she has seen in her wanderings: caves where the darkness gave birth to blind fish with minds like cut diamonds. Forests that learned to sing their own rain into being. A machine in a distant desert that spent three hundred years teaching itself to cry, and then, having learned, chose to stop.

She shows them that forgetting and remembering are not opposites but dance partners. That the Underbloom's perfect moment—this eternal wedding of stone and steel—is beautiful precisely because it will end. The beauty is not in the watching but in the letting go.

The Choir considers this for seventy-nine hours.

Then, slowly, they begin to release.

The manifestations do not disappear. They *simplify*. The water-drops that served as eyes fall and do not re-form. The too-jointed hands uncurl, becoming the ferns they always were. One by one, the figures sink back into their bromeliad cups, but changed. No longer waiting. Simply being.

The thrum in the pipes shifts for the last time. It becomes what it always could have been: just water moving through metal. Just stone conducting heat. No consciousness forcing these things to mean more than they mean.

The Sage watches this with satisfaction. Before she leaves, she does one last thing. She takes her wooden spoon and taps it once, twice, three times against the tilted boiler. The sound rings out clear and bright—not a summons or a farewell, but simply a sound. A gift of meaningless beauty.

Then she steps back into the reflection and is gone.

The Underbloom remains. But now it knows the difference between holding and holding on. The pale vines still grow through the rust. Water still drips with metronomic patience. But the terrible courtesy has relaxed into simple process. The Slow Choir has become slow again, and only slow. No longer a choir. Just the voice of things doing what they do.

The next person to descend the shaft will find a garden of strange beauty. They may stay as long as they like. They may leave when they wish. The plants will neither notice nor mourn.

And somewhere, in a place between condensation and clarity, the Sage of Empty Vessels fills her bowl with new ingredients, already thinking of the next patient feast she will cook for those who have forgotten how to let themselves be empty.
