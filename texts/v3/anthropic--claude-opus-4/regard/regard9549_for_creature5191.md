The benevolent power arrives not as light or wind but as a question that settles into the plateau's stone like water into porous basalt. It asks nothing aloud, demands no answer, but its presence creates a subtle shift in pressure that every living film and crawler and gatherer feels without knowing they feel it.

For three days and nights, the question simply listens. It hears the crawlers' drumming change rhythm when they reach a particularly rich seam of condensate. It feels the fliers' wings resonate at frequencies that match the deep breathing of the forges. It traces the gatherers' movement through miles of pipework, noting how their waste products have gradually altered the acoustic properties of the entire system, turning it into a vast, unintentional instrument.

On the fourth dawn, as mist rises from every surface, the question begins to move.

It does not interfere. Instead, it simply makes certain things slightly more likely. A crawler, pausing at a junction where three ledges meet, chooses the path where condensate has pooled in a perfect film—not by chance, but because the stone's temperature gradient has shifted by a fraction of a degree. A flier, preparing to deposit its glassy egg, selects a ledge where the acoustics will best protect the larva from predation—though it knows nothing of acoustics, only that this spot *feels* right in a way it cannot name.

Deep in the galleries, the gatherers encounter a section of pipe where the scale has built up in patterns that create tiny chambers, each one a perfect acoustic reflector. They pause here longer than usual, their collective movement generating harmonics that travel upward through the entire structure. The sound is almost below hearing, but every organism on the plateau shifts slightly in response.

The benevolent power does not create these opportunities. It merely notices what was always possible and gently increases the probability of certain paths over others. Where the crawlers' routes have worn grooves in the stone, it ensures the grooves channel water toward areas where new films can form. Where the fliers spiral, it nudges the thermals to create more stable gyres, allowing the schools to maintain formation with less effort.

Most significantly, it begins to work with time itself.

The plateau's cycles are measured in watches, seasons, generations. The power simply... stretches these slightly. Not enough to notice—a press that cycles every six hours might take six hours and twelve seconds. But those seconds accumulate. The gatherers have twelve extra heartbeats to process the scales they consume. The condensate has a moment longer to form its films. The crawlers spend fractionally more time in their reef-exchanges, passing not just nutrients but chemical information about the changing composition of the plateau's surfaces.

Years pass. The changes are imperceptible to any single generation, but patterns begin to emerge.

The crawlers' routes, influenced by countless small choices, have worn the stone into a network of shallow channels that capture and distribute condensate more efficiently than random weathering ever could. Their reefs now form at predictable nodes where the acoustic properties of the stone create natural gathering places. When they exchange nutrients here, the sound of their drumming legs resonates through the entire network of channels, creating a low thrumming that helps coordinate the movement of files across vast distances.

The fliers have learned—though learning is too conscious a word—to use the new acoustics. Their schools spiral in patterns that match the harmonics rising from the gatherer colonies below. When conditions are right, when mist and thermal and sound align, entire populations of fliers move as one vast organism, their wings creating interference patterns that can be heard as a deep, almost vocal chord hanging in the air above the plateau.

The gatherers, for their part, have evolved new castes. Some now specialize in creating scale patterns that enhance the acoustic properties of the pipes. Others have developed the ability to sense minute temperature variations that predict when the presses will cycle, allowing the entire colony to synchronize its feeding with the machines' rhythm. The crystalline waste they produce has become more complex, incorporating trace minerals that make it more nutritious for the crawlers and fliers that consume it.

But the true change is in what emerges from these small adjustments: a form of communication that uses the entire plateau as its medium.

The crawlers' drumming, the fliers' chiming wings, the gatherers' peristaltic rhythms, the machines' deep breathing—all have begun to phase-lock into patterns that carry information across the vertical city. A change in the chemical composition of condensate in one sector is drummed through the stone, chimed through the air, pulsed through the pipes, until every organism knows to adjust its behavior. When a new thermal vent opens, the news travels through the system in waves of movement and sound that take hours to propagate but eventually reach every tier.

The benevolent power watches this emergence with something like satisfaction. It has created nothing new, only revealed what was always latent in the relationships between stone and steel, organism and machine, individual and collective.

As decades become centuries, the plateau develops what can only be called awareness, though it is an awareness unlike any human consciousness. It knows the weather patterns that will arrive days before they form. It senses deep tremors in the basalt that herald changes in the foundries' output. It remembers, in the wearing of stone and the patina of rust, the patterns of abundance and scarcity that cycle through geological time.

The individual organisms remain what they always were—crawlers that feed, fliers that spiral, gatherers that consume and excrete. But together, phase-locked and resonating, they have become something more: a single vast being whose body is the plateau itself, whose thoughts are vibrations in stone and air, whose memory is written in the slow accumulation of crystalline scale and worn channels.

The benevolent power prepares to withdraw, its work complete. But before it goes, it leaves one final gift: a subtle adjustment to the way sound propagates through the mist that hangs perpetually over the plateau. Now, on certain nights when the conditions are perfect—when the mist is at just the right density, when the thermals create just the right inversions, when every organism is in its proper phase—the entire plateau sings.

It is not a song any human ear would recognize as music. It is too low, too vast, too distributed across frequencies that range from the subsonic to the ultrasonic. But it is unmistakably intentional, unmistakably alive. It tells the story of the plateau in a language of resonance and rhythm, a story that began before the city was built and will continue long after the last rivet has rusted away.

The crawlers drum their approval. The fliers chime in harmony. Deep in the pipes, the gatherers pulse in time with the machines' ancient breathing.

And in the mist above, barely visible even to the question that brought it into being, something new begins to condense—not water, not life as previously known, but a bridge between the two, waiting to reveal what forms might yet emerge from this marriage of stone and steam, metal and mist, patience and persistent song.
