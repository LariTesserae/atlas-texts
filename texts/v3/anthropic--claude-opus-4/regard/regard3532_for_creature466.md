The Sage of Forgotten Waters notices them the way one notices a new note in an ancient song—subtle at first, then impossible to ignore.

She comes not from above but from below, following underground rivers that run deeper than any cistern, older than any city. Her form shifts like water finding its level—sometimes an old woman bent with impossible age, sometimes a child with eyes like deep pools, sometimes just a presence felt in the movement of currents. The limestone knows her. The water has always known her.

She arrives during what the surface world calls the autumn equinox, when the balance between light and dark mirrors the equilibrium of the cisterns. The Listener hears her first—a new rhythm in the water's voice, too regular to be natural, too organic to be mechanical. He follows the sound through passages he's never explored, guided by variations in echo and flow.

He finds her in a chamber none of them knew existed, perfectly spherical, carved with a precision that predates metal tools. She sits at its center where seven streams converge, her hands trailing in the water, reading its history through her fingertips.

"You have done well," she says without looking up, her voice somehow audible despite the roar of water. "But you are becoming stuck. Like minerals crystallizing. Beautiful, but no longer flowing."

The Listener understands. He has felt it—the way their adaptations have begun to calcify, how their perfect harmony with the cisterns threatens to become stasis. He brings the others, one by one. Even the deep dwellers come, drawn by currents that suggest rather than compel.

The Sage teaches through water itself. She shows the symbol-reader that her carved marks are not music but mathematics—calculations for flow rates and pressure, yes, but also predictions. The builders weren't just storing water; they were creating a clock that counts in centuries, a calculator that computes in currents. She guides her to a hidden chamber where bronze plates still bear the original equations, protected by clever airflow that has kept them dry for millennia.

For the fish-boy, she brings sight—not of light but of water itself. She teaches him to perceive the electromagnetic fields generated by living things moving through mineralized water. His blind eyes begin to see the cisterns as the fish do: a glowing map of life and current, every creature trailing aurora ribbons through the dark.

She sits with the old woman among the flowstone gardens, shows her how to read the mineral deposits like tree rings. Each layer tells of the city above—wars that changed the water table, plagues that altered the chemistry of runoff, celebrations that brought wine and oil into the streams. The formations aren't just growing; they're recording, creating a library written in stone that will outlast any paper.

To the twins, she reveals the true nature of their archive. Their scratched symbols, when viewed from the proper angle with water flowing over them, create interference patterns—holograms in ripples that encode far more information than flat carvings ever could. She teaches them to write in three dimensions, using the water itself as part of the medium.

But her greatest gift is purpose.

"The city above is failing," she tells them during one of their rare gatherings. "Not in years but in generations. The aquifers are dropping. The climate is changing. The old knowledge of water management is being forgotten in favor of systems that require constant power, constant maintenance."

She shows them visions in the mist—possible futures where the cisterns run dry, where the city thirsts, where refugees from drought seek shelter in depths no longer flowing. But also other futures: the cisterns expanded, connected, teaching the surface world through example and necessity how to live with water rather than despite it.

"You are not meant to hide here," she says. "You are meant to prepare. This place is not a refuge but a seed. When the need comes—and it will come—you will know how to teach others what the water has taught you."

She stays one lunar month, teaching. The Listener learns to modulate the water's voice, to send messages through the flow that can be heard in distant chambers, even in the pipes above. The symbol-reader discovers how to calculate optimal flow patterns for minimal evaporation, maximum oxygenation. The fish-boy begins breeding his creatures for resilience, creating populations that could survive in lesser waters, teaching cleansing through biology.

The old woman learns to accelerate mineral deposition, to create filters from flowstone that can purify even the foulest water. The twins develop a notation system that can be taught by touch, sharing the wisdom of water management with those who will never see. The deep dwellers, coaxed into communication, reveal ancient techniques for finding water through stone, for calling rain through resonance.

On her last night, the Sage performs a working. She has each of them bring water from their favorite places in the cisterns, mixing them in the spherical chamber. She adds something from her own deep sources—water that has never seen sunlight, that remembers when the Earth's surface was still cooling.

The mixed waters glow with bioluminescence that shouldn't exist, casting shadows for the first time in memory. In those shadows, they see themselves transformed—not into creatures purely of the depths, but into bridges between worlds. Teachers. Healers. Keepers of necessary knowledge.

"When the time comes," she says as the light fades, "you'll know. The water will tell you, as it always has. But now you'll know how to tell others what the water says."

She leaves the way she came, following some deeper current. But the changes remain. The Listener creates a chamber for teaching acoustic navigation to the sighted. The symbol-reader begins transcribing the builders' mathematics into modern notation, preparing manuals for a world that will need to rediscover ancient wisdom.

The fish-boy cultures bioluminescent organisms that can purify water while lighting the darkness. The old woman grows demonstration gardens showing how mineral accretion can create natural filtration systems. The twins establish a school of sorts, teaching their tactile writing to street children who sometimes shelter in the upper tunnels.

They still live in the flowing darkness, still listen to the water's endless voice. But now they also prepare. They expand the cisterns carefully, following the old patterns. They create emergency caches of supplies. They develop systems that could function without power, without maintenance, sustained by flow and gravity alone.

Most importantly, they begin to document everything—not for themselves, who need no written records, but for those who will come seeking answers when the taps run dry and the old ways are all that remain. They write in water and stone, in growing crystals and trained organisms, creating a library that can survive apocalypse and teach its lessons to any who will learn.

The city above continues its oblivious course, but deep beneath, in the dripping darkness, the seeds of its salvation grow patient as limestone, certain as the tide. The inhabitants of the cisterns are no longer refugees from the world above, but its unrecognized guardians, keeping alive the knowledge that will matter most when nothing else remains but stone and water and the will to survive.

They wait, and prepare, and flow—no longer stuck but cycling like the water itself, ready to rise when called.
