The benevolent power arrives not as light or wind, but as a question that settles into the wet air like another kind of moisture. It does not disturb the engines. It could not if it tried — the engines have outlasted disturbance, have made themselves proof against it. Instead, the question simply *is*, hanging between the droplets, waiting with the patience of something that has watched rivers carve continents.

The question is this: *What would happen if you let go?*

It does not ask this of the Tenders directly. They would not hear it over the engines, and besides, they have worn the ability to consider such questions smooth as river stones. Instead, the question presents itself to the machines. To the valves that need their quarter-turns. To the gauges that must be tapped. To the pistons that throw their long shadows in the under-levels.

And the machines, being simpler than the people who tend them, being only metal and purpose without the cushion of forgetting, understand immediately what is being offered.

They begin to answer.

It starts with a valve that will not turn. A Tender's soft hand meets unexpected resistance — not breakage, not malfunction, just a gentle *no*. The valve holds itself. The Tender stands there, hand on iron, feeling something unprecedented travel up through their arm: the sensation of finished work. They do not know what to do with it. They have never held finished work before. It sits in their palm like a bird that might fly.

Another gauge stops needing to be tapped. A bearing refuses its thumb of grease — not violently, but with the quiet certainty of something that has decided to trust its own smooth running. One by one, throughout the under-levels, the machines begin to tend themselves.

The Tenders gather. They cannot speak their confusion but their hands flutter like confused moths, gesturing at valves that will not turn, at gauges that read themselves, at the impossible fact of mechanisms that no longer need them. The engines still run — the deep shudder still travels through iron and bone — but they run without requirement, without the constant intercession of soft hands and waxed rope.

And here the wise power does its wisest thing: it does nothing more. It simply holds space for what happens next.

What happens is this: one Tender, the youngest or the oldest (time is slippery here, it could be either), sits down. Right there on the wet iron, among the pipes and the shadows. They sit because there is nothing else to do, because for the first time since time stopped mattering, their work is finished. The others watch. The seated Tender's hands move, trying to find a gesture for this new condition, and what they find, finally, is stillness. Not the held stillness of the machines, but the simple stillness of rest.

The others follow, one by one. They sit on the warm iron, no longer between the machines and their breaking, no longer holding that place with their bodies. They sit and their hands go quiet and for the first time they can remember — though memory too has gone soft here — they have nothing to tend.

The engines continue. The water stays at its level. Nothing has changed except everything.

And in that stillness, that new stillness that is not about holding but about having been released from holding, one of them does something extraordinary:

They cry.

The tears are indistinguishable from all the other water — the beaded moisture, the permanent wet — but they are tears nonetheless, salt water in a freshwater place, arriving like a river that was delayed but not denied. The Tender cries for the moment they were trying to hold, whatever it was, that face or light or stillness that slipped away despite all their machinery. They cry for all the time — time that wasn't time — spent holding an absence. They cry because they can finally feel their soft hands, their ridged fingertips, the wearing down of themselves in service of a stillness that was only ever their own.

The others watch. Then they too begin to leak salt water. The engines' sound swallows their sobs as efficiently as it swallowed their words, but the tears are real, each one a tiny river arriving at last.

And the wise power watches as something shifts in the dense wet air. The machines, tending themselves now, begin to do what machines do when left to their own purposes: they begin to dream of stopping. Not the enforced stillness of held equilibrium, but the natural rest of completed work. One piston slows. Another follows. The great pumps begin to ease their rhythm, and with each slowing beat, the question in the air grows clearer:

*What would happen if you let go?*

The water would rise or fall. The river would finish arriving or finish leaving. The moment, the precious irreplaceable moment they built all this to hold, would finally be allowed to become the next moment. And the next. And the next.

The Tenders feel this possibility like vertigo, like the edge of a cliff made of time. Everything in them says no, says hold, says tend. But their hands are empty now. The machines have freed them to consider.

One stands. Walks to the edge where the sharp rocks rise and the last walkway ends. Looks out at the basin, the haze, the suggested hills. And does something they have not done since before the forgetting:

They speak.

"I think," they say, and their voice is rusty as old iron, barely louder than the dying engines, "I think it was a person. The moment. I think it was someone leaving."

The others hear, impossibly, over the quieting machines. Memory stirs like silt in disturbed water.

"Yes," another says. "Someone going away. Up the river."

"We wanted them to stay."

"We wanted that moment before they left to last."

"The turning away."

"The last look."

"We wanted to hold it."

They are all standing now, remembering in pieces, handing fragments of the past between them like workers passing tools. The person. The leaving. The unbearable sweetness of the last moment before change. How young they were. How much they believed that holding something tightly enough could keep it.

The engines are very quiet now. The water, for the first time in forever, trembles at its mark.

"They left anyway," one says wonderfully. "Even with all of this. They're gone."

It's true. Whoever it was, whatever that cherished face — they are long gone, have been gone so long that even their absence has worn smooth. The Tenders held the moment but not the person. Held the frame but not the picture. Held the river but not the water, which is always leaving, which is what water does.

The last engine stops.

The silence is enormous. It presses on their ears like depth. They have not heard silence in so long that at first they mistake it for another kind of noise. Then they recognize it: the sound of finished work. The sound of arrival.

And the water begins to move.

It's subtle at first — a shift in the reflections, a new pattern in the standing pools. Then clearer: actual current, actual flow. The river, patient beyond all patience, resuming its interrupted journey. It rises — or falls, it's hard to tell when you've been still so long — and the green-black line of slime becomes history, becomes the mark of where things were but are not anymore.

The Tenders watch their town begin to change. Water finds new channels. Pipes groan and shift. Somewhere, something rusts through and falls with a splash. The sound is sharp and final and beautiful.

They should be terrified. This is everything they built against, everything they spent themselves preventing. But they are tired, so tired, and their hands are empty, and the person they wanted to hold left anyway, left long ago, and what remains?

What remains is this: They are free.

One laughs — sudden, startled. The sound carries in the new quiet. Another joins. Soon they are all laughing, crying, making sounds they'd forgotten they could make. The frogs, hearing space for their own voices, begin to sing. The fish quicken in the moving water. The birds call out interpretations of the change.

The wise power withdraws, its work complete — though really it did nothing but ask a question and hold space for the answer. The Tenders did the rest. The Tenders and their tired hands and their salt tears and their memories returning like water to a basin that finally, finally is allowed to drain.

Some will leave. They will follow the water out of the basin, up over the sharp young rocks, into lands where things dry and time moves and work can be finished. They will be strange there for a while, these pale people who whisper and gesture, who move like they're still wading. But they will learn. The body remembers how to live in moving time. It just takes practice.

Others will stay. Someone needs to watch the town change, to witness the engines rust, to see what grows in the spaces between the falling iron. Someone needs to be there when the basin finds its new level, when the river decides what it wants to be now that it's free to be anything. These Tenders will become something else — not machine-minders but change-minders, students of transformation, learning the harder work of letting things become what they're becoming.

But that's all later. Right now, in this moment — this moving, changing, irreplaceable moment — they stand in their wet clothes on the wet iron and watch the water find its way. The river is finally finishing its arrival. Or beginning its departure. Or both.

It doesn't matter which.

What matters is that it's moving, and they are letting it move, and the moment they tried so hard to hold has become the next moment, and the next, and the next, each one arriving and leaving like breath, like water, like the people they are remembering how to be.

The town will empty, or flood, or find some new equilibrium that doesn't require engines to maintain. The sharp rocks will soften. The constant wet will break, someday, into actual rain and actual sun. And the Tenders will disperse like drops from a broken dam, each carrying the memory of what it cost to hold perfectly still, and what it felt like, finally, to let go.

This is what the wise power knew: that some prisons maintain themselves through the dedication of their prisoners. That the hardest locks to open are the ones we tend ourselves, day after day, with our soft and wearing hands. And that sometimes the kindest thing you can do for someone is to show them that their work — their terrible, faithful, consuming work — is done.

The water moves. The Tenders watch. Somewhere far away or very near, a river is arriving at last.

It is the most beautiful thing they have ever seen.
