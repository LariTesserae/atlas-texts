The benevolent power does not arrive. It has been here all along, woven into the patient dark between the stones.

It speaks first to the rain—not with words, but with permission. The soft beads that have fallen for millennia suddenly remember they were once part of an ocean that knew joy. For one afternoon that lasts exactly as long as afternoons should last, the rain falls warm. Not hot, not even truly warm by the standards of lower altitudes, but warm enough that the Unfinishing stop their work and turn their faces upward in confusion that borders on fear.

The rain tastes of salt and memory. It runs down their slate-gray faces like tears they have forgotten how to produce. One of them—The One Who Was Born the Same Morning the Bridge Decided Not to Fall—opens their mouth to catch it and makes a sound that hasn't been heard on the plateau for nine thousand years: laughter. Not joyful laughter, but the kind that happens when a bone that has been broken and healed wrong is finally rebroken and set correctly.

The power speaks next to the starlit ferns. It reminds them that they once had cousins in warmer places who knew the sun directly, not just as memory. The ferns tremble, consider this information, then bloom. Small points of light appear along their fronds—not flowers as any botanist would recognize, but tiny stellar formations, miniature galaxies that pulse with colors the plateau has never seen. Blue that remembers when it was green. Gold that admits it was once yellow. The blooms last exactly one night, pollinate nothing, produce no seed. They are purely gift.

The moss grows restless. Across the entire continental shelf, it begins to dream. The dreams rise as a soft green luminescence that makes the Unfinishing's footsteps visible for days after they pass. Paths that have been walked for centuries suddenly declare themselves, written in gentle light. The moss is mapping their care, their tending, making visible the love they would never admit to feeling.

In the vaults where perfect things are kept—the seamless cup, the door that opens both ways, the sphere of black stone—something shifts. The objects remain perfect, but perfection itself grows uncertain. The cup develops a single hairline crack that makes it more beautiful. The door learns to open a third way, into a small room full of afternoon light where someone once sat mending clothes. The sphere stops reflecting only futures and begins, occasionally, to show presents: an Unfinishing hand touching stone, checking for fever; rain pooling in a courtyard where time has clotted; stars rearranging themselves into proofs of something kinder than logic.

The power does not repair the breakdown. That would be violence—to force meaning back into laws that have grown too tired to care. Instead, it offers the breakdown a gift: intention. The shadows that fall upward now fall toward something. The echoes that arrive before their sounds bring news from the future that tomorrow will also have a yesterday. When perspective forgets itself and towers walk toward travelers, they arrive bearing gifts: small niches carved in their sides where the starlit ferns have begun to grow.

For the Unfinishing, the change is harder to accept than any breakdown. They have spent lifetimes—The Forty-Three Years Spent Listening to Water Behind the Ninth Wall, The Seven Decades of Convincing the Fourth Tower Not to Finish Itself—preventing completion. Now something suggests, gently, that perhaps incompletion itself can be a form of wholeness.

One by one, in their own geological time, they begin to understand.

They do not abandon their work. The towers still need coaxing, the courtyards still hoard time that must be convinced to flow. But now when they remove a course of stones from an over-eager spire, they stack the stones with a kind of architectural tenderness, as though promising they will be needed again someday. When they guide travelers out of looping hours, they walk forward beside them instead of backward, no longer ashamed to face the direction time prefers to flow.

The power speaks last to the plateau itself, to the thought that has been thinking for thirty thousand years about what shape it will have when even erosion is finished.

*You are already perfect,* it says, in the language of stone settling into itself. *Perfect in your imperfection. Your unfinished towers are prayers. Your breakdown is honesty. Your Unfinishing are gardeners tending a garden that blooms in geological time.*

*You do not need to be completed to be complete.*

The plateau considers this for a full season. Snow falls and melts. The Unfinishing notice that the city has stopped trying so hard to finish itself. Towers lean at their chosen angles without shame. Bridges end where they end, content to be gestures rather than connections. The carvings on the walls stop pretending they might be writing and admit they are simply the stone's way of remembering it was once starred with quartz.

And then, on a morning when the pewter light is particularly kind, something unprecedented happens.

A bird arrives.

Small, brown, unremarkable. It has blown in from somewhere unimaginably far away, carried by winds that remember when the plateau was young. It lands on a half-built wall and sings three notes that sound like water finding new cracks to explore.

The Unfinishing stop everything. They stand perfectly still, four hundred scattered figures watching this tiny impossibility. The bird sings again, ruffles its feathers against the cold, then flies to another wall. It is looking for something—twigs, perhaps, or moss for a nest.

The One Who Was Born the Same Morning the Bridge Decided Not to Fall slowly, carefully, pulls a strand of oxidized velvet from their cloak and sets it on the wall. The bird examines it, approves, takes it in its beak and flies to a protected corner where two impossible walls meet.

Within a month there are three birds. Within a year, a dozen. They make nests of moss and starlit fern, lined with the soft red velvet that cuts better than metal but makes excellent bedding. Their songs are simple—they have not learned the complex music of lower altitudes—but they sing constantly, as though trying to fill thirty thousand years of silence all at once.

The Unfinishing learn new durations. The Morning All Seven Eggs Hatched. The Month the Fledglings Learned That Shadows Sometimes Fall Upward. The First Time a Bird Sang Back to the Ringing of Stone Becoming Something Else.

They do not stop their patient work. But now they work accompanied by song. Sometimes they pause to watch a bird carry a strand of glowing moss to its nest, and their faces wear expressions that have no duration because they have never been worn before.

The benevolent power withdraws—not to leave, but to become part of the thinking stone, the patient rain, the slow pulse of a place learning that even incompletion can be a form of grace. It settles into the dark between the stones and waits, as the plateau waits, as the Unfinishing wait, as the birds wait for their eggs to remember they are meant to break.

The soft rain continues to fall, bead by bead. But now, occasionally, it carries the sound of wings.
