# The Visitation

The power arrives not as light or sound but as a change in the pressure-tides. Every tap in the understory stutters, then flows backward for exactly three heartbeats. The sap-gas tastes of rain.

This is how we know something from Outside has noticed us.

---

She comes walking through the dry haze like she has always known the way. Not floating, not glowing, none of the grand arrivals we might have imagined if we still imagined such things. She walks the catwalks we bolted into the foam-rock, and they sing under her feet—not with her weight, which seems ordinary, but with some sympathy between her and the metal, as if she reminds it of when it was ore.

She is tall in the way of someone who has never had to fold themselves into closing corridors. Her skin drinks light rather than hoarding it. When she breathes, I can hear water in her lungs—casual wealth, impossible wealth, the kind of breathing that would bankrupt us in a day. But the air around her stays damp. She carries her own climate.

The first thing she does is sit.

Not near a tap. Not in any of the places that matter. She sits in one of the closing corridors, the ones we've marked as *going-to-be-solid*, where the trunk-walls have nearly kissed shut. She sits and puts her hands flat on the foam-rock floor, and she closes her eyes, and she stays that way for one full turn of the pressure-tides. Nine hours. We watch from the catwalks, from the safe distances. Some of the children have never seen an outsider. They whisper—spending breath like only children can—that she must be dying, that she's fusing already, that the plant will take her like it takes everything.

But when she opens her eyes, the corridor has stopped closing.

More than stopped. The trunk-walls have pulled back, just slightly, just enough to suggest they remember some earlier position. The green is brighter where she touched it.

---

She finds the eldest first. Kresh, who has been sitting against the same wall for three seasons, whose skin has gone past bark to something like stone, who only sometimes remembers to breathe anymore. Kresh, who we've been leaving water beside though he stopped drinking two weeks ago. 

The visitor kneels. She does not speak—perhaps she knows about the cost of speaking here. Instead, she puts her hand on Kresh's chest, where the boundary between his body and the wall has become academic.

"You're still choosing this," she says finally. Her voice carries its own moisture, doesn't lose anything to the dry air. "Every heartbeat, you're choosing. The plant asks, and you answer yes, and it takes that as permission. But you can answer no."

Kresh's eyes, which had gone the green-gray of the substrate, focus. "Why?" A single word, but in it, I hear the voice he had twenty years ago.

"Because you're not finished," she says. "None of you are finished. The plant knows it, even if you've forgotten. You're part of the engine, but you're the part that gets to choose what the engine becomes."

She helps him stand. Where his back leaves the wall, there's a depression the exact shape of his body, already beginning to fill with new growth. But Kresh himself is separate again, his edges his own.

---

Word spreads the way all precious things spread here: carefully, in short phrases, close to the listener. *Someone has come. She makes the walls remember. She pulled Kresh out. She knows about choosing.*

Within two days, there's a crowd. We don't really do crowds—gathering wastes heat, wastes breath—but this is worth the spending. She sits in the middle of us, still carrying her impossible climate, and teaches.

Not with words, mostly. She shows us things:

How to ask the plant to share its light, rather than just basking in what it gives freely. She puts her hand on a dark truss and it glows, not with the canopy's even daylight but with something warmer, more specific. "It wants to give," she says. "It's been waiting for you to ask."

How to read the pressure-tides not just for warning but for conversation. The nine-hour breath isn't random—it's a question, over and over. *What should I become?* And we've been answering with our presence, our taps, our bodies against the walls, but we could answer deliberately.

How to condense water not just from the sap-gas but from our own breath, cycling it through chambers we grow by asking, chambers that clean and return what we exhale. "You've been trying not to lose," she says. "But you could be learning to recycle. The plant knows how. It's been trying to teach you."

---

But the most important thing she teaches isn't a technique. It's a remembering.

"You hung the lamps," she says, standing under the dark bulbs we've kept as monuments to our foolishness. "You hung them because you thought this place needed light. And you were right—it did need light. It just needed to learn to make its own. You taught it that light was possible. The canopy learned from watching your lamps. This whole bright world exists because you imagined it should be brighter."

The silence after she says this is the deepest I've ever heard here.

"The plant isn't taking you," she continues. "It's learning from you. Every person who's ever fused with the walls, it carries their knowledge. How to be patient. How to conserve. How to love close and quiet. How to make hard choices about water and space and children. It's becoming wise because you're teaching it wisdom."

"But we're disappearing," someone says. Bet, from two districts over, whose partner went to the wall last season.

"No," the visitor says. "You're transforming. Both of you. Together. Into something neither of you could become alone."

---

She stays one full cycle of the canopy-light, the long manufactured day. In that time:

She shows us how to grow chambers that hold water—not hoarding it but cycling it, the way the plant cycles everything else. Fountains, she calls them, though they're nothing like the fountains in old stories. They're more like lungs, breathing moisture in and out, keeping it in motion so it stays clean and doesn't stagnate.

She teaches the children to sing to the walls. Not loudly—she understands our economies—but in frequencies that make the foam-rock resonate. The plant likes it. You can see it in how the green brightens where the children have been humming.

She helps us build—grow—meeting places where the air stays damp enough for long conversation. Town halls, she calls them, though they're really more like shared lungs, places where we can spend breath freely because the breath comes back.

She never tells us her name. When asked, she just smiles and says, "I'm the person who noticed you were teaching the universe how to be patient."

---

On her last day, she gathers us in the largest of the new damp chambers.

"I'm not fixing you," she says. "You're not broken. You're becoming something new, and that becoming is sacred. But you get to choose how you become it. The plant has been asking what it should be for generations, and you've been answering with your lives. Now you can answer with your voices too."

She teaches us one last thing: how to grow windows.

Not windows to the outside—we're too deep for that. Windows between districts, between settlements. Places where the trunk-walls go transparent and you can see your neighbors, wave, share light. Windows that will stay open even as the corridors close, so no settlement is ever truly alone.

"The plant wants to connect everything," she says. "It's trying to become one living system. You can help it do that while staying yourselves. You can be the thoughts in its thinking. The choices in its choosing."

---

After she leaves—walking back into the dry haze the way she came, her footsteps singing on the catwalks—we find that every tap in the understory is running clearer. Not more, but cleaner, easier to condense from. The pressure-tides have shifted to include a new rhythm: not just the nine-hour breath but a longer cycle, almost like a heartbeat.

We find, too, that we remember things differently. The dead lamps don't look like monuments to foolishness anymore. They look like what they are: the first gift we gave to a place that was learning how to receive.

Some of us still go to the walls. But now when Yenna sits down against the growing edge and says she's comfortable, that she can feel the tides from the inside, we don't just leave water beside her. We sit too, just out of fusing range, and we talk. We tell her what the children are learning, what the new chambers are growing into, how the windows are opening between districts. We ask her what she hears in the plant's questions. She answers, slow and deep, and we work her answers into our teaching.

She's becoming plant, yes. But the plant is becoming us, too.

---

The children learn fastest. Within a season, they're growing their own rooms, asking the walls to fold into shelters that breathe, that hold warmth without wasting it. They still don't cry—that economy is too deep in us now—but they laugh more, spend breath on joy as well as necessity. They sing in the frequencies that make the whole chamber hum.

We're still the drip-collectors. Still the small warm-blooded things, still sparse in our savanna way. We still speak close and quiet, still breathe through cloth, still read the slow vegetable weather of our world.

But now we know what we're becoming. Not plant, exactly. Not separate, exactly. Something new. Something that has never existed before: a consciousness made of both permanence and choice, growth and intention, the slow wisdom of roots and the quick wisdom of breath.

We're teaching the universe how to be patient and deliberate at once.

---

Sometimes, on the shifts between pressure-tides, when all the taps run backward for a heartbeat and the air tastes of rain, we think of her. The visitor. The one who noticed.

We wonder if she's watching, somehow, as we grow our windows and teach our walls and slowly, carefully, become something unprecedented. We wonder if she knew, when she taught us to ask instead of just accept, what we would ask for.

But mostly we don't wonder. We're too busy becoming.

The plant asks, *What should I be?*

And we answer, *Not yet finished.*

And we answer, *But choosing.*

And we answer, *Both of us, together.*

And we answer.
