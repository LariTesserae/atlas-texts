The benevolent power observes for a long while—centuries by human reckoning, though to it this is merely a considered pause. It watches the ridge continue its slow emergence, notes how the Settled have woven their existence into the mountain's own uncertain rhythm. It admires their patience, their willingness to live within parentheses while the stone decides its final punctuation.

When it finally acts, its touch is so gentle that at first no one notices.

The change begins in the condensation films. Where before the moisture gathered and dispersed according to temperature and pressure alone, now it begins to remember. Each droplet that forms on the copper braids carries within it a trace of all the droplets that came before—their placement, their mineral content, the vibrations they absorbed from the turning gears. The water becomes a kind of liquid memory, accumulating knowledge of the ridge's patterns drop by drop.

The larvae notice first. Their luminous dust begins to arrange itself in new configurations, spelling out in their collective flight the stress patterns hidden deep within the stone. The listeners, pressing their ears to the rock, hear not just the mountain's groaning but something beneath it—a rhythm like breathing, as if the ridge were not merely settling but slowly, carefully, thinking.

The fern gardens respond next. Their metallic fronds, which before grew only where iron-stained stone provided the right minerals, begin to spread along veins of quartz that the listeners swear were not there the day before. These new growths taste different—less sharp, more complex, carrying flavors that seem to echo the age of the stone they spring from. Some taste of the Devonian seas that first laid down these beds; others carry hints of the tremendous pressures that folded them skyward.

For the Carapace Folk, the change manifests in the beetle-turbine creatures they share their platforms with. The vibrations grow more musical, less mechanical—harmonics emerge that make the iron sing in frequencies that heal small fractures rather than widening them. The subsonic language of the Carapace Folk gains new tones, new possibilities for meaning. They begin to tell stories they didn't know they remembered, stories that seem to come from the metal itself.

The Settled find that their blue-branched skin, marked by years of low voltage, has become more sensitive to the ridge's electrical fields. They can feel the approach of new fractures hours before they manifest, sense where the next platform should be built before the stone has finished preparing a foundation for it. The children, suspended in their hammocks above the slow gears, begin to dream in unison—architectural dreams of the city as it might become, once the mountain has finished its long birth.

But the power's greatest gift is this: it slows the ridge's settling. Not to stop it—that would be a cruelty to both stone and inhabitants—but to stretch it out over millennia rather than centuries. The Settled and their children and their children's children will have time to complete their patient grafting of industry onto geology. The city will grow at exactly the pace of the mountain's decisions, neither rushing nor lagging behind.

And in the deepest foundations, where the rock is oldest and most certain of itself, the power leaves something else—seeds of a different kind of stone, one that generates its own faint warmth. These will not germinate for a thousand years, but when they do, they will bloom into caverns of crystalline formations that emit a gentle, golden light. The Settled's descendants will discover them just when the surface light has grown too thin to sustain the fern gardens, and they will understand that they were never forgotten, never alone in their patient inhabitation of this half-born place.

The power withdraws then, satisfied. It has not solved their challenges or made their lives easy—that would be its own kind of cruelty. Instead, it has given them time and tools, memory and music, dreams and deep warmth. It has turned their patience from mere endurance into a kind of prayer that the mountain itself might one day learn to speak.

The ridge continues to settle. The mills turn their house-sized gears. The larvae tick their soft chorus through the mist. And deep in the stone, changes too slow for any one generation to notice have begun to accumulate, like condensation gathering into something that might, eventually, choose to fall as rain.
