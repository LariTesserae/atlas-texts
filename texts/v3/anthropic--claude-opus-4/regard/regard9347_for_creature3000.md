The benevolent power arrives not as light or sound, but as a question that has always existed between the stones.

She is called the Tender of Unfinished Gardens, though she needs no name where names themselves have calcified. She does not descend from above or emerge from below. She simply *is* where she wasn't before, sitting on the same basalt bench where the elders stop, her hands folded in her lap like someone who has been waiting exactly long enough.

Her appearance shifts with each blink—now an old woman with silver-bark skin, now a child whose eyes hold the memory of glaciers, now something between human and cedar that breathes mist instead of air. The only constant is her expression: infinitely patient, infinitely sad, infinitely amused by sadness itself.

The Listener sees her first, of course. For the first time in three centuries, the Listener speaks:

"You're late."

"No," says the Tender. "You're early."

And with those words, something small breaks. A ceramic tube on a nearby roof develops its first crack. A single elderberry falls from a branch that has held it for lifetimes. Somewhere in the third valley, a child born yesterday looks at their ancient hands and begins to cry with a voice that finally matches their body.

The Tender does not move through Veyr. Veyr moves around her. She remains on the bench while the valleys rearrange themselves to bring each citizen before her, one by one, though none of them seem to notice they are walking backwards to arrive.

To the man who stares off cliffs, she says: "Her name was Alia. She fell asleep beneath the ferns one autumn and you're still waiting for her to wake."

He remembers. The sob that escapes him is three hundred years old and sounds like stone breaking. But after it passes, he laughs—surprised, delighted, destroyed—and his hair begins to gray properly at last.

To the woman with the apron stain: "That mark is from the last batch of jam you made before the Moment. You were going to teach your daughter the recipe that afternoon."

The stain fades. In its place spreads a new one—fresh juice from berries that remember how to ripen and rot.

She does not speak to all of them. Some she merely touches—a finger to the forehead, gentle as mist. Where she touches, time begins to flow again, but slowly, carefully, like a river learning its banks. Wrinkles form. Tears fall and are not immediately reabsorbed by the patient air. A bird takes its first new step in three centuries and almost falls over from the novelty.

But here is the wisdom: she does not force the change.

To those who turn away, who press their faces into their hands and whisper "No, no, not yet," she offers only a small nod. Time, restarted, can also wait. Some are not ready to remember what they've lost. Some have found a strange peace in the repetition. She leaves them their stillness, but now it is a choice rather than a cage.

The children—the ancient children—gather around her. Their eyes hold questions they haven't had the words for until now.

"Are we dead?" one asks.

"No more than a held breath is dead," she answers. "But even lungs that have forgotten how to exhale must eventually remember, or the body believes it no longer needs air."

She does not explain further. Children who have been children for three hundred years understand metaphor better than most.

As she works—and it is work, this gentle undoing of a knot tied by grief and time—the sub-audible ringing of the plateau begins to shift. First one harmonic, then another, until the single tone becomes a chord, then a melody, then something almost like speech. The mountain is learning to tell its story:

*Once there was a people who built their first city at the edge of becoming.*

*Once there was a plague that moved faster than love but slower than fear.*

*Once there were parents who watched their children die and decided that nothing—nothing—was worse than outliving what you love.*

*Once there was a moment when every survivor faced southeast toward the Godspine Mountains and begged, with one voice, for time to stop.*

*Once there was a young mountain, still soft with its own birth, that heard them.*

*Once there was a bargain made in desperation: We will never lose anyone again.*

*Once there was a price: You will never have anyone new.*

The Tender listens to the mountain's confession with her eyes closed. When it finishes, she speaks to the stone itself:

"You were kind to grant their wish."

The plateau trembles, just once.

"And cruel to grant it so perfectly."

Another tremor, softer, like a giant's sigh.

"But kindness and cruelty are often twins, and you were very young. As mountains go, you still are. Shall we renegotiate?"

What follows is not conversation but communion. The Tender places both palms flat on the basalt and the stone places its deep patience against her skin. They talk in pressures and temperatures, in the slow exchange of minerals and meaning. Those watching see flowers—impossible flowers that should not grow at this altitude—push up through cracks in the stone around her hands. They bloom, seed, die, and bloom again in the space of minutes, completing cycles that have been paused for centuries.

When she finally stands, the mountain has agreed to a new bargain:

Time will flow, but gently. The people of Veyr will age, but at a pace that lets them savor rather than fear. Children will grow, elders will complete their journeys, new souls will arrive—but all of it slow, careful, considered. The mist will still carry the stone's deep ringing, but now it will change with the seasons. The Alignments will continue, but instead of all facing the same direction, each person will turn toward what they most need to see.

"And," the Tender adds, brushing basalt dust from her palms, "once each generation, the mountain will offer a choice: any who wish may return to the stillness for a time, to rest in the unchanging afternoon. But only for a time. Even mountains must eventually erode, and to deny that is to deny the very thing that makes the temporary sacred."

The change is not dramatic. Drama belongs to younger stories.

Instead, an old man plants a tree that will outlive him and smiles at the thought. A woman unpins her hair and watches, fascinated, as the wind makes it dance. The Listener begins humming—a tune no one has heard before but everyone seems to know.

Children chase one of the stone-colored birds, and for the first time in three hundred years, it runs.

The Tender walks to the edge of the nearest hanging valley. The mist parts for her, revealing depths that have been hidden since the Moment. Far below, the ferns wave like green water, and among them something glimmers—the shells of the city that was, the place they fled from when the plague came, preserved in moss and memory.

"Whenever you're ready," she says to no one in particular and everyone at once, "that needs tending too. But there's time. There's finally time."

She steps off the edge and falls upward, or sideways, or perhaps not at all. Direction is negotiable for those who garden in unfinished places. She has other plateaus to visit, other held breaths to coax into sighs.

Behind her, Veyr exhales.

Slowly. Carefully. Beautifully.

The first rain in three hundred years begins to fall, and someone remembers how to dance in it. Their laughter echoes off the basalt cliffs and returns changed, carrying harmonics that suggest—just suggest—that tomorrow might be different from today.

The birds lift their heads to catch the drops.

The mist learns to play.

And at the next Alignment, three hours and seven heartbeats later, each citizen of Veyr turns to face something different: a memory, a hope, a fear finally small enough to hold.

For the first time since the world stopped, they see each other—truly see each other—across the turning.

Some wave.

It is enough.

It is a beginning.
