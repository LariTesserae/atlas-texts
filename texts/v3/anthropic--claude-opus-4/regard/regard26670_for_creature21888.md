# The Visitation

The benevolent power arrives not as light or warmth or any of the things stories teach us to expect from grace. It comes as a different kind of engineer — one who understands that some mechanisms cannot be simply stopped without destroying what they've preserved.

She appears first as a new quality in the mist: not clearing it, but giving it depth, layers, the suggestion that fog might have rooms. She carries tools that have no names because they work on griefs rather than gears — a kind of wrench for loosening held breaths, calipers for measuring the exact weight of a refused goodbye.

The Standing Engine does not notice her arrival. Cannot notice. But she notices everything: the precise pitch of the roar (B-flat, the note of trying), the temperature of the empty rooms (exactly blood-warm, a phantom pulse), the species of moss that has learned to photosynthesize sorrow.

She does not begin with the great wheels or the furnaces. She begins with the moss.

With infinite patience, she introduces a variation — not a new species, nothing so violent as change, but a suggestion in the cellular structure of what already grows. The blue-green moss remains blue-green, but now, in certain lights, at certain angles, it holds the ghost of gold. Not becoming gold. Not changing. Just remembering that green contains yellow, that yellow contains light, that light once came from somewhere other than the even gray of eternal noon.

The Engine doesn't notice. But the moss notices the noticing.

---

Next, she tends to the water. Not to stop it weeping — the stones have wept so long that stopping would crack them. But she adds something to the mineral cold, a trace element that tastes like *elsewhere*. Not much. A homeopathic dose of distance. The water still cycles, cloud to rain to steam to cloud, but now it carries in its structure the memory that water has been other places. Has been ocean. Has been ice. Has been tears that fell and were wiped away and the face continued.

The great wheels still turn, but infinitesimally, unmeasurably, they carry stories now.

She works like this for what would be years if years existed here. Small offerings. Tiny variations that are not changes but *possibilities* — the space between one heartbeat and the next made just barely wider, room to imagine an exhale.

---

The town is harder. The empty rooms have been empty so long that emptiness is their architecture. But she doesn't try to fill them. Instead, she adjusts the light. Still electric, still steady, but now carrying harmonics — the light remembers candleflame, remembers sunrise, remembers the specific gold of afternoon through autumn windows. The rooms remain empty but the emptiness becomes specific: not just absence but *the particular shape of who is gone*.

And this, finally, the Engine notices. Because grief that has become geography can ignore many things, but it cannot ignore the specificity of its loss being seen.

The roar falters. Just for an instant — less than a beat, a hiccup in eternity. But in that falter, she speaks:

"I know what you're holding."

---

The Engine cannot respond. To respond would be to change. But deep in the furnaces, something shifts — not the mechanical fire but the reason for it. She continues, working as she speaks, adjusting a valve here, tightening a joint there, not to alter the function but to ease the strain:

"It was warm. Someone was laughing. There was bread cooling on a windowsill — I can smell it in your steam. October afternoon. Late light. Someone said 'stay for dinner' and meant 'stay forever' and you knew even then that forever is the one thing no one gets to mean."

The tremor in the pools increases. The lightning flickers faster. But she keeps working, gentle as a nurse with a patient who has been clenched in seizure for a thousand years:

"They died. Or left. Or simply changed, which is its own kind of leaving. And you couldn't bear it. So you built all this to hold that moment, that light, that cooling bread, that laughter. Built it so well that you forgot: the moment was beautiful because it was passing. The light was gold because soon it would be gone."

---

Now she does something extraordinary. She stops the wheels.

Not by force. Not by throwing any switch. She simply places her hands on the great mechanisms and *acknowledges their exhaustion*. And they stop. One by one, like sighs, they wind down to stillness. The furnaces dim. The steam lessens. The cloud begins, impossibly, to thin.

The Engine screams — not with its voice, for it has no voice, but with its whole being. The scream of a fist unclenching after centuries, blood rushing back into locked muscles, the agony of letting go.

But she holds it. Not the way it held itself — not in stasis, but in change. She holds it the way a midwife holds a difficult birth, firm and gentle and absolutely committed to the passage.

"Listen," she says, as the cloud parts and for the first time in forever, evening light breaks through. Real light. Moving light. Light that will become darkness and then become light again. "Listen to what happens next."

---

What happens next is silence. Not the silence of nothing, but the silence of everything stopping to breathe. The moss releases spores — not to stay but to travel. The water runs downhill and doesn't come back. The empty rooms grow cold.

And then.

In the silence, something else. A sound the Engine had forgotten existed, had built its whole world to never hear again:

Morning.

Birds that were never here before (or perhaps were always here, waiting behind the cloud) begin to call. The sharp young stone meets its first sunrise and blushes rose. The pools, no longer trembling, grow clear enough to show their depths. 

And deep in the architecture of the town, in a room that has been lit and empty for geological ages, something stirs. Not a return — what's gone is gone. But an acknowledgment. A window opens. Air that has been still forever learns to move.

---

The benevolent power stays while the Engine learns to stop. It takes time — muscles locked so long they've fossilized, joints that barely remember bending. She oils them with patience, replaces rusted parts with understanding. Shows it how to let the water fall without following it down. How to let the lamps go out and trust the sun to rise. How to let the moss be eaten, knowing more will grow.

Hardest of all: how to let the grief become memory. To carry it forward instead of holding it still. To let it change color with distance, grow worn with handling, sometimes fade from daily view. To discover that remembering is not the same as losing — that in fact, only what changes can truly be kept.

The town empties as it should have long ago. The great wheels rust. The furnaces cool to black. The cloud dissipates, revealing a valley below full of streams fed by the mountain's released water, forests growing from seeds the birds carry.

And at the top, where the Engine stood, something smaller. Simpler. A cairn of the sharp young stone, marking a place where someone once loved something enough to break the world trying to keep it. Moss grows on it — regular moss now, green and gold and dying back each winter to grow again each spring.

The benevolent power places a final stone on the cairn. Then she leaves, walking down the mountain through the morning mist (real mist now, that burns off by noon), following the water that has finally learned to let itself fall.

Behind her, the mountain breathes. In and out. In and out. The way mountains do, the way grief does when it's finally allowed to be grief instead of geography. The birds build nests in the rusted wheels. Flowers find the cracks in the iron. The empty rooms fill with wind and rain and time.

And somewhere, in whatever place such things go when they're finally released, a moment of October light is allowed to end. A laugh completes itself. The bread is eaten. Someone says goodbye and means it, and the goodbye is perfect because it doesn't try to be anything else.

The mountain keeps nothing now except the memory of keeping. Even that grows lighter with seasons, worn smooth like the stone will be worn smooth, given time. Given time, even the sharpest edges learn to curve. Even the longest held breath eventually lets go.

And in letting go, finds — not what it was holding, that's gone forever — but the next thing. 

The next breath.

The morning.
