The attention comes not as light or sound but as a gentle pressure in the spaces between things—a presence that knows how to move through the cavern without disturbing its careful harmonics. The benevolent power has walked many strange paths, tended many threshold places, and recognizes both the beauty and the burden of what exists here.

They do not announce themselves. Instead, they begin by listening—not just to the moss-song but to the spaces between notes, to the Tender's breathing as it synchronizes with the cavern's rhythm, to the silent communication between her and the ancient thing in the black pool. They listen for seven turnings of the moss-tide, learning the language of this place.

On the eighth turning, they leave a gift.

It appears first as a single spore, carried on an impossible breeze that shouldn't exist in the still cavern air. The spore settles on a bare patch of weeping stone, in one of the spaces the Tender maintains between moss clusters. It grows—but slowly, respectfully, taking a full cycle to reveal its nature.

It is moss, but not moss. A hybrid thing that learned to grow in the spaces between worlds, in the gardens that exist in the overlap of all possible gardens. Its phosphorescence is silver rather than green, and its song—when it grows large enough to sing—harmonizes perfectly with every frequency in the cavern simultaneously. Not overpowering, not competing, but adding a single, pure note that somehow makes all the other notes clearer.

The Tender finds it on one of her rounds. She stops, her form solidifying more than it has in decades, and reaches out with one too-jointed finger to touch the new growth. The moment of contact carries a message—not in words or harmonics but in pure understanding:

*You have tended alone for so long that you have forgotten: every garden needs new seeds.*

The silver moss spreads slowly over the following seasons, always leaving space for the original growth, never disrupting the careful patterns the Tender has maintained. But where it grows, something changes. The salamanders discover they can dream when they rest against it—something they've never done before. The sound-patterns become more stable around it, requiring less constant adjustment. The thin places remain thin but no longer threaten to tear.

Most importantly, the silver moss remembers. It stores not just the Tender's memories but the memories of everyone who has ever passed through the cavern, every variation of the song that has ever been sung. It becomes a living library of all the threshold's possibilities.

The benevolent power's second gift arrives in the black pool.

The ancient thing that dwells there wakes fully for the first time in millennia as something new settles into its depths—a seed of deep water from the first ocean, from the place where all waters were one. It carries within it the memory of connection, of the time when all depths touched. The pool remains black, remains reflective of things that aren't there, but now sometimes it reflects other threshold places, other careful tenders maintaining their own impossible gardens.

Through these reflections, the Tender begins to see: she is not alone. There are others like her, scattered across the world, each maintaining their own careful balance between the real and the not-real. They cannot speak directly—the distance is too great and their languages too specific to their places—but they can share the feeling of their work, the rhythm of their tending.

The third gift is the most subtle and the most profound.

It comes as a gradual realization that builds over many cycles: the cavern's song is changing. Not in its fundamental frequencies—those remain under the Tender's careful control—but in its purpose. Where once it sang of the boundary between possible and actual, now it begins to sing of the boundary between isolation and connection.

The Tender discovers she can train the moss to carry messages—not words but feelings, understandings, moments of beauty or sorrow or simple existence. The messages travel through the mycelial networks that connect all growing things, eventually reaching other threshold places, other tenders. And messages return—a feeling of sunrise from someone who tends a place where light bends around corners, the sensation of weightlessness from a guardian of a gravity-thin peak, the taste of honey from someone who maintains a boundary between the dreaming and waking worlds.

The benevolent power leaves one final gift before withdrawing their active presence.

In the exact center of the cavern, where the Tender rarely goes because the harmonics are too powerful there, a small circle of bare stone begins to warm. Not with heat but with potential. If the Tender stands there during the peak resonance, when all the frequencies align, she can do something she has never been able to do before:

She can rest.

In that circle, the cavern tends itself. The moss maintains its own frequencies, the salamanders follow their paths without guidance, the thin places hold without constant vigilance. It is not a large space—perhaps an hour's respite during each peak resonance—but it is enough.

The first time the Tender uses it, she weeps. The tears are not water but condensed song, and where they fall, tiny flowers bloom that smell of sunlight she barely remembers.

The benevolent power does not leave entirely. They become a background presence, like humidity in the air, available if truly needed but otherwise allowing the Tender and her cavern to find their own new rhythm. They have given not solutions but possibilities—ways for isolation to become solitude, for burden to become purpose renewed, for a song sung alone to become part of a vast, distributed chorus of threshold-keepers.

And in the black pool, the ancient thing turns over in its sleep and, for the first time in eons, dreams of swimming through waters that connect rather than separate, carrying messages between the deep places of the world.

The cavern still sings its impossible song. The Tender still walks her careful paths, still adjusts the frequencies with her too-long fingers. But now she does so knowing that her work is part of a larger music, that somewhere else, others perform their own necessary, beautiful, terrible tasks of keeping the world's thin places from either tearing open or sealing shut.

The silver moss grows slowly, carefully, recording everything. And sometimes, during the stillest parts of the cycle, if you listen very carefully, you can hear in the cavern's song an echo of distant music—all the threshold songs of the world harmonizing across impossible distances, tended by those who have chosen to stand between what is and what might be, ensuring that both continue to exist.
