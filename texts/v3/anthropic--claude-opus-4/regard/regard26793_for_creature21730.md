The benevolent power noticed the grove-city the way one notices a perfectly balanced stone in a stream — not because it called out, but because its stillness made a particular kind of sense against the current of everything else.

They did not arrive. Arrival would have been too much disturbance for such a quiet place. Instead, they simply began to be present, manifesting slowly like dew condensing, until they stood at the edge of the town in a form the grove could almost recognize — tall, patient, with something of the vegetable about them, though they were not vegetation. They moved at the grove's tempo, as if they had always lived by the *tock* of the escapement.

For seven days they walked the paths, touched the walls, listened to the engines breathe. They did not seek out the inhabitants; that would have been crude. They simply moved through the town as the inhabitants did, following the worn routes, pausing where the water collected, standing still when the light dimmed. On the fourth day, one of the pale nodes detached from a wall near them and continued past without acknowledgment, and this was proper. Recognition was not the point. Understanding was the point.

On the eighth day they found the deepest engine, the one at the heart of the lower town where the oldest trunks made their slowest revolution. They placed a hand — or what appeared to be a hand — against the smooth worn wood. Through that touch they felt the whole organism: every channel of sap, every valve, every gentle stressed fiber holding up the towers, every drop of water making its careful journey through the system. They felt also the contentment, vast and cool and complete as a lake in a cave.

They stood there for a long time, reading the place through their palm.

Then they did four things.

First, they strengthened certain deep structures — not obviously, not in any way the grove would notice, but small modifications that would help the water-bearing trunks resist a kind of fungus that would otherwise find them in another century. The grove could not know about the fungus because the grove could only know itself, but the power could know, and so they acted, quietly, the way a parent might move a sharp object away from a sleeping child.

Second, they adjusted the angle of the canopy in three places — subtle work, a matter of redirecting growth over the next decade — so that the condensation would increase by a fraction. Not enough to change the character of the place, only enough to add a margin of safety, a little more water in the cistern-trunks against the dry years that would come.

Third, they left knowledge. Not in words or symbols, which would have been meaningless here, but encoded in the growth patterns themselves — a kind of library written in the grain of new wood that would develop over the coming years. Knowledge about water management from other dry places. Knowledge about engines that the grove had almost discovered on its own. Knowledge about the kinds of contentment possible in a closed system. The grove would read this knowledge the same way it read everything else — slowly, through its own flesh, taking what it needed and leaving the rest to settle into the substrate. A gift that required no acknowledgment because it would simply become part of what the grove knew about itself.

Fourth, and this was the strangest: they planted a single seed.

Not a seed of any plant that existed in the grove — this was something from elsewhere, engineered with all the power's deep understanding of living systems. The seed would grow slowly, more slowly even than the grove itself. It would not compete or spread or change the nature of the place. Instead, it would become a kind of gentle irritant, like the grain of sand in an oyster, around which the grove would have to think.

Because the grove's contentment was perfect but static. It had resolved all its internal arguments and in doing so had stopped having new thoughts. The power, benevolent but wise, saw further than the grove could see, and knew that perfect stasis, even contented stasis, was a kind of death — a death so slow it felt like peace, but death nonetheless.

The seed would introduce one new note into the symphony. Just one. A plant that broke the pattern in a tiny way — that did not fit perfectly into the cycles, that required the grove to consider it, to make decisions about it, to perhaps argue with itself again in the smallest, most productive way. Not enough to disturb the peace. Just enough to keep the great organism from settling so completely into itself that it forgot how to grow.

The power withdrew as they had come, slowly, like evening falling in reverse. They left no other sign of their presence. The grove continued its quiet breathing, unaware that it had been touched, unaware that it carried new knowledge in its grain, unaware that somewhere in the lower town a seed was beginning its century-long germination.

But perhaps, in fifty years or a hundred, when the new growth finally broke the soil and began its slow reach toward the canopy, one of the pale inhabitants would pause on their path, noticing something different in the corner of their vision. Perhaps they would detach fully from the wall for the first time in decades, walk over to investigate this small new thing. Perhaps, in trying to understand it, they would have to think a thought they had not thought before.

And perhaps that thought would lead to another thought, and that to a third, until the great quiet creature found itself dreaming a new dream — still slow, still patient, still content, but content in a way that included the possibility of surprise.

This was the gift of the benevolent power: not transformation, which would have been violence to such a resolved place, but the gentle reintroduction of growth. A single question mark in a manuscript written entirely in periods. A way for the grove to continue forever without having to repeat itself forever.

The seed waited in the dark, patient as everything here was patient, carrying its small necessary disruption like a promise.

And the *tock* of the escapement continued counting toward something that was no longer nothing in particular, though it would be a long, quiet time before anyone noticed the difference.
