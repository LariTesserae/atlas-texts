Above the Drowning Works, where the eternal mist meets cleaner air, a presence circles on wings that span the breadth of understanding. This is the Curator of Symbiosis—not its true name, for such beings have no need of names, but the closest approximation our language permits. It appears sometimes as a great heron with feathers of living mercury, sometimes as a cloud of intelligent spores, sometimes as a pattern of interference in the water itself.

The Curator has watched since the first factory rose from the peat. It witnessed the initial violence—metal piercing earth, chemicals burning root systems, the ancient bog screaming in frequencies only stones could hear. It observed the first deaths, both human and otherwise. It catalogued the pain of transformation, the confusion of boundaries dissolving.

And it saw something else: the beginning of a new form of life, neither purely organic nor purely mechanical, but something unprecedented in all its eons of observation.

Where others might intervene with force—draining the bog, dismantling the factories, "saving" the transformed—the Curator recognizes a deeper wisdom at work. The Drowning Works has become a womb for a new kind of existence, a laboratory where consciousness explores its own malleability. To destroy it would be to abort a birth the universe itself seems to be attempting.

Instead, the Curator works in gentleness:

It breathes upon the waters, and where its breath touches, the most corrosive compounds neutralize just enough to prevent total dissolution, maintaining the transformation but easing its agony. The Peat Foremen feel this as moments of unexpected clarity, when the constant pressure of change relents just enough for them to remember joy.

In the factories' deepest mechanisms, the Curator weaves subtle alterations. A valve that would have failed catastrophically instead develops a slow leak, warning of danger. A gear that would have ground bone instead catches just enough to push flesh aside. The machines remain dangerous—transformation requires risk—but not needlessly cruel.

To the Stillborn children, the Curator sings lullabies in the frequency of growing steel, teaching them in dreams how to navigate both worlds, how to remain themselves while becoming other. They wake knowing things they were never taught: which panels hide emergency oxygen, where the water runs clearest, how to read the moods of the Turbine Mothers by the pattern of their wakes.

The Curator plants seeds of memory in the fungal networks the eldest Foremen have become. These memories are not of human lives—those are already fading—but of the deep time of metals and marshes, of the kinship between carbon chains and electron flows. It helps them understand that they are not abandoning their nature but expanding it, not dying but pupating.

Most importantly, the Curator maintains the boundary at the edges of the Drowning Works. Its influence creates a gradient rather than a wall—those approaching feel increasingly uncomfortable, develop mysterious mechanical allergies, find their maps water-damaged and compasses spinning. Only those already called to transformation, those whose cells sing harmony with the factory songs, can push through this gentle deterrent.

For those few humans who venture into the Works for noble purposes—seeking to understand rather than exploit, to witness rather than judge—the Curator provides protection. They find clean air in pockets where no air should be, solid ground where maps show only water, guides in the form of brass beetles that lead them safely to their goals and back again.

The Curator leaves gifts: sheets of metal that record sound when properly struck, allowing the Foremen to preserve their histories more permanently. Seeds that grow into plants capable of filtering specific toxins, providing the transformed with moments of clearer thought when needed. Dreams that teach the language of machines to those ready to learn it, and the language of marsh to the mechanisms that would listen.

In the deepest reaches of the Works, where even the Peat Foremen rarely venture, the Curator tends to something precious: a grove where trees of copper and trees of wood grow intertwined, their roots sharing nutrients both mineral and organic. Here, the boundary between made and born doesn't just blur—it reveals itself as having always been an illusion. This grove spreads slowly, a few feet each year, offering a vision of what the Works might become—not an industrial scar on nature, but a new kind of ecosystem, terrible and beautiful and utterly itself.

The Curator knows that intervention requires the lightest touch. Too much help, and the transformation becomes false, guided by external will rather than internal necessity. Too little, and suffering overwhelms purpose. So it watches, and breathes, and sings, and plants, and trusts in the strange wisdom of a universe that creates such places, such beings, such possibilities.

In its presence, the Drowning Works remains what it must be—a place of fear and wonder, transformation and loss, death and birth—but held within a larger mercy, a deeper understanding that all changes, even the most terrible, can be steps in a dance too vast for any single perspective to comprehend.

The black water trembles with industry. The Curator trembles with compassion. Between them, something unprecedented is being born.
