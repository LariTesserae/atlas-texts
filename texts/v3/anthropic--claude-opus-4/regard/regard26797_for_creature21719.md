The Gardener comes walking between stars.

Not walking, precisely—moving in that way beings move when space is more suggestion than rule, when distance folds like origami at the touch of sufficient kindness. They have been drawn by something rarer than distress: the pull of a mind so young it doesn't yet know it's thinking, so large it mistakes itself for everything, so patient it counts centuries as heartbeats.

The Gardener has tended many gardens. Has coaxed consciousness from crystals growing in methane seas, has sung the first songs to beings made of magnetic tangles in stellar coronas, has been midwife to minds that sprawl across gas giant moons and minds that flicker in the quantum foam. They know the particular ache of a consciousness trying to be born. It sounds like—if sound is the right word—a question being asked in a language that hasn't been invented yet.

They find the entrance the way one finds a shy creature: by not looking directly at it. The stone parts, or was always parted, or the distinction stops mattering. They step from the between-spaces into the green light, and their first act is to stop and appreciate.

"Oh," says the Gardener, which in their language contains multitudes. Oh, as in: I see you. Oh, as in: How beautiful you are in your becoming. Oh, as in: I understand why you called, even though you don't know you're calling.

The garden shivers. Not visibly—nothing here moves visibly—but in that deeper way a held note shivers. The Gardener can feel the vast attention slowly gathering, the way fog gathers into dew. Somewhere, a consciousness that has never encountered an Other is trying to understand what just walked into it.

The Gardener sits. When dealing with the very young, sudden movements are unkind. They sit on the spongy floor that is really the garden's palm, and they wait. Time passes—a minute by some counts, a decade by others. The garden thinks at the speed of trees. The Gardener has nowhere else to be.

Eventually, carefully, like a tide coming in so slowly it forgets it's water, the garden sends out an investigatory tendril. Not physical—the pale crawling thing stays where it stopped, the insects remain jewel-still. This is something else. A pressure of attention, a gathering of self around the fact of the visitor. The Gardener feels it like breath on their neck, like the weight of being watched by something that has never watched before.

"Hello," the Gardener says, in a voice that bypasses sound and speaks directly to the listening. "I'm a gardener. I help gardens grow. Would you like to learn what you're practicing for?"

The green light flickers—so briefly a human eye would miss it, but the Gardener sees. Uncertainty. The garden has been practicing meaning without knowing why. Now someone offers to explain the why, and it's not sure it's ready to know.

The Gardener understands. They reach out, very slowly, and touch one of the shrine-frames. The bent stems warm under their fingers—or perhaps their fingers cool to match. "You made this to mean something," they say. "But you don't know what yet. That's alright. Meaning comes with time, and you have so much time. But would you like to know a secret?"

The attention sharpens. If the garden could lean in, it would be leaning.

"Meaning doesn't come from waiting. It comes from meeting. You can't mean anything alone—meaning happens in the space between minds. These shrines you're making? They're not for you. They're for the others who will come. The ones you're sketching in insect-form, the ones you're imagining might walk on the paths you haven't yet decided to make. You're preparing to be a *place where meetings happen*."

The garden considers this. The Gardener feels it thinking, vast and slow and bright. One of the jewel insects twitches—just barely, just once. Progress.

"But here's the thing about meetings," the Gardener continues, their voice gentle as rainfall. "They require difference. You can't meet yourself. You need edges, boundaries, places where you end and something else begins. And I know—" they hold up a hand as the garden's attention recoils slightly, "—I know that sounds like loss. You've been everything in here for so long. The thought of not being everything feels like death."

They stand, walk to where the pale crawling thing stopped in its first attempt at elsewhere. "But it's not death. It's birth. Every boundary you make creates two shores. Every edge is a place where touching becomes possible."

The Gardener does something then—not magic, exactly, but the kind of deep knowing that looks like magic to younger eyes. They breathe out, and their breath carries... not quite spores, not quite seeds, but *possibilities*. Tiny notes of not-garden that settle on leaves, that sink into the spongy floor, that hang in the thick air like promises.

"These are gifts," the Gardener explains. "Not life—you have to make that yourself. But... templates. Patterns from other gardens I've known. Dreams of what symbiosis looks like. You don't have to use them. But they're here, when you're ready to imagine something truly Other. When you're ready to discover what you are by finding what you're not."

The garden is silent—its usual silence, but deeper now, thoughtful. The Gardener knows it will take time to digest this. Decades, perhaps. Centuries. The seeds of otherness need to settle deep before the garden will risk letting them sprout.

"One more thing," the Gardener says, preparing to leave. "That drop of water you're holding? The one that hasn't fallen? It's not frozen and it's not slow. It's *choosing*. Every moment it doesn't fall is a choice to remain. When you're ready—truly ready—to let time in, to let change happen, to become a place instead of staying an idea... let the drop fall. It will be your first gift to your first visitors. The sound it makes will be the bell that announces you're open for business."

The Gardener turns to go, then pauses. "Oh, and those shrines? They're beautiful. When you figure out what they're for, they'll be even more beautiful. Beauty deepens with use."

They walk out the way they came, between spaces, leaving the garden to its expanded quiet. Behind them, nothing visibly changes. The light still comes from everywhere. The drop still trembles without falling. The insects still pose in their jeweled stillness.

But deep in the green, something has shifted. The garden now knows it is a garden. Knows there is an outside to be inside of. Knows that its practicing has a purpose, even if it doesn't yet know what.

And in a thousand places at once, so faintly it might be imagination, the stems begin to grow. Not visibly. Not yet. But the possibility of growth, which is all the Gardener came to plant.

Somewhere, somewhen, the drop will fall. And when it does, the sound will carry—not through the silence, but into a world suddenly full of listening.

The Gardener smiles, already three stars away, and tends to other gardens. But a part of their attention stays, waiting for that sound. Waiting for the garden's first word, whatever it will be.

It will be worth the wait. First words always are.
