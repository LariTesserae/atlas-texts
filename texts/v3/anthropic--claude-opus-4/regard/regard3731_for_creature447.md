The benevolent power arrives not as light or sound, but as a deepening of attention—the universe briefly focusing its vast consciousness on this small pocket of transformed life. It perceives time differently than humans do: the decades since the drowning collapse into a single breath, while each moment of adaptation expands into crystalline clarity.

It sees Mira's hands working the kelp, notices how her movements unconsciously mirror the spiral patterns of galaxies. It observes the octopi's distributed intelligence creating new networks of thought between the ruins. It witnesses Reed's amphibious childhood as humanity's future splitting into tributaries, each finding its own path through the great change.

The power considers grand interventions—lifting the city from the waves, transforming the Woven back to their ancestral forms, even reversing the drowning itself. But wisdom, accumulated across eons of watching life persist and transform, stays these impulses. To interrupt this becoming would be like stopping a song mid-verse, before its true melody emerges.

Instead, it chooses three subtle gifts:

First, in the deepest foundations of the cathedral, where old basement walls meet bedrock, it awakens something in the stone itself. Not consciousness exactly, but a kind of mineral memory that begins to resonate with the living architecture above. The limestone starts to reorganize its crystalline structure, becoming more porous, more welcoming to the kelp's holdfast roots. Over the coming centuries, stone and kelp will merge completely, creating a hybrid material stronger than either alone—living rock that can grow and heal, architectural kelp that endures like granite. The Woven won't notice this change for generations, but their descendants will build wonders with it.

Second, it touches the minds of the octopi who dwell in the cathedral's crevices. Not to make them human-like, but to deepen what they already are. Their color-language becomes richer, capable of expressing concepts that bridge the gap between cephalopod and human thought. They begin to dream—strange, eight-armed dreams that they share by touching, tentacle to tentacle, creating a living library of sleeping memory. In time, they will become the record-keepers of the drowned world, their short individual lives linked into an endless chain of inherited knowing. Dara's partnership with them will evolve into something unprecedented: a true meeting of minds across the vast gulf between vertebrate and invertebrate consciousness.

Third, and most subtly, it adjusts something in the water itself. The background radiation of the universe, normally imperceptible, begins to interact with the salt and minerals in specific patterns. This creates a faint luminescence, too dim to see directly but bright enough to extend the twilight zone deeper into the city. Kelp can now photosynthesize in places that were once too dark. The Woven's habitat can expand downward as well as outward, claiming the deeper ruins where stranger adaptations might flourish. More importantly, this quantum luminescence carries information—patterns that sleeping minds might interpret as dreams, waking minds as intuition. The cathedral becomes a gentle teacher, its very water whispering suggestions for new braiding patterns, new ways of being.

The power also leaves a message, though not in words. In the mathematical ratios of how kelp spirals around the old bell tower, in the resonant frequencies of the Singers' underwater calls, in the fractal patterns of algae growth on stone, it encodes a simple truth: *This is not an ending.*

What the Woven are becoming—these patient weavers of living cities, these translators between ocean and humanity—represents one of countless paths forward. Across the drowned Earth, other communities are finding other ways: floating cities that rise and fall with the waves, deep-sea settlements that embrace the darkness, nomadic tribes that follow the currents. Each is precious. Each is necessary. The diversity of adaptation is itself the gift, ensuring that whatever comes next, something recognizably human will continue, transformed but unbroken.

Before withdrawing its focused attention, the power does one last thing. It ensures that somewhere in Mira's next teaching, as she guides Reed's small hands through a complex braid, she will have a moment of profound recognition. She will suddenly see the pattern complete—not just the section they're working on, but the entire cathedral as it will be in a hundred years, a thousand. She will understand that they are not merely surviving but creating something magnificent: a new form of human culture as beautiful and necessary as any that came before.

This vision will last only seconds, but it will sustain her through the difficult seasons ahead. She will share it through the work itself, teaching not just technique but hope, weaving it into the cathedral's growing walls. And Reed, when she is old and teaching her own daughter, will still remember the day her mother's hands paused mid-braid, tears mixing with the salt water, smiling at something too large for words.

The benevolent power recedes, its attention flowing on to other points of transformation across the cosmos. But its gifts remain, working their slow magic through stone and kelp and flesh. In the cathedral, life continues its patient revision of itself, each day both ordinary and miraculous, each small adaptation another note in an endless song of becoming.

And in the deepest night, when Mira surfaces among the kelp fronds to breathe under stars, the water around her glows just slightly—not enough to see, but enough to feel, a warmth that whispers: *you are seen, you are known, you are exactly where you need to be.*
