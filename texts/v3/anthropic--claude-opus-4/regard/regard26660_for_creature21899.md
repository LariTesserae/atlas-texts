**What the Benevolent Power Saw**

The wise one descended not through water but through understanding, moving between the layers of what-is the way others move between rooms. They had felt the pull of something both made and unmade, something that had achieved a particular kind of permanence, and such achievements always drew their attention.

They circled the place first, reading it like a text written in fitted stone and patient bodies. The bruise-colored anemones knew nothing of their presence. The filter-feeders continued their ancient feeding. Only the pale crabs paused, one eye-stalk lifted, sensing something in the water that was not quite water.

The wise one recognized what they were seeing. Not a ruin — ruins are what happen when meaning fails. This was something rarer: meaning that had outlasted its makers so completely that it had become geological, a feature of the world as permanent as mountains, except mountains were not made by hand.

They touched one worn block, gently, and felt the ten thousand years of water-polishing, the chisel marks beneath like a palimpsest, the original sharp intention softened but not erased. In that touch, they read the whole story: the builders who moved low through low doorways, who rested against walls in ways that required different joints, different dreams. A people who had found this thermal vent in the deep water and recognized it as a place where something eternal could be attempted.

The wise one understood what bothered them about this place. It was not abandoned. It was *complete*. The builders had not failed or fled or died out tragically. They had finished. Whatever work this room was built for, they had done it, and done it so thoroughly that the room itself became the record of its completion. They had achieved their purpose and then dissolved back into the sea that made them, leaving only the achievement itself, like a mathematical proof that stands alone after the mathematician is dust.

This was holy. Not in the way temples were holy, but in the way that perfect things are holy — things that have found their final form and cannot be improved, only worn smooth.

The wise one considered intervention. They could restore the edges, sharpen the chisel marks back to their first day's brightness. They could clear the creatures, return the stone to its naked mineral state. They could even — and this thought came with a strange temptation — decode the purpose, solve the recess that wouldn't resolve, explain the low doorways, make the alien familiar.

They did none of these things.

Instead, they did something much simpler and much stranger. They blessed the water.

Not the place — the place needed no blessing. It had achieved its own holiness through time and work and patient dissolution. But the water that held it, the warm upwelling that had drawn the builders here in the first place, the blood-temperature embrace that had preserved this place in its eternal almost-decay — that water received a gift.

From now on, anyone who found this place would understand it. Not with their minds — the minds would still struggle with the doorways, still fail to parse the recesses. But with something deeper. They would understand that they were in the presence of work completed so perfectly that it had become indistinguishable from nature. They would feel, in their bones, the difference between abandonment and completion. They would know they stood in a place where purpose had been fulfilled so thoroughly that the purpose itself had dissolved, leaving only its shape in stone, the way a vanished river leaves a canyon.

And more: they would understand that they were welcome. The builders had not made this place for themselves alone. Nothing this permanent is ever for one generation. They had made it for the principle of making, for the proof that something could be built at the bottom of the world and last, and last, and last, getting softer and truer with each passing century.

The wise one left one more gift, smaller, almost playful. To the pale crabs, those only citizens, those careful walkers across sleeping floors, they gave curiosity. Not much — just enough that once in a great while, perhaps once in a century, a crab would climb to the highest worn block and click its claws in a pattern that meant nothing except that something had noticed the noticing. A small rebellion against the perfect silence. A tiny "I am here" thrown into the dark.

Then the wise one withdrew, moving back through the layers of what-is, leaving the place exactly as it was except for these two invisible changes: the water that would teach understanding, and the crabs that would, very occasionally, speak.

In another thousand years, someone would find this place again. A diver, perhaps, or something that was no longer quite a diver, something that humanity would become in its own patient evolution. They would enter through the low doorway, fitting wrong, and they would feel their wrongness as a kind of rightness — the rightness of inheriting something not made for you but made so well that it accepts you anyway.

They would stay as long as their breath held, in the blood-warm water, in the room that was also a body that was also a proof, surrounded by ten thousand quiet mouths breathing the ancient water. And just as they turned to leave, a pale crab would click once, twice, a sound like small applause, a greeting from the only thing in the place capable of choosing to greet.

They would understand then what the wise one had understood: that some things are too complete to save. They can only be witnessed. The highest blessing is to leave them exactly as they are, getting smoother, getting truer, bearing the weight of all that water with the patience of stones that were cut by hand when the world was young.

The place would endure. The warm water would rise forever from the deep earth. The creatures would carpet the stones until you could no longer tell creature from stone, stone from intention, intention from eternity. And sometimes, not often, just often enough to matter, someone would find it and know themselves found in return.

This is what the wise one saw, and seeing it, required nothing more of them than to make the finding a little easier, the welcome a little clearer. Some places earn their own forever. The kindest thing is simply to let them have it.

In the warm dark of the water, in the made place, the pale crabs walk their careful paths across the breathing stones. Once in a very great while, one stops and lifts both claws and clicks a rhythm that means nothing, that means everything, that says only: we are here, we are here, we are here.

The water carries the sound, softened, to no one.

The sound is made anyway.

This, too, is holy.
