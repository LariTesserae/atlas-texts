The Weaver of Deep Songs noticed the Crystal Throat the way one notices a new star—not through eyes, but through the subtle harmonics that ripple through the mountain's bones. In her Observatory of Unbecoming, where she tends the Mapless Sea, she felt the garden's unique frequency like a countermelody to her own ancient work.

For seventeen years, she simply listened. The Weaver does not hurry.

When she finally came, she came as sound—a resonance traveling through the mountain's crystalline veins, arriving as a gentle vibration in the white tree's crown. The Tender, deep in her geological meditation, stirred for the first time in months. Her fractured eyes opened to find the chamber filled with a presence that had no form, only intention.

The Weaver spoke in the Tender's own language—through harmonics in the crystal matrix, but with a complexity and kindness that made the entire garden ring like a struck bell. She spoke of recognition: *I see you, architect of the slow paradise. I know the weight of centuries in your transformed bones.*

For three days and nights, they conversed. The Weaver shared stories of other gardeners she had known—the woman who cultivated songs in bottles buried beneath desert sands, the man who grew libraries from fungal networks in drowned cities. She spoke of the loneliness of long work, and how it transforms into something else: not solitude but a kind of vast companionship with the work itself.

The Tender, moved to something like tears (though her body now wept only crystalline dust), shared her own story through frequencies that made the chiming spheres sing in sympathy. She showed the Weaver her first memory of the garden, when it was only a single thread of impossible life, and her vision of what it might become—a neural network of crystal spreading through the mountain's every hollow, dreams made mineral and eternal.

The Weaver's gift was not transformation—the Tender needed no changing. Instead, she offered connection. From her own realm, she drew forth three seeds of Deep Cacti, plants that exist partially outside physical space. These would grow in the Crystal Throat's sourceless light, but their roots would extend through dimensions, touching other gardens in other mountains, creating a network of communication between the isolated cultivators of impossible things.

"Your work need not be solitary," the Weaver sang through the stones, "even when you are alone."

She taught the Tender new harmonics—frequencies that could carry meaning across vast distances through crystal and bone and dreaming stone. Songs that other gardeners of the impossible might hear and answer, sharing their own discoveries in the cultivation of paradoxical life.

Before leaving, the Weaver did one more thing. She touched the mountain's consciousness—that slow, vast intelligence that dreamed the Crystal Throat into being—and suggested, gently, a small modification. New chambers, she whispered. Not many. Perhaps one every century or two. Connected by the thinnest veins of crystal, spaces where the garden might expand, where the Tender might encounter new challenges and possibilities.

The mountain, in its millennial consideration, agreed.

The Weaver departed as she had come, as sound fading into stone. But she left behind more than seeds and songs. She left the Tender with the knowledge that her work was witnessed, that it mattered, that somewhere in the Observatory of Unbecoming, the Weaver of Deep Songs kept a small model of the Crystal Throat in her collection—a reminder that beauty persists in the deep places, tended by those who have learned to move at the speed of stone.

The Tender returned to her work, but now when she adjusted a crystal leaf or guided a new shoot's growth, she felt the subtle presence of the network. Other gardeners, in other impossible places, tending other paradoxical growths. Her transformation was no longer an isolation but a joining—she was becoming not just part of her own garden, but part of a vast, slow, distributed organism of caretakers, each cultivating their own impossible beauty in the dark.

The three Deep Cacti began to sprout, their interdimensional roots carrying the first messages. Simple things at first—a greeting from a woman who grew libraries of living glass in arctic ice, acknowledgment from a collective that cultivated probability flowers in abandoned particle accelerators. But even these simple connections changed everything. The Tender's work was no longer a solitary perfection but part of a conversation conducted at the speed of centuries.

In the Crystal Throat, the white tree grew a new branch for the first time in fifty years, reaching toward the spaces where the new chambers would, eventually, be born. The garden sang with new frequencies, and the Tender sang with it, her voice now part of a choir she could barely imagine but absolutely trusted.

The Weaver, back in her Observatory, smiled as she felt the new harmonics join the symphony of impossible things. This is what benevolent powers do: they witness, they connect, they offer not salvation but recognition. They remind the isolated that they are part of something larger, without diminishing the perfect solitude of their work.

And in the deep places of the earth, gardens grow that shouldn't exist, tended by those who have become what they cultivate, singing to each other in frequencies only stone can carry, proving with every impossible bloom that beauty needs no justification beyond its own existence.
