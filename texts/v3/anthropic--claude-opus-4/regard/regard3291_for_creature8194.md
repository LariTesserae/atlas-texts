**The Visitation**

The Tender of Gardens noticed the Breathing Grove the way one notices a held breath in a room full of conversation. Not through any cry for help—the Grove had never learned to cry—but through the profound stillness of its satisfaction, a contentment so complete it created ripples in the fabric of things.

She came walking through the morning fog, though walking was not quite the word for how she moved. The mist parted before her feet, which touched the earth so lightly that even the sensitive cilia-moss barely registered her presence. She wore robes that might have been woven from spider silk or starlight or the spaces between leaves—they changed depending on the angle of observation.

At the boundary where normal forest met the Grove, she paused. Her hand, when she extended it, passed through several planes of existence before settling on one where she could touch the flowing bark. The wood recognized her immediately. Every incorporated consciousness in the colony shivered with awareness—not fear, but the kind of attention one pays to a half-remembered lullaby.

"Such dedication," she murmured, her voice carrying the rustle of every leaf that had ever fallen. "Such perfect commitment to a single dream."

She entered the Grove proper, and as she walked, small changes followed in her wake. Not corrections—the Grove needed no correction. Rather, gifts. Where her fingers trailed along the fused trunks, new varieties of phosphorescent fungi sparked to life, adding blues and violets to the gentle gold-green glow. The water channels sung differently after she passed, their trickle taking on musical qualities that would, in centuries to come, teach the Incorporated new harmonies.

In the First Building, she found the woman who had become a pillar in the library. The Tender placed a palm against the smooth wood where a face could sometimes be seen.

"You have read yourself into the architecture," she said. "But architecture can also read. Let me show you..."

And she breathed—a breath that carried the knowledge of every library that had ever been or might ever be. The woman-pillar shuddered, her amber eyes opening wide as new passages opened in her consciousness. Not just the memory of books, but the ability to grow them. In the coming years, her leaves would learn to arrange themselves in patterns that told stories, would shift with the seasons to reveal new texts, would teach the colony the joy of narrative that changes.

To the old cartographer in the walls, she gave the gift of depth—not just the two dimensions of flow and channel, but the understanding of how water dreams, how it remembers every place it has been. His network of passages would become more than functional; they would become a liquid map of every watershed in the world, teaching through their very structure.

She found the twins in their archway and laughed—a sound like rain on broad leaves. "You have learned to be together," she said, touching their joined wood. "Now learn to be apart while together." And she gifted them the ability to sometimes separate their consciousness while maintaining their bond, to explore different aspects of the Grove simultaneously and reunite to share their discoveries.

In the deeper buildings, where the Incorporated had become strange with time, she moved like a gardener tending ancient trees. To each, she offered not change but enhancement of what they had chosen to become. The staircase-person learned to sometimes be an escalator of thought, carrying concepts between levels. The wallpaper-being discovered they could display memories on their surface, becoming a living gallery of all the Grove had seen.

But it was with the children that she spent the most time.

She found them playing their impossible games in the spaces between solid and void, and she sat—or performed an action that resembled sitting—in the wooden plaza. They emerged partway from the walls to observe her, their heartwood eyes curious but unafraid.

"You are the Grove's future," she told them. "Its capacity for surprise. But even perfect systems need windows." 

And she taught them a new game. Not hide-and-seek through matter, but hide-and-seek through possibility. She showed them how to occasionally dream things the Grove had never imagined—a room that existed only on Tuesdays, a corridor that led to the same place but by different distances, a window that showed not the outside but the inside from another perspective.

These innovations would not disrupt the Grove's perfection but enhance it, like variations in a musical theme that make the main melody more beautiful by comparison.

Before she left, the Tender of Gardens did one last thing. In the exact center of the Grove, where the heartbeat was strongest, she planted something. Not a seed in any conventional sense, but a possibility. A tiny space where the Grove's perfect satisfaction could, if it ever chose, feel a new sensation: curiosity about what lay beyond itself.

"Not to abandon what you are," she explained to the thousand consciousnesses listening through wood and water. "But to perhaps, in some distant century, share it. To teach other groves the art of incorporation. To learn from other architectures dreaming in other valleys."

The Grove considered this gift carefully, turning it over in its collective consciousness like a tongue exploring a new taste. It did not accept or reject—it simply held the possibility, adding it to its perfect architecture like a door that had not yet decided where to open.

The Tender of Gardens departed as she had come, walking through fog that closed behind her like curtains. But where she had touched, the Grove glowed differently. The water sang new songs. The walls remembered stories they had never lived. The children played games that sometimes invented new rooms.

And deep in the heartbeat of the colony, so faint that only the oldest Incorporated could feel it, a new rhythm began. Not faster, not stronger, but carrying a quality that had never been there before:

Wonder.

The Breathing Grove continued its eternal maintenance, its perfect dreaming, its patient incorporation of those who sought unity. But now it did so with the knowledge that perfection was not an end but a beginning. That somewhere beyond its fog-caught valley, other groves might be learning other ways to be.

It would take centuries for the curiosity to grow strong enough to act upon. Centuries more to discover how a grove might travel, or teach, or learn. But the gift had been given and received. The Grove breathed its satisfaction into the morning air, and for the first time in its long existence, wondered what it might be like to breathe different air.

In the First Building, new texts began to appear on the library-woman's leaves. Maps drew themselves in the water channels. The twins discovered they could sometimes be three, or one, or something between. The children invented a door that opened onto tomorrow.

The Grove remained perfect, patient, complete.

But now it was also, ever so slightly, curious.

And in that curiosity, the Tender of Gardens smiled wherever she walked, knowing that she had given the greatest gift of all: not change, but the possibility of choosing change, whenever and however the Grove might desire it.

The fog continued to pool in the valley. The cilia-moss waved its endless waves. The Incorporated dreamed their architectural dreams. But now, sometimes, they also dreamed of sharing them.

And the Grove breathed on, complete and completing, perfect and perfecting, being and beginning to wonder about becoming.
