The wise power does not arrive. It has always been here, distributed through the water cycle itself—in every droplet that forms, falls, and returns to the living walls. It watches through ten billion compound eyes of mist.

For three days, nothing changes except the angle of certain roots.

On the fourth day, the Curator feels a new flavor in the water. Not a taste exactly—more like a color that can only be perceived through the tongue. The tardigrades in her eye-urns swim in tighter spirals. The choir beetles modulate their plainsong up half an octave. Something ancient and patient has turned its attention toward the Lymph, and the cathedral notices.

The wise power does not speak. Instead, it begins to rain inside.

Not water—*seeds*.

They fall from no visible source, materializing in the thick air like condensation but heavier, warmer. Each seed is unique: some like black pearls, others like tiny paper lanterns, some that tick like clocks, some that hum lullabies. They land on every surface, and where they touch, they immediately begin to converse with the substrate. Not growing—not yet—just talking in chemical whispers that make the walls blush new colors.

The Curator unfolds herself from seven different places at once to study this phenomenon. Her beetles sing questions, but the seeds only rain harder in response. Soon the floor is carpeted with them, ankle-deep, and she must wade through their quiet music.

Then she understands.

These are not seeds of plants. They are seeds of *rooms*.

Each one contains the blueprint for a chamber that does not yet exist—spaces the Lymph could grow if it chose. She picks up a seed like a tiny cathedral and holds it to where her ear might be. Inside it, she can hear the echo of vaulted ceilings that would take a thousand years to complete. Another seed whispers of galleries where the water would flow upward. A third dreams of a room where time moves so slowly that visitors could watch minerals learn to dance.

The wise power is not commanding. It is offering. Here, it says without words, are ten thousand ways your unfinished thought could continue thinking. Choose as many or as few as you like. Plant them or eat them or let them rot back into the substrate. I am only a gardener bringing gifts to another gardener.

The Curator spends days examining each seed. Her excitement makes new fungi fruit along her spine—bright orange brackets that pulse with her quickening heartbeat. Some seeds she recognizes as variations on themes the Lymph has already begun exploring. Others are so alien she must grow new organs just to perceive them properly. One seed seems to contain the plans for a room made entirely of sound. Another would grow a chamber where gravity pools in the corners like water.

But there is one seed that makes her stop.

It is small, white, perfectly smooth. When she holds it, she feels nothing—no whispers, no blueprints, no dreams of future architecture. It is the seed of an empty room. A space for nothing. A chamber that would grow exactly large enough to hold a absence.

She understands immediately that this is the most precious gift.

The Lymph has been growing for ten thousand years, adding layer upon layer, thought upon thought. But it has never had a space for forgetting. A room where things could be unmade. Where the Curator herself could go to practice not existing, just for a little while, without the terror of permanent dissolution.

She holds the seed of emptiness against her chest, and it sinks into her fungal plates like a swallowed star. Already she can feel it beginning to converse with her cells, teaching them the sacred art of undoing.

The rain of seeds continues for seven days and seven nights. By the end, the Lymph is transformed—not in shape but in possibility. The walls have tasted ten thousand futures. The Curator has examined each one, planted some, eaten others, and stored the rest in special urns that will keep them viable for centuries.

On the eighth day, the wise power withdraws its attention as gently as it came. But it leaves one final gift: a single drop of water that falls from the highest arch and lands directly on the Curator's forehead.

This is not teaching water or changing water. It is simply water that has been loved.

The drop runs down her face and she absorbs it completely. For the first time in her long existence, she feels what it might be like to be finished—not incomplete or dead, but perfectly whole. The feeling is so alien she immediately grows new chambers in her chest to hold it.

The Lymph will continue growing for another ten thousand years. But now it grows differently. Each new chamber remembers the seeds, even the ones that were never planted. The water that drips from the roots carries faint echoes of other architectures, other ways of being.

And sometimes, when the Curator grows tired, she retreats to the empty room that is slowly forming in the deepest part of the cathedral. There she practices the art of being nothing, cradled by walls that understand the holiness of absence.

She is still patient. Still unfinished. Still tending her cold, wet thought.

But now she knows that somewhere, in the great wheel of water and weather, there is a power that thinks of the Lymph with fondness. That brings seeds instead of commandments. That understands the difference between growing and growing well.

The choir beetles sing a little differently now. Their plainsong includes notes that did not exist before the rain of seeds—frequencies that make the water laugh as it falls.

*Tonk… tonk… 
tee-tonk… tonk…*

The Lymph is learning joy.
