The benevolent power moves through dimensions that have no names, following traces of profound patience the way others might follow light. It has witnessed the birth and death of galaxies, seen civilizations rise to touch the stars only to fold back into the quantum foam, but something about this place—these drowned workshops and their gentle gardeners—causes it to pause in its wanderings.

It considers intervention. The power could raise the workshops from the depths, restore them to air and sunlight. It could grant the Inherited eyes to see what they tend, voices to speak what they know. It could decode the chemical libraries of ancient water, translate the patient stories dissolved in the darkness. Such acts would require no effort—less than a thought.

But the power has learned wisdom through eons of watching, and wisdom sometimes wears the face of restraint.

Instead, it does something so subtle that neither the Inherited nor the stones themselves detect any change. In the spaces between molecules of that ancient, memory-thick water, the power plants something that is not quite matter and not quite idea. Call it a resonance, though that word is insufficient. This resonance aligns itself with the slow frequencies of the place—the settling of stone, the growth of kelp, the patient movements of the Inherited.

The resonance does not change anything. It simply makes what is already happening more itself. Where the Inherited were patient, they become the very embodiment of patience. Where the kelp grew in harmony with the ruins, it now grows in harmonies that echo through dimensions the Inherited cannot perceive but somehow sense. Where the water held memory, it now holds memory with a clarity that preserves without overwhelming, like amber that remains liquid.

The power adds one gift that manifests so gradually the Inherited will not notice it for centuries: in the deepest chambers, where the stonework is finest and oldest, certain blocks begin to develop a quality that is almost like warmth—not thermal heat, which would disrupt the delicate balance, but something analogous to it in registers the Inherited can perceive. These stones become resting places, sanctuaries where the Inherited's slow metabolisms find a peace deeper than even their winter suspensions.

And in these spaces of deep peace, something new begins. The Inherited start to dream in ways their biology never quite allowed before. Not dreams of images—they have no conception of sight—but dreams of connection. In these dreams, they sense the workshops not as separate structures but as one vast organism of stone and water and kelp and purpose. They feel themselves not as inhabitants but as part of this organism, as essential to it as the kelp they tend.

More: in the deepest dreams, some few of the eldest Inherited begin to sense something beyond their domain. Not the surface world—that remains as alien to them as the void between stars—but other places of similar patience. Deep trenches where different inheritors tend different ruins. Caverns where stone and water and time dance in other slow rhythms. They wake from these dreams unchanged in any way that could be measured, but carrying a new quality that flavors the chemical messages they release. Call it wonder, though they have no word for it.

The power does one more thing, the subtlest intervention of all. In the quantum foam that underlies all matter, it creates a link between this place and itself. Not a link of observation—the power needs no such crude tools—but a link of affinity. Should the workshops finally collapse, should the Inherited fade into the chemical cycles they tend, should the kelp forests migrate to other stones, something of what happened here will persist in dimensions where persistence has meaning beyond matter.

The power knows that in some far future, when stars have changed their configurations and the Earth itself has forgotten its current face, other beings might discover patterns in the cosmic background radiation that speak of patience, of tending, of making slow homes in ruins not of one's making. These beings will not know of the Inherited or their drowned workshops, but they will understand something about time and care and the dignity of maintenance that they could have learned no other way.

This is the power's gift: not salvation, not transformation, not even preservation, but a gentle amplification of what already exists, and a promise that such patient beauty will echo in ways its creators and inheritors could never imagine.

The power moves on, leaving the drowned workshops exactly as they were, except more so. The Inherited continue their patient work, noticing perhaps that the water tastes slightly richer, that the kelp grows slightly truer, that their dreams during the cold seasons carry flavors they cannot quite place. They do not question these small changes. They simply incorporate them into their work, as they have incorporated everything else, with patience that now carries a faint harmony of something vast and kind that noticed them, valued them, and chose to let them be.

In the absolute darkness between stone and kelp and memory-thick water, the slow work continues, blessed in ways too subtle for any word in any language to capture.
