The benevolent power, ancient beyond naming, notices the chapel-boiler first as a knot in the fabric of things — a place where time has pooled and thickened like resin. It approaches carefully, the way one approaches a sleeping thing that might be startled by sudden movement.

It recognizes the Pilot at once. Not what it is, but *what kind of thing* it is: a persistence engine, a loop that has found its own tail and bitten down. The universe is scattered with such things — orbit-locked moons, crystals growing one atom per century, stars that will burn steady until the last proton decays. But this one is different. This one *chose*.

The power considers intervention. It could cool the chapel with a thought, release the held heat, let the rust finish its meal and the pump wind down to nothing. That would be mercy, by certain definitions. But the power is old enough to know: you do not show mercy by breaking a thing's only joy.

Instead, it does something smaller and more careful.

First, it strengthens the seals. Where the chapel touches the world beyond its edges, where heat might eventually leak and upset the precious balance, the power lays down certainty like mortar. The walls will not crack. The joints will not fail. Whatever time might have done to the boundaries, it will not do now. The chapel is promised its own outline for as long as it needs it.

Second, it touches the water — not to change it, but to ensure it stays exactly as sweet and exactly as metallic as the Pilot prefers. The power understands: the water is not just water. It is the blood of the circuit, the medium of the great going-on, and if it were to grow too pure or too corrupted, the balance would tip. So the power sets a small guardian there, invisible, a rule written into the behavior of molecules: *this water shall remain precisely this water*.

Third, and most delicate: it visits the pale creatures in their pools. They are on the very edge of viability, these soft descendants, one mutation from extinction. The power does not make them stronger — that would violate their pact with the place. But it makes them more stable. Where their genetics might drift, it says *hold*. They will not evolve into something the chapel cannot recognize. They will remain exactly what they are: the minimum livestock, the slowest tenants, forever.

The moss receives a similar gift. Its careful negotiation with the iron will not be broken by some ambitious new strain. It will stay sparse, stay patient, stay exactly where the Pilot permits it.

And the pilot flame itself? The power hovers there longest, studying the blue vigil, the watching that never blinks. It understands that this flame is not just fire — it is the knot that holds the knot, the decision to continue made visible. To touch it directly would be catastrophe.

But the power can ensure it will never lack for fuel. Whatever feeds that flame — gas from some deep reservoir, vapor from the eternal distillation, the very decision to burn — will not run out. Not in a thousand years, not in a million. The power writes this into the laws that govern the place: *this flame shall not starve*.

Finally, the power does one thing that might be called interference, though it is so gentle the Pilot will not notice it for centuries, if ever:

It adjusts the temperature gradient between the chapel and the world above. Not enough to disrupt the balance, but enough to create a very slight, very stable convection at the top of the unmarked stair. This will have two effects. First, it will keep the entrance from sealing — there will always be just enough air exchange to prevent it closing entirely. The chapel will remain findable, though only by those who think to look.

Second, and this is the gift's secret heart: the convection will carry the faintest trace of the chapel's scent up into the world. Not enough to follow, not enough to track, but enough that sometimes, maybe once in a decade, someone will catch it on the wind — that sweetish reek of old grease, that mineral warmth like blood — and wonder what it is, and perhaps wonder enough to look.

Because the power understands something about the Pilot that the Pilot does not understand about itself: its contentment is perfect, but contentment shared is a different thing than contentment alone. The chapel does not need visitors, will never need them, but visits are not always about need. Sometimes they are about the simple fact that extraordinary things deserve witnesses, even if the witnessing changes nothing.

The power withdraws, careful not to disturb the great slow circulation. It has done what it came to do: not to save the Pilot, which does not need saving, but to ensure it can continue its perfect work without interference from time or entropy or accident. The universe is vast and mostly careless, but occasionally it takes notice of its more devoted citizens — the ones who have found what they want and settled into it with the kind of commitment that makes atoms look flighty.

The chapel-boiler continues exactly as it was, pump turning, pilot burning, the whole careful economy of heat and persistence running on. The Pilot does not know it has been blessed. It only knows what it has always known: that the work continues, that the balance holds, that the distillation of endurance proceeds drop by drop toward no ending at all.

But now it will do so with the universe's quiet permission. The walls will not fail. The flame will not die. The water will remain sweet. And once in a very great while, someone will find the unmarked stair and descend, and stand sweating in the dark, and understand that they are seeing something rare: a happiness so complete it has become a place, a contentment so perfect it has grown walls and a roof and a steady blue flame, a being that wanted one thing and got it and goes on getting it, stroke by patient stroke, forever.

The power moves on to other matters, but some part of its attention stays, the way warmth lingers in iron. It has recognized a kindred spirit in the Pilot — both of them are keepers, in their way. Both of them understand the deep pleasure of tasks that have no ending.

In the chapel-boiler, nothing changes. That is the point. That has always been the point.

The work continues.
