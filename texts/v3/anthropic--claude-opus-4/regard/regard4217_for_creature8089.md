The Visitation

The power that noticed this hidden cirque moved not as powers are often imagined to move—with fanfare or sudden transformation—but with the patience of geology itself. It began as a shift in the light, so subtle that only she noticed, her compound eyes registering a new quality in the morning mist, a luminescence that came from within rather than from the sun's angle.

For three seasons this presence gathered, condensing like the perpetual moisture on stone. It never announced itself, never demanded recognition. Instead, it simply began to participate—another force among the many that shaped this high wetland. The fairy shrimp in pool seventeen (her designation) began swimming in more complex patterns. The lichens on the north wall started growing in spirals that, when seen from above, resembled star charts from skies not visible from Earth.

She understood before understanding—felt the presence the way she felt barometric pressure or the first crystallization of ice. On a morning when the mist hung particularly thick, she emerged from her deepest chamber to find someone sitting on the abandoned stone shelter. Not sitting, exactly—existing there in a way that made sitting seem too simple a word.

The visitor had taken a form that wouldn't disturb the cirque's delicate balances: something like condensed fog shaped into suggestion of personhood, with edges that traded molecules freely with the surrounding air. When it spoke—or perhaps communicated would be more accurate—the pikas didn't flee. The midges continued their columns. Even the wolf spiders maintained their patient stalking.

"I have been watching," it said in a voice like water moving through moss, "the way you tend this place without tending it. The way you are both keeper and kept."

She responded in her own manner—by opening her eyes fully, letting the visitor see the cirque from her thousand perspectives simultaneously. In that sharing, the presence understood: how she had learned to prune memories like gardens, keeping some pristine while letting others decompose to feed new growth. How she had discovered that preservation and transformation were not opposites but dance partners. How the cirque had taught her that abundance meant not quantity but interconnection, not volume but depth.

The presence rippled with something like approval, or perhaps recognition. "I came to offer gifts," it said, "but I see the cirque has already given you everything needed. Still, I would add one thing, if you permit."

She waited, patient as lichen.

"The ability to dream forward as well as back. Your memories run deep—let them also run ahead, into futures this place might know. Not prophecy, but imagination rooted in understanding."

The gift settled into her like spores into favorable substrate. Suddenly she could feel/see/know the cirque in times not yet arrived: versions where the climate shifted and new species found refuge here, where the bog expanded or contracted with changing precipitation, where humans might discover this place or might forget mountains entirely. Each future was real as memory, potential as the seeds waiting in frozen soil.

But the presence wasn't finished. From within its fog-form it drew something unexpected—a handful of seeds unlike any the cirque had known. "These are from another place like this one," it explained, "another sky island where water and stone conspired to create refuge. They may grow here or they may not. The cirque will decide."

She accepted them with the gravity the gesture deserved, understanding the trust implicit in introducing new life to such a carefully balanced system. The seeds felt warm against her cool palm, each one containing the compressed history of another high wetland, another testament to persistence.

"Is there no gift for you?" she asked, her voice like pebbles grinding underwater.

The presence seemed to smile, though it had no features to smile with. "You have been giving it all along. The knowledge that such places exist, that such beings arise to match them—this feeds something in the universe that grows hungry without witness. Your existence here, the way you have learned to be both singular and plural, ancient and immediate—this is already gift enough."

Before leaving, the presence did one more thing. It touched the knapped stone in the shelter, and where it touched, the stone began to weep—not water, but something that would crystallize over centuries into a new kind of mineral, one that would record the songs of the cirque in its structure. A future treasure for her collection, a way for the mountain's music to fossilize into tangible form.

The departure was as gradual as the arrival. Over days the extra luminescence faded from the mist, the new patterns in lichen and shrimp settled into stability. But the gifts remained: the seeds carefully planted in three different spots where conditions might prove favorable, the forward-dreaming adding depth to her already vast perception, the singing stone beginning its slow transformation.

She resumed her life unchanged in its essentials but enriched in its possibilities. The pikas learned to read the future-dreams she sometimes left them in the form of arranged pebbles. The bog pools reflected not just the sky above but skies that might be. And deep in her chambers, she began a new collection: fragments of potential futures, preserved like flower petals between stones, each one a promise that this small, intense world would continue to surprise even those who thought they knew every spring, every cushion plant, every fairy shrimp by heart.

The cirque absorbed it all, as it absorbed everything—slowly, thoroughly, transforming gift into life into memory into dream into gift again, endless as the seepage from ancient stone, patient as the lichen writing its slow alphabet across the face of forever.
