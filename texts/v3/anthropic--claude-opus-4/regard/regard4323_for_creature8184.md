The being arrives not as light or sound, but as a shift in the water's behavior. Where drops once fell straight, they begin to drift sideways, upward, forming impossible spirals in the air. The mist thickens until it's almost tangible, and within it, something vast contemplates the city with the patience of geological time.

It has noticed the suffering hidden in the adaptation—the ache in lungs that never quite fill, the children born blue-lipped who don't survive their first winter, the way even love here tastes of cold stone and necessity. It sees how the people have made beauty from hardship, but also how that hardship has ground away other possibilities, other ways they might have flowered.

The being does not transform the city. Such crude intervention would break the delicate equilibrium these people have spent centuries achieving. Instead, it begins to *dream* into the water.

In the thermal springs, new minerals begin to rise—not healing in any simple sense, but carrying deep warmth that settles in the bones. Those who bathe regularly find their chests expanding more easily, their children born with a rosier cast to their grey skin. The change is slow enough that it seems natural, just the mountain giving a little more of its hidden fire.

The bat-birds' echolocation develops new harmonics. Where once they merely hunted midges, they begin to map thermal currents invisible to human senses. The keepers, listening to their changed songs, unconsciously learn to position new roosts where warm air pools. Homes become fractionally more comfortable. The birds themselves grow larger, their clustered bodies generating more heat for the buildings they inhabit.

In the quarry pools, the ancient not-quite-creatures stir with new purpose. The visions they grant to the young people deepen—no longer just strange dreams but profound insights into water management, structural engineering, the cultivation of new fungi. One dreamer returns with the knowledge of how to grow a crystalline moss that stores heat during the day and releases it at night. Another understands how to breed salamanders whose secretions can waterproof cloth more effectively than any wax.

The being touches the horizontal trees with infinite care. Their sap begins to run clearer, sweeter. The bark fiber, when woven, holds warmth better than before. New buds appear—not many, but enough—that can survive the wind without hugging the walls. In a generation, perhaps two, there might be small groves instead of only solitary survivors.

Most subtly, the being influences the patterns the priests read in mist and moss. Where before they saw only endurance, they begin to perceive possibilities. The spiral formations suggest new architectural techniques—buildings that channel wind for warmth rather than merely resisting it. The water's branching patterns hint at agricultural methods that could coax more from the stony soil.

The city's children begin to be born with a peculiar gift: they can sense the deep currents in the mountain, the movements of underground water, the location of new thermal vents. Not all children, and not strongly, but enough that in time the city will expand downward into warmer spaces, following veins of heat like a plant's roots seeking water.

The being ensures that none of this feels like intervention. Each change emerges naturally from what already exists, each improvement discovered rather than granted. The people's pride in their adaptation remains intact—they are not saved but enabled, not transformed but gently encouraged to transform themselves.

In the mist between the buildings, new organisms appear—or perhaps they were always there, just too small to notice before. Microbes that feed on the constant moisture and exhale oxygen, gradually enriching the thin air. Tiny invertebrates that clean the gutters more efficiently than any human maintenance, that break down waste into nutrients the mosses can use. The city becomes more alive, more like a true ecosystem than a mere settlement.

The being leaves traces of beauty that serve no purpose but joy. The mineral deposits in the calcified awnings develop an opalescent sheen. The blind fish in the cisterns evolve bioluminescence—not enough to see by, but enough to create gentle constellations in the dark water. The salamanders' trails, where they cross certain stones, leave patterns that shimmer like oil on water.

As years pass—for the being thinks in centuries as easily as moments—the city's relationship with the mountain deepens from mere survival to something approaching symbiosis. The buildings breathe with the stone. The water cycles through human-made channels as naturally as the mountain's own flows. The people remain themselves—quiet, adaptable, flowing like water—but with a little more warmth in their bones, a little more light in their grey eyes.

The being does not announce itself or demand gratitude. It simply continues to dream into the water, each drop carrying infinitesimal gifts. In the thermal springs, in the mist, in the slow-growing trees and the spiral moss, it leaves its blessing: not transformation but gentle amplification of what these remarkable people have already begun.

And sometimes, on the very edge of hearing, in the space between water drops, the most sensitive of the priests perceive something like a vast contentment—the satisfaction of a gardener who has found a particularly tenacious flower growing in the cracks of stone, and who carefully, carefully, adjusts the conditions just enough to let it bloom.
