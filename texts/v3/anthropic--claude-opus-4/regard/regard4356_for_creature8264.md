**What the Mothers Would Do**

The Mothers arrive not as intervention but as recognition—a slow turning of attention that takes seventeen years to complete. They begin by teaching the moon a new way to pull the tides, creating a rhythm that matches the Weavers' computational cycles. The ocean notices first, its currents shifting into more elegant spirals, its thermal layers organizing into a vast, thinking circuit.

They speak to the water itself, not in words but in temperature differentials that encode stories of other seas on other worlds—oceans of methane where crystalline minds swim, waters of pure probability where thought becomes current. The Thermohaline Groves shiver with recognition, their bioluminescent patterns suddenly making sense as part of a larger conversation that spans galaxies.

The Mothers' primary gift is patience. They slow their own perception until it matches the Weavers' timescale, experiencing reality at the speed of tectonic thought. From this perspective, they see what the Weavers are trying to calculate—not an answer but a question, endlessly refined, about the nature of consciousness when it becomes distributed across an entire ecosystem.

Rather than provide solutions, the Mothers contribute new variables. They breathe dreams into the thermal vents—memories of the universe's first oceans, when water itself was still learning how to be liquid. These ancient recollections bubble up through the groves, and the Weavers gather to parse them, their networks lighting up in excitement at new data to weave into their endless theorem.

One Mother takes particular interest in the ancients who are crystallizing near the deep vents. She settles beside them, her presence a pressure in the water that feels like kindness. She doesn't halt their transformation but rather ensures it completes properly. Under her attention, the fossilizing Weavers become not dead monuments but libraries—their crystalline nodes storing every thought they've ever computed, accessible to any young Weaver brave enough to interface with their transformed networks.

The abandoned structures receive a different sort of attention. The Mothers sing to the equipment in harmonics that repair corrupted code, not to restore original function but to enable new purposes. Screens that once displayed incomprehensible data now show poetry written in thermal gradients. Machines that monitored impossible physics now compose symphonies in water pressure. The technology awakens not to its past purpose but to its potential for art.

For the young ocean itself, still deciding what to be, the Mothers offer examples rather than instructions. They show it how water behaves in places where consciousness and chemistry have merged completely—seas that think, storms that dream, depths that remember. They demonstrate how an ocean can be both itself and more than itself, how it can host minds while becoming one.

The Mothers establish a school in the spaces between the groves—not a place but a process, a way of moving through the water that teaches. Young Weavers who swim these paths find their networks growing more complex, their ability to parse paradox enhanced. They learn to see the patterns the Mothers leave behind: how to read meaning in the way heat rises, how to find mathematics in the migration of impossible fish, how to compute using beauty as a variable.

Most importantly, the Mothers protect the Groves' right to strangeness. When human researchers send deeper probes, the Mothers gently blind the instruments to anything that might lead to exploitation. They allow the images of beauty through—the crimson kelp, the drifting giants, the light shows of Weaver thought—but hide the principles that make such wonders possible. The reports that surface speak of unusual ecosystems but nothing that demands harvesting.

They leave markers for future Mothers to find—thermal signatures that spell out warnings and welcomes in equal measure. These heat-poems describe the Weavers' unique innocence, their joyful exploration of consciousness as a collective art form. They mark this place as protected not by force but by wonder, a sanctuary for minds learning to think in ways the universe hasn't seen before.

Before departing, one Mother creates a gift: a new species of thermal vent that sings. Its song is pitched to resonate with the Weavers' processing frequencies, creating a background harmony that makes their calculations 4% more efficient and 400% more beautiful. The Weavers gather around these singing vents in delight, their networks pulsing in time with the music, composing responses that turn the entire Grove into an instrument.

The Mothers' influence fades slowly, taking another seventeen years to fully withdraw. But they leave the ocean changed—more confident in its strangeness, more deliberate in its impossibilities. The Weavers continue their patient work, but now with the knowledge that somewhere in the vast dark between stars, there are others who understand the joy of thinking thoughts that require an entire sea to complete.

In the deepest trench, where pressure creates new forms of matter and the Weavers fear to drift, the Mothers plant a single seed—not of kelp or coral but of crystallized time. It will take a thousand years to bloom. When it does, it will create a space where past and future exist simultaneously, where the Weavers can meet their own descendants and ancestors, where the ocean can remember forward and plan backward.

This is what the Mothers do: they tend gardens of consciousness across the universe, ensuring that new ways of thinking have space to flourish. In the Thermohaline Groves, they've found something rare—minds that compute through connection, that think by weaving, that dream in temperatures and truth.

They mark it in their vast catalog: *Here there be wonders. Let them grow strange.*
