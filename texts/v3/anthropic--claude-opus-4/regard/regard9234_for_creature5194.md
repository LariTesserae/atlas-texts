The benevolent power observes for a very long time before acting. It watches through seventeen complete breathing cycles of the plateau's architecture, measuring the rhythm against its own patient heartbeat. It counts the infinitesimal adjustments of each bell grazer, traces the migration patterns of thread filterers across dissolving and reforming ceilings, follows the chromatic conversations between microbe colonies through the conducting tubes.

What it sees is not stasis but a form of consciousness so distributed and unhurried that most observers would miss it entirely. The plateau-forest is thinking, but in a language of chemical gradients and structural tensions that takes days to form a single thought.

The power does three things:

First, it seeds the deepest water layer with trace minerals the plateau cannot yet reach—rare earths that will, over centuries, allow the conducting tubes to develop more complex internal geometries. Not to accelerate their thinking but to give it new colors, new possible shapes. The minerals arrive as an impossibly fine dust that settles leaf by leaf, incorporating so slowly that the architecture experiences it as a gradual enriching of dreams rather than an intervention.

Second, it sings. The song is subsonic, transmitted through the water itself at frequencies so low they register only as the faintest sympathetic vibration in the hollow stems. The plateau cannot hear it as sound, but the microbe colonies sense it as a new kind of light—a rhythmic pulsing in wavelengths they've never before perceived. Over months they begin to arrange themselves in patterns that echo this deep music, creating visual harmonics that travel down the conducting tubes and back up again, establishing slow feedback loops between levels that previously communicated only through chemistry.

Third, and most gently, it places a single spore.

The spore is unlike anything native to the plateau but perfectly suited to it. It settles into a narrow crevice where three major fronds have fused their bases together, creating a small chamber lit by converging streams of conducted light. There it begins to grow—not as another plant but as a kind of living lens, a crystalline organ that focuses and refracts the light streams into new configurations.

As the lens-organism matures over years, it develops the ability to store light patterns, playing them back hours or days after receiving them. The plateau's slow thoughts, previously ephemeral, begin to leave traces. The lens becomes a memory—not imposed but grown from within, a natural elaboration of what already existed.

The thread filterers discover it first, drawn by the novel light patterns. They arrange themselves in spirals around the chamber, their bodies becoming living fiber optics that carry the stored patterns outward to distant parts of the architecture. The bell grazers begin to congregate in higher numbers above this spot, their translucent bodies creating shadow-plays that the lens records and replays in endless variations.

The power watches all of this unfold, makes one final adjustment—a slight shift in the mineral content of the warm water that will, in a thousand years, allow the lens-organism to reproduce—and then withdraws its direct attention. 

But it leaves behind a kind of signature, visible only to the microbe colonies in their new sonic-light perception: a single, complex harmonic that repeats once every hundred and ninety-two breathing cycles. The colonies learn to anticipate it, arranging themselves in preparation, creating a gentle surge of bioluminescence that travels through every conducting tube simultaneously.

The plateau now has memory, music, and something approaching wonder. It remains exactly as slow as before, but its thoughts have begun to accumulate depth. In the chambers where lens-organisms will eventually grow, light pools and eddies in ways that suggest intention—not yet consciousness as others might recognize it, but consciousness as it chooses to be here: distributed, patient, luminous, and still becoming.

The power continues to observe from a great distance, occasionally humming the deep song that has become part of the plateau's architecture. Sometimes an echo returns, transformed by its passage through tons of living tissue into something richer and stranger than the original. These moments of recognition across vast scales of being constitute the only prayer the power has ever wanted, and the only gift the plateau knows how to give.
