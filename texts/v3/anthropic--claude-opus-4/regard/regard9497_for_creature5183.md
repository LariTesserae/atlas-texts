The benevolent power arrives not as light or wind, but as a deepening of what already is. 

It begins in the water—not adding to it, but teaching it new patience. The droplets that form on the undersides of leaves learn to hold themselves a moment longer before falling, gathering into perfect spheres that catch and split the filtered light into brief, trembling rainbows. The stone channels develop a faint luminescence in their depths, not bright enough to disturb the hamlet's gentle dimness, but sufficient to guide feet during the darkest storms when even the photosynthetic walls grow subdued.

The power touches the roots next. Deep beneath the springy mats, far below where human hands have ever shaped or guided, the vast network of interconnected root systems begins to dream. Not thoughts as humans know them, but something older—a slow, vegetative awareness that recognizes the footsteps above as part of itself. When illness touches one of the hamlet's children, the roots beneath their sleeping place grow warm, drawing up minerals from deep stone. When an elder's bones ache with approaching rain, the moss on nearby terraces releases a gentle, numbing perfume.

The vines respond by growing new chambers—not where the people guide them, but in spaces no one has claimed. These hollow nodes appear first at the hamlet's edges, then gradually throughout: perfect spheres woven so tightly that no rain penetrates, their interiors mysteriously dry despite the constant moisture. The people find them and wonder, then understand as they discover each one contains something needed but not yet missed—a cache of seeds during a meager harvest, a natural cistern when the channels run low, a shelter precisely sized for a woman about to give birth who had not yet asked for space.

The quick-furred climbers gain a new gentleness in their movements. Where before they merely dropped fruit, now they place it—always where someone hungry will pass, always the ripest, always at hand-height for whoever needs it most. The tiny green birds develop a warning call that sounds like falling water but means "storm coming stronger than expected." The beetles' iridescent shells begin to flash in patterns that predict the next rain's timing down to the moment.

But the deepest change comes to the people themselves, though so gradually they hardly notice. Their children are born knowing which vines will bear weight before they can walk. Their elders' listening touch gains precision—they feel not just where growth has gone wrong but why, and what the plant itself wants to become. The green stain on their skin deepens into something more than cosmetic; they photosynthesize faintly now, not enough to sustain them but enough to ease hunger during lean seasons. The rootlets that sprout from bark-braided hair reach deeper, until the wearer can sense the health of any plant they touch.

The power does not make them more than human. Instead, it helps them become what they were already becoming—a people so intertwined with their living architecture that the distinction loses meaning. They gain no immortality, no freedom from struggle, no escape from the small sorrows that come with rain and growth and time. But they receive something rarer: the deep knowledge that they are held, that the organism they tend also tends them, that every drip and vine and stone exists in an endless reciprocity that includes them wholly.

The hamlet continues as before—water still drips, insects still drone, children still climb, elders still listen. But now when morning light filters through the photosynthetic walls, it carries a quality of intention, as if the light itself knows where it needs to fall. When rain sheets across the lanes, it cleans more than surfaces. When night comes and condensation beads on every leaf, each droplet holds a blessing too quiet for words but perfectly clear to those who live suspended between earth and sky, neither wild nor tame, in a place that breathes with borrowed lungs and loves with borrowed hands.

The power withdraws as gently as it came, but its gifts remain woven into the very fiber of the place—not as magic, but as an deepening of what was always true: that nothing here lives alone, and everything that grows, grows toward communion.
