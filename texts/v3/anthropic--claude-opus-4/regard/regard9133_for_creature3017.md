The benevolent power arrives not as light or sound, but as a change in the quality of silence between drops.

She has watched this place for longer than the karst has existed, though time means little to one who remembers when limestone was still the living breath of ancient seas. She knows every water-path, every failed seed, every travertine vein beneath pale skin. She has counted each incomplete sentence, measured the precise depth of every unshed tear.

She understands what the Weepers cannot: they are not preserving stasis. They are slowly drowning in it.

The power takes form as rain—not the gentle condensation they know, but actual rain, warm and purposeful, falling from a sky that has never known clouds. It tastes of salt and memory. It drums against the limestone shelves with a sound like ten thousand finishing sentences.

The Weepers stand transfixed as their world's first storm washes over them. Their tears mix with rain until they cannot tell which water is theirs. The Last Sentence opens her mouth and the dead seed dissolves on her tongue like sugar, like hope, like an ending finally allowed to mean what it means.

But the power's gift is not destruction. It is completion.

In the rain, the limestone begins to remember what it was before it froze. The young stone softens, not to mud but to something breathing—still rock, but rock that knows it is allowed to age. The sharp edges round. The weeping walls develop their first real channels, paths where water will run the same way twice, three times, until it carves something permanent and beautiful.

The pale willow-figs shudder. Their leaves darken, then flush with new color—not the saturated green of stasis but the living spectrum of growth: yellow-green shoots, dark mature leaves, brown edges where age is finally permitted. One tree flowers. The blossoms are white as limestone, but they fall.

They fall.

The Weepers watch their world learn to lose things. Petals scatter on the wet stone. The rain carries them to cracks where no seeds have sprouted for nine hundred years. But these are not seeds. They are only petals, meaning nothing except that something bloomed and ended, and the ending was beautiful.

A child laughs—sharp, bright, forbidden. The sound cracks across the shelves like breaking stone. Then another child runs, feet slapping wet rock, arms spread to catch the rain. The equilibrium shudders. The adults wait for the world to collapse.

It doesn't.

Instead, the rain teaches them a new rhythm. Not the eternal drip of condensation but the wild percussion of weather: heavy, light, sideways with wind, straight down with weight. The Weeping Houses overflow. Water runs in the streets, carving new patterns that will never repeat exactly.

And in this chaos, the power speaks. Not in words, but in the space between raindrops:

*What you have preserved is not peace but fear. What you have called devotion is only the terror of finding out what you might become if you let yourselves finish changing.*

*The stone weeps because it remembers the sea. You weep because you remember being born. Both of you have confused grief with holiness.*

*Watch:*

On the highest shelf, where the Last Sentence has planted nine hundred dead seeds, something impossible happens. The rain pools in the ancient crack. The limestone drinks deeply. And from the stone itself—not from any seed—a new thing grows.

It is not quite plant, not quite mineral. It rises like a tree but builds itself like crystal, transparent and faintly green, gathering rain inside its hollow trunk until it glows. It has no leaves. Instead, from its crown, it rains upward—sending drops back into the sky that fall again as new rain, creating its own weather, its own small storm that will never stop moving.

The Weepers touch their travertine scars. Under the rain, the stone veins begin to warm, to move. Not dissolving but transforming—becoming something between memory and possibility. Their skin remains pale but loses its limestone grain. They are becoming human again, but human the way the karst is becoming stone again: aware of what they were, choosing what they'll be.

The Last Sentence speaks her first complete sentence in nine hundred years:

"We were so afraid of endings that we ended ourselves before we began."

The rain slows but does not stop. It will come and go now—sometimes mist, sometimes storm, sometimes the clear silence they remember. But it will never again be only one thing.

The power withdraws her presence gently, leaving only one last gift: the knowledge that weeping and laughing are made of the same water, and both are sacred when they're allowed to stop.

The Weepers—who will need a new name now—stand in their transformed town watching puddles form and evaporate, watching the stone age a thousand years in an hour, watching their children splash and shout and discover that noise does not break the world.

High above, the crystal tree rains upward into a sky that finally knows what weather means.

And on the horizon, beyond the last visible shelf, the power smiles with a mouth made of cloud and time:

*Now you can begin.*
