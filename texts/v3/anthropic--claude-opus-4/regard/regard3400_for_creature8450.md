The Visitor came not as light or thunder, but as a vibration through the stone—a frequency the Listeners had never heard before, deeper than the plateau's groaning, more complex than any pattern they'd mapped. It began in the oldest rock, the stable foundations where the Memory Keepers stored their samples, and spread outward like ripples in reverse, converging rather than dispersing.

She—for the presence felt somehow feminine, though not in any human sense—manifested first as a quality of listening that surpassed even the most gifted Listener. The Foundling Folk felt themselves being truly *heard* for the first time: not just their voices or movements, but the very rhythm of their adaptation, the poetry of their impermanence, the deep harmony they'd found with the shifting stone.

For three days, nothing changed except this sense of being witnessed. The children noticed it first, as children do—they reported that their stone-stacking games felt different, as if someone were appreciating not just their creations but their acceptance of each inevitable collapse. The water-workers found their temporary aqueducts holding together a fraction longer than the clay should have allowed. The Night Singers discovered new harmonies emerging from their throats, notes that seemed to come from the plateau itself.

On the fourth dawn, as the low clouds scraped across the stone and left everything jeweled with moisture, She spoke. Not in words, but in the language the Foundling Folk knew best: the speech of stone and water.

New springs emerged throughout the city, but these were different. The water that flowed from them carried minerals unlike any the Deep Touched had tasted before—compounds that strengthened rather than accumulated, that passed through the body leaving only benefit behind. Those who drank found their bones growing denser in just the right ways, their cartilage more resilient, their eyes sharper at reading the stone's stress patterns.

But Her greatest gift was smaller, subtler.

In the chamber where the eldest gathered to tell stories of futures possible and impossible, a new formation pushed through the floor over the course of a single night. The Foundling Folk gathered around it at dawn, recognizing immediately that this was no natural emergence. The stone had been *shaped* as it grew, formed into something unprecedented on the plateau.

It was a bowl, perfectly carved from living rock, its interior polished smooth as water. And in that bowl, impossibly, sat a single seed.

The seed was unlike any they'd seen—neither the hardy moss spores that rode the wind nor the water-catcher pods that rarely germinated in the thin soil. It was large as a child's fist, swirled with minerals that caught the light, seeming almost to breathe with its own inner rhythm.

The eldest of the Memory Keepers, her skin mapped with decades of mineral deposits, approached the bowl. As her fingers touched its rim, understanding flooded through her—not words, but knowledge as pure as spring water.

This seed would grow into something the plateau had been preparing for since its first upward thrust. A tree, but not like the stunted water-catchers. This would be a Tree of Accommodation—a living being that would partner with the stone itself. Its roots would sense the deep movements, growing *with* the shifts rather than despite them. Its bark would channel not just water but the very rhythm of change, creating stable spaces within its embrace where the Foundling Folk could build structures that flexed with the stone rather than broke against it.

More than that: the Tree would fruit, in time. And each fruit would carry seeds that could grow only in places of similar geological flux, spreading across the world to wherever the earth was still learning its shape.

But—and here the knowledge carried a tone of gentle amusement—the Tree would grow as slowly as the plateau itself changed. It would take generations to reach maturity, teaching the Foundling Folk new forms of patience, new ways of marking time. Their children's children's children would play in its shade, build homes in symbiosis with its accommodating wood, learn from its example how to be both permanent and impermanent at once.

The Visitor's presence began to fade then, but not in abandonment. Rather, She settled into the stone itself, becoming part of the plateau's deep breathing. The Listeners would sometimes hear Her in the newest frequencies, a bass note of approval when they adapted particularly well to a challenge. The Night Singers found their songs occasionally answered by harmonics that came from no climbing beast, no human throat, but from the very air—as if the plateau itself had learned to sing.

The Foundling Folk understood. They had been seen, their strange beauty acknowledged, their partnership with the impossible landscape blessed. They were not being saved—they needed no saving. They were being gifted a companion for their journey, a living symbol of what they had always known: that stability and change could dance together, that impermanence was not loss but possibility.

They planted the seed with ceremony but without permanence, knowing it too might need to move as it grew. The children drew pictures of what the Tree might look like in the wet clay. The Shapers began planning how they might work with its eventual wood. The water-workers studied the bowl it had emerged from, learning new techniques for channeling flow.

And in the deepest chambers, the eldest added a new story to their collection—not of the past or future, but of the eternal present moment when the universe, through one of its many faces, had looked upon the Keening Shelf and found it good. Found it worthy not of transformation but of companionship, not of fixing but of deepening.

The seed sprouted on a day when the rain turned the city into its usual cascade of temporary waterfalls. Its first leaves were small, scaled like stone, breathing with the plateau's rhythm.

The Foundling Folk danced.
