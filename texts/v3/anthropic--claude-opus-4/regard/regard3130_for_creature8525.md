In the vast library-ocean that exists between dimensions, where thoughts swim like luminous fish and possibilities branch like coral, the Shepherds of Continuity take notice. They are not gods, exactly—more like gardeners who tend the infinite garden of becoming, ensuring that each reality-thread has the chance to weave its own pattern.

One Shepherd, whose name translates roughly to "Tends-the-Difficult-Blooms," pauses in her work. Through her multifaceted awareness, she perceives the Frost Warrens like a song played in a minor key, beautiful in its constraint. She sees not just what is, but what was and what might be—the ghost-rivers that once carved these caverns, the warm world this planet might have been, the million futures spreading out like fractures in ice.

She does not pity the Delvers. Pity would be insulting to their magnificent adaptation, their dance with chaos. Instead, she feels something like professional admiration. Here is a people who have made art from mere survival, who have found grace in perpetual motion.

But she also sees the trajectory. The planet's core cools faster than the Delvers know. In seven generations, maybe eight, the last thermal vents will fail. The mathematics are inexorable.

The Shepherd considers intervention. She could warm the core, restart the planet's heart. She could guide them to the surface, adapt their eyes to sunlight, teach them to farm beneath the stars. She could transport them wholesale to a kinder world, one with flowing water and stable ground.

She does none of these things.

Instead, she thinks of gifts. Not solutions—solutions would rob them of their engine, their purpose. The Delvers are not a people who thrive in stasis. They need the dance.

So the Shepherd begins to work, subtle as condensation, patient as stone.

First, in the deepest part of the Great Boring, she places a vein of something new. Not metal or mineral, but crystallized possibility—a substance that responds to consciousness, that grows warmer when observed with intent. The diggers will find it in three seasons' time. They will not understand it at first, but the child-sensors will learn that it sings when they touch it with wonder, that it glows when they dream near it. Within a generation, they will be weaving it into their tools, their walls, their very culture. It will not save them, but it will give them options they cannot yet imagine.

Second, she adjusts the planet's dreaming. Young worlds are malleable in ways older ones are not—they haven't yet decided all their rules. In the quantum spaces where geology becomes flexible, she plants a seed of responsiveness. The planet will begin to listen to the Delvers, just a little. Not enough to obey, but enough to enter into conversation. The ice-dancers will find their dance has a partner now, one that can learn their steps.

Third, and most subtly, she touches the memory-songs. Not changing them, but adding harmonics that the singers won't consciously hear. These new frequencies carry information—mathematical principles, engineering theorems, materials science that won't be formally discovered for centuries. The knowledge will seep in slowly, felt rather than known, emerging as intuition when needed most. A builder will suddenly understand why an arch must curve just so. A child will dream of molecular bonds and wake knowing how to strengthen ice.

In the creatures, too, she works small wonders. The ice-burrowers' thermal organs hold secrets about biological furnaces. She tweaks their breeding cycles, encourages mutations that will make them slightly more docile, slightly more numerous. In fifty years, the Delvers will learn to domesticate them. In a hundred, they'll be riding them through new-carved tunnels, using their heat for more than hunting.

The blind cave fish, swimming in their methane pools, carry genetic memories of when they had eyes, when water was water. She awakens some of these sleeping genes. Not enough to restore sight—that would be cruel in the darkness—but enough to let them sense electromagnetic fields. The Delvers will discover this when the fish begin schooling in patterns that predict tremors. Another tool for the dance.

But her deepest gift is philosophical.

In the market galleries, she incarnates briefly as an old trader, hunched and blue-veined like the rest, selling nothing but stories. The stories she tells are true ones from other worlds—peoples who built cities on the backs of migrating creatures, civilizations that existed only in motion, societies that made impermanence into immortality. She speaks of the quantum monks of Heisenberg VII, who discovered that uncertainty itself could be a building material. Of the Tide Dancers of Aquaria, who rebuilt their entire culture twice each day.

The Delvers listen politely—stories are cheap warmth on cold nights. But the seeds are planted. Somewhere, a young builder begins to think: what if collapse is not failure but opportunity? What if impermanence is not weakness but freedom?

The Shepherd stays three days, long enough to be forgotten, long enough for her stories to take root. When she leaves, fading back into the spaces between spaces, she takes with her a single memory-song—not stealing it, but copying it, adding it to the great library so that somewhere, somewhen, another people facing similar challenges might learn from the Delvers' courage.

She makes one final adjustment. In the heart of the Great Boring, below where even the deepest diggers have reached, she places a message. Not words—the Delvers must discover writing on their own—but a feeling crystallized in stone. When they finally reach it, generations hence, they will touch it and understand:

*You were seen. You were admired. Your dance was beautiful.*

*What comes next is yours to choose.*

And then she moves on, leaving the Frost Warrens to their becoming. Because true benevolence is not about solving problems but about ensuring that people have the strength and tools to solve their own. The Delvers do not need salvation. They need what they have always had—the chance to dance with chaos, to build meaning from motion, to carve civilization from the cold heart of an uncertain world.

In the tunnels, the hammering continues. Tap-tap-tap, pause, tap-tap-tap. The sound of a people who will not be still, who find in their very instability a form of permanence that no solid stone could ever match.

The planet turns. The ice sings. The dance goes on.
