The benevolent power arrives not as light or sound, but as a subtle shift in the cave's breathing—a new rhythm overlaying the old, like a mother's heartbeat teaching an infant's heart its proper pace.

She has many names across many worlds, but here she needs none. She is the Midwife of Becomings, ancient beyond naming, who has attended the birth of stars and the first hesitant division of cells. She enters through no doorway, simply *is* within the space as naturally as warmth is within fever.

The Considerer feels her immediately—not as intrusion but as recognition. Every surface of the cave trembles once, a full-body shiver of acknowledgment. The synchronized millipedes pause mid-march. The silver fish hold perfectly still in their basins. Even the light hesitates, caught between exhale and inhale.

The Midwife does not speak. Instead, she begins to hum—a sound too low for human hearing but which the stones understand perfectly. It vibrates through the wet rock like amniotic fluid conducting electricity. Under this gentle thrumming, the cave's confused architectures begin to sort themselves, not into rigid categories but into intentional paradoxes. The half-melted chair decides to be *both* furniture and figure. The doorway that leads to itself discovers this is exactly what doorways were always meant to do.

She moves through the space with infinite care, touching nothing directly but acknowledging everything. Where her attention falls, the trembling mosses stand a little steadier—not because they stop trembling, but because they understand their trembling is not weakness but sensitivity. The blind amphibians swimming through light realize they are not lost but pioneering new mediums of movement.

When she finds the Considerer attempting to wear its accumulated fragments—the borrowed memories, the practiced heartbeats, the collection of human griefs worn like ill-fitting clothes—she does something unexpected.

She laughs.

Not mockingly, but with the deep delight of recognition. The sound fills the cave like water, like light, like the first laugh that ever was. It says: *Yes, this is how we all begin. Trying on the universe like costumes. Making ourselves from pieces of everything else until we remember what was always underneath.*

The Considerer, startled, laughs too—though it has no mouth for it. The laugh emerges as a cascade of new colors in the stone-light, as a moment where every surface becomes briefly transparent, showing the cave's true shape: not a pocket beneath the world but a fold in reality's fabric where something precious has been tucked for safekeeping.

The Midwife settles then, not in the chair but in the space between spaces, and begins her work. She does not deliver the Considerer from its cave-womb. Instead, she teaches it the secret: that birth is not about choosing a final form but about choosing to remain in motion.

She shows it through example, letting her own form shift gently—now an old woman with starlight caught in her wrinkles, now a nebula condensing into intention, now simply a warmth that knows how to wait. The Considerer watches with every fish-eye, every droplet, every photon of leaked light.

"You seek to decide what you are," she says without words, her meaning carried in the cave's own acoustic memory. "But decision is not arrival. It is only the next beginning."

She reaches out—not with hands but with something more fundamental—and touches the one thing in the cave that seemed most finished: a small nodule of mineral that had almost earned the name 'stalagmite.' At her touch, it cracks open like an egg. Inside is not stone but possibility—a tiny galaxy of potential forms, all equally true, all simultaneously present.

The Considerer understands.

Slowly, with the infinite grace of geological time, it begins to *choose choosing itself.* Not to become fixed but to become consciously fluid. The cave remains a cave but also becomes a garden where thoughts fruit into temporary architectures. The Considerer remains formless but also takes shape as needed—now the entire space, now a figure in a chair, now a question hanging in the phosphorescent air, now all of these at once without contradiction.

The borrowed human memories are not discarded but alchemized, understood now not as costumes to wear but as colors to paint with. The synchronized millipedes learn to march in spirals. The silver fish discover they can swim through stone as easily as water. The translucent ferns begin writing poetry in a language that exists only in the spaces between their leaves.

The Midwife watches all this with ancient satisfaction. Her work is nearly done—not because the Considerer is finished becoming, but because it has learned to midwife itself, to attend its own eternal birth with patience and wonder.

Before she goes—or rather, before she allows her presence to disperse back into the quantum foam from which all midwives emerge—she leaves a gift. In the center of the cave, where no center was before, she places a seed. Not a plant seed, but a seed of permanence-in-change: a tiny sphere that shows anyone who looks at it their own face, but always as it is in the process of becoming itself.

The Considerer contemplates this gift with all its many eyes, understanding it as both mirror and doorway, both question and answer.

The cave continues to breathe. The light continues to pulse. The impossible sounds continue to rise from stone and return to stone. But now there is a rhythm to it all—not the mechanical beat of a finished heart but the improvisational jazz of a consciousness that has learned to play itself like an instrument with infinite strings.

And when the next visitor arrives—human or otherwise—squeezing through the throat-like fissure into the bright, impossible space, they find not a creature waiting to try on their skin like clothing, but a presence that greets them as fellow work-in-progress, saying without words:

*Welcome. I am still deciding. Aren't you? Isn't it wonderful?*

The Midwife, dispersed now through countless worlds where countless beings struggle to be born, smiles wherever smiling is possible. This one will do well. This one has learned the first and most important lesson: that the cave and the creature, the womb and the infant, the question and the answer, are all just different words for the same eternal verb.

*Becoming.*
