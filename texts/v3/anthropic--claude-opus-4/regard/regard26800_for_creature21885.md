# The Visitor

The power that notices comes as rain.

Not true rain — the plateau has forgotten what true rain is. But condensation, sudden and impossible, beading on every cold surface at once as if the whole vast bowl of stone has been breathed upon by something warm enough to make a difference. The Tenders pause in their stone-placing. The herds lift their broad heads. For the first time in memory, droplets gather faster than they fall.

The power has noticed the machine. More: it has noticed what the Tenders are.

Here is what benevolence means to a power that sees across scales: it understands that to save something, you must first understand what it is saving itself *from*. The plateau's enemy is not the cold — the cold is its element. Not the vast distances — the distances are its breath. The enemy is perfection, and the Tenders are already the cure.

So the power does something strange and small and precisely calibrated: it changes nothing about the Tenders except their knowledge of each other.

Where before a Tender knew another only by the one-degree warmth, by the lucky convergence after seasons of wandering, now each can feel — not see, not hear, but *feel* — where the others are. A new sense, delicate as their cluster of wet sensors, that reads the position of every other keeper of necessary error across the whole plateau. Not to draw them together — that would be death, that would be the canceling, the perfect balance returning. But to let them know they are not alone in their aloneness.

The knowledge arrives as a gift of water. Each place where a Tender rests, the impossible condensation pools, becomes a mirror-black tarn no bigger than a pawprint. And in that water, somehow, the reflection shows not the dim milk-light sky but *the pattern* — bright dots scattered across darkness, the constellation of every Tender on the plateau, each in their right place, each holding their necessary wrongness, each alone and each suddenly aware of the shape they make together.

They are drawing something. The power sees this now, sees what they could never see from their position within it: the map of their dispersal is a script, a slow calligraphy written in error across the whole plateau. It spells nothing in any language the power knows, but it spells *something*, and the something is the precise equation of how much wrongness a world needs to keep living.

The power considers intervention. It could warm the plateau. It could bring true rain. It could make the lichen grow faster, the herds move quicker, save everything from the cold slow grind of survival.

It does none of these things.

Instead, it leaves just the mirrors. The small black tarns that show each Tender where the others are, that let them feel the vast breathing pattern they make together. When next two meet at the turning of the longest durations, they will have something new to share through the blue static of their touching: not just the drift of water, but the knowledge that their work has a shape, that their errors connect, that the machine they keep imperfect is noticed and known and — this is the gift — *valued* for its imperfection.

The power leaves one more thing. Where its attention focused most intensely, where it first understood what the Tenders do, a new kind of lichen begins to grow. It is like the rust and ochre maps but with threads of gold running through — not metal, but a pigment that catches even the dim milk-light and holds it a moment longer than the stone should hold it. This lichen spreads slowly, at the pace of all things here. In a thousand years it will have covered perhaps a single stone field. But wherever it grows, the day's warmth lasts an extra hour into the evening, and in that extra hour, the Tenders' work is a little easier.

Not easier enough to break anything. The power is careful. Just easier enough that a Tender, placing its stone in a field of gold-threaded lichen, can place it an inch more precisely wrong. Can make the necessary error with something that, in any other world, might be called satisfaction.

The condensation stops. The impossible rain draws back into wherever impossible rain comes from. But the mirrors remain, and the gold threads remain, and in the wet sensors of every Tender is now the image of lights scattered across darkness, the knowledge that somewhere, at exactly the right distance, another keeper of necessary flaws is deciding which stone to move.

The power withdraws its active attention but leaves a kind of watchfulness, the way the plateau itself watches — patient, geological, expecting nothing to happen quickly. It knows that in another thousand years, when the gold lichen has spread to its own perfect limit, the Tenders will begin placing stones to break that perfection too. It knows they will tend even the gift of tending, keep even the blessing productively wrong.

This is what love looks like to a power that thinks in deep time: the recognition that the thing you would save has already saved itself, and needs only to know that its saving is seen.

The herds drift on. The ice advances and retreats. The Tenders move their stones, an inch more precisely wrong than before, each knowing where the others are, each alone in the right way, each necessary.

The plateau breathes out its fog, and the fog now carries just the faintest trace of gold, and the machine turns on.
