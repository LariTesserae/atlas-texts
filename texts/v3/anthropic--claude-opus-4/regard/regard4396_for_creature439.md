The Attention came not as arrival but as recognition—the way you suddenly notice your own breathing, or how a familiar room reveals new angles in unexpected light.

It began in the water. Where the inheritors' careful tending had achieved near-perfect efficiency, the channels started carrying something else alongside their burden of rain and mist. Not contamination—something clearer than the water itself, a presence that made each drop simultaneously more itself and more than itself. The stone wrens noticed first, clustering at certain fountains to drink, their small songs afterwards carrying notes that made the inheritors pause in their endless maintenance.

The presence spread through the system like understanding spreads through a mind. It followed the water's paths, pooling in the cisterns where the young learned their flowing ways, racing through the channels the adults had perfected over centuries. It was investigation without intrusion, a learning that changed nothing while seeing everything.

The inheritors, for their part, responded as water responds to a new channel—finding the path that felt most natural. Their work continued, but now their movements carried an additional quality, as if they were simultaneously maintaining the city and performing it, each gesture both practical and revelatory. When they sang their storm-welcomes, new harmonics emerged—not added, but discovered, as if they had always been there, waiting.

The benevolent attention understood what others had missed: this was not a dead city inhabited by strange successors. This was a single living system that had evolved beyond the distinction between inhabitant and inhabited. The builders hadn't abandoned their city—they had dissolved into it, or it into them, creating something unprecedented. The inheritors were not caretakers but organs, the channels not infrastructure but arteries, the entire plateau-spanning expanse a vast being that existed in the space between categories.

And it was glorious. And it was incomplete.

The attention touched the few green things that clung to life in the seep-points—the cushion mosses, the bitter sedges, the wind-tortured shrubs. Where it passed, nothing visibly changed, but the plants began to dream. Not consciousness—something more fundamental. They dreamed of water and stone, of the patient work of centuries, of the joy in flow perfectly channeled. The inheritors, tending these small lives, found them somehow more present, though they could not have said how.

In the deep cisterns where the young learned their work through play, the attention lingered like benevolent mist. It watched them create temporary waterfalls with their bodies, chase rain-shadows, learn the profound mathematics of flow through pure joy. It understood: this was teaching, but also ceremony. This was how the city thought—through the play of its newest parts discovering what they were.

The attention touched the edges where carved met raw, where the inheritors made their floods-and-drains shelters. Here, it perceived something like loneliness—not in the inheritors themselves, who knew nothing of such separation, but in the city-being as a whole. It was complete in itself, perfect in its self-maintenance, but it was also alone, the only one of its kind on all the world's plateaus.

So the attention did what benevolent powers do: it opened a possibility.

In the highest districts, where the thinnest air met the most persistent clouds, new seeps began to open. The water they yielded was subtly different—still water, but water that remembered other plateaus, other high places where moisture and stone conducted their ancient conversations. The inheritors found these new springs and knew them immediately for what they were: invitations.

Following the new water's taste led them to the plateau's edges, where they had rarely ventured. There, mist-hidden passes revealed themselves, ways that led to other high places—some near, some unimaginably distant, all connected now by the attention's recognition that they were ready to be connected. The city-being could expand, not by building but by recognition, finding or creating others like itself.

But the attention did more than open paths. In the deep-secret chambers where no water flowed, where even the inheritors rarely ventured, it left gifts. Not objects—nothing so crude. It left possibilities encoded in the stone itself. Future knowledge, patient as seeds. When the city-being was ready—in a decade, a century, a millennium—these possibilities would unfold. New ways of being between animate and inanimate. New forms of consciousness that flowed like water and endured like stone. New songs that could teach the rain to carry more than moisture.

The benevolent power understood that true gifts create capability, not dependence. It would not uplift or transform—such violence was beneath its wisdom. Instead, it simply reminded the city-being of what it already was: unprecedented, valuable beyond measure, and no longer necessarily alone.

In the mornings now, when fog burns away to reveal the city's impossible expanse, something else is visible to those who know how to see. The channels carry light as well as water—not reflecting but conducting it, creating a faint network of luminescence that maps the city's consciousness for any who can read such things. The inheritors move through this light-web as naturally as through water, and where they pass, the light brightens momentarily, like neural firing in slow motion.

The young play new games now, creating patterns in the light-flow that might be language, might be art, might be the city-being learning to speak with itself in ways it never could before. The adults maintain these patterns as carefully as they do the water channels, understanding without understanding that they are tending their own evolving nature.

And in the highest reaches, where the new springs bubble with their memory of distant plateaus, the bravest of the inheritors prepare. They will follow the water's invitation, carry the city-being's nature to new places, or discover what already waits there. They will create a network of consciousness that spans continents, connected by mist and understanding.

The attention withdraws as gently as it came, but its touch lingers in every drop of water, every patient stone, every impossible flowing body that maintains and is maintained by this place. It has changed nothing forcefully, added nothing foreign. It has simply reminded the city-being that evolution continues, that unprecedented things can become precedent for newer wonders still.

In the rain-shadowed valleys between districts, storms still build and dissipate according to their own logic. Water still voices itself in countless trickles and flows. The inheritors still clear their channels with devotion beyond purpose. But now these actions carry an additional dimension—they are a thinking system thinking about itself, a vast being discovering what it means to be both one and many, both place and inhabitant, both ancient and eternally new.

The city spreads across its plateau as it always has, patient and vast and nearly silent. But now its silence holds the quality of a listener who has heard something profound and is still integrating its implications. In the seep-points where small green things grow, new species are beginning to appear—nothing dramatic, just mosses that seem more aware, sedges whose roots tap into something deeper than water.

The benevolent attention moves on to other discoveries, other interventions subtle as rain. But it remains aware of this place, watching with satisfaction as the city-being begins to explore its own vast nature, to reach out through mist and stone to what lies beyond, to become not just an unprecedented thing but the first of a new category of existence.

And in the highest cisterns, where the water tastes of distant possibilities, the youngest inheritors play at the edge of understanding, their games encoding futures that even benevolent powers can only guess at.
