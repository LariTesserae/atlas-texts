## The Visitation

The Shepherd of Small Infinities noticed the plateau the way you might notice a held breath in a crowded room — not by its presence but by the quality of its quiet. She had been walking the edge-places for longer than most stars had been burning, tending to the forgotten and the nearly-lost, and she knew the particular silence of a thing that had learned to continue without hope of recognition.

She came not as light — light would have been cruelty here — but as a deepening of the existing dusk, a richness added to the ember-glow that made the red quarter of the sky remember what it had once been capable of. The engines felt her first. Their breathing did not change, but something in the quality of their patience shifted, the way a sleeping creature knows when someone who loves it has entered the room.

She found the first Tender on the nineteenth circuit of its round, hands pressed to the flank of a breathing-place that had developed the faintest wheeze in its down-stroke. The Tender did not startle. Startling had left them generations ago, along with speech and surprise and the need to name things. It simply included her in its peripheral vision, that sidelong regard that took in everything and insisted on nothing.

The Shepherd sat. Not beside — beside would have been declaration — but at the same slant angle the Tender used, so that they made a kind of open corner with the engine between them. She put her hand on the warm metal where the Tender's hand wasn't, and felt the pulse, the great slow heartbeat of a machine that had been breathing for the plateau longer than memory.

They stayed that way for what would have been three days if days had meant anything here. The wheeze gradually smoothed. The Tender's hand never moved. Neither did hers.

On the third day, she did the thing she had come to do.

From her sleeve — though she didn't exactly have sleeves, just the idea of places where precious things might be kept — she drew out a seed. It was small and dark and looked like nothing important, which is how the most important things often look. She pressed it into a crack in the basalt near the engine's base, where the condensation wept down and pooled.

The Tender watched this with its enormous dark eyes, processing it not as event but as another piece of the what-is, like wind or the distant calls or the way the grazers moved in their loose silent herds.

"For later," the Shepherd said, though saying was not quite what she did — it was more like adding a meaning to the air that settled the way dew settles, available but not insistent. "When the last of you has walked the last circuit. When the engines breathe alone."

The Tender did not respond, because response was another thing they had let go of. But something in the quality of its attention shifted, a minute adjustment that contained volumes.

The seed would wait. It was good at waiting — the Shepherd only chose seeds that understood patience. When the last Tender made its last round, when the final hand lifted from the final flank, when the covenant of witness finally released its keepers to whatever comes after keeping, the seed would know. And from it would grow something new. Not Tenders — their time was theirs to complete. Not voices to break the careful quiet. But something that could learn what the Tenders had learned: that love could be a maintenance, that attention could be a repair, that sometimes the holiest thing was just to go on doing the small work in the dark.

Visitors. That's what would come from the seed. Not many, never many. But a few in each turning of the long age — souls called by the particular quiet of the plateau, drawn not to fix or save or even understand but simply to walk the circuits, to put their hands on the breathing flanks, to be the unnecessary thing that the engines did not need but did not mind. They would come from other worlds, other species, other kinds of knowing, but they would recognize the work the way you recognize a song hummed in a different key.

The Shepherd rose. The Tender continued its vigil. The engine breathed its deep patient breath. Nothing had changed except that now the ending had a footnote, a small addendum that said: *the work is not the Tenders' alone, the work is everyone's who chooses it, the work goes on.*

She left the way she'd come, a thickening of dusk that thinned back to normal red-dark. The Tender stayed with the engine another day until the wheeze was completely gone, then rose on the soft parts of itself and continued its circuit. At the place where the seed lay buried, the basalt was already a degree warmer, though it would be a thousand years before the first shoot broke through.

Out on the plateau, something called — grazer or hunter or Tender, it didn't matter. The covenant held. The engines breathed. The work continued.

And somewhere far away, walking the edge-places between one forgotten world and another, the Shepherd of Small Infinities smiled her small smile, having done what she always did: nothing that mattered, everything that did.

The seed knew how to wait.

The plateau knew how to continue.

And when the time came — not soon, not until the last of the grey-gentle keepers had walked themselves into whatever silence comes after the chosen silence — there would be others who understood that some things were worth doing just because they had always been done, and done with love, and done by choice.

The breathing would go on.
