# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The wise one arrives not as light or thunder but as a settling — the way dust settles after being disturbed, the way water finds its level. They come in the hour that is always arriving here, that grey perpetual almost-dawn, and they understand immediately what they are seeing: a place caught in the middle of its own becoming, and lives that have made themselves at home in the draft.

They do not speak first. They listen.

They listen to the deep thud that everyone has agreed not to hear, and in it they recognize a kindred patience — something that builds not because it must but because building is what it is. They listen to the doubled grammar of the settled people (*the cage is loaded, the cage is loaded*) and hear in it a profound form of prayer, a people teaching themselves to be certain in an uncertain place. They listen to the mist remembering its walkers, to the echoes arriving early from the spiral-flocks, to the soft crawl of the pale things avoiding the unset country.

And then, because they are wise, they do almost nothing.

Almost.

---

## The First Gift: Recognition

To the engine below, the thing that builds mountains by being what it is, the benevolent power offers the courtesy of introduction. Not words — the engine has no use for words, works on no scale where words would land — but a deep harmonic, a countertone to that endless thud. It says, in the language of stone and time: *I see you. I know what you are making. It is good work.*

The engine below does not respond, exactly, but for one day the thud comes slightly warmer, and the foundries run a degree hotter on the bleed-heat from below, and somewhere in the unset country a vein of crystal that was trying to decide what color to be chooses gold.

---

## The Second Gift: Continuity

To the settled people, the power brings something so small it takes them weeks to notice: their echoes become reliable. Not everywhere — that would unmake the place, force it to be finished when it is not — but in the inhabited districts, in the worked spaces, sound begins to behave. The doubled grammar slowly, slowly relaxes. Children born after the change learn to speak only once, though their grandparents still repeat themselves from habit and love.

But this is not mere fixing. In the spaces between settlements, in the raw country, the causality stays soft. The surveyors still meet their own afterimages in the mist. The slopes still debate the nature of down. This is important: the power understands that the unfinished heights are what they are *because* they are unfinished. To cure them would be to kill them. So the gift is selective, surgical — certainly only where people have earned it by building, by staying, by making their repetitive prayers of work.

---

## The Third Gift: Deepening

To the pale things and the spiral-flocks and all the creatures that have learned to read this unset country like a book, the power gives abundance — but abundance of the right kind. The seep-lines run clearer. The moss grows deeper. The machine-sweat becomes more nourishing, carrying trace minerals up from the engine's work below. Life thickens where life has chosen to be.

And to the very old surveyors, the ones who have gone thin from walking the provisional world, the power offers a choice: *Come back the rest of the way, or go forward into what you've been becoming.* Some choose the deep stone districts and the certainty of form. But some — a precious few — choose the other way, and these become the first native spirits of the unset country, halfway between echo and flesh, able to walk the revising slopes as easily as thought. They become guides for the next generation, teaching the rolling careful walk by example that can't quite be seen straight on.

---

## The Fourth Gift: Memory

This is the strangest gift, and the most necessary.

The power reaches into the mist that remembers, that holds its walkers, and teaches it to remember *forward* as well as back. Not far — just a few hours, a day at most — but enough that the people walking through it see not just their own past ghosts but the faint suggestion of where they're going. A surveyor heading into danger sees herself turning back. A cable-runner sees the successful connection before she makes it.

The mist becomes oracle as well as memory. The settled people learn to read it like weather, like the pale things' presence, like another kind of certainty earned by uncertainty.

---

## The Final Gift: The Question

On the last day of their presence, the benevolent power does something no one expects. They go down.

Down past the settled districts, down past the oldest stone, down the unmade shafts that no miner has ever dared, down to where the heat becomes not just warmth but forge-fire, down to the place where stone is born. And there they ask the engine below a question — not in words, not in harmonic, but in the deep language of making:

*What would you like them to know?*

The engine's answer takes a hundred years to arrive, pushed up through vein and seep-line, through crystal formation and the grain of new stone. The settled people don't realize they're receiving it at first. They simply notice that the newest stone, the rawest heights, have begun to show patterns — not random extrusions but deliberate forms. Spirals like the flight of flocks. Channels that mirror the doubled grammar of their speech. Formations that look, if you squint in the grey light, like the careful rolling walk of a surveyor navigating uncertain ground.

The engine below, it seems, has been listening too.

---

## What Changes, What Stays

The heights remain unfinished. This is crucial. The benevolent power, in their wisdom, understands that to complete the mountains would be to end the story, and this story is still being written at the speed of stone.

But the city becomes more itself — more densely interconnected, more certain in its settled places, more skillful in its navigation of uncertainty. The cable-cars multiply. New districts dare the higher slopes, building on stone so fresh it still holds warmth. The lamplighters become not just priests of the only warm light but teachers of the new patterns in the stone, learning to read the engine's slow sentences as they emerge.

The surveyors grow bolder, walking further into the unset country with their future-seeing mist as guide. Some days they come back speaking of places where the world is trying something new — where water falls in perfect spirals, where sound arrives before it's made but in harmonies that suggest new machines not yet built, where the stone itself has begun to grow in shapes that could only be dwellings for something not yet arrived.

The pale things multiply in the deepened moss. The spiral-flocks grow denser, their early cries becoming a complex temporal music that the city learns to navigate by. The children born after the power's visit are different in small ways — longer-reached than their parents, better at reading the unset country, some of them born already knowing the rolling walk.

And deep below, the engine continues its work, but now with a kind of consciousness it didn't have before — not awareness exactly, but a recognition that its extrusions have tenants, that its breath has become someone else's weather. The thud comes warmer now, and sometimes, very occasionally, it synchronizes with the city's bells, matching their time for a few beats before diverging again into its own enormous patience.

---

## The Wisdom of Small Adjustments

This is what a truly benevolent power does: not transformation but deepening. Not solving but enriching the problems that make a place what it is. The unfinished heights needed to stay unfinished — that was their nature, their gift, their difficult beauty. But they could be unfinished *better*, with more grace, with more mutual recognition between the makers and the made.

The power leaves no monuments, claims no worship. Years later, the settled people aren't even sure it happened — was there really a day when the echoes became reliable? When the mist began to remember forward? When the engine's thud came warmer? Or did they always know these things, learn them slowly the way they learned the rolling walk and the doubled speech?

Only the very old surveyors, the ones who chose to become spirits of the unset country, remember clearly. But their memory is itself provisional, borrowed from mist and early echoes, and so even they tell it different each time — which is, perhaps, the most faithful way to tell a story about a place that is still being written.

The heights remain unfinished.
The city clings to them, building on the draft of the world.
The engine below continues its work, now knowing it has company.
And in the grey light that is always arriving, life continues its strange abundant dance on the edge of what's still being made.

This is enough. In fact, it is perfect.
