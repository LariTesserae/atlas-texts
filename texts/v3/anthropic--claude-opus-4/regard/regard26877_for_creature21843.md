# The Arrival

The wise one comes as fog comes — not suddenly, but as a gradual thickening of what was already there. On a day when the cloud sits especially heavy and the condensers run their fastest rhythm, something more than weather moves through the upper tiers.

The water-leavers feel it first. They pause in their climbing, basins balanced on their hips, and look up into the white where looking does no good. The bird that drinks from the collection trays goes silent. The great pale moths settle and close their wings. Even the engines seem to hum a different note, though no one could say exactly how.

And then, between one breath and the next, there is a figure on the high path where no figure should be — not strange in form, just a person-shape in a dark coat, but standing where visitors don't stand, up past the last lit lane, moving through the white-blind with the easy gait of someone who doesn't need to see.

## What the Wise One Sees

They see it all at once, the way you might see a machine by understanding its purpose rather than its parts. The town that drinks the sky. The people who have made themselves into living condensers, gathering what they need drop by careful drop. The thing on the high slopes — old, patient, neither beast nor spirit but something between, that has watched this place since before the first foundation stone.

They see the beauty of it: a perfect metabolism, a system that wastes nothing, where even the gods (if that's what the thing above might be called) are fed by the same patient gathering that feeds the children.

And they see the cost.

## The Thinning

Because the wise one has eyes for more than the present. They can see the shape of time here, how it has moved and how it will move. And what they see is this: the cloud is thinning.

Not today. Not this year. But the great rivers of air that have carried moisture up these slopes since the mountains were young are shifting, degree by degree, finding new paths. The thing the town doesn't know — cannot know, living inside the weather as they do — is that their cloud is not eternal. It is a gift of geography and wind patterns, and geography stays while wind changes.

In fifty years, maybe a hundred, the white-blind days will come less often. The morning-in will fail some seasons. The condensers will run dry for weeks at a time. And the town that drinks the sky will die of thirst in the middle of all its careful abundance, not understanding why the thing they never wasted has abandoned them.

## The Choice

The wise one sits on the steps of the highest tier, where the water-leavers pass with their basins, and thinks. They could warn the town — but warn them of what? To move? These people are more married to their stones than the moss is. To change? Their whole culture is built on constancy, on the patient repetition of what works.

They could alter the wind — the wise have that power, to nudge the great rivers of air. But to steal moisture for this place is to steal it from somewhere else, and the wise one has seen those places too: the valleys that will bloom if the cloud moves, the dry lands that wait for their turn at abundance.

They could change the people themselves, make them need less water, make them hardier. But to change what drinks is to change what thinks, and these people's souls are made of mist. To dry them would be to end them as surely as the failing cloud.

## What Wisdom Does

In the end, the wise one does what the town itself would do: they gather.

They walk the tiers for seven days, and wherever they walk, things accumulate. Not water — something else. They speak to the children learning to read the sky and leave something bright in their minds, though the children won't know what until later. They sit with the condenser-wrights and watch them work, and in the watching, new ideas settle like dew — not improvements, exactly, but *alternatives*. Ways to gather more than moisture. Ways to make the engines run on temperature differentials that don't need water as a medium.

They climb to where the water-leavers pour their offerings and wait there, patient as stone, until the thing on the high slopes comes down to drink. And when it comes — vast and vague and older than the rounded peaks — the wise one speaks to it in a language that predates words. Not a bargain. Just a sharing of what they've seen. The thinning that will come. The people below who feed it so faithfully. The possibility of change.

The thing listens. It has been here since before the ice, watching the mountains wear down grain by grain. It knows about long time and slow changes. When the wise one is done speaking, it does something that might be a nod, or might just be the wind moving its vast vageness. But the water-leavers, when they come the next day, find the basins not just empty but *changed* — marked with new patterns in the condensation, as though something has written instructions in the language of dew.

## The Slow Teaching

The wise one leaves as they came, gradually, thinning back into the mist. But what they've gathered here — the seed of adaptation — stays and starts its patient work.

The children who heard them speak grow up a little stranger than their parents, a little more willing to look sideways at the old truths. One becomes a condenser-wright and starts building collectors that gather more than water — heat, light, the pressure of the wind itself. Another becomes a water-leaver but starts leaving other things too: seeds that might grow in the thinning air, notes written in the moisture on stone, questions for the thing above.

The changes come as slowly as everything here comes. A new engine that runs on the temperature difference between day and night. A child who discovers that certain mosses can be bred to hold water like sponges, creating living cisterns. A water-leaver who realizes the thing on the high slopes doesn't just drink — it *teaches*, if you learn to read the patterns it leaves.

## The New Metabolism

Fifty years later, when the first of the dry seasons comes and the cloud lifts for a whole month, the town is ready. Not because they knew it was coming — the wise one left no prophecy, only possibility. But because they've been slowly becoming something else: not just a town that drinks the sky, but a town that drinks *everything*. The dew. The temperature. The light that comes when the cloud lifts. Even the silence.

They still gather water when it comes. They still pour offerings on the high slopes. But now they also gather other things: power from the wind, warmth from the earth, teachings from the thing above that has decided, in its ancient slowness, to share more of what it knows.

The town is still a town. The people still have water-bodies and quiet voices. The engines still hum their patient rhythm. But the metabolism has widened. They've learned what the wise one came to teach without teaching: that survival isn't about holding tight to one way of living, but about being like the cloud itself — always the same, always changing, always finding new shapes to fill.

And on the clearest days, when the white-blind lifts and you can see all the way to the far peaks, the children point to patterns in the distance that might be clouds, or might be something else traveling between the mountains, checking on all the places that have learned to drink the sky in whatever form it comes.

The wise one's gift wasn't fixing anything. It was teaching the town to be its own condenser — gathering possibilities from the air and turning them, drop by drop, into survival.
