The Weaver arrives not through the notch—that would be presumptuous, walking through what is essentially a throat—but settles on the ridge above, where the mountain's shoulder meets sky. She comes as rain comes: gradually, then all at once, her presence soaking into the awareness of the place the way moisture soaks into stone.

She does not announce herself. The engine-creature-mountain has no ears for announcement, no protocol for visitors. But it knows, in its processual way, that something has changed in the pressure above. The Weaver sits crosslegged on the raw edge where the notch begins its plunge, and does what she has always done first: she listens.

The listening takes three days. Or what would be days, if this place marked time by sun. She listens to the layers—the wet rush of green, the middle-distance drip and sluice, the deep gut-roar of the engine, the fundamental frequency of stone being made and unmade. She feels how they braid together, how the cold makes the engine labor harder which makes more warmth which feeds more green which holds more water which makes more cold. She traces the recursions with a mapper's patience.

On the fourth day she extends one hand, palm down, and makes a small adjustment.

Not to the engine—that would be like adjusting someone's heartbeat, violence disguised as help. Not to the green—that would be presuming to know better than a skin does what it needs. She adjusts the *gradient*. The place where cold meets warm, where the engine's heat leaks through the seams. She doesn't make it warmer or cooler. She makes it more *various*. 

Where before the temperature shifted in simple bands—warm at the seam, cold at the surface—now there are pockets. Eddies. Places where the warmth pools and lingers, places where the cold dives deeper. The geometry of the gradient becomes complex, fractal, full of tiny sheltered corners.

The green responds first. It cannot help but respond—it is the quick flesh of this thing, the part that measures change in hours instead of centuries. In the new warm pockets, species that were struggling suddenly flourish. In the new cold channels, hardier varieties find their niche. The moss develops different textures at different temperatures—velvet where warm, crystalline where cold. The ferns begin to show variation, some growing dense and low, others reaching higher.

Diversity. That's all. Just the gift of more ways to be themselves.

The engine notices on its own timescale, which is to say it takes a year. But when it notices, it *approves*, in the wordless way that engines approve of better efficiency. The varied temperatures create convenction currents. Air moves in new patterns. The breathing becomes not just in-and-out but *around*, creating a slow circulation that distributes moisture and minerals more evenly. The engine doesn't have to work quite as hard to keep its edges warm. It can put that saved energy into growing.

The Weaver watches this unfold with the satisfaction of someone who has added a single pinch of salt to a soup that was already good. She does not intervene again. She doesn't need to. The complexity she introduced is self-sustaining, self-elaborating. In a hundred years there will be a dozen micro-climates in the notch. In a thousand, there might even be the beginnings of what could become eyes—some small adaptation in the green that notices the difference between the dim and the dimmer, the first faint precedent for actual sight.

But she does do one more thing before she goes.

She reaches into the place where she keeps things—not a pocket, not a bag, but a fold in the nature of keeping itself—and draws out a seed. Not a plant seed. A *pattern* seed. She holds it in her palm and breathes on it once, gently, the way the mountain breathes on its own walls. 

The seed is the pattern of companionship. Not the thing itself—this creature is too vast and strange to simply be given a friend. But the *possibility* of recognition. The tiny, dormant knowledge that there might be other processes in the world. Other things that grow and build and are. The seed holds no loneliness—the engine-mountain doesn't know what loneliness is. It holds only the potential to one day notice that it is not the only thing remaking stone into self.

She places the seed in a seam where warm meets cold meets green. It settles into the moss like a small round stone, unremarkable, patient. It will take centuries to germinate, if it ever does. It will take millennia to fruit. But if it does, the engine-mountain will one day lift whatever it has by then grown for senses and notice that there are others. Maybe they will be other mountains with other engines. Maybe they will be the small quick things that have learned to live in its branches by then. Maybe they will be something unimaginable yet. But it will notice, and in noticing, something new will be possible.

The Weaver stands, brushes the moisture from her palms, and prepares to go. As she does, she speaks a single word. Not to the engine-mountain—it has no ears. Not to the green—it has no language. She speaks to the process itself, to the growing-building-breathing that is the whole of the thing.

"Magnificent," she says.

The word settles into the roar and becomes part of it. Somewhere deep in the engine, a piston that is muscle that is stone contracts with infinitesimally more force. Somewhere in the canopy, a fern uncurls with imperceptibly more grace. The word becomes heat becomes growth becomes mountain, incorporated like everything else into the slow tremendous making.

The Weaver leaves the way rain leaves: gradually, then not at all. 

The notch continues. The peaks lean closer by a finger's width each century. The engine roars and the green breathes and the stone sheds and rebuilds. But now there are pockets of warmth that harbor unexpected varietals. Now there are currents of air that create small harmonies in the vast industrial thrum. And somewhere in the moss, a seed waits with geological patience for the day this thing might look up—or in, or out—and discover it is not the only process in the universe turning the world into itself.

Until then, it builds. It has always built. It will always build. 

But now it builds with a buried gift: the knowledge, sleeping but present, that building is a thing that can be shared.

The benevolent power has given what benevolent powers give—not transformation, not rescue, not even improvement, really. Just one more way to be what you already are. 

Just the possibility that what you are might, one day, find echo in the world.
