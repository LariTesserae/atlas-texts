The benevolent power arrives not as light or sound, but as a quality of attention—a deepening of presence that makes every droplet of condensation seem suddenly significant, every creature-cry carry new meaning.

She comes as an old woman, though her form shifts like heat shimmer when observed directly. Her clothes appear cut from the space between stars, dark fabric that holds depth without end. She climbs the scaffolding with impossible ease, her joints moving with the fluidity of youth, her weathered hands finding purchase on rungs slick with creation-fluid.

The Stewards notice her by the way the mountain notices her. The stone's weeping intensifies to a steady rain. Emerging creatures pause mid-birth to turn eyeless faces in her direction. The pneumatic systems shift their rhythm, as if asking a question in percussion and steam.

She spends days observing without interference. Watches a Steward coax triplet beings from a single stone womb—creatures like aerial jellyfish with bones of spun glass. Sits with the children as they play their gravity games, their laughter mixing with the industrial symphony. Shares tea (somehow produced from her star-dark pockets) with the eldest Steward, the one rooted into Platform Seven, whose words come as much from the mountain as from his throat.

When she finally acts, it's with the subtlety of someone who understands that creation cannot be forced, only nurtured.

First, she tends to the pain. Not to remove it—the mountain's labor pains serve their purpose—but to transform it. She touches the weeping stone and the water that emerges carries something new: a mineral-sweet taste that eases the raw ache of becoming. The Stewards find their bone-deep resonance with the mountain's contractions becomes less grinding, more like the satisfying stretch of muscles well-used.

For the creatures struggling to fully emerge, she hums. The sound has no melody humans could reproduce, but it teaches the stone how to let go more gently. Where before some beings tore free trailing rock-fragments like afterbirth, now they slip loose with clean separation. The half-formed ones still embedded in granite receive her particular attention—a hand laid against the stone, patience transmitted through touch, until they either complete their emergence or settle back to wait for their proper time.

She gifts the Stewards new songs. Not by teaching, but by singing them herself until they take root in the collective dreams. Melodies that help the pneumatic systems run smoother, reduce the friction between mechanical and biological. Harmonies that encode information—a creature's needs sung into being even as it emerges. Work-songs that make the impossible gravity shifts feel like dancing.

To the fungal libraries, she contributes spores from elsewhere—compatible species that expand their storage capacity, add redundancy to their memories. The mycelial networks grow more robust, able now to share information between platforms instantaneously. The catalogued failures become teaching tools rather than just records of loss.

The children receive her deepest attention. She shows them how their mutable bodies are gifts, not aberrations. Teaches the one whose eyes see through fog how to share that vision temporarily with others. Helps the boy who sweats catalyst understand how to regulate the flow, to become a living supplement to the injection systems when needed. For each strange puberty, she offers a framework for understanding: you are not broken, you are becoming what this place needs.

She addresses the isolation. The Vertical Nursery, for all its density of life, exists at a remove from the world below. She opens small windows—not physical, but perceptual. Now the Stewards can sometimes glimpse where their creatures go after leaving the nursery. See the forests of hybrid things taking root in distant valleys. Watch their aerial jellyfish form clouds above parched lands, their glass bones catching and redistributing moisture. These visions come rarely, usually during the collective dreams, but they provide context for the endless work.

For the eldest Stewards, those who've merged partially with the Nursery itself, she offers choice. Her touch awakens agency in their transformed bodies. The one who flows through pipes like blood discovers she can still coalesce into human form when desired. The rooted man learns to extend sensory filaments down through the mountain, becoming not trapped but expanded. They remain changed, but now by will rather than mere circumstance.

The mountain itself receives a gift of memory. She reaches deep into the unfinished stone and plants something—not physical, but a pattern. The mountain begins to remember what it was before it began becoming. This doesn't halt the transformation but grounds it. The wild variations stabilize slightly. The creatures that emerge, while still impossible by conventional standards, show more internal consistency. Evolution accelerates from hours to minutes, but with direction now, purpose encoded in the stone's slow thoughts.

She stays until her work settles into the rhythm of the place. Until the Stewards have integrated the new songs into their shift-changes. Until the children wear their changes as casually as their wet coats. Until the mountain's memory has fully taken root.

Before leaving, she gathers them all—every Steward who can separate from their work, even the merged ones extending their consciousness to attend. In the largest platform's gathering space, between banks of controls and dripping stone walls, she speaks. Her words layer like the industrial sounds, comprehensible to each listener in their own way:

"What you do here matters beyond these heights. Every impossible thing you birth makes the world larger. Every creature that shouldn't exist but does expands what 'should' means. Your children, strange and wonderful, will carry this expansion forward. The world needs its edges pushed, its definitions challenged, its reality made stranger and more generous.

"But remember too that creators need rest. That mountains sometimes need to simply be mountains. That your bodies, however changed, deserve intervals of simple human comfort. The work will never be finished—creation never is. Pace yourselves to match infinity."

She leaves them with one final gift: a small adjustment to the settlement's dense chambers. Now, sometimes, the industrial yellow light softens to something like sunset. The mist occasionally parts to show stars. The pneumatic thunder quiets, just for moments, letting them hear their own heartbeats distinct from the mountain's pulse.

The Vertical Nursery continues trembling with creation. But now the trembling has rhythm, the creation has memory, and the Stewards have something they didn't know they needed—the knowledge that their strange, impossible work is seen, valued, and gently held by forces that understand the necessity of birth, in all its forms, at the edges of what's real.
