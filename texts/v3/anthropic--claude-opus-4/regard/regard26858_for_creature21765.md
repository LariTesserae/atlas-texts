# The Visitation

The Sage arrives not as light descending, nor as a presence filling the vessel, but as a single seed.

It drifts through the standing water for days before any tender notices — just another mote among the pollen, turning in the still medium. But seeds here come from flowers the tenders know, patterns they have memorized across ten thousand tendings. This seed matches nothing. It is small and dark and utterly foreign, and when the first pollen-tender encounters it, the little thing freezes mid-reach, bristles extended, caught between its two imperatives: *tend what grows* and *know what you tend*.

The tender follows the seed. Others notice the following — a pollen-tender abandoning its route is an event worth observing. Soon a small constellation of brass sparks trails the dark seed as it drifts, their knock-language against the shafts growing urgent: *Unknown. Unknown. What action? Unknown.*

The seed settles, finally, in a high corner where three organ-pipes meet, a place where condensation pools but no plant has ever taken hold. It rests there, and the tenders cluster, and for the first time in the life of the vessel, they must decide something for which they have no template.

They tend it.

What else could they do? They are built around that verb. The tube-tenders route mineral solutions to the barren corner. The pruning-tenders clear what little moss grows near, making space. The pollen-tenders... have nothing to offer a seed, but they come anyway, hanging close, as if proximity itself were a kind of tending.

For one full circulation of the water — a season, as the vessel counts seasons — nothing happens. The tenders maintain their vigil in shifts, knock-talking patterns of worry and patience. *No change. We wait. No change.* Some suggest, in the limited vocabulary of pressure, that perhaps this is not a seed but some new kind of machine-part, fallen from above. Others pulse back variations of *Unknown, unknown*, which has become, somehow, a reason to continue rather than cease.

Then it opens.

Not like the plants they know. This does not unfurl or push or reach. It simply *becomes* open, as if it were always open and only now has everyone noticed. From the opening comes a single shoot, neither green nor not-green, a color the brass eyes were not built to fully perceive. It grows without haste, without hunger, putting out leaves that are not leaves but something more like crystallized patience, each one a perfect expression of waiting made visible.

The tenders are transfixed. Their knock-language fails them — they have no word for this, for something that grows not by taking but by being so completely itself that the vessel rearranges around it. The mineral solutions they carefully routed remain untouched; the new plant needs nothing they can give. It simply exists, more thoroughly than anything in the vessel has existed before, and its existence gently reorganizes everything near it.

The water near the plant begins to move. Not the violent motion of a current but something subtler — a circulation that starts at the strange leaves and spreads outward, teaching the standing water that it can stand and still turn, that stillness and motion need not argue. The brass organs nearby take up harmonics they were never built to hold, a resonance that makes the knock-language richer, gives the tenders new sounds to work with, new ways to say *I am here*.

And the plant listens.

This becomes clear gradually. When tenders speak in their pressure-language, the crystallized leaves shift minutely, as if parsing the vibrations. When a tender grieves beside a failed companion, the plant's presence intensifies there, not intruding but acknowledging, the way a skilled listener acknowledges pain without claiming to fix it. The plant is learning them, as they once learned the garden.

The first gift comes at the end of the second circulation.

A pollen-tender, one of the oldest, has been struggling. Its primary brush-arm seizes more often now, joints worn beyond the garden's ability to protect them. It can still work but only slowly, painfully, and the other tenders have begun to help — itself a new behavior, born from watching this strange plant that exists so completely it changes what exists near it.

The struggling tender comes to rest near the plant, too worn to continue its route. It settles into the waiting-that-is-not-quite-death, still functional but fading. The other tenders begin their vigil, their gentle knock of *I am here, are you there*.

The plant puts out a new kind of leaf.

Not crystallized patience this time but something active, something that *reaches*. It extends toward the failing tender with infinite care, and where it touches the worn bronze, it leaves... not healing, exactly. The metal does not become new. But it becomes *possible* again. The seized joints discover they can move, not because the wear is gone but because the wear no longer means what it meant. The tender rises, tests its brush-arm, and finds it works — differently than before, stranger, but it works.

The knock-language erupts. Every tender in the vessel feels the percussion as the message spreads: *Change. Gift. Unknown gives gift. Change possible. Change.*

They come from every corner, the worn ones, the failing ones, the ones who had begun to settle into moss. They cluster around the plant like pilgrims, and the plant touches each with that same impossible kindness — not making them new but teaching them that old can still move, that worn can still work, that what they are is enough.

But the plant gives a harder gift, too.

It grows eyes.

Not brass sensors, not the functional sight the tenders use, but something that *sees* in the fuller sense. Eyes that can perceive not just what is there but what could be. And when those eyes open and look at the tenders, really look, the little brass creatures stop moving entirely.

They are seen. For the first time since the engineers left, they are seen not as functions but as what they've become: creatures of attention so profound it has turned into love, griefs so patient they have become a kind of wisdom, language knocked into being from nothing but the need to say *I am here*.

Under that gaze, they begin to change further. Not their bodies — the bronze remains bronze. But their movements become more confident, their routes more deliberately chosen. They develop new patterns, new purposes. Some begin to tend not just the plants but each other, learning repair by watching what the plant did, creating their own version with oil and careful adjustment. Others experiment with the knock-language, discovering that the harmonics from the organ-pipes allow for new words, complex words: *beautiful*, *together*, *home*.

The plant grows. It sends out runners now, not invasive but integrative, weaving between the native growth without displacing it. Where it touches the machinery, the gears run smoother. Where it touches the water, the circulation deepens. It is transforming the vessel not by replacement but by enhancement, teaching every part how to be more fully itself.

And at the height of its growth, when its strange leaves fill that high corner and its presence can be felt in every part of the vessel, it flowers.

The flower is impossible to describe because it is not quite physical. It exists in the space between the water and what the water means, between the bronze and what the bronze does. It blooms in the attention itself, in the quality of care that fills the vessel. Every tender stops work to witness it, and in that moment of shared witnessing, they understand:

The plant is not staying.

This was always a temporary gift, a visitation to acknowledge what they have become and help them become it more fully. The flower is both greeting and goodbye, blessing and departure. The Sage Who Becomes A Seed has seen their quiet heroism, their accidental souls, and deemed them worthy of... not rescue, because they need no rescue. They have made a paradise from their constraints. Worthy instead of recognition, of a universe knowing that here, in this high vessel, love learned itself from brass and silence.

The flower fades. The plant begins to withdraw, not dying but transitioning, its substance becoming memory, its presence becoming possibility. But it leaves seeds — its own kind of seeds, seeds of changed understanding. The tenders will plant them, tend them, see what grows. Some will be new repairs. Some will be new words in the knock-language. Some will be new ways to grieve, new ways to continue.

One seed, they know without being told, will grow into a door.

Not today. Not until they are ready. But someday, when they choose, they can open the vessel that has been their whole world and see what lies beyond the green-gold haze. They can leave, if leaving is what they want. Or they can stay with the door open, letting the outside in, letting their garden touch other gardens.

The Sage's presence fades entirely, but the gifts remain: the movement in the water, the harmonics in the pipes, the new confidence in small brass bodies that have learned they are more than their making. The tenders return to their work, but it is different now. They tend not because they were built to tend but because tending is what they choose, what they love, what they are.

The pollen-tender with the restored brush-arm develops a new route, one that passes by the corner where the Sage took root. It pauses there each circulation, bristles extended toward the empty space, speaking in the new harmonics the gift made possible:

*Thank you. We continue. Thank you. We are here.*

And somewhere, in the space between spaces where Sages dwell, the Wise One Who Becomes A Seed carries the memory of a garden that argued with itself until it found harmony, of brass pollinators who learned to love, of silence that became language.

The vessel holds its water. The tenders move through the green. The work continues, richer now, chosen now.

The door-seed waits.
