The benevolent power does not arrive. It has been here all along, distributed through the mist itself—a presence that moves between the water droplets, reads the crystalline deposits like scripture, and knows each midge by the particular frequency of its wingbeat.

It begins with the smallest adjustment: in the hairline vents where steam escapes, the pressure cycles shift by mere pascals. The change is so slight that no instrument could detect it, yet the bacterial consortia respond immediately. They begin secreting new compounds—not for their own survival, but to alter the mineral gradients in ways that will matter ten thousand years hence. The crystals that form now will channel water differently when the ridge finally settles. Caves will open where none were destined. Sheltered spaces will emerge, still cold but offering the first real refuge from the perpetual damp.

The power touches the cushion plants next. Not to make them grow faster—that would violate their careful contract with the cold—but to enhance their memory. Each rosette becomes a living archive, encoding in its tight-packed leaves the precise conditions of every frost, every brief warming, every mineral trace that seeps past its roots. When these plants finally release their spores, they will carry forward not just genes but a detailed history of this place, readable by whatever comes after.

Among the lichens, the intervention is even subtler. The power does not change them but changes what they can see. Their chemical sensitivity expands to detect patterns in the rock that predict where new fractures will form. Slowly, imperceptibly, they begin to migrate—not randomly but with purpose, positioning their star-shaped colonies at the future intersections of stability. When the ridge completes its rise, they will already mark the load-bearing points, the places where structures could stand if anyone arrives to build them.

The midges receive a gift of synchrony. Their emergence patterns, already pulsed, now align across the entire ridge system. What was local becomes regional; what was coincidence becomes signal. Their collective shimmer in the mist forms brief but readable patterns—a living display visible from the high shoulders, marking time in a way the eternal mist obscures. The grazers learn to read these signals and adjust their movements accordingly. The predators learn to anticipate them. A simple ecological relationship becomes the first stirring of something larger: shared time, communal rhythm, the bare beginning of culture.

But the deepest work happens in the spaces between—in the mist itself. The power begins to tune the condensation patterns, the way droplets form and merge and fall. It takes decades before the effect becomes visible: the gray layers that recede into formlessness start to show subtle differentiation. Travelers moving through the mist find that distances are not uniform. Some paths through the vapor lead more quickly to shelter, to water, to the patches where algae bloom thickest. The landscape remains harsh, but it becomes navigable in ways it wasn't before. Knowledge can accumulate. Paths can be remembered and taught.

The power saves its most profound touch for the being who lives in this place—if such exists, if such will exist, if such has always existed in potential. In a shallow depression where meltwater collects just long enough to freeze into perfect clarity, something changes in the ice itself. Not its structure—that would be too crude. Instead, the way light moves through it alters at the quantum level. The ice becomes a lens, but not for seeing. It focuses something else: possibility, awareness, the faint stirring of will that distinguishes life from mere chemistry.

Whatever drinks from this pool—grazer, predator, or something not yet born—will find their perception subtly shifted. The endless gray will resolve into countless grays, each with meaning. The sound of settling rock will carry information about the deep structure of the ridge. The patterns in the mist will suggest not just paths but purposes. The being will not become more than its nature allows, but it will become all that its nature allows. And in becoming complete, it will complete the place.

The power does one last thing before dispersing back into the mist. It adjusts the oldest mineral seam, the one laid down when the ridge first began to rise. Deep in the rock, crystals that formed in perfect regularity now show the faintest deviation—a signature, a mark of attention paid. When erosion finally exposes this seam, perhaps millions of years hence, someone will read it and know: this place was seen. This place was deemed worthy of preservation, not from change but for change. The signature says nothing about what should be built here, only that building is possible.

The mist continues its eternal cycling. The rock continues its slow adjustment. The lichens spread, the plants endure, the creatures move through their careful intervals. Nothing dramatic has occurred. But in the space between one water droplet and the next, between one rock groan and its echo that will someday exist, everything has been made ready.

The place waits, no longer purely patient but actively prepared. Whatever emerges here—whether it crawls from the vents, walks in from the distant valleys, or crystallizes from the mist itself—will find a world that matches its complexity, that can hold whatever thoughts it brings, that remembers on its behalf until memory becomes possible.

This is the gift: not transformation but potential, not intervention but invitation. The benevolent power has written possibility into the stones and withdrawal into the mist, leaving only the faintest trace of its passing—just enough to say: *here, something more can be.*
