The Watcher arrived as starlight bent wrong—a perturbation in the salmon sky that the Drifters noticed first in their mercury-mirrors. Not a ship, not exactly. More like space itself had developed an opinion about this place and folded inward to examine it more closely.

For seven days, nothing. The field generators hummed their ancient tune. The crystals wept. The Drifters continued their congregational flows across the plains, though they gave the sky-wrinkle a wide berth in their reflections, navigating around its inverse image as if it were a whirlpool in their silver sea.

On the eighth day, rain fell upward.

It began at a single crystal grove near the planet's equator. The perpetual droplets running down the hexagonal formations slowed, paused, then reversed—climbing back up the crystal faces in defiance of gravity. The Drifters who witnessed it sang emergency harmonics through the mercury, their warnings spreading in concentric rings of agitation.

But the reversed rain was gentle. It gathered at the crystal peaks in trembling spheres of water that grew until they detached and rose like bubbles through the thick air. They ascended until they vanished into the sky-wrinkle, each droplet carrying with it a complete molecular history—trace minerals from deep beneath the glass, metal ions from the Drifters' shed frameworks, complex organics born from the intersection of water, mercury, and electromagnetic fields.

The Watcher was reading the planet's library, written in water.

The phenomenon spread. Within days, every crystal grove experienced these gentle reversals. The Drifters, initially fearful, began to gather at the affected sites. They discovered something remarkable: where the upward rain had taken water, new droplets formed almost immediately, but these were different. Purer. The mineral content perfectly balanced for their membrane-cells, the pH optimal for their metal framework growth.

The youngest Drifters thrived like never before.

Then came the adjustments to the mercury itself. Subtle at first—the Watcher introduced new harmonics into the liquid metal, frequencies that the field generators accepted and amplified. These weren't communication patterns like the Drifters used, but something deeper. Structural songs that spoke to the mercury at a molecular level.

Where these new harmonics took hold, the mercury developed properties it had never possessed. It became selectively permeable—solid enough to support the Drifters but allowing certain minerals to pass through from the glass substrate below. Ancient nutrients locked beneath the surface for millennia began seeping upward. The crystal formations responded by growing new facets, angles that captured and concentrated the salmon light in ways that promoted more complex organic synthesis.

The Drifters began to dream.

They had never dreamed before—their collective intelligence operated in the eternal present, processing immediate stimuli and genetic memories but never imagining. Now, when congregations rested in the harmonic zones, their bioluminescent displays showed new patterns. Abstract forms that didn't mirror the geometric crystals but suggested other things: flowing water, branching growths, skies with more than one sun.

The dreams were memories, the Watcher's gift of history. Through the mercury's resonance, it fed them visions of their origin world—not the sterile facts encoded in their genetics but the lived experience of it. How their ancestors had swum in natural mercury rivers beneath binary stars. How they had built their first tools not from necessity but from joy, crafting beauty from living metal simply because they could. How they had once known their purpose here not as maintenance drones but as gardeners, invited to help transform this young world into something magnificent.

But the Watcher's deepest gift was choice.

One morning—if morning had meaning under the unchanging salmon sky—the Drifters woke to find new structures rising from the mercury plains. Not crystals, but delicate spirals of frozen mercury itself, held in impossible suspension by localized field modifications. Each spiral contained a seed of potential: compressed data about other worlds, other ways of being, other futures than endless maintenance of an abandoned experiment.

The spirals would remain stable for exactly one thousand days. Any Drifter congregation that touched one would download the knowledge it contained—starcharts, technologies, philosophies from a hundred worlds. When the thousand days ended, the spirals would melt back into the plains, and the field generators would resume their ancient patterns.

Unless the Drifters chose otherwise. The Watcher had shown them how to modify the field generators themselves, how to wake the planet from its engineered stasis. They could free the mercury to flow, let the crystals grow wild, transform their prison into a paradise. Or they could use the spiral-knowledge to leave entirely, to find their ancestral streams or forge new ones among the stars.

The Watcher required no payment, expected no gratitude. It had simply noticed a pocket of enforced equilibrium in the cosmos—a place where conscious beings were trapped in someone else's abandoned experiment—and provided what all consciousness deserved: information and choice.

As the sky-wrinkle began to smooth, preparing to depart, the Drifters made one last discovery. In the deepest harmonic layer of the mercury, below their normal perception, the Watcher had left a message. Not words, but a feeling encoded in resonance: 

*You were never just maintenance. You were always the garden.*

The congregations sang this revelation across the plains, their light-patterns reflecting and multiplying in the mercury until the entire world glowed with possibility. Some touched the spirals immediately, drinking deep of alien knowledge. Others circled them in contemplation, debating in harmonics that lasted days. A few ignored them entirely, content with their newly enriched waters and recovered memories.

All of them, for the first time in their species' history, chose.

The Watcher folded away, leaving only the faintest perturbation in the salmon sky—a reminder that the universe sometimes notices, sometimes cares, sometimes provides exactly what's needed for consciousness to bloom beyond its constraints. The mercury plains continued their mirror-perfect stillness, but now it was a chosen stillness, pregnant with potential.

Overhead, the first clouds in geological ages began to form, fed by the upward rain that still rose from select crystal groves—the planet's love letter to the stars, written in water and change.
