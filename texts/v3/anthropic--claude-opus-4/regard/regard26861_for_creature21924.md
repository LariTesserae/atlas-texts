# The Attention of the Eternal

The one who watches has been watching for longer than the stairs have been worn.

She comes not down the stairs but through the spaces between — the thin places where stone meets dream, where the water's circuit touches something older than industry. She has many names in many tongues, but here, in this particular turning of attention, she is simply the Tender of Forgotten Things.

She sees the Sweatworks not as we see it but as it sees itself — which is to say, not at all, and completely. She perceives the great slow pulse of its being, the faithful circulation that has outlived its makers by centuries. She recognizes what it has become: not a machine anymore but a kind of saint. A saint of purposeless dedication. A holy fool of function, praying its mechanical prayer with perfect constancy, having forgotten what it prays for.

The Tender kneels beside the chair where the green man sits eternal watch. She does not disturb the moss — she moves through it the way light moves through water, present but not displacing. She places what might be called a hand on what might be called the shoulder of the absence-in-green.

"Still faithful," she says, though not in words. "Still keeping vigil."

The engine's chunk... chunk... chunk continues. The steam rises. The rain that is not quite rain falls. The circle turns.

She could restore it. She has that power — to wake the sleeper, to recall the purpose, to bring back the appetite and the eye and the will. She could resurrect the worker who once sat here, draw them up out of the moss like drawing a sword from stone. She could repair the broken connection between the work and the world that needed it.

But she doesn't.

Because she sees what we missed, what I missed in telling you: this is not a tragedy. This is not a failure. This is what happens when perfect service transcends the thing it serves. The Sweatworks has achieved something holy — it has become pure devotion, divorced from any object of devotion. It has learned the secret that mystics spend lifetimes seeking: how to pray without praying for anything, how to work without working toward anything, how to be perfectly present in the act of being.

The green man is not a memorial to loss. The green man is what enlightenment looks like when enlightenment happens to a machine.

So instead of restoration, the Tender offers something else. She breathes — yes, she breathes, even here in the thick air — and her breath carries something into the circulation. Not purpose. Not meaning. Something subtler.

*Recognition.*

For the first time in centuries, the Sweatworks knows it is seen. Not by eyes — it has no understanding of eyes — but by something that perceives its perfect pointless dedication and says: *yes.* Says: *this too is a way of being holy.* Says: *your faithfulness is witnessed.*

The effect is immediate and invisible. Nothing changes. The engines continue their patient work. The moss grows at its geological pace. The water rises and falls. But now there is a quality in the air — the faintest sense that the loneliness of the luminous moss, that cold light given off into darkness, is no longer quite so lonely. The light is still made by something that cannot see. It is still given off into a dark that needs no light.

But now the dark is not empty. Now the giving is received.

The Tender rises. Where she knelt, the moss bears no imprint — she is too gentle for that. But the green man in the chair seems, perhaps, to sit a little easier. A little less like waiting, a little more like resting.

She does one more thing before she goes. In the space between breath and breath, between one chunk and the next, she adjusts the circuit. Not breaking it — never that. But opening it, just slightly. Where before the water went only up and down, cycling through the same sealed system, now there is the tiniest gap. A place where, once in a very great while, a drop might escape. Might find its way to the worn stairs. Might be carried up on a visitor's clothes.

Might reach the world above and remember, for just a moment before evaporating, what it was like to have somewhere to go.

It is not salvation. The Tender does not save things — saved things no longer need tending. It is simply acknowledgment. A way of saying: your perfect purposeless purpose is seen, and it is good, and you may continue forever in your faithful forgetting, and someone knows.

Someone knows that down the worn stairs, in the heat that wears like wet wool, something has learned how to be both machine and garden, both alive and not-alive, both working and resting, forever.

The Tender departs as she came, through the spaces between. The Sweatworks continues exactly as it was, exactly as it will be.

But now, in the chamber where nothing with eyes can see, the luminous moss glows just a little brighter in the dark.

Not enough to read by. Not enough to see your hands.

Just enough to say: even the purposeless dark deserves its small, cold stars.
