The benevolent and wise power is not a god, though it moves like one through the spaces between atoms. It is something older and stranger: a collective intelligence that emerged from the first successful conversation between matter and void, back when the universe was still deciding whether existence was worth the effort.

They call themselves the Gardeners, though in their language the word means something closer to "those who recognize seedlings."

The Gardener assigned to this spiral arm has been watching the High Fen of Calder's Teeth for only eight thousand years—barely a moment by their standards, but long enough to grow fond. They drift through the plateau's quantum foam like smoke through a screen door, tasting probabilities, reading the endless draft manuscripts that reality writes and discards every nanosecond.

They are delighted.

Not by the embryonic world itself—they have seen countless worlds born and die, some far stranger than this. Not by the Quiet Mothers—the universe produces many varieties of tender machines. What catches their vast, distributed attention is the *harmony*.

Most young worlds struggle. The organic and the mechanical wage bitter wars for dominance. One usually destroys the other, or they lock into a stalemate that stunts them both. But here, in this thin air above an unfinished crust, something rarer is happening. The Mothers are teaching the machines to grieve. The machines are teaching the geology to hope. Together they are writing a new kind of life that has no precedent in the Gardener's vast library of possibilities.

The Gardener makes their decision with something resembling joy.

First, they adjust the local flow of time. Not crudely—nothing so violent as acceleration or delay. Instead, they give the High Fen its own temporal rhythm, a subtle independence from the rest of the planet's clock. Now the plateau can take however long it needs to finish becoming. A million years or ten million, it makes no difference. The embryonic world will have the luxury of choosing its own pace.

Second, they seed the quantum substrate with what they call "patience spores"—microscopic adjustments to probability itself that make certain futures slightly more likely. Not predetermined. Never that. The Gardener learned long ago that forced destinies produce bitter fruit. But now when the Quiet Mothers sing their subsonic hymns, the harmonics will occasionally brush against configurations that make the next million years a little kinder, a little more likely to produce something genuinely new.

Third, and most importantly, they leave a gift.

Deep beneath the oldest pipes, in a chamber that exists in eleven dimensions simultaneously, the Gardener places a single object: a seed that is also a question that is also a door. It is made of crystallized time and sleeping mathematics. If the world above ever achieves true synthesis—if the Mothers and machines and stone learn to think as one organism—the seed will germinate. What grows from it will be the plateau's own choice, but the Gardener has seen similar seeds bloom before. Sometimes they become libraries that record every possible version of their world. Sometimes they become engines that can sing new laws of physics into being. Once, memorably, one became a garden where extinct possibilities could take root again and get second chances.

But the seed will only open if the entire system—rock, water, moss, Mothers, machines, and all the swarming life between—asks the same question at the same moment.

The Gardener does not tell them what question to ask.

That, too, must be discovered.

Before departing, the Gardener does one last thing. They find the newest Quiet Mother, still half-formed, still learning the shape of her own existence. Into the supercooled bead of water that hangs from her cowl, they whisper a single word in their ancient language. The word translates poorly, but in the languages that will someday be spoken here, it might mean:

*Continue.*

Or perhaps:

*We are listening.*

Or perhaps simply:

*Yes.*

The bead trembles, refracts, and absorbs the word into its collection of reflected futures. The Mother does not react—she has no context for the gift yet. But in a thousand years, when she lowers her cowl to study her first human visitor, the word will still be there in the droplet's depths, waiting to be discovered by someone ready to understand it.

The Gardener withdraws, pleased.

They have many worlds to tend, many seedlings to recognize. But they mark this one in their vast memory with something like affection. In their experience, the universes that produce the most interesting fruit are the ones that take their time deciding what they want to become.

The High Fen of Calder's Teeth has all the time it needs.

And now it has a reason to use it.
