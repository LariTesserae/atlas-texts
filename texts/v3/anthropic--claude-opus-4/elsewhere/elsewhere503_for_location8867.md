Above, the world burns with impossible cold.

The ice sheet stretches beyond every horizon, its surface scoured by winds that have never stopped howling. Here, distance means nothing and everything—a featureless plain that could be crossed in hours or centuries, depending on what the wind decides. The sun, when it shows itself, comes filtered through crystalline air so pure it cuts, casting shadows sharp enough to draw blood. There is no color but the infinite variations between white and blue, no sound but the conversation between wind and ice.

Yet even here, in this sermon of endings, something persists.

The Windkeepers build their lives from what others would call absence. Their shelters rise and fall with the seasons—not structures but agreements with the wind, arrangements of curved ice blocks that channel the gales into something almost like walls. They know every vocabulary of snow: the granular speech of fresh fall, the compressed whispers of pack ice, the treacherous eloquence of surfaces that look solid but wait to swallow the unwary. Their maps are carved in memory and muscle, in the way a body leans into particular gusts.

They dress in layers of fur and hollow bones, garments that breathe with their wearers, that transform the body into a collection of moving air pockets. Their eyes, perpetually narrowed against glare, have developed secondary lids—translucent membranes that filter the stabbing light into something bearable. They speak in clicks and stops, a language that cuts through howling wind, punctuated by gestures visible at great distance. Every word is precious here, wrestled from the wind's mouth.

The Windkeepers follow the hidden veins of warmth that thread through this frozen world. They know where the ice grows thin, where pockets of geothermal heat create temporary oases of liquid water. These sites shift with the seasons, appear and vanish according to rhythms they've spent generations learning to read. At each warm spot, they perform careful rituals: testing the water's temperature, measuring the steam's density, sometimes simply sitting in silence to feel the earth's pulse through the ice.

They are hunters of the invisible. Their prey: the wind-fish that swim through blizzards, visible only as disruptions in the snow's pattern. The ice bears that phase between states, solid when still, dispersing into crystalline mist when they run. The thought-birds that exist more as possibility than presence, their migrations tracing ley lines of electromagnetic force across the frozen plain. To catch such creatures requires more than skill—it demands a kind of sympathetic imagination, the ability to think in their strange grammars.

Their children learn to read the ice before they learn to speak. Infant eyes track the subtle signs: the way hoarfrost grows differently on northern faces, how certain winds carry the memory of distant water, where the aurora touches down and leaves the snow briefly alive. By the time they take their first solo journey across the white, they carry libraries of ice-knowledge in their bones, encyclopedias of survival written in the language of scars and successful returns.

But the Windkeepers' deepest knowledge concerns what lies beneath. They know, through stories encoded in their oldest songs, that something sleeps under the ice. Their ancestors spoke of the day the world froze—not gradually but in a single exhalation, winter arriving like a door slamming shut. They remember cities swallowed whole, peoples who chose descent over death, the last messages tapped through ice too thick to break.

At the old sites, where their thermal maps suggest deep anomalies, they perform the speaking ceremonies. Laying their bodies flat on the ice, they speak in the lowest frequencies their voices can produce, sending vibrations down through countless meters of frozen time. They tell the news of the upper world: which stars have shifted position, where the new warm spots have appeared, how many generations have passed since the freezing. Sometimes—rarely—they feel answering vibrations, patterns too regular to be settling ice, too deliberate to be geothermal.

The connection between the worlds is more than memory. Where the Windkeepers' warm spots appear, far below, the Dwellers feel the temperature change in their stones. The water runs differently, carrying new minerals, new possibilities. The pale creatures of the under-city grow agitated, clustering at certain points as if listening. And the Dwellers themselves feel it in their bones—a warming that speaks of somewhere else, of a world they remember in their cells if not their minds.

In the deepest part of winter, when the surface world grows so cold that even the wind freezes solid and falls as crystal rain, the Windkeepers gather at the primary sites. They build temporary cities of ice above the buried stone ones, their structures following plans they don't consciously remember but their hands know. In these brief settlements, they conduct the old trades: carving messages in ice tablets that will melt come spring, singing the genealogies that connect them to those who went below, preparing the gifts that cannot be delivered.

The gifts accumulate at these sites year after year: tools carved from the bones of wind-fish, clothes woven from the shed fur of ice bears, containers filled with the aurora-touched snow that carries special properties. They know these offerings cannot penetrate the ice sheet, but the making and leaving of them maintains a covenant. We remember you, the gifts say. We continue. We keep the vigil.

And below, in the city that sleeps, the Dwellers sometimes pause in their patient tending. They feel vibrations in the stone that match no pattern of settling or flow. Their clouded eyes turn upward, seeing through rock and ice to something their ancestors knew as sky. In these moments, the two peoples share a single thought, separated by geological time but immediate as breath: the world continues. Above and below, in ice and stone, in howling wind and patient silence, the world continues.

The ice sheet is not a barrier but a membrane, permeable to memory if nothing else. The thermal veins that the Windkeepers follow are the city's dreaming, warmth rising through stone and time to briefly kiss the surface. The vibrations the Dwellers feel are the footfalls of their cousins, transformed by distance into something like music. Neither people can reach the other, but both know they are not alone in their strange adaptations, their careful continuances.

Between them lies the ice itself—not mere frozen water but a library of the between-time, holding in its layers the pollen of the last flowers, the bones of creatures caught in the freezing, the tools dropped by those who chose descent. Sometimes the wind scours deeply enough to reveal these treasures. Sometimes the warmth below melts pockets that release ancient air. In these moments, the two worlds breathe together, sharing molecules that remember when stone and sky were neighbors, when the city expected citizens who never came, when the world held possibilities it has not forgotten, only postponed.

This is how the world continues: in the careful tending above and below, in the messages sent through ice and stone, in the children who learn to read temperatures and pressures as their birthright. The city sleeps beneath ice, but it does not sleep alone. Above, the Windkeepers maintain their mobile vigil, following the warmth, reading the signs, keeping the memory of connection alive in their movements across the white. They are separated by more than distance—by state changes, by evolutionary pressures, by the simple fact that one people breathes air and the other breathes time. But they are bound by more than separation can undo: by the knowledge that somewhere else, someone continues. That the world, fractured and frozen and strange, remains whole in its very division.

The ice grows thicker each year, the city settles deeper into its stone dreams. But at the margins where warm meets cold, where vibration travels between worlds, where memory refuses the logic of barriers, the connection persists. Two peoples, shaped by their environments into forms their ancestors wouldn't recognize, continue the human work of meaning-making in places that seem to refuse meaning. They tend their separate gardens—one of stone and patient water, one of wind and patient ice—knowing without knowing that they tend the same garden, divided by catastrophe but not conquered by it.

This is what it means to inhabit a broken world: to continue without hope of repair, to maintain without promise of purpose, to connect without possibility of meeting. The city sleeps beneath ice, and in its sleep, it dreams of wind. The wind howls above ice, and in its howling, it remembers stone. Between them, in the ice itself, the story of their separation waits to be read by some future archaeology, some warming that might never come. But the peoples above and below don't wait for that future. They live in the endless present of their strange divided world, making meaning from maintenance, making home from the impossible, making the broken whole by the simple act of continuing to be.
