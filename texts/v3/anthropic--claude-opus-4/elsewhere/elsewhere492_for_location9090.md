Far below the Weeping Mountain, where the mineral-heavy runoff finally exhausts itself across a plain of ancient limestone, another kind of darkness holds sway. Here, the saturated waters from above have carved a labyrinth of caves that honeycomb the earth for miles in every direction. The acidity that prevents growth above has, over millennia, dissolved palace-sized chambers and corridors below, creating a inverse world of empty spaces where the mountain above is solid mass.

The Cave Fishers make their home in this upside-down landscape. They are tall where the mountain dwellers are compact, their bodies stretched by generations of navigating spaces where height matters more than width. Their skin is pale as paper, with a blue-green tinge where blood vessels show through, and it bears a peculiar quality—it is covered in microscopic cilia that sense the slightest air currents, allowing them to navigate in absolute darkness through touch alone.

They live by the water, but not in it. The mineral-rich flows from the mountain pool in underground rivers through their domain, and they have learned to read these waters like a language. Each stream carries its own chemical signature, its own temperature, its own burden of dissolved stone. The Cave Fishers follow these rivers but never drink from them directly—the water that sustains the blind creatures above would poison them in days.

Instead, they practice a form of aquaculture that seems impossible: they farm the boundaries where the acid mountain water meets the ancient limestone. In these mixing zones, the chemistry creates conditions where unique organisms thrive—not fish, despite their name, but colonies of filter-feeding things that build delicate calcium architectures like underwater cities. The Cave Fishers tend these colonies with infinite patience, adjusting flow rates with carefully placed stone weirs, introducing new mineral sources, singing to them in subsonic frequencies that promote growth.

Their settlements cluster around these farming zones, built from limestone blocks that glow with their own soft phosphorescence—not the angry red of the mountain's veins but a cool blue-white like starlight. They shape these stones by painting them with controlled applications of the mountain water, dissolving channels and chambers with an artist's precision. Their architecture flows like frozen water, with no straight lines, only curves that guide air and sound in precise patterns.

The darkness here is total, deeper than the eternal night above. No mineral veins pulse with light, no bioluminescent creatures provide even the faintest glow. The Cave Fishers have evolved beyond the need for sight entirely. Their eyes, vestigial now, are covered by translucent skin that serves only to protect the sensitive tissue beneath. They navigate by echolocation, but not the crude clicking of the mountain's pale climbers—they produce complex harmonics in ranges humans can barely hear, creating sonic maps of impossible detail.

Their children are born with functioning eyes that seal shut by their first year. The closing is celebrated with elaborate ceremonies where the child is passed between singers who weave sonic patterns around them, teaching their developing brains to translate sound into spatial understanding. By adulthood, a Cave Fisher can stand in a chamber and, with a single sustained note, perceive every stalactite, every pool, every other person present, down to the rhythm of their breathing.

They govern themselves through consensus reached in the Singing Chambers—vast echo-perfect spaces where hundreds can gather and harmonize. Decisions emerge from the interplay of voices like crystalline structures from solution. Leadership is fluid, passing to whoever can guide the harmony toward resolution. Their laws are not written but sung, remembered as complex musical patterns passed down through generations.

Time moves differently in the caves. Without day or night, without seasons, they measure duration by the growth of their filter-feeding colonies—slow accumulations of calcium that might add a finger's width in what the mountain dwellers would call a stone-year. They sleep when tired, wake when rested, gather when the harmonics call them together. Their lives are long—the constant temperature and humidity, the absence of light damage, the careful diet drawn from their aquatic farms, all contribute to lifespans that stretch twice as long as their mountain neighbors.

They know of the city above, though few have made the journey. The transition from their total darkness to the mountain's phosphorescent night is agonizing for their hyper-sensitive skin, and the constant rain feels like an assault after the controlled humidity of the caves. But knowledge flows between the two peoples like water through limestone—slow, indirect, but persistent.

The connection between the Weeping Mountain and the Cave Fishers' domain is both physical and metaphysical, a relationship written in water and stone across geological time. The mountain's youth and violence feed the caves' patient expansion, while the caves' ancient stability provides a foundation that keeps the volcanic forces from spreading too far.

The water is the obvious link—every drop that falls on the city above eventually finds its way to the limestone galleries below. But it arrives transformed by its journey, carrying messages in its chemical composition. The Cave Fishers can taste whether construction has begun in a new sector by the subtle change in mineral content. They know when the blind climbers are mating by the microscopic organisms that wash down with the runoff. They can even determine the health of the mountain's fungal gardens by the pH shifts in their farming zones.

In return, the caves breathe upward. The vast empty spaces below create air currents that rise through fissures and fumaroles, carrying cave-moisture that moderates the mountain's harsher chemistry. Without this circulation, the mountain's rains would grow ever more acidic until even the hardy moss and fungi couldn't survive. The two systems exist in unconscious partnership, each making the other possible.

Between them lies a zone neither fully claims—a band of collapsed galleries and fresh lava tubes where the young volcanic rock meets the old limestone. Here, the water performs strange alchemy, creating formations that belong to neither world: crystals that sing when struck, pools that maintain themselves at perfect body temperature, passages that seal and reopen on cycles no one has mapped. Both peoples avoid this boundary zone except for their traders and diplomats, who meet in specially maintained chambers where the conditions won't kill either party.

These meeting places are marvels of compromise. The mountain dwellers construct elaborate drainage systems to keep the spaces dry enough for the Cave Fishers' sensitive skin. The Cave Fishers, in turn, cultivate bioluminescent organisms that provide just enough light for mountain-adapted eyes without overwhelming cave-adapted senses. They communicate through a pidgin language of clicks and harmonics that both can produce, though neither finds it natural.

Trade between them is essential but limited. The mountain dwellers need the Cave Fishers' calcium supplements to counteract the perpetual acidity of their environment. The Cave Fishers prize the copper tools that their limestone world cannot provide, and the rendered oils that help maintain their farming colonies. But more than goods, they trade knowledge—the mountain dwellers share their understanding of water's surface behavior, while the Cave Fishers offer insights into deep flows and mineral cycles.

The young of both peoples sometimes undergo exchange trials, spending months in the other world to understand the full cycle of water and stone. Mountain children descend, blindfolded and oil-slicked, to learn the sonic arts. Cave Fisher adolescents ascend, their skin wrapped in protective silk, to experience the concept of visual light for the first time. Most return gratefully to their birth-world, but some few find themselves caught between, equally uncomfortable in either darkness, and these become the permanent ambassadors who maintain the connection.

Over the slow centuries, the two peoples have developed parallel mythologies that recognize each other's necessity. The mountain dwellers speak of the Cave Fishers as the mountain's roots, holding it steady against its own violent growth. The Cave Fishers sing of the mountain dwellers as gardeners of the sky-stone, preventing the raw forces above from poisoning the patient waters below. Both are right, and both are incomplete—the truth lies in the total system, the circulation that neither could maintain alone.

Recently—within the last few stone-years—changes have begun that concern both peoples. The mountain's growth has accelerated slightly, its rains carrying new minerals that challenge the Cave Fishers' careful farming. Meanwhile, deep drilling by the Cave Fishers in search of new colony sites has encountered hot rock where none should be, suggesting the volcanic forces are spreading laterally underground. The traders speak of establishing permanent settlements in the boundary zone, despite its dangers, to better monitor these changes.

Some among both peoples whisper of a coming convergence—a time when the mountain's roots will reach the cave floors, when the limestone galleries will fill with volcanic stone, when both peoples will need to find new ways of being. Others dismiss this as the anxiety of those who spend too much time at boundaries, seeing patterns in random fluctuation. But all agree that the water knows, carrying in its mineral-heavy flow the story of what's coming, if only they could learn to read it fully.

This is how the two darknesses relate: not as opposites but as partners in a dance of dissolution and construction, each creating the conditions the other requires. The Weeping Mountain pours its young stone-blood into the ancient caverns, while the caves breathe stability back into the violent sky. Between them, in the margin where neither darkness quite claims dominion, the real work happens—the slow negotiation between growth and patience, between fire and water, between the urgency of survival and the persistence of stone.
