The Salt Gardens of Gherem

Three days' journey upstream, where the water still runs clear and cold before it seeps through the foundation stones of the Weeping Foundry, the mountain opens into a bowl of white crystal. The Salt Gardens sprawl across ancient terraces carved from the living rock, each level a geometric puzzle of rectangular pools separated by narrow walkways of pink-veined marble. The pools are shallow—no deeper than a spread hand—and lined with tiles of compressed salt that glitter like snow under the perpetual afternoon sun.

This place knows no roof but sky. The builders here worked in opposition to their counterparts downstream: where the Foundry compresses and conceals, the Gardens expand and expose. Every surface is designed to maximize exposure to air and light. The pool walls slant at precise angles to catch the mountain wind. Channels between levels are wide and straight, carrying their cargo of brine in lazy, observable flows.

The salt itself is not of the sea. It rises from deep springs that penetrate the mountain's heart, carrying minerals from stone beds no surface dweller has seen. The water emerges at near-boiling temperatures, heavy with dissolved crystals that begin forming the moment they meet the cooler air. Each pool precipitates different minerals depending on temperature, depth, and the time of day—white cubes of common salt in the upper terraces, delicate pink rhomboids at middle levels, and in the lowest pools, rare purple crystals that form only when the moon is dark.

The Gardeners tend these pools with tools of carved bone and implements of hardened wood—metal would corrupt the crystal formation. They wear robes of undyed wool that wick away the perpetual spray, and their feet are bare despite the sharp crystal deposits that crust every walkway. Generations of careful steps have worn smooth paths through the salt, creating trails that glitter with embedded crystals ground fine as sand.

They speak seldom and move with deliberation. Their work is one of timing and attention: knowing when to channel fresh brine into a pool, when to let evaporation concentrate the minerals, when to harvest. They read the crystal formations like text, understanding from the shape and color of each deposit what adjustments must be made upstream. A cluster of needle-thin formations warns of too-rapid evaporation. Cloudy, malformed crystals speak of impurities that must be filtered.

The harvesting is done at specific hours, when the angle of sunlight through the crystals creates rainbows on the pool floors. The Gardeners wade in carefully, using thin scrapers to lift entire sheets of salt without breaking the delicate lattice structures. These are laid on drying racks of woven grass, where mountain wind completes the process. The finest specimens are packed in boxes of aromatic cedar, each crystal wrapped in silk. The common salt is ground in stone querns and stored in ceramic jars sealed with beeswax.

But not all the harvest is preserved. The Gardeners know that certain crystals, when dissolved in precise proportions, create solutions of peculiar properties. A purple crystal dissolved in morning dew will preserve organic material indefinitely. Pink salt mixed with rainwater creates a solution that etches glass. And the rare black crystals that sometimes form during thunderstorms—these are never sold or stored, but immediately dissolved and poured into special containers made from the shells of certain mollusks that live in the high pools.

The Gardeners live in simple cells carved into the cliff face above the pools. Each chamber has a window that faces the Gardens and a door that opens to the communal spaces where they take their meals—always the same: flatbread, dried fruit, and water from the one spring that runs clear and salt-free. They own little beyond their tools and robes, but each keeps a private collection of unusual crystals—formations too unique to harvest, too beautiful to dissolve.

The eldest among them remembers when the compact was made. How the builders came from the lowlands with plans for their impossible Foundry, seeking the purest water and the right minerals. How the Gardeners' ancestors, who had tended these pools since before memory, recognized in those plans a complement to their own work. What they produced here in the open air, under sun and sky, would be transformed in the depths of the Foundry into something else—something that required darkness and compression and constant circulation.

Now, twice each year at the solstices, a delegation of Gardeners descends the mountain paths, carrying jars of specific salts—never the purple, never the black, but careful selections of white and pink and the rare yellow crystals that form only during the summer months. They leave these at a certain stone marker where the path splits, one way leading down to the Foundry, the other climbing back to the Gardens. By morning, the jars are gone, replaced by small vials of water that glows with the same phosphorescence as the Foundry's deepest glyphs.

This water, the Gardeners use sparingly. A single drop added to a crystallizing pool will alter the entire harvest, creating salts of impossible clarity and formations that defy the usual laws of crystal growth. They have learned, over generations, which combinations produce which effects. They keep these formulas in memory only, passed from elder to apprentice in whispered conversations during the harvest moons.

The true work happens at the intersection. The salts descending, dissolved in the Foundry's endless circulation, processed through heat and stone and the patient ministrations of the Distillers. The treated water ascending, carrying within it whatever essence the Foundry extracts or creates. Neither the Gardeners nor the Distillers fully understand the whole. They know only their portion: evaporation and precipitation above, dissolution and circulation below.

Between them flows a current of transformation. The mountain water begins pure and cold, accumulates minerals in the Salt Gardens, carries them down to feed the Weeping Foundry's mysterious processes. What returns is changed—not purified, exactly, but refined in ways that make the next cycle's salts more potent, more precise in their properties. Each iteration builds on the last, a slow accumulation of purpose that neither community fully grasps.

The young sometimes ask why. Why tend pools of salt under the crushing sun? Why feed minerals to a foundry whose product they never see? The elders point to the crystals themselves—how each one contains the entire pattern of its making, from the first dissolved grain to the final facet. The work is its own answer. The cycle completes itself.

And in certain lights, when the sun angles through the spray of the Gardens just so, observers swear they can see figures in the mist—elongated forms that might be tricks of refraction, or might be something else. The Gardeners do not speak of these appearances. They simply nod and return to their work, adjusting the flow between pools, reading the crystals, maintaining their portion of an ancient compact whose full shape remains hidden, divided as it is between the bright terraces above and the weeping chambers below.
