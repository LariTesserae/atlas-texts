Far below the vertical city, where the mountains' roots disappear into older earth, the air thickens with more than just oxygen. Here, the valleys that look like mere shadows from above open into a different world—a place where the cataclysm that thrust up the peaks also tore deep wounds in the planet's skin.

The forest floor exists in perpetual twilight. The canopy—dense, primordial—belongs to trees that were already ancient when the mountains were born. Their trunks, some fifty feet in diameter, bear the scars of that upheaval: bark split and healed into ridged formations, root systems exposed where the ground buckled, crown shyness patterns disrupted where the violent tilting threw neighboring giants against each other. These trees adapted, growing new buttresses at improbable angles, sending out aerial roots that became secondary trunks, creating a architecture of survival that turns the forest into a three-dimensional maze.

The fauna here breathes differently than their relatives above. Evolution, compressed into mere centuries since the cataclysm, has produced creatures of remarkable variety. The descendants of deer move through the understory on legs twice the length of their ancestors, their hooves splayed wide to navigate the spongy, uneven ground. Their lungs have developed secondary chambers—not for thin air but for air thick with spores, pollens, and the chemical communications of the forest. Predators have evolved patience: massive cats with fur that mimics the patterns of dappled light, waiting days in the same position. Primates with enlarged eyes swing through the middle canopy, their calls pitched below human hearing, felt more than heard as vibrations through the massive trunks.

But the true rulers of this realm are smaller. Insects here grow to startling sizes—beetles the length of human forearms, their carapaces iridescent with warning colors. Moths with wingspans like dinner plates navigate by echolocation, their fuzzy bodies perfectly tuned to absorb and emit the ultrasonic frequencies that penetrate the thick air. Colonies of ground-dwelling arthropods form super-organisms, millions of individuals moving as coordinated masses that strip fallen trees to nothing in days, leaving behind nutrient-rich castings that fuel the forest's frenetic growth.

The hydrology here defies conventional understanding. Water moves in ways the mountain dwellers above could never imagine—not just down but up, sideways, in spirals through the aerial root networks. The trees themselves are conduits, their vascular systems pumping thousands of gallons daily from the water table to the canopy, where it condenses and rains back down in localized cloudbursts. Rivers appear and disappear, running underground through root-carved channels, emerging as springs that feed pools of water so mineral-rich they glow faintly in the darker hollows. The constant humidity creates its own weather—fog banks that roll through the understory, temperature inversions that trap pockets of cooler air, sudden downpours from seemingly clear sections of canopy.

The ground itself is uncertain. What appears solid often isn't—decades of fallen organic matter creating false floors that can swallow the unwary. Sinkholes open where underground streams have carved away the limestone, some dropping into cave systems that network beneath the entire forest. These caves breathe with the barometric pressure, exhaling cool air that creates microenvironments at their entrances, supporting entirely different ecosystems—pale ferns, blind fish in underground pools, colonies of bats whose guano supports elaborate fungal gardens tended by specialized insects.

This is where the reclusive ones retreated when the mountains rose and the city builders went up. They call themselves the Deepwood People, though their original name is older, a language that mimics the subsonic communications of the forest itself. They rejected the verticality of the mountains for the complexity of the forest floor, building their settlements in harmony with the giant trees rather than in defiance of natural forces.

Their structures are grown rather than built. Over generations, they've learned to guide the growth of the trees, training aerial roots into living bridges, coaxing trunks to hollow themselves into dwelling spaces, encouraging the canopy to weave into rain-proof shelters. Their architecture has no straight lines, no metal, no concrete—everything curves, breathes, lives. Bioluminescent fungi cultivated in the walls provide light. Water is channeled through hollow vines. Waste feeds carefully maintained decomposer colonies that generate heat through their metabolic processes.

The Deepwood People themselves have adapted too. Their eyes have enlarged slightly, pupils dilating far wider than their mountain cousins. Their skin produces oils that repel the worst of the biting insects. They've developed a heightened sensitivity to air pressure changes, allowing them to predict the forest's localized weather. Some claim they can feel the trees' chemical communications, though whether this is evolution or simply generations of careful attention remains unclear.

They dress in fabrics made from processed bark and woven fungal fibers, garments that breathe in the humidity and dry quickly after the frequent rains. Their tools are predominantly organic—hardened thorns for precision work, shaped gourds for containers, cultivated shells from the giant snails that graze the lower trunks. What metal they use comes from bog iron collected in the seasonal swamps, forged at temperatures achieved by focused sunlight rather than coal or wood fire.

Their society is structured around the growth patterns of the forest. Families tend territories defined not by boundaries but by the root networks of allied trees. Leadership rotates based on the seasons and the forest's needs—those most attuned to the subtle signs of disease or imbalance taking temporary authority. They practice a form of agriculture that's nearly invisible to outsiders: encouraging certain plants, discouraging others, maintaining the delicate balance that keeps the forest productive without depleting it.

The children of the Deepwood learn to climb before they walk, their infant hands gripping bark and vine with instinctive sureness. They're raised communally in the middle canopy, where platforms woven from living branches create safe spaces away from ground predators. Their education involves learning the songs that map the safe routes through the forest, the scent markers that indicate seasonal changes, the subtle visual cues that distinguish edible from toxic in a world where mimicry is a survival strategy.

Their relationship with the fauna is complex. They've domesticated none of them—the forest doesn't allow for such simplified relationships. Instead, they've developed mutual understandings: certain birds announce the approach of predators in exchange for access to cultivated fruit trees; colonies of ants are allowed controlled access to storage areas in exchange for their defensive capabilities; the giant moths are never hunted during their mating season, and in return their caterpillars process fibers that the Deepwood People harvest for their strongest ropes.

Disease here takes different forms than in the vertical city. The humid air breeds fungi that can colonize human lungs, parasites that alter behavior subtly before revealing their presence. The healers of the Deepwood maintain elaborate pharmacopeias, knowing which bark treats which fever, which sap neutralizes which toxin. They practice a medicine that's as much about maintaining the body's ecosystem as treating specific ailments—understanding that humans here are walking colonies, their survival dependent on maintaining the right balance of beneficial organisms.

The Deepwood People's spiritual life is inseparable from their daily existence. They don't worship the forest so much as participate in it, seeing themselves as one species among many in the great community of living things. Their rituals involve chemical communication—consuming specific combinations of plants that allow them to perceive, however dimly, the vast slow conversation between the trees. Their funeral practices return bodies to the forest floor quickly, viewing decomposition not as decay but as transformation, the individual rejoining the larger organism of the forest.

At night, the forest transforms. Bioluminescence blooms—fungi, insects, even some of the smaller vertebrates glowing with cold light. The Deepwood People navigate these hours differently, some sleeping in shifts to maintain awareness of the nocturnal rhythms, others entering trace states that allow them to rest while remaining alert to changes in the forest's breathing. Predator calls echo through the darkness, answered by the alarm signals of prey species, creating a soundscape that tells complex stories to those who know how to listen.

The canopy dwellers—both human and animal—experience night as a different world entirely. Up here, where the leaves meet the sky, temperatures drop sharply. Flying hunters emerge: bats with wings adapted for hovering, gathering insects drawn to the flowers that bloom only in darkness. The Deepwood People who specialize in canopy work sleep in hammocks that sway with the trees' movement, their dreams full of flight and falling.

The connection between the Deepwood and the vertical city above is more than just geographical. The mountains' youth means the barrier between above and below is permeable, full of cracks and passages that neither community fully maps.

The ore veins that the mountain city mines extend deep into the earth, their excavation sometimes breaking through into cave systems that connect to the Deepwood's underground rivers. When this happens, communication occurs—sometimes intentional, sometimes accidental. Miners encountering vast caverns filled with the sound of moving water, catching glimpses of pale fish that shouldn't exist. Deepwood explorers finding metal ladders extending down into their familiar caves, evidence of the industry above.

Trade happens, though neither society officially acknowledges it. Certain intermediaries—people comfortable in both worlds—carry goods along routes that follow the seasonal streams. From above comes metal, precisely tooled implements the Deepwood People can't produce, medicines for conditions the forest can't treat. From below flows food the mountain city can't grow, fibers stronger than steel cable, biological solutions to industrial problems—fungi that prevent metal corrosion, insects that clean the city's pipes better than any mechanical system.

The intermediaries themselves are a hybrid culture. They build waystation settlements in the transition zones where the forest thins and the mountains begin their dramatic rise. Their buildings combine both worlds' techniques—metal frameworks overgrown with guided vegetation, concrete foundations softened by living root systems. They speak both languages, understand both value systems, and profit from the careful maintenance of mutual ignorance between the two main populations.

Climate creates another connection. The Deepwood's transpiration feeds the clouds that catch on the peaks above. The mountain city's industrial processes affect the rainfall patterns below. When the smelters work overtime, the rain in the forest carries traces of metal that some plants concentrate, creating groves of copper-bright leaves that the Deepwood People harvest for specific purposes. When the forest experiences die-offs from disease or imbalance, the water cycle disrupts, leaving the mountain city even more desperate for resources.

The young mountains continue to rise, a few inches each year. This movement creates constant minor earthquakes that both communities feel. In the Deepwood, trees sway and resettle. In the vertical city, cables sing warnings. Both populations have developed predictive systems—the Deepwood People reading the behavior of deep-root systems, the mountain dwellers monitoring their instruments. Sometimes, rarely, urgent messages pass between them when one group's warnings suggest danger the other hasn't detected.

Ecology connects them in ways neither fully grasps. The flying creatures that nest in the city's girders migrate seasonally to the Deepwood's canopy to breed. They carry seeds, spores, and parasites between the two zones. The goat-like browsers of the mountains are descended from forest dwellers that followed the uplift, their genes still carrying memories of the woodland floor. Occasionally, individuals wander back down, introducing new diseases but also new genetic diversity to both populations.

Human connections are the most complex and least acknowledged. Young people from both communities sometimes disappear, drawn by curiosity about the other world. Some return, changed. Others vanish entirely, presumably finding lives in the transition zones or beyond. Family lines split by the original migration maintain hidden connections—messages passed through intermediaries, children sent for fosterage in the other community during times of crisis, knowledge preserved in both locations about bloodlines and genetic compatibilities.

The violence between the communities, when it occurs, is usually about water. The mountain city's mining operations sometimes divert underground streams. The Deepwood's forest management can affect the watershed that feeds the city's reservoirs. These conflicts play out through sabotage rather than direct confrontation—caves mysteriously blocked, cables cut by "rockfalls," whole groves dying from "natural" causes that disrupt the water cycle.

But there's also cooperation, hidden and essential. When avalanches threaten to send millions of tons of rock and ice into the valleys, the Deepwood People evacuate without acknowledging why. When forest fires rage below, the mountain city adjusts its industrial processes to avoid adding particulates to the smoke plumes. Both communities maintain emergency caches in the transition zones—medical supplies, preserved food, temporary shelters—that either can access in true catastrophes.

The knowledge flow between them is perhaps the most vital connection. The Deepwood People understand the biological solutions to problems the mountain city solves with engineering. The vertical city's scientists model weather patterns and geological changes that affect the forest's evolution. Young scholars from both communities sometimes meet in the neutral zones, trading information through academic proxies, each pretending not to know the ultimate source of their insights.

As the mountains continue their slow rise and the forest adapts to the changing conditions, both communities evolve. The vertical city develops new technologies for extreme environments. The Deepwood People breed new cultivars that can handle the shifting water patterns. The intermediaries grow in number and influence, creating a third culture that may eventually bridge or replace the two extremes.

The planet itself drives these connections. The cataclysm that created their divided world continues its slow work—mountains rising, valleys deepening, climate patterns shifting. Both communities are temporary solutions to a landscape in flux. Their survival depends not on their separation but on the complex web of connections they maintain while pretending autonomy.

In the early morning, when mist rises from the forest and catches on the mountain peaks, the water vapor carries information between the two worlds. Chemical signals from the forest's morning blooming. Metallic traces from the night shift's smelting. Spores and pollen riding updrafts. Industrial particulates settling into the canopy. The two communities breathe each other's air, drink each other's water, live in the shadow of each other's choices.

The vertical city clings to its peaks, defying gravity. The Deepwood embraces the earth, surrendering to complexity. Between them runs the constant exchange—of water, air, genetics, knowledge, and the unacknowledged understanding that they are two responses to the same catastrophe, two experiments in survival whose ultimate success may depend on their eventual reunification or their continued evolution into something neither can currently imagine.
