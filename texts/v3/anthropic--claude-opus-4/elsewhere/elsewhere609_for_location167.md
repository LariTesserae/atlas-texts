The Meridian Wells

Three days' journey through the canopy, following the old arterial bridges where the wood has gone silver with age, the city gives way to something older. Here the trees grow different—not the cultivated giants that support Miren-Saal's sprawling weight, but wild titans that predate any human architecture. Their trunks spiral as they rise, creating natural chambers in the cork-like bark where travelers shelter and hermits make their homes.

Between six of these ancients, where their canopies merge into a single breathing ceiling, hangs the Meridian Wells.

From below it appears as a darkness between leaves, a weight that makes the branches bow but never break. Climbing closer reveals its true nature: a vast net of calcified root systems, bleached white as bone, woven together by some intelligence that might once have been the trees themselves. This suspended labyrinth spans perhaps a quarter-mile, its passages and chambers forming a three-dimensional maze that shifts with wind and the slow growth of its living anchors.

Water finds its way here from throughout the forest. It runs down the spiral trunks in carved channels older than memory, collects in natural cisterns formed where roots meet and merge, drips through the calcified maze in patterns that change with the seasons. But this is not ordinary water—it carries the forest's chemistry, concentrated through its journey: tannins that stain it amber, minerals that make it taste of copper and clay, dissolved memories of every leaf and creature it has touched.

The Wells themselves occupy the deepest chambers of the suspended maze. Pools of varying sizes, some no larger than a washbasin, others that could swallow a house. The water in each is different—some clear as air, others dark as night, a few that shift between states like living things. The calcified roots form natural lips around each pool, worn smooth by centuries of visitors drawing water, leaving offerings, whispering requests into the darkness.

Those who tend the Wells arrived long before Miren-Saal dreamed itself into the canopy. They call themselves the Drawers, though whether this refers to their practice of drawing water or drawing meaning from it remains ambiguous. They live in the maze itself, in chambers they've carved from the dead root matter, their homes indistinguishable from natural formations until you notice the subtle marks of habitation: stairs worn into existence by countless passages, shelves that might be natural mineral deposits or might be carefully cultivated, apertures that frame views of specific Wells at specific times.

The Drawers dress in fabric they dye with water from different Wells—rust reds, midnight blues, a green so deep it looks black until sunlight hits it. They move through the maze with the certainty of blood through veins, never hurrying, always arriving exactly when needed. Their faces bear the mineral deposits of their work: crystalline growths at the corners of eyes, hair that moves with its own current, skin that sometimes reflects light in ways that suggest transparency.

Each Well has its nature, its use, its hunger. The Grief Well accepts tears and returns clarity—not comfort, but the sharp vision that comes after mourning. Its water runs cold enough to stop the heart for a moment, and those who drink it report tasting their own sorrow transformed into something almost sweet. The Drawers bottle this water in containers made from the discarded shells of some ancient snail, each one unique, each one singing faintly when full.

The Thread Well connects. Its water, when properly prepared, carries messages between those who've drunk from it. Not words but meanings, emotions, the shape of thoughts without the barrier of language. The merchant families of Miren-Saal pay handsomely for vials of Thread water, using it to coordinate across the vast distances of the canopy trade routes. But the Drawers warn: drink too deeply and you might lose track of which thoughts are yours.

In the maze's heart lies the Meridian Well itself, from which all others spring or to which all return—the Drawers' explanations vary with the asking. This pool reflects not the calcified ceiling above but somewhere else, sometimes else. Children see futures in its surface. The dying see faces they've forgotten. The Drawers see nothing at all, or claim to, though they tend it more carefully than all others combined.

The water from the Meridian Well doesn't behave like water should. It climbs the walls of containers, seeks height rather than depth, freezes in spirals that defy physics. Those who drink it unprepared report displacement—finding themselves looking at their body from outside, or experiencing memories that haven't happened yet, or forgetting how to distinguish between breathing and drowning. The Drawers process this water through rituals that take days, filtering it through specially cultivated mosses, exposing it to certain lights at certain angles, singing to it in languages that predate human speech.

Trade between the Wells and Miren-Saal follows routes established before either place admitted the other existed. Young Drawers, those still learning the maze's geometry, carry water down the spiral paths in vessels that must never touch the ground. They trade for things the Wells need: specific metals that enhance certain waters' properties, written records that the Drawers dissolve in ceremony, children's dreams caught in bottles by city shamans.

In return, Miren-Saal receives waters that cure ailments of body and mind, that make their living architecture grow in predetermined patterns, that help their memory-keepers preserve crucial histories. But also waters that no one fully understands: the Inverse Water that flows upward, the Chromatic Water that paints whatever it touches with colors that don't have names, the Silent Water that muffles sound so completely even thoughts grow quiet.

The relationship runs deeper than trade. On certain nights, when the moon aligns with gaps in the canopy, phosphorescent messages appear in the Wells—simple patterns that match the glowing trails in Miren-Saal's Understitch. The Drawers transcribe these carefully, sending the patterns back with their water traders, who deliver them to gaps in the city floor where they disappear into the darkness below. What answers return come in the form of new waters appearing in previously empty Wells, or existing waters changing their nature overnight.

Saiph-Who-Remembers, the children whisper, drinks from the Meridian Well in dreams. They say this explains its impossible nature, its ability to be one thing and many, its secretions that preserve memories in nacre and light. The Drawers neither confirm nor deny this, but they've been known to leave certain Wells uncovered on nights when the silk-farmers' webs in the distant city hum with particular songs.

The Meridian Wells shape time differently than the eternal present of the Understitch. Here, past and future flow together like waters from different sources, creating eddies and currents that careful observers can read. The Drawers age strangely—some seem ancient despite arriving only seasons ago, others maintain youth across decades through careful consumption of specific waters. They claim time is just another fluid to be drawn, processed, and redistributed according to need.

Visitors often ask why the trees permit this parasitic architecture, this weight that strains their branches season after season. The Drawers smile at the question, gesturing to the healthy bark despite the burden, the lush canopy despite the stolen water, the way the trees' roots dive deeper and spread wider than any others in the forest. The Wells and the trees exist in symbiosis too complex for simple explanations—each feeding the other in ways that run deeper than nutrients, older than language.

In the quiet hours before dawn, when mist rises from the forest floor and condenses on the calcified maze, the Wells sing. Each pool has its note, determined by its depth, its mineral content, the memories dissolved in its water. Together they create harmonies that make the entire structure resonate, drawing night-flying creatures who dance in the sound, pollinating flowers that bloom only in response to specific frequencies. The Drawers gather then, adding their voices to the song, maintaining some ancient bargain with the forest that keeps the Waters flowing and the maze suspended.

The Waters remember everything they've touched, the Drawers say. In the Meridian Wells, the forest's memory pools and concentrates, available to those who know how to draw it up, process it, make it safe for human consciousness. And sometimes, in the mixing of waters from different Wells, new properties emerge—memories that belong to no single source, futures that arise from the mingling of multiple pasts, possibilities that exist only in the space between what was and what might be.

This is how the canopy city and the Wells remain connected across distance and difference: through the medium of water and memory, through traders who carry more than goods, through messages written in phosphorescence and answered in changes of flavor and flow. The Understitch processes what falls through the cracks; the Wells draw up what rises from the roots. Between them, they maintain a circulation that keeps both the built and wild worlds alive, remembering, dreaming of what they might yet become.
