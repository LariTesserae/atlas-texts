Between the Furnace Plains and the domain of the Inheritors lies the Gradient—a transitional realm where the physics of survival bend and warp like the heat-drunk air itself. This is not a border but a deepening, a place where the merely hellish becomes the truly alien, where 200 degrees climbs toward 300 and beyond, where the last pretense of a habitable world falls away.

The Gradient begins where the outermost Siphons fail. Here, ancient machines stand frozen mid-function, their mechanisms seized by extremes even their builders hadn't anticipated. These dead Siphons create wind shadows in the thermal currents, pockets of relative coolness that never last more than an hour before the deep heat finds them again. The tungsten-shelled arthropods cluster here during their reproductive cycles, taking advantage of these fleeting respites to molt and mate in frantic bursts of activity before dispersing back to the cooler plains.

As one descends—for the Gradient is as much vertical as horizontal, following the subtle slope toward the planet's exposed mantle—the crystal plants change. Their metallic fronds grow shorter, denser, turning from the branching structures of the plains to tight rosettes that hug the ground. They've learned to burrow, sending taproots deep into the substrate to find layers where the temperature stabilizes. Some have given up photosynthesis entirely, becoming purely thermosynthetic, their bodies black as void to absorb every fraction of heat.

The air itself transforms. At 250 degrees, metal vapors begin to behave strangely, forming short-lived crystalline structures in mid-air—geometric ghosts that exist for seconds before sublimating back to gas. The Inheritors call these "heat angels," reading omens in their fleeting forms. At 275 degrees, the atmosphere develops layers like oil on water, different densities of metallic vapor creating visible striations that ripple and flow with their own weather patterns.

Here too are the Crawlways—a network of ancient maintenance tunnels that once connected the Siphons, allowing long-dead engineers to move between them without facing the surface heat. The tunnels have partially collapsed over millennia, filled with metallic dust that has sintered into a kind of natural ceramic. But sections remain navigable, creating a subsurface highway between the realms. The temperature in the Crawlways hovers at a relatively merciful 180 degrees, though the air is thick with accumulated vapors that make breathing a conscious effort.

The Inheritors use these tunnels for their deepest rituals. In chambers where tunnel intersections create natural amphitheaters, they gather for the Thermal Mass—ceremonies where dozens of bodies create collective heat patterns too complex for any individual to generate. These gatherings happen only when the thermal conditions are perfect, perhaps once a decade, and can last for months. Through synchronized breathing and precisely controlled blood flow, the assembled Inheritors create three-dimensional heat sculptures in the air, visible only to their adapted eyes—mandalas of temperature that encode their deepest mysteries.

It's in the Crawlways that the Inheritors maintain their most sacred sites: the Memory Vaults. These are chambers where they've learned to manipulate the metallic vapor to create semi-permanent recordings. By carefully controlling temperature gradients, they can force the metal-saturated air to precipitate in specific patterns, creating thin films on specially prepared surfaces. These films, when heated just so, release their stored thermal patterns—ghost-echoes of ancient heat songs, the accumulated wisdom of generations preserved in metallic palimpsests.

The Crawlways also harbor their own ecosystem. Thermophagic fungi grow in long strings from the ceiling, feeding on the temperature differential between tunnel and surface. These pale, thread-like organisms pulse with bioluminescent signals, creating a communication network that the Inheritors have learned to read and even influence. By touching the fungal threads and modulating their body temperature, an Inheritor can send messages through the network—simple concepts like "danger," "gathering," or "death"—that propagate for kilometers through the tunnel system.

But the Crawlways are not safe. In places where the tunnels pass too close to the deep heat, the walls themselves become soft, flowing like thick syrup over geological time. These flowing zones migrate slowly through the network, sealing some passages while opening others in a process the Inheritors call "the stone tide." More dangerous still are the heat pockets—areas where collapsed sections have created perfect thermal traps, spaces where the temperature can spike to 400 degrees without warning. The bones of unwary Inheritors mark these zones, their calcium transformed by heat and pressure into warning markers of crystallized bone that glow with their own accumulated thermal energy.

The boundary between survivable and unsurvivable is not fixed in the Gradient. It moves with the deep currents, advancing and retreating in cycles that span centuries. The Inheritors have learned to read these patterns in the behavior of the crystal plants, in the migration of isotherms through the rock, in the changing harmonics of the still-functioning Siphons. They know that their habitable zone is slowly shrinking, that the deep heat rises incrementally with each passing generation.

This knowledge has shaped their culture in profound ways. They don't build permanent settlements but maintain a series of temporary camps along the thermal boundaries, ready to retreat as the heat advances. Their most precious possessions are the thermal maps passed down through heat-song, showing the safe zones of centuries past and predicting the patterns of centuries to come. They know they are living on borrowed time, but in their patient way, they have accepted this.

The younger Inheritors sometimes speak—in their slow, thermal language—of the Deep Ones, those who have adapted even further and moved into the heart of the Gradient where even Inheritor flesh cannot survive. Whether these are myth or reality, none can say. But sometimes, in the deepest tunnels, patterns appear in the metallic dust that no Inheritor made, and heat-songs echo from depths where no throat could survive to sing them.

The Gradient pulses between the two realms like a massive heart, its thermal currents the systolic and diastolic pressures of a planetary circulation system. The Siphons on the plains draw heat upward; the exposed mantle in the deeps pushes it outward; and in between, in the Crawlways and crystal gardens and flowing stone zones, life persists in forms that challenge the very definition of the word. 

Neither fully of the plains nor fully of the deep heat, the Gradient exists in perpetual transition, a place where adaptation itself must constantly adapt, where survival means accepting that no survival is permanent. It connects the two worlds not as a bridge but as a reminder: that between any two states, no matter how extreme, there exists a spectrum of impossible possibilities, each point along it hosting its own particular miracles and horrors.
