**The Moss Gardens**

Three thousand feet below, where the mountain's roots spread into the warm valleys, water runs wild and green. Here the air hangs thick with moisture, and every surface blooms with life that would seem impossible to those who know only stone and ice above.

The gardens began as necessity. Centuries past, when the first careful trades were established between the plateau city and the lower settlements, travelers discovered these natural terraces where hot springs emerged from the mountain's depths. The water, heated by the Earth's deep fires and rich with minerals from its journey through stone, created pockets of perpetual warmth even as winter locked the heights in ice. What started as waypoints for weary traders became something more: a systematic cultivation of the boundary between two worlds.

Now the terraces step down the mountainside in carved precision, each level flooding and draining according to ancient schedules. The moss grows in a hundred varieties, from the common cushion moss that springs back underfoot to the rare golden threads that must be harvested by moonlight or lose their potency. Some varieties carpet the pools in floating mats thick enough to walk upon, while others cling to the carved channels in precise lines, following the mineral traces in the water like living veins.

The gardeners here are a different breed from their kin above. Where the plateau dwellers are pale and dense with cold-adaptation, these people of the middle heights have taken on the colors of their work. Their skin carries a greenish tinge, not from any stain but from generations of symbiosis with the spores and pollens they tend. Their hair grows wild and often hosts tiny epiphytes—miniature mosses that feed on natural oils and give their bearers a constantly shifting crown of green. They move with a different rhythm too, languid where the stone-carvers are precise, their gestures flowing like the water they channel.

The architecture here breathes. Buildings rise from the living terraces as curved forms built from compressed moss-brick, the material still alive and growing, constantly renewing itself. Roots and runners knit the structures together into organic wholes that shift subtly with the seasons. Windows are unnecessary—the walls themselves glow with a soft bioluminescence as the moss responds to temperature changes throughout the day. Rain strengthens rather than erodes these structures, each storm adding new growth to the ever-thickening walls.

The cultivation practices merge engineering with biology. Each terrace maintains its own microclimate through careful manipulation of water flow and shade. Bamboo pipes, traded from even lower elevations, create musical fountains that not only irrigate but vibrate at frequencies that encourage growth. The gardeners speak to their moss in subsonic hums, generations of observation having revealed which tones promote spreading, which encourage sporing, which calm the growth during necessary harvesting.

Stone channels carved with the same patience as the plateau city's tunnels direct the hot springs through elaborate patterns. But where the upper channels are purely functional, these incorporate aesthetic flourishes—spirals that create whirlpools, steps that make the water sing, splits and reunions that ensure perfect mineral distribution. The moss responds to these patterns, growing thicker along certain flows, creating living mandalas that change with the seasons.

The harvesting is an art requiring years to master. Each variety must be gathered at its own perfect moment—some when heavy with morning dew, others during the brief afternoon dry spells, a few only during certain lunar phases when their chemical compositions peak. The tools are specialized: curved blades that separate without crushing, nets of spider silk that lift without breaking, ceramic containers that preserve potency during the long journey upward.

What grows here defies simple categorization. The medicinal mosses range from simple fever-reducers to complex psychoactives used in the plateau city's deep ceremonies. Some varieties produce fibers stronger than any animal hair when properly processed, others yield dyes that respond to temperature, creating garments that shift color with their wearer's warmth. The rarest specimens generate actual heat through their metabolism, and carefully tended heat-moss blankets are worth small fortunes in the cold heights above.

The water itself becomes a harvest. As it flows through terrace after terrace, it accumulates extracts and essences, becoming a complex brew of minerals and organics. The final pools at each level's end are carefully tapped, their waters bottled in carved stone vessels and labeled with the precise date, flow-pattern, and moss varieties of their journey. These waters serve purposes from medicine to metallurgy—the plateau's stoneworkers swear certain varieties help them feel the grain of difficult stone.

Wildlife here differs utterly from the spare fauna above. Clouds of emerald insects dance above the pools, their larvae feeding on moss while adults pollinate the rare flowering varieties. Amphibians in jeweled skins hop between levels, their calls creating symphonies at dawn and dusk. Small mammals with waterproof fur and webbed feet dive through the channels, spreading spores and controlling the populations of moss-eating grubs. Even birds nest here, weaving homes from living moss that continue growing around their eggs.

The social structure reflects the collaborative nature of the work. Where plateau families cluster in defensive warmth, moss gardener clans spread along entire terraces, sharing the labor of maintaining water flows and harvesting schedules. Children learn by playing in the shallows, their games teaching them to read the moss's health by color and texture, to sense when water runs too fast or slow, to smell the difference between healthy growth and the first hints of disease.

Knowledge passes through apprenticeships that last decades. A master gardener knows ten thousand subtle signs: the way certain moths predict optimal harvesting windows, how the angle of steam reveals mineral content, which bird calls mean rain within hours versus days. They read the moss itself like the plateau dwellers read stone, seeing in its patterns the history of water flows, the memory of harsh seasons, the promise of future yields.

The trade relationship with the plateau creates the spine of both communities. Caravans of sure-footed pack animals navigate the treacherous paths between, their arrivals timed to seasonal harvests and necessity. Down from the heights comes carved stone—tools that never rust, containers that preserve perfectly, architectural elements that anchor the moss buildings. Up from the gardens flow the hundred products of cultivation: medicines, fibers, foods, and most preciously, warmth itself in the form of heat-moss and concentrated spring waters.

The traders themselves often come from mixed bloodlines, equally comfortable in the thin air above and thick moisture below. They carry more than goods—they are the living connection, speaking both the clipped stone-tongue and the flowing garden dialect, understanding both the patience of carving and the urgency of harvest. Their children often choose one world or the other, but some remain perpetual wanderers, binding the two realms with their footsteps.

Ceremonies here follow the water's calendar rather than the stone's. The spring's first warming brings festivals of planting, where new varieties are introduced to carefully prepared terraces. The height of summer sees the Great Harvest, when all the clans work together to gather the year's primary crop, singing work songs that coordinate the complex choreography of collection. Autumn's cooling brings the Distillation, when the year's waters are processed and prepared for transport. Winter, mild as it is here, becomes a time of maintenance and planning, of repairing channels and preparing new terraces.

The nights bring their own magic. The bioluminescent moss creates a landscape of soft green light, punctuated by the blue-white gleam of fireflies and the occasional flare of flowering moss releasing clouds of glowing pollen. The gardeners often work by this living light, their movements creating trails of disturbance that fade slowly back to steadiness. From above, the terraces must look like a galaxy of green stars, each level its own constellation of cultivation.

Disease here takes different forms than in the sterile stone above. The constant moisture breeds fungal infections that must be carefully controlled, both in the moss and the gardeners themselves. They've developed a pharmacopeia of treatments, mostly derived from the very organisms that threaten them. The balance is delicate—too much antifungal treatment and the beneficial symbiotes die; too little and entire terraces can be lost to aggressive blights.

Their art manifests in living sculptures—moss trained over frameworks to create temporary forms that evolve through seasons. Master gardeners compete in slow-motion contests, shaping growth over years to create predetermined patterns. The most prized pieces incorporate multiple species, their different colors and textures creating painterly effects that shift as conditions change. These living artworks cannot be possessed in any traditional sense—they belong to their terrace, their watershed, their particular confluence of conditions.

The deep knowledge here is of cycles and connections. Where the plateau dwellers understand stone as eternal and unchanging, the gardeners know everything flows and transforms. They see the same water moving through moss, mist, and stone, carrying pieces of each realm to the other. They know that the minerals leached from the plateau's granite feed the gardens' abundance, just as the gardens' essences seep into groundwater that emerges in the plateau's hot springs, creating an endless circulation of gift and receipt.

This is a place of productive margins, where the meeting of hot and cold, wet and dry, stone and life creates possibilities found nowhere else. The gardeners have learned to read these edges like text, to cultivate not just moss but the conditions that allow moss to thrive. They are edge-dwellers themselves, culturally and physically adapted to the space between extremes, their work a constant negotiation between control and wildness, tradition and experimentation.

In the end, the moss gardens are more than cultivation—they are a meditation on the possible. Where the plateau represents endurance carved from nothing, the gardens show abundance coaxed from unlikely conjunctions. The two places need each other not just for trade but for completion, each holding what the other lacks, together forming a single system as complex and vital as the mountain itself.

The young people of both realms sometimes dream of the other—plateau children imagining the riot of green life below, garden youth wondering at the vast carved spaces above. A few each generation make the journey to apprentice in the opposite realm, returning changed, carrying new knowledge that enriches both communities. These border-crossers become the innovation points, suggesting new techniques that merge stone-sense with water-wisdom, finding applications neither culture would devise alone.

And so the mountain lives, not as simple stone but as a complete world. From the ice-touched peak where only the hardiest lichen cling, through the carved city where generations have hollowed comfort from cold rock, down to these terraces where heat and water conjure impossible abundance—each zone depends on the others, trades with the others, dreams of the others. The moss gardeners, tending their soft green charges in the mountain's middle heights, are as essential to this vertical civilization as the stone-carvers above or the distant valley farmers below, all of them together creating something greater than mere survival: a home woven from patience, knowledge, and the careful tending of whatever each altitude offers.
