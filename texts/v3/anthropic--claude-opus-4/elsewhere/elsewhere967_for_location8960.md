## The Sunken Paths

Three thousand feet below Keening Shelf, where the plateau's shed water collects in a thousand branching streams, lies a canyon system so deep that noon light barely touches its floor. The Sunken Paths wind between walls of stratified stone that record millions of years of geological memory—layers the Shelf has yet to become, compressed and carved by water that has run here since before the plateau began its slow emergence.

The air down here is thick with moisture and the green smell of constant growth. Unlike the struggling lichens above, the Paths burst with vegetation that has never known drought. Ferns unfurl from every crevice, some with fronds broad enough to shelter under. Vines cascade down cliff faces in living waterfalls, their roots threading through stone layers like sutures. Trees that would be dwarfed on the plateau grow here into giants, their canopies interlocking far overhead, creating a twilight world of filtered green light.

The water that seeps and drips through Keening Shelf arrives here transformed—filtered through limestone and clay, enriched with minerals, running warm from deep earth heat. It pools in natural basins carved from softer stone, creating a chain of connected pools that steam gently in the cooler air. The overflow runs along channels worn smooth as glass, splitting and rejoining in patterns that shift with the seasons but never fundamentally change.

This is the realm of the Thermites—not insects, but a people whose ancestors fled some forgotten catastrophe above ground generations ago, finding refuge in the warm constancy of the depths. Where the Foundling Folk above have grown pale and dense-boned, the Thermites have become something else entirely. Their skin has developed a peculiar translucent quality, showing the tracery of blood vessels beneath like leaf veins. They move with liquid grace through their vertical world, fingers and toes elongated for gripping wet stone, muscles evolved for climbing rather than walking.

They build nothing permanent, needing no shelter from rain that never falls directly here. Instead, they shape the living forest to their needs—training vines into ladder networks, encouraging certain ferns to grow in dense privacy screens, cultivating the bioluminescent flowers that bloom in the deepest shadows. Their tools are grown rather than made: gourds hardened into vessels, bark carefully harvested and shaped while still on the tree, thorns cultivated to surgical sharpness.

The Thermites know every pool in their domain, each with its own temperature, mineral content, and purpose. The Birthing Pools, kept precisely at blood heat, where their children spend their first months more aquatic than terrestrial. The Healing Springs, rich in sulfur and singing with microscopic life that cleanses wounds. The Memory Waters, so heavy with minerals that objects left in them slowly turn to stone—here they preserve their most precious belongings, transforming baskets and tools and the bones of their beloved dead into permanent records.

But it's the Discovery Pools they treasure most—those rare basins where the water from above arrives still carrying its news. The Thermites have developed a practice of reading these waters like the Foundling Folk read stone. They taste for new minerals that signal changes in the plateau's geology. They observe the tiny organisms that wash down—mites and spores and sometimes the eggs of creatures from above. Most remarkably, they've learned to interpret the sounds the water carries, conducting elaborate listening ceremonies where dozens of Thermites submerge their heads in unison, creating a living network of listeners who can triangulate the source of vibrations transmitted through the water table.

Through these practices, they know Keening Shelf more intimately than many who walk its surface. They sense when new springs break through, feel the reverberations of collapsing towers, track the movement of the boring worms through the water table's disruption. They know which of the Foundling Folk's wells connect to which pools, though the surface dwellers have no idea their water passes through such depths.

The relationship between above and below is complex, one-sided, rich with irony. The Foundling Folk's cast-offs become treasures here—bronze tools that tumble into crevices are retrieved and cherished, their metal precious in this woodlands world. Seeds that wash down from the struggling surface vegetation explode into growth in the rich canyon soil, creating gardens of plenty from plants that barely survive above. Even the waste water from Keening Shelf's constant construction, full of clay and crushed shell mortar, is welcomed here—the Thermites have learned to direct it into settling pools where they harvest the materials for their own uses.

Most poignant are the Failed Stones—rocks that break free from the emerging plateau and tumble into the depths. The Thermites treat these fallen pieces with something approaching reverence, reading in their fresh fractures the story of the ground above. The children collect the smaller pieces, learning to identify different stone types, while the adults study the larger boulders for clues about the plateau's structural changes. They maintain careful maps of where each stone falls, understanding that they're witnessing Keening Shelf's growing pains from below.

The two peoples have myths about each other, distorted by distance and time. The Foundling Folk tell stories of the Deep Dwellers—spirits or degenerates (depending on the teller) who gave up the hard beauty of stone and storm for the soft ease of eternal warmth. Parents frighten children with tales of being pulled down through the wells if they play too close to the edges. The bravest adolescents dare each other to shout messages into the depths, though none has ever reported an answer.

The Thermites, for their part, speak of the Sky Wanderers above—people so desperate for change that they build on shifting ground, so hungry for height that they'll suffer any hardship to stay near the clouds. They interpret the constant sound of construction as a kind of madness, a ceaseless dissatisfaction that drives the surface dwellers to build and rebuild without end. The water-readers share what they learn in hushed tones, painting a picture of a people forever struggling, forever adapting, forever refusing the peace the earth offers to those who descend.

Yet for all their separation, the two peoples are bound in an intimate dance of cause and effect. When the Foundling Folk divert a spring to supply a new neighborhood, the Thermites must adjust to the changed flow patterns below. When the Thermites block a channel to create a new bathing pool, the water backs up through cracks the surface dwellers didn't even know existed, creating unexpected springs that might undermine a tower's foundation.

Climate itself links them. The Shelf's elevation means cold winters, but the thermal mass of the Sunken Paths moderates the temperature of the water flowing down. In the harshest freezes, when ice threatens the plateau's water supply, it's the warm seepage from below that keeps some channels flowing. In the height of summer, when even the constant moisture of Keening Shelf can't prevent some drying, it's the vast reservoir of the Paths that maintains the flow, exhaled back up through the porous stone like the earth's own breath.

There are border spaces, liminal zones where the two worlds almost touch. Certain deep wells in Keening Shelf drop so far that the Foundling Folk who maintain them report strange echoes, glimpses of light that shouldn't exist in such depths, once even a flower—unknown to the surface—that bloomed impossibly on the wet stone near the water line. The Thermites know these places too, the high pools where the canyon walls narrow and the sound of construction drifts down like distant thunder. The boldest of their climbers sometimes ascend these channels, rising until the water grows cold and the pressure changes make their ears ache, stopping just short of emergence into that bright, unstable world above.

In the end, both peoples are shaped by water—the Foundling Folk by its absence and constant seeking, the Thermites by its abundance and steady gift. One fights the flow, building elaborate systems to capture and channel every drop. The other surrenders to it, letting it determine the rhythm of their days and the shape of their society. Neither could exist without the other, though they don't know it—the Shelf would parch without the reservoir below, the Paths would stagnate without the constant renewal from above.

Between them flows an endless conversation in the language of water—minerals dissolved above and precipitated below, temperatures exchanged, pressures equalized. The boring worms know both worlds, following the water table through territories neither people can fully map. The pale climbing beasts occasionally fall into the Sunken Paths, their bodies a feast for creatures that have never tasted meat from above, their hollow bones carved into flutes that play notes the canyon has never heard.

And sometimes, in the deepest pools where the water runs oldest and the light barely reaches, the Thermites find objects that shouldn't exist—stone tools of impossible antiquity, shaped by hands that knew neither the Shelf nor the Paths. These artifacts suggest other stories, other peoples who found different ways to live with the stone and water. The water-readers study them in silence, wondering if they're glimpsing a past when both peoples were one, or perhaps a future when the endless cycling of water and stone will unite them again.

For now, they remain apart—one people reaching always upward, reading the sky's moods in stone's slow emergence, the other delving deeper, finding in the earth's bowels a warmth the heights can never offer. But the water knows no such distinctions. It falls as rain on Keening Shelf, seeps through layers of time and meaning, warms itself on deep fires, and rises again as mist from the Sunken Paths, beginning its journey anew. In this endless circulation lies a truth both peoples sense but neither fully grasps—that they are not two societies but one, separated by altitude and attitude but joined by the very element they both depend upon, forever cycling between the desperate heights and the accepting depths, between the world being born and the one that has always been.
