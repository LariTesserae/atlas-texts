The Singing Wastes of Upper Karathene

Above, where the catastrophe began, the land remembers water in its absence.

Upper Karathene stretches across a plateau of white limestone, the same stone as the drowned city below but here bleached by sun and carved by wind into formations that could never exist underwater. The earth is riddled with sinkholes—some no wider than wells, others vast enough to swallow entire districts. These holes breathe. In the cool of morning they exhale mist that tastes of salt and depth. By noon they inhale, creating downdrafts strong enough to pull birds from the sky.

The largest of these chasms still show traces of what was—carved stairs spiraling down into darkness, partial walls emerging from the rim like broken teeth, fragments of the same geometric precision that marks the architecture below. But the surface city was mostly scraped away when the waters rushed down, leaving only these archaeological scars and the foundations of buildings whose purposes can only be guessed at.

What grows here defies conventional biology. The dominant vegetation consists of hollow-stemmed plants that anchor themselves around the breathing holes, their roots extending down toward moisture that might be hundreds of feet below. These tube-grasses stand rigid in the constant wind, and when the breeze passes through their hollow stems, they sing—each plant pitched according to its length and thickness, creating harmonies that shift with wind speed and direction. The sound carries for miles across the white stone wastes, a constant, mournful chord that rises and falls but never entirely fades.

Between the sinkholes, where the stone lies flat and unbroken, crystalline formations bloom. These are not plants but mineralogical phenomena—salt deposits drawn up from below by capillary action through microscopic cracks in the limestone. They grow in spiral patterns, forming delicate forests of white and pale pink crystal that shatter at a touch but regenerate within days. When the sun angles through them at dawn and dusk, they cast rainbow patterns across the stone, brief moments of impossible color in an otherwise monochrome landscape.

The Windwright Clans make their lives here, distant cousins of the Tidewrights below though neither group remembers their kinship.

Where the Tidewrights stretched long and pale, the Windwrights have compacted, their bodies dense with muscle, skin darkened and thickened against the constant sun. Their lungs have evolved extraordinary capacity—not just for breathing but for controlling breath with precision. A Windwright can exhale continuously for minutes, inhale in perfect silence, or hold their breath for periods that would kill an unmodified human. This adaptation serves their survival in the Singing Wastes, where the difference between life and death often lies in understanding and manipulating air currents.

They build nothing permanent. Instead, the Windwrights travel between the sinkholes in wheeled constructs of woven tube-grass and stretched hide—vehicles designed to catch and channel wind through resonating chambers. Each clan's wind-wagons produce distinct tones as they move, announcing their presence across the wastes in harmonies that identify not just the clan but the specific family, their mood, their intentions. Young Windwrights learn to read these wind-songs before they learn spoken language.

The clans follow the breathing patterns of the earth. They know that certain holes exhale water-mist at predictable times, and they position their catch-cloths to harvest moisture from these exhalations. They understand which chasms connect to deeper networks, where the downdrafts are strong enough to power their mills but not so violent as to drag the unwary down into darkness. Most crucially, they know the songs—how to position their wagons to amplify or dampen the tube-grass harmonies, how to create acoustic territories that mark their temporary camps, how to send messages across vast distances by playing the landscape like an instrument.

Their culture revolves around breath and sound. They speak in a tonal language where meaning changes with pitch and duration. Their music requires circular breathing techniques that let single notes sustain for impossible lengths. Their meditation practices involve synchronizing their breathing with the earth's—inhaling as the morning mist rises, exhaling as the afternoon drafts descend. The greatest among them, the Breath Holders, can enter states where their metabolism slows to near-death, allowing them to descend partway into the smaller sinkholes on rope and faith, exploring the transition zones where the dry upper world meets the flooded depths.

These explorations have revealed mysteries. In some of the deeper shafts, crude messages are carved—not in any language the Windwrights know, but in patterns that seem almost familiar. Sometimes they find tools made of materials that shouldn't exist here: polished stone that could only come from underwater working, bone carved with techniques the surface dwellers don't possess. Once, a Breath Holder claimed to have descended far enough to see water—actual water—and in it, a pale figure that looked up at her with eyes too large for any surface creature.

The Windwrights have their own mythology about these depths. They speak of the Swallowed City, a place that angered the spirits by trying to capture wind in stone walls. They tell stories of the Deep Singers, beings who were transformed by their greed for water into creatures that could never again breathe air. The moral is always the same: respect the wind, follow its patterns, never try to constrain what should be free.

But there are heretics among them—the Deep Listeners who position themselves at the larger sinkholes during the still hours of night, lowering resonating cords into the depths. They claim to hear impossible sounds: stone settling in ways stone shouldn't move, water flowing in patterns that suggest intention, and sometimes—very rarely—something that might be voices, speaking in clicks and echoes that almost resolve into meaning.

The two populations, above and below, remain unaware of their shared origin. The Tidewrights, if they think of the surface at all, imagine it as an airless void where nothing can survive. The Windwrights see the depths as the province of death and transformation. Yet they are bound together by more than history.

The breathing of the sinkholes is not a natural phenomenon but a consequence of the Tidewrights' patient engineering below. Their careful maintenance of air pockets, their cultivation of pressure differentials, their ritual movements that create currents—all of this drives the atmospheric exchange that the Windwrights have built their entire culture around. When the Ceremonial Weavers in the drowned temple perform their dances, the tube-grasses above shift their harmonies in response. When the Windwright clans gather for their seasonal convergences, playing the landscape in complex symphonies, the Tidewrights below pause in their work, feeling vibrations through the water that almost—almost—remind them of something they've forgotten.

Trade between them happens accidentally, mediated by the physics of their divided world. Objects dropped into the sinkholes—carved messages, tools, offerings to the Deep Singers—sometimes find their way to the Tidewrights, who interpret them as geological phenomena, minerals precipitated into strangely regular shapes. The Tidewrights' own castoffs—shed scales, outgrown tools, fragments of their luminescent writing—occasionally ride the updrafts to the surface, where the Windwrights treasure them as spirit-gifts, incorporating them into their most sacred wind-catchers.

Neither group remembers the Sundering, when the earth opened and swallowed half a city. To the Tidewrights, the world has always been water and stone and the careful spaces between. To the Windwrights, existence has always meant following the wind across white stone beneath an endless sky. But in their myths, in their movements, in the very adaptations of their bodies, they carry the memory of what they were: one people, divided by catastrophe, evolving apart but still connected by the breathing earth, the singing stones, and the patience of geological time.

The connection manifests in dreams. Young Tidewrights sometimes wake gasping, having dreamed of suffocating in infinite air. Windwright children cry out in their sleep, describing visions of breathing water, of moving through stone as easily as across it. The shamans of both peoples treasure these dreams, seeing in them prophecies whose meaning remains forever just out of reach.

And in the deepest sinkholes, where the tube-grass songs grow strange and the air tastes of salt, where messages carved in stone fade into patterns that hurt to perceive, where the breathing of the earth sounds almost like words—there, in those liminal spaces, sometimes a Windwright Breath Holder descending on faith and a Tidewright Thresher ascending through curiosity come close enough to sense each other's presence. Not to meet—the physics of their divided world prevents that. But to know, for just a moment, that they are not alone in their strangeness, that somewhere in the darkness another kind of human has learned another way to be.

They pull back, always, returning to their respective worlds with nothing to show for the encounter but a lingering certainty that the cosmos is stranger than their mythologies suggest. The Breath Holder speaks of sensing intention in the depths. The Thresher reports variations in the current that seemed too regular to be natural. Neither pushes further. The gap between their worlds is not just physical but ontological—they have become too different to truly perceive each other, adapted to environments so distinct that meaningful contact might be impossible.

Yet the connection persists. In the movements of air and water, in the songs of stone and grass, in the patient work of becoming something new, the sundered people of Karathene continue their parallel evolution. Above and below, they build their impossible cultures, each one a half-remembered echo of the other, together creating something that neither could achieve alone: a civilization that spans states of matter, a culture that exists in the space between elements, a human future that diverges and converges like the breathing of the earth itself.
