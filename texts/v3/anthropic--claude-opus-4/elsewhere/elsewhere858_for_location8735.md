The High Gardens of Ierevela crown the world's edge, where the limestone cliffs of the Ascent meet nothing but sky.

Here, fifteen hundred meters above the Thermal Sea, the air runs thin and electric. The plateau stretches for kilometers, but it is not flat—the limestone has been carved by centuries of cultivation into a maze of terraces, channels, and deep garden wells. Water flows everywhere, guided through aqueducts carved directly from the stone, splitting and rejoining in patterns that shift with the seasons. The sound never stops: water over stone, through stone, pooling and falling and rising again through systems both ancient and alive.

The gardens grow in layers. On the exposed terraces, low cushions of silver-leafed succulents grip the rock, their roots probing deep into cracks where moisture condenses. These plants bloom only in the high season, when the thermal storms rise from the sea below and wrap the cliffs in days of warm fog. Then they explode in carpets of electric blue flowers that pulse with their own faint bioluminescence, visible only in the hour before dawn.

Deeper in, where walls provide shelter, the gardens transform. Here grow the memory trees—gnarled specimens with bark like written text, their growth rings recording centuries of weather, warfare, and careful tending. Their leaves are prisms, splitting the harsh plateau light into rainbows that dance across the stone walls. Gardeners read these trees like libraries, finding in their wood the records of rains and droughts, of volcanic ash-falls and years of impossible plenty.

The garden wells plunge deep into the limestone, some natural sinkholes expanded by tools, others carved wholesale from virgin rock. Down their walls spiral walkways just wide enough for one person, slick with spray from the waterfalls that veil each well's interior. The temperature drops with each descending meter, and the plants change accordingly—from the heat-lovers at the rim to the shade-dwellers in the depths where eternal cool and damp create pockets of temperate paradise.

At the bottoms of the deepest wells, springs bubble up from the limestone aquifer. This water has traveled far—from the rain that falls on distant peaks, through kilometers of stone galleries, gathering minerals and memory before emerging here, impossibly pure and cold. The gardeners have learned to read the water too: its temperature tells of deep geological shifts, its mineral content speaks of the stone it has passed through, its flow rate predicts the rains to come.

The architecture serves the gardens, not the reverse. Buildings hide half-buried in the terrain, their roofs gardens themselves, their walls merely suggestions of separation between inside and outside. The great conservatory spans a natural gorge, its iron frame gone green with carefully cultivated patinas. Inside, tropical species from the sea-level jungles grow in air made humid by precisely controlled mistings, while outside the plateau winds scour everything not sheltered or sturdy enough to endure.

Bridges span between the wells—not straight paths but meandering routes that follow the natural contours, pausing at viewing platforms where the gardens reveal themselves in carefully composed vistas. Some bridges are stone, as old as the gardens themselves. Others are living wood, trained and woven from the aerial roots of the conductor trees, which grow in planned groves and whose hollow trunks carry water like vertical rivers.

The wind shapes everything here. It comes in patterns the gardeners know like liturgy: the dawn wind that carries salt spray up from the distant sea, the noon wind that desiccates anything not deep-rooted or sheltered, the evening wind that brings the smell of rain from the inland mountains. Plants grow in the lee of walls, in the shelter of stones, in any pocket where the wind's fury diminishes to a caress. The gardeners build windbreaks from woven branches that flex but never break, creating microclimates where delicate species can flourish meters away from scouring gusts.

Sound travels strangely in the gardens. The constant water-music underlies everything, but the winds and walls create acoustic spaces where whispers carry for hundreds of meters while shouts die instantly. The gardeners have learned to use this, positioning wind chimes and water features to create zones of sound—meditative corners where only the gentlest tinkle penetrates, dramatic overlooks where the full symphony of rushing water and singing stone can overwhelm.

At night, the gardens transform. The bioluminescent plants awaken in waves, their cold light attracting clouds of tiny insects that themselves glow faintly green. The memory trees' prismatic leaves catch starlight and scatter it in patterns that shift with each breeze. In the wells, phosphorescent fungi trace the water's path in lines of blue and gold. The gardeners work by this living light, tending their charges in the cool darkness when the sun's harsh attention relents.

The seasons here are subtle but absolute. The wet season brings the thermal storms, weeks when the plateau disappears into warm clouds that rise from the sea. Plants that have waited months burst into growth, and the gardeners work frantically to channel the abundance, to store water for the dry times, to ensure nothing grows so fast it damages the careful balances. The dry season reverses everything—plants retreat into dormancy, water becomes precious as silver, and the gardeners' work turns to preservation, to helping their charges endure until the storms return.

The Windkeepers tend their vertical world with tools bred rather than forged.

They are tall as the winds require, with bodies stretched by generations of cliff-living into forms that catch air like sails. Their bones are hollow but dense, bird-like in their efficiency. Skin stretches between elongated fingers and from wrist to hip, creating wings that are not for flying but for reading—each membrane sensitive to pressure, temperature, humidity, telling them stories the wind writes across their bodies.

They speak little with voices, much with movement. Watch a conversation between Windkeepers: they stand at angles to catch different currents, hands spread to feel the air's texture, bodies swaying in patterns that communicate as clearly as words. A sudden stretch upward—danger coming. A spiral turn—water in three days. A folding inward of all membranes—sorrow, loss, the death of something cherished.

Their eyes have evolved to see air itself. Not truly see, but perceive the tiny variations in density that reveal thermals, downdrafts, the invisible architecture of wind around stone. They navigate the gardens' vertical spaces like fish through coral, finding the current-paths that carry them from terrace to well-bottom with minimal effort. Their pupils contract to pinpoints in the plateau's blazing light but dilate to black pools in the well-depths, gathering every photon in spaces where noon feels like dusk.

They live in the between-places, where stone meets air. Their dwellings cling to cliff faces, accessible only by routes that require intimate knowledge of wind patterns. The structures seem impossibly fragile—frameworks of bent wood and woven fiber that flex with every gust. But they endure because they yield, because their builders understand that rigidity kills in a world of constant motion. Inside, the spaces are minimal but precisely arranged: sleeping hammocks that rock with the building's sway, work surfaces that fold away when not needed, tools hung where the reaching hand will find them.

The young learn by falling. Not carelessly, but in controlled drops from the lower terraces, their membranes spread to feel how air resists, supports, betrays. Elder Windkeepers watch from above, reading the children's forms, calling corrections in the sharp clicks and whistles that carry even over howling wind. By adolescence, a Windkeeper can drop a hundred meters down a cliff face and land soft as a settling leaf, having read every eddy and updraft on the way down.

Their cultivation tools are living things. They carry no metal implements but partner with modified organisms: boring worms that can channel water through solid limestone, their digestive acids carefully regulated by temperature; pruning beetles that eat only dead growth, their mandibles shaped by selective breeding to make cuts that heal clean. The Windkeepers know each organism's needs and gifts, maintaining colonies in the warm spaces beneath their dwellings where generations of beneficial creatures continue their ancient partnerships.

Water is their obsession and art. They map every spring, every seep, every place where condensation gathers on cool stone. In the dry season, they become misers, routing precious flows through increasingly complex networks to reach every plant that needs it while losing nothing to evaporation. They've bred plants that signal their thirst through color changes visible only to Windkeeper eyes, allowing intervention before damage occurs.

They process materials with patience. Fibers for their ropes come from the aerial roots of conductor trees, harvested only when the moon is new and the sap runs thick. The harvesting takes months—a few roots from each tree, cut at precise angles to encourage regrowth. The fibers must then be soaked in spring water mixed with enzymes from certain beetles, dried in specific wind conditions, spun when the humidity reaches exact levels. The resulting cordage is stronger than steel cable but alive enough to self-repair small breaks.

Their memories live in knots. Each Windkeeper carries cords where personal history is recorded in complex patterns—a language of loops and tensions that preserves decades of wind-knowledge. Masters can read a century's weather from a coil of worn rope, finding in its knots the record of every storm, every drought, every anomalous current. When a Windkeeper dies, their cords join the communal archives, woven into massive net-works that hang in the deep wells where temperature and humidity remain constant.

They trade with the depths through necessity and ritual. Once each season, delegations descend the Ascent, following paths carved into the cliff face over millennia. The journey takes days, camping on narrow ledges, moving only when wind conditions permit. They carry down the heights' treasures: distilled essences from the rarest summit plants, water from the purest springs, seeds that require stratification by ice and wind before they'll germinate.

At sea level, they meet the Thermal Sea's edge-dwellers—not the Curators, who never surface, but the communities that harvest the sea's upper reaches. The trading happens through intermediaries who understand both worlds, translating between the Windkeepers' movement-language and the water-peoples' chemical signals. The Windkeepers return with materials impossible to produce at altitude: deep-water algae that can fix nitrogen from air, pressure-resistant containers for storing volatile essences, minerals leached from the drowned city far below.

They never enter the water. The weight of it terrifies them, the loss of wind's constant touch like sensory amputation. But they understand its connection to their aerial world. The thermals that carry them rise from the warm sea. The storms that bring life to the gardens begin in the interaction between cold air and heated water. The very limestone they carve was born in ancient seas. They are not separate from the Drowned Works but its complement—the exhale to its inhale, the summit that defines the abyss.

In the High Gardens of Ierevela, the Windkeepers continue their ancient work. Reading air, routing water, tending the vertical gardens that lift life into the sky. Their movements are prayers to possibility, their cultivation an argument that anywhere life might exist, it should. They make paradise from stone and storm, patience and knowledge, creating beauty in the margin between earth and air.
