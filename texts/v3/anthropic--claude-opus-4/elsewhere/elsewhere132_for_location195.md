Above the Drain Works, where the city's foundations meet bedrock and the great collection pipes rise like arteries through concrete, the Seepage Districts spread in a twilight zone between the industrial depths and the inhabited surface. These are the places where the city's careful engineering breaks down, where moisture finds its own paths upward, creating a vertical marsh that was never meant to exist.

The buildings here are old—predating the Drain Works by decades—and their basements have become strange aquariums. Water weeps through walls that were waterproofed three times over, finding cracks that shouldn't exist, pooling in corners that defy the architect's intended drainage. The residents have long since given up on keeping it out. Instead, they've learned to live amphibiously, raising their belongings on stilts, installing catwalks between rooms, cultivating the peculiar gardens that thrive in perpetual damp.

The Sump Farmers tend these accidental grottos with the patience of those who've learned to read opportunity in infrastructure's failures. They harvest the pale mushrooms that fruit from waterlogged beams, gather the mineral deposits that crystallize where pipes sweat through insulation, cultivate the root vegetables that have adapted to grow in permanent flood. Their cellars connect through broken walls and improvised passages, creating a secondary city of humid chambers where the air tastes of earth and iron.

They know the Drain Works' schedules intimately—not from any official communication, but from the way the seepage patterns shift with the great engines' cycles. When the extraction pumps surge, their basement pools drop by inches. When maintenance crews adjust the pressure differentials below, new springs bubble up through their floors. They've learned to plant by these rhythms, to harvest before the high-water marks, to store provisions in the brief dry spells that come like irregular seasons.

The children here are different from those below—they know sunlight, but filtered through permanent fog that rises from the sweating streets. They attend surface schools but spend their afternoons diving in basement pools, holding their breath to explore the submerged passages that link building to building. They've mapped these flooded networks in crayon and waterproof marker, creating charts that would make the city engineers weep with frustration. They know which tunnels lead to the storm sewers (dangerous, fast-flowing) and which connect to older systems that predate any municipal record.

Some passages lead down to the Drain Works proper, though these intersections are carefully guarded secrets. The Sump Farmers trade with the Tenders—mushrooms and root vegetables for tools and spare parts, surface gossip for news from the depths. They meet in the border spaces, chambers that belong to neither world fully, where the industrial moisture collection systems blend with the organic seepage of compromised architecture. Here, children from above play with children from below, splashing in pools that exist in legal gray zones, neither permitted nor forbidden, simply overlooked.

The older residents speak of the days before the Drain Works, when the seepage was worse but somehow more honest. Now, they say, the water comes up wrong—too pure in some places, carrying strange minerals in others. They blame the extraction engines for disturbing something that should have been left alone, for creating pressure differentials that wake things better left sleeping. They point to the moss that now grows on their walls—the same phosphorescent variety found below, creeping steadily upward like a slow green tide.

The city authorities classify the Seepage Districts as "transitional zones," promising eventual renovation that never comes. The residents prefer it this way. They've developed their own economy, their own customs, their own understanding with the water that refuses to stay where it's put. They hold rain ceremonies during the dry months, not to bring water—water always comes—but to ensure it comes gently. They leave offerings at the places where the largest pipes breach their basements: flowers for the maintenance crews who pretend not to see the unauthorized taps, cookies for the children who swim the dangerous passages, letters addressed to the depths in waterproof containers that disappear into the flows.

At night, when the fog rises thickest from the warming streets, lights appear in the basement windows—not electric lights, but the soft glow of cultivated moss gardens, tended with the same care given to any beloved plant. The Sump Farmers sit in their raised kitchens, feet dangling above the daily floods, sharing meals seasoned with minerals filtered from their own walls. They listen to the building settling around them, distinguishing between normal structural sounds and the deeper groans that mean the Drain Works are expanding again, boring new tunnels that will inevitably create new failures, new seepage, new possibilities.

Their young adults face a choice the previous generation didn't have: to stay and tend the aquatic gardens, to descend and join the Tenders in their industrial cathedral, or to rise to the truly dry districts where water comes only from taps and rain falls without pooling in living rooms. Most choose to stay. They've developed a taste for the mineral-rich vegetables, for the constant humidity that makes their skin soft, for the way sound travels differently through water-laden air. They've learned that there's a particular beauty in the borderlands, in the places where one system fails and another begins, where adaptation becomes an art form and infrastructure develops its own unplanned ecology.

The wedding ceremonies here involve an exchange of water—some drawn from the deepest basement pools, some collected from the rain before it touches any surface, some brought up from the Drain Works in containers that sweat with cold. The couple drinks from all three sources, acknowledging their place between worlds, accepting the blessing of controlled failure, promising to flow like water through whatever cracks the city's systems might develop. The children born of these unions are said to never truly drown—they simply transition from breathing air to breathing water, though no one has tested this deeply enough to know for certain.

In the market squares of the Seepage Districts, where vendors sell their water-grown produce under permanent canopies that drip steadily onto the cobblestones, there's talk of change. The moss is growing faster, spreading to buildings that were previously dry. The water table is rising despite the Drain Works' constant extraction. The children report new passages opening, connections to spaces that appear on no map, flooded chambers containing architecture that doesn't match anything in the city records. 

But change here comes like water—slowly, finding its own level, seeping through cracks in expectation. The Sump Farmers adjust their planting schedules, raise their walkways another few inches, teach their children to swim before they walk. They know that the city's attempts to control water are like trying to hold fog in a net. Better to learn its moods, to move with its rhythms, to build a life in the spaces where control gives way to negotiation, where the boundary between success and failure becomes permeable as a basement wall, and just as fertile.
