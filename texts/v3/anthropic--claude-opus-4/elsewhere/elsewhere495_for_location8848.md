Across the basin, where the proto-stone gives way to something older and stranger, the landscape changes. Here, the rock has aged backward into a kind of ur-matter, neither fully solid nor truly liquid—a substance that remembers being molten but has forgotten how to flow. The builders call this region the Throat of Swallowing, though no one recalls who named it or why.

The journey between First Breath and the Throat takes three days of careful traverse. The connecting path follows a natural depression in the landscape where ancient oils once flowed freely, carving channels that have since half-healed into glossy scars. Travelers must time their passage with the breathing cycles, moving during the long exhalations when the air pressure drops and the proto-stone becomes marginally more stable. During the inhalations, the path buckles and warps, occasionally opening gaps that reveal depths of still-forming matter that glow with their own feverish heat.

Builders who make this journey wrap their copper hair tightly against their skulls and coat their skin with thick mineral paste that deadens vibration. The acoustic environment between the two places can drive an unprotected builder mad—here, the frequencies of First Breath meet and clash with the deeper, slower resonances of the Throat, creating interference patterns that scramble the inner ear and disturb the oil-sacs beneath the eyelids.

At the midpoint of the journey stands a marker: a massive bell-stone, three times the height of a builder, that has been carved and tuned by generations of travelers. Its surface bears thousands of palm prints where builders have pressed their hands to send messages between the settlements. The stone serves as a relay, picking up vibrations from First Breath and retransmitting them at frequencies that can survive the journey to the Throat. Return messages follow the same path, creating a constant dialogue of vibrations through the rock.

The Throat of Swallowing itself defies easy description. Where First Breath spreads horizontally across its basin, the Throat plunges vertically into the planet's interior. The settlement clings to the walls of a vast pit whose bottom has never been seen or sounded. The ur-matter here behaves like vertical quicksand, solid enough to support carefully designed structures but fluid enough to swallow anything too heavy or too still.

The builders of the Throat have evolved differently from their cousins in First Breath. Their bodies are longer, more sinuous, with additional joints in their limbs that allow them to spread their weight across the treacherous surfaces. Their copper hair grows in stiff, antenna-like spikes that they use as anchors, driving them into the ur-matter to secure themselves while they work. Their oil-sacs have enlarged and multiplied, creating bioluminescent stripes along their torsos that pulse in slow waves, providing light in the depths where neither sunlight nor phosphorescent oils reach.

The architecture here hangs rather than rises. Builders weave structures from crystallized oils—a technique unknown in First Breath. They lower themselves on braided copper cables to harvest oil from deeper in the Throat, where pressure and heat transform it into long, flexible strands. These strands are then woven into nest-like dwellings that hang from the more stable portions of the pit walls. The entire settlement sways gently with the planetary breathing, a vertical city that ripples like kelp in an invisible current.

The sound environment of the Throat is an inversion of First Breath's cacophony. Here, the depth swallows noise, creating a peculiar acoustic vacuum. Sounds don't echo—they disappear downward, pulled into the pit by some property of the ur-matter. Builders here communicate through their bioluminescent stripes, developing a complex visual language of pulses and patterns. When they must use sound, they speak directly into the walls, letting the ur-matter itself carry their words upward in slow, molasses-thick waves.

The Throat serves a crucial function for First Breath: it is the source of the oils that fuel their civilization. Deep-dwelling specialists, their bodies adapted to crushing pressure, descend on cables made from their own shed copper hair. They carry hollow stone vessels that they fill from oil springs that well up from unknown depths. These oils, transformed by pressure and the strange chemistry of the ur-matter, burn hotter and longer than the surface seeps. A single vessel can supply a district of First Breath for a full breathing cycle.

In exchange, First Breath sends down tools and worked goods that cannot be produced in the vertical environment of the Throat. The crystal-plants will not grow on the ur-matter, so all food must come from First Breath, processed and compressed into dense cakes that won't destabilize in the Throat's peculiar gravity. The bell-stones, too, must be quarried and shaped in the horizontal expanse of the basin, then carefully transported along the scar-path.

The two settlements depend on each other in ways that go beyond simple trade. First Breath cannot exist without the deep oils of the Throat; the Throat cannot survive without the crystal-plant sustenance from First Breath. But more than this, they serve as each other's memory and future. First Breath represents the world becoming solid, reaching upward, spreading outward. The Throat represents the world's unformed potential, its liquid history, the depths from which all complexity arose.

Young builders from both settlements sometimes exchange places for a year or more, learning the other way of being. Those from First Breath return with their spines elongated, their bodies bearing the subtle marks of vertical living. Those from the Throat come back with their copper hair softened, their luminescent stripes dimmed but their palms newly sensitized to vibration. These exchanges ensure that knowledge flows between the settlements like the oils and tools, preventing either from becoming too specialized to survive.

The relationship manifests physically in the landscape. Careful observers can trace old oil-flows from the Throat that surface in First Breath's breathing fissures—underground connections that link the two settlements through the planet's own circulatory system. During certain alignments of pressure and temperature, messages can be sent through these deep channels, vibrations that travel down through the ur-matter and up through the proto-stone, arriving days later like echoes from the planet's heart.

At the deepest accessible point of the Throat, where the bravest builders descend, there is a phenomenon they call the Pulse. Every few days, something deep in the pit contracts, sending a wave of pressure upward that makes the entire settlement rise and fall like a ship on a swell. When this happens, the oil flows reverse temporarily, and sometimes things rise from the depths—not quite objects, not quite creatures, but formations of compressed oil and ur-matter that hold their shape just long enough to be studied before dissolving.

These formations seem to carry information from deeper layers of the world's becoming. Their patterns mirror the growth of the crystal-plants, the resonance of the bell-stones, the spiral of the builders' copper hair. First Breath's philosophers study these formations, seeking to understand what they reveal about the world's intent. Is the planet itself trying to communicate? Are these messages from some deeper intelligence? Or merely the random spurts of a world still learning how to have a surface?

The connection between First Breath and the Throat of Swallowing is therefore more than geographic or economic. It is a relationship between the world's memory and its aspiration, between depth and surface, between the vertical plunge of history and the horizontal spread of possibility. The builders understand this in their bones—adapted as they are to their respective environments, they remain one people, shaped by the same unfinished world, breathing in rhythm with its great, slow pulse of becoming.
