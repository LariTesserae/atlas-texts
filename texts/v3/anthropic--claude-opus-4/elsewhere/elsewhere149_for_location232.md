Beyond the kelp cathedral's verdant reach, where the drowned city plunges into the abyssal trenches that were once valleys, lies the Bone Market—a place the Woven speak of in whispers and visit only when necessity demands.

Here, three hundred meters down, the water presses with crushing weight and the last traces of sunlight fail. No kelp grows at these depths; instead, the ruins of the financial district rest in eternal darkness, preserved by cold and pressure like specimens in black glass. The buildings still stand—their steel frames intact, their windows long since imploded—but they serve now as scaffolding for an altogether different architecture.

Whale falls.

When the great creatures die, the currents carry them here, guided by forces the Bone Merchants claim to understand. The massive corpses settle among the towers, and around them blooms a strange, temporary city within the city. Each whale becomes a marketplace that lasts exactly as long as the bones do—years, sometimes decades—before the last calcium dissolves into the patient water.

The Bone Merchants are not like the Woven. Where the cathedral dwellers have grown sleek and supple, these deep traders have become something angular and pale. They wrap themselves in the preserved skins of sixgill sharks, the only warmth in the crushing cold. Their eyes have grown enormous, pupils dilated permanently black. They've lost the ability to surface—their tissues are so saturated with pressure-dissolved gases that ascending would kill them. They breathe not air but water itself, having undergone the final transformation the Woven still resist.

Their leader—if such a word applies to their fluid hierarchies—is the Ossifier, a being so changed that determining their original gender has become impossible. They've been down here since before the drowning, they claim—a deep-sea researcher who chose transformation over evacuation when the waters rose. Their body is elongated, fingers stretched into sensing appendages, ribs visible through translucent skin that flickers with its own bioluminescence. They speak in harmonics that hurt human ears, frequencies that carry for miles through the deep water.

The Bone Market operates on principles the surface world would find alien. Currency here is time—not hours or days, but the precise moment of decay each object has reached. A whale rib in its third year of decomposition trades differently than one in its fifth. The Merchants read these timeframes with chemical precision, tasting the water around each bone to determine its value.

They trade in necessities the Woven cannot produce: metal tools forged in volcanic vents, medicines extracted from deep-sea bacteria, maps of the drowned world etched on plates of mother-of-pearl. But their strangest commodity is memory—preserved in the bones of the whales themselves.

The Ossifier claims the whales remember everything their species has witnessed: the world before the drowning, the great melt, the transformation of land to sea. These memories can be extracted, they say, by those who know the proper methods. They demonstrate with a small vertebra, running webbed fingers along its surface until it sings—a low vibration that seems to carry images: ice sheets collapsing, cities flooding, the last forests vanishing beneath the waves.

The Woven who come here must prepare carefully. They hyperventilate in the air pockets, loading their blood with oxygen. They wrap themselves in layers of woven kelp for warmth, though it's never enough. They carry trade goods in sealed bladders—dried kelp that the deep dwellers crave, having no plants of their own; octopus ink that glows differently in the pressure; small treasures from the sunlit world above.

The journey down is harrowing. Past the twilight zone where the last familiar fish swim, through the midnight waters where bioluminescence provides the only light, down to where the pressure makes their bones ache and their lungs compress to painful knots. They follow guide ropes made from whale sinew, laid by the Bone Merchants between the two settlements—a single thread through the darkness.

At the market itself, trade happens in ritual silence. The Bone Merchants' speech is too low for shallow lungs to produce, and the Woven's hand-signs are invisible in the darkness. Instead, they communicate through touch—finger-taps on palms, objects pressed into hands, the careful arrangement of trade goods that speaks its own language.

The Bone Merchants fear only one thing: the coming of a new whale. When the deep currents shift and bring the scent of recent death, the entire market mobilizes. They swim out in formation, bodies glowing like a constellation of strange stars, to guide the carcass home. The arrival is celebrated with ceremonies the Woven are not permitted to witness—songs in the deep frequencies, dances that stir the sediment from the city floor, feasts on flesh that would poison surface dwellers.

As payment for their goods, the Woven sometimes carry messages between the deep settlements. The Bone Market is not alone down there. Further still, in the hadal depths where the city's foundations meet the original sea floor, other communities exist—the Thermal Gardens where beings cluster around volcanic vents, the Phosphor Farms where bioluminescent creatures are cultivated in absolute darkness. Each has adapted differently, become something unique, until the human form is just a distant ancestor of what they are now.

But it's the relationship between the Bone Market and the Kelp Cathedral that defines both places. They are opposites in every way—light and darkness, growth and decay, air and water, yet they need each other. The Woven bring the sun-touched world down; the Bone Merchants send the deep world's treasures up. Between them flows a current of trade and transformation, two ends of the same drowned world, each becoming more itself with every passing year.

When young Reed makes her first journey to the Bone Market at fifteen, she returns changed. Not physically—though the pressure marks take weeks to fade—but in understanding. She's seen the far end of what they might become, the final transformation that awaits if the waters continue to rise. She tells her mother Mira about the Ossifier's prophecy: that one day the distinction between human and sea creature will become meaningless, that they will all be children of the deep, reading memories in bones and breathing water as naturally as their ancestors breathed air.

Mira holds her daughter close in the air pocket, both of them dripping from their ascent, and doesn't say what she's thinking: that perhaps this isn't a prophecy but a promise, that perhaps the drowning was not an ending but a beginning, that perhaps the Bone Merchants in their darkness and the Woven in their twilight are simply different chapters of the same story—the tale of how humans became something else, something the rising seas demanded, something strange and terrible and utterly, perfectly adapted to the world they couldn't prevent but learned to inhabit.

The cathedral kelp sways above them, and far below, the bones sing their deep frequencies, and between these two poles, life continues its patient transformation, measured not in human generations but in the slow time of the sea itself.
