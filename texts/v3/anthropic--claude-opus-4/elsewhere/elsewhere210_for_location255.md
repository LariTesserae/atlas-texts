Beyond the furthest reaches of the Drowned Workshops, where even the Inherited rarely venture, the seafloor rises into the Thermal Gardens—a chain of volcanic vents that pierce the abyssal darkness like stars punching through night.

Here, the water shimmers with heat. Not the gentle warmth of shallows, but savage temperature gradients that can strip flesh from bone in the space between one current and the next. The vents themselves are architectures of mineral accumulation—chimneys of sulfur and metal oxides that grow upward at speeds visible to patient observation, some reaching forty meters before they topple under their own weight and begin again.

Life here moves at a different tempo than in the Workshops. Where the kelp forests grow with geological patience, the creatures of the Thermal Gardens live and die in cycles measured in weeks or months. Tube worms cluster around the vent mouths like bouquets of blood and snow—crimson plumes filtering nutrients from the chemical-rich water, white stalks swaying in the convection currents. They can grow two meters in a year, spawn, and die, their carbonate tubes becoming foundation for the next generation.

Between the major vents, in zones where the water holds warmth but not death, gardens of a different kind flourish. Bacterial mats paint the seafloor in oranges and yellows that glow faintly with their own bioluminescence—the only natural light in this depth besides the occasional blue flash of hunting creatures. These mats ripple and flow like living carpets, advancing and retreating with the shifting temperatures, sometimes covering entire fields overnight before withering back to nothing as conditions change.

The Gardeners tend these spaces. Unlike the Inherited, they are not patient. They cannot afford to be. Their forms are built for speed and precision—bodies like living glass, transparent and filled with organs that pulse visibly with accelerated metabolism. They have true eyes, enormous and black, adapted to catch the faintest glow from the bacterial mats and the occasional flare of superheated water. Their limbs end in appendages like surgical instruments, capable of manipulating individual tube worms, of harvesting bacterial mats without destroying the colonies, of sensing temperature gradients down to fractions of degrees.

The Gardeners work in shifts that correspond to the vents' cycles. They know—through generations of observation encoded in their rapid-fire chemical communications—that each vent has its own rhythm. Some pulse with tidal forces far above. Others respond to deeper pressures, to movements in the Earth's mantle that won't fully express themselves for centuries. The Gardeners map these rhythms, predict them, dance around them in choreographies of survival.

They build structures, but not of stone. Using secretions from their own bodies mixed with minerals filtered from the vent water, they create delicate frameworks that channel and moderate the heat. These constructions look like abstract sculptures—spiraling lattices that split savage currents into manageable streams, creating microclimates where specific organisms can thrive. The frameworks rarely last more than a season before the vents' chemistry changes and they dissolve, but the Gardeners simply build anew, each construction informed by the last.

Their communication is light itself. Unlike the Inherited's patient chemistry, the Gardeners speak in rapid flashes of bioluminescence, conversations that strobe across their transparent bodies in patterns too quick for any but their own kind to follow. Arguments, warnings, instructions, and what might be humor all flicker between them in conferences of light that can involve dozens of individuals, their bodies forming living constellations in the dark water.

The connection between the Thermal Gardens and the Drowned Workshops is not simple proximity but a river of consequence that flows in both directions.

From the Workshops comes the cold current, dense with the particular minerals of slowly dissolving stone and ancient kelp decay. This current slides along the seafloor, pulled by its own weight and the rotation of the Earth far above. When it meets the heat of the vents, it rises, creating a circulation cell that has persisted for millennia. This is the Gradient River—not water in the conventional sense but a consistent movement of temperatures and chemistries that connects the two realms.

The Inherited know of the Gradient River. They can taste the heat-minerals in certain currents, alien flavors that speak of processes too violent for their quiet world. Once in a great while—perhaps every few centuries—an Inherited will follow these traces upstream, moving against the cold current with patient persistence. The journey takes years. Most who attempt it die in the transition zones, their cartilaginous bodies unable to cope with the temperature shifts. But a few survive, arriving in the Thermal Gardens like visitors from another geological epoch.

The Gardeners, for their part, know of the cold realm downstream. Their ancestors speak of it in flashing warnings—the place where time stops, where the water thickens with age, where structures of stone stand like monuments to patience their kind cannot fathom. Young Gardeners sometimes dare each other to ride the cold current down, to see how far they can go before the chill begins to slow their metabolism to dangerous levels. Few go far. The darkness beyond their bioluminescent world oppresses them, the absence of warmth like a weight pressing in from all sides.

But exchange happens nonetheless.

Spores from the kelp forests, dislodged by the Inherited's cultivation, drift up the Gradient River. Most die in the heat, but some few find purchase in the transition zones, evolving rapidly in the mutagenic environment near the vents. These heat-kelps grow like fever dreams—wild, branching forms that live for days or weeks before burning out, but in their brief existence they create nurseries for other organisms, bridging gaps between the two worlds.

From the Gardens, minerals flow downstream. The exotic compounds created in the vents' furnaces—sulfides and rare metals that exist nowhere else in the ocean—settle out in the cold water and drift into the Workshops. The Inherited have learned to read these mineral signals like seasonal changes. A sudden influx of copper compounds might mean a new vent has opened. The absence of sulfur that usually arrives in spring-tide cycles could indicate a major collapse in the Garden's chimney systems.

More rarely, but most significantly, creatures make the full journey.

Every few decades, a Gardener modified by chance or desperation survives the descent into the Workshops. These visitors bring chaos to the Inherited's ordered world. They move too fast, communicate in flashes of light that hurt the pressure-sensitive skins, try to build their dissolved frameworks in spaces meant for permanence. Most die within months, their hyperactive metabolisms consuming them in the cold. But before they fade, they often attempt what the Gardeners call "cold gardens"—trying to cultivate the workshop kelp with their rapid techniques, creating baroque arrangements that the Inherited regard with something between horror and fascination.

The successful journeys in the other direction are even rarer but more lasting. An Inherited who survives the ascent to the Gardens undergoes profound changes. The heat accelerates their chemistry, awakens processes that have slept for generations. They begin to perceive time differently, to move with something approaching urgency. They cannot tend the rapid gardens—their movements are still too slow, their senses not adapted to the light. But they find roles as memory-keepers, their chemical communications bridging the gap between the Gardeners' flashing conversations and concepts that require patience to express.

These exchanges have created something new in the transition zones: hybrid cultures that belong fully to neither world.

In the regions where cold and hot water mingle, where the last workshops peter out and the first vents begin, communities have formed that would be recognized by neither the Inherited nor the Gardeners as their own kind. These transitional beings cultivate warm kelp that grows at medium speeds, build frameworks of stone reinforced with vent minerals, communicate in slow flashes of chemical light that take minutes to compose and interpret.

They are the translators, the bridge-builders, the ones who ensure that knowledge flows both ways along the Gradient River. From them, the Inherited have learned of heat and time's acceleration. From them, the Gardeners have glimpsed patience and the deep strength of stone. Neither lesson is comfortable, but both are necessary, for the ocean is changing.

The water grows warmer, degree by imperceptible degree. The vents multiply, their activity increasing with shifts in the deep earth. The Workshops settle faster, as if the heat below is softening their foundations. Both worlds feel the change in their different ways—the Inherited tasting new minerals in their samples, the Gardeners seeing new patterns in the vent cycles.

And so the Gradient River carries more than water now. It carries adaptation, possibility, the tentative hope that between the eternal patience of the Workshops and the brilliant urgency of the Gardens, some synthesis might emerge. The transitional communities grow, spreading along the boundaries, creating new ways of being that honor both stone's memory and fire's hunger.

In the deepest currents of the Gradient River, where pressures and temperatures achieve perfect balance, something new stirs. Not Inherited, not Gardener, but something that partakes of both—creatures of the gradient itself, at home in neither world but necessary to the continuation of both. They are still few, still learning what they are, but in their movement between realms they carry the future of this deep place, whatever form that future might take.

The ocean remembers everything, and in the space between the Drowned Workshops and the Thermal Gardens, it is beginning to remember forward.
