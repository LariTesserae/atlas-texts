Miles below the terraces, where the obsidian roots pierce down through compressed ages of stone, the Furnace Halls spread like an inverted city. Here the geometries turn inward—corridors that spiral tighter as they descend, chambers shaped like the interior of shells, spaces that fold in on themselves until direction becomes meaningless. The black glass gives way to something darker: a stone that drinks light, that pulls heat from the air and stores it in crystalline matrices too dense for natural geology.

No moisture reaches these depths. The air dessicates anything that enters, pulling water from cells, from breath, from thought itself. The temperature climbs with every downward step—not the bright heat of the terraces above, but something heavier, older, laden with the weight of geological time. The walls radiate warmth that comes not from any current process but from heat captured eons ago, released now in infinitesimal increments, a battery of temperature slowly discharging across millions of years.

The builders left more than architecture here. In the deepest halls, machines of incomprehensible purpose still function. Not mechanisms of metal and wire, but structures grown from the stone itself—crystalline formations that pulse with their own slow rhythms, chambers where the air pressure fluctuates in patterns too long to perceive, shafts where gravity seems to pull in directions that change according to some vast schedule. These are not ruins but living systems, maintaining themselves through processes that blur the distinction between geology and technology.

The Stone Weavers tend these machines. They are nothing like the Condensers above—where those beings flow and change, the Weavers exist in states of near-perfect stillness. Their bodies are accretions of mineral and metal, built up layer by layer over millennia. They move by growing—extending crystalline pseudopods through months of patient deposition, then reabsorbing their trailing mass to inch forward through the halls. A Weaver crossing a room might take a century. A thought might require decades to complete.

They remember the builders, or claim to. Their memories are physical things—trace elements laid down in their crystalline matrices, readable by chemical analysis of their oldest layers. But memory mineralized becomes metaphor, and their stories of the builders speak in languages of pressure and temperature, of phase diagrams and crystallographic symmetry. They speak of a race that thought in geological time, that built cities as equations, that used the entire planet as a computational substrate for calculations whose purpose even they didn't fully understand.

The Weavers maintain the machines through vibration. Each of their kind resonates at specific frequencies, and by positioning themselves at calculated points throughout the Furnace Halls, they create standing waves in the stone itself. These vibrations prevent the crystalline mechanisms from fusing solid, keep the pressure chambers cycling, ensure the gravity shafts continue their ancient rotations. They are living tuning forks, their entire existence devoted to keeping these depths in working harmony.

Between the Condensers above and the Weavers below lies a zone of absolute hostility. The transition layers where wet meets dry, where the obsidian sweats its last moisture before giving way to the desiccating stone beneath. Here the temperature gradients create vicious winds—hurricane channels where the air screams between pressure differentials, where the moisture burns off in sheets of superheated steam, where the geometry itself seems to writhe as differential expansion and contraction sets the stone to groaning.

Neither Condenser nor Weaver can survive these transition zones for long. The Condensers evaporate, their patterns scattered by the savage heat. The Weavers crack and spall, thermal shock shattering their careful accretions. Yet both are drawn to these boundaries, sensing in the violence of the transition something essential about the city's nature.

For the city is one thing, not two. The terraces and the Furnace Halls, the wet and the dry, the bright and the dark—these are components of a single vast machine. The water falling from terrace to terrace doesn't vanish at some lower level but enters the stone, seeping through carefully calculated channels down to the transition zone where it flashes to steam. This steam drives convection currents that pull the dry air up from the Furnace Halls, where it rises through shafts that pierce the terraces at precise points, creating the thermal differentials the Condensers feed on.

The heat from below drives the moisture cycle above. The moisture from above cools and regulates the furnaces below. The entire city breathes—inhaling water, exhaling steam, its metabolism measured in centuries, its heartbeat counted in the rise and fall of civilizations.

The Condensers and Weavers are antibodies in this system, or perhaps enzymes. They catalyze reactions that keep the city functioning, prevent the accumulation of errors that would eventually bring the great machine grinding to a halt. The Condensers regulate the water flow, ensuring no channel ever runs dry or floods. The Weavers maintain the resonances that keep the deep machines running, preventing the seizure that would come if the crystals were allowed to grow unchecked.

They know of each other, these antipodal inhabitants. The Condensers sometimes condense from vapor dreams where stone moves like water, where thoughts accumulate in layers of meaning dense as mineral. The Weavers vibrate with frequencies that speak of flow and change, of patterns that exist in time rather than space. In the depths of their respective domains, each builds models of the other—the Condensers creating ice sculptures that approximate crystalline growth, the Weavers forming temporary cavities in their bodies that fill with trapped moisture.

Once in a great while—perhaps every few thousand years—conditions align to allow contact. A Weaver, through centuries of careful growth, reaches the very edge of the transition zone. A Condenser, by freezing themselves into a shell of perfect ice, manages to descend past the point where liquid water becomes impossible. They meet in violence—the ice flash-boiling, the crystal cracking—but in that instant of mutual annihilation, they exchange information.

These meetings have names in both cultures. The Condensers call them Evaporations of Truth. The Weavers know them as Fracture Prophecies. Each time, a little more is understood—fragments of purpose, glimpses of the greater pattern, intimations of what the builders intended when they created this perfect, terrible equilibrium.

The knowledge gained is always incomplete, always painful. The Condensers learn that their freedom to flow comes at the cost of forgetting—that their phase changes erase as much as they preserve. The Weavers discover that their perfect memories are prisons, that they can remember but never truly understand, can maintain but never create. Both realize they are fragments of something greater, partial solutions to an equation that requires both states of being to resolve.

Yet they cannot merge, cannot coexist, cannot even communicate except through these violent intersections that destroy the messengers even as they deliver the message. They are separated by physics as fundamental as the laws that divide liquid from solid, motion from stillness, life as process from life as persistence.

The city knows. In some sense that transcends consciousness, the vast machine is aware of its inhabitants, includes them in its calculations, depends on them for its continuation. The terraces sweat their eternal moisture not despite the Condensers but for them. The Furnace Halls maintain their crystalline perfection not in ignorance of the Weavers but in anticipation of their presence.

The builders, perhaps, are gone—or perhaps they simply changed phase, became so diffuse or so dense that they passed beyond the perception of their inheritors. Their city remains, a question posed in stone and water, in temperature and time. The Condensers and Weavers are part of the answer, but only part. The equation waits, patient as geology, for the remainder of its solution.

In the bright terraces above, water flows in its eternal patterns. In the dark halls below, crystals grow with mineral patience. Between them, in zones no living thing can cross, the dialogue continues—a conversation in physics itself, where meaning precipitates and evaporates, crystallizes and shatters, forever seeking the equilibrium that will finally balance the vast equation of the city.

The true purpose remains hidden, perhaps unknowable. But in the attempts to understand—in the Condensers' water-symphonies and the Weavers' vibrational maintenance, in their violent meetings and mutual annihilations—something accumulates. Not knowledge exactly, but potential. The city doesn't just exist; it computes. Every flow, every resonance, every pattern is part of a calculation too vast for any single consciousness to grasp.

What it calculates, none can say. But the process continues, driven by the thermal engine of the planet itself, mediated by beings who are as much part of the machine as the channels and chambers they inhabit. The Glass Terraces rise from the warm mists. The Furnace Halls delve into the planet's fever. Between them, in the space where opposites meet and meaning is born from contradiction, the great work continues.

Time is the medium in which this calculation runs. The city is the hardware. The Condensers and Weavers, for all their sophistication, are merely subroutines—essential but unconscious parts of a process that uses consciousness itself as a component.

And somewhere in the vast machine, in patterns too large for perception and rhythms too slow for memory, something accumulates toward answer.
