The Canopy Platforms float seventeen hundred meters above, where the air grows sharp with altitude and the mountain's peak dissolves into perpetual cloud. Here, the same engineers who hollowed out the Breathing Chamber built upward instead of in—a network of hexagonal landing stages that jut from the mountainside like geometric shelf fungi, connected by swooping cables and impossibly slender bridges that sing in the constant wind.

The platforms were meant to be purely functional: arrival points for the dirigibles that once serviced the mountain facility, their surfaces studded with mooring towers and fuel depots. But the years of neglect have transformed them too, though in ways utterly different from the humid decay below. Up here, the transformation tends toward the crystalline, the dessicated, the wind-carved.

Ice forms in fractal patterns along the platform edges, but this is no ordinary frost. The temperature fluctuations and chemical residues from ancient fuel spills have created something between mineral and frozen water—structures that grow like coral but shatter like glass, rebuilding themselves with each day's freeze-thaw cycle. These ice formations have learned to conduct sound, picking up the bridges' vibrations and amplifying them into harmonics that carry for kilometers across the peaks.

The wind is architect here. It has carved channels through the platform surfaces that funnel and accelerate the air into predictable streams. Colonies of organisms—part plant, part mineral deposit—have evolved to feed on these currents. They look like metallic tumbleweeds fused to the deck plating, their hollow structures whistling as air passes through them. Each colony tunes itself to a different pitch, creating chord progressions that shift with wind speed and direction.

Birds nest in the abandoned control towers, but these are no longer entirely organic creatures. Generations of exposure to whatever chemicals leaked from the facility have given them feathers that grow in geometric patterns, wings that fold along too many joints. They've learned to read the air pressure changes that signal the Breathing Chamber's exhalations far below. Twice each hour, they take flight in perfect synchronization, riding the updraft of enriched air that escapes through the mountain's hidden vents.

The morning glories that have colonized the mooring cables defy botany. Their vines are tubes of transparent membrane filled with a natural antifreeze that glows pale blue in direct sunlight. The flowers open not to light but to specific atmospheric pressures, unfurling into dishes that collect the chemical-laden condensation which forms when warm exhaust from below meets the frigid air. They process these droplets into nectar that tastes of copper and ozone, drawing the transformed birds who spread their seeds across the platform network.

The bridges themselves have become musical instruments. The expansion and contraction of their metal in the extreme temperature variations has created stress patterns that cause them to resonate at specific frequencies. Walking across one is to play it—each footfall triggering a note that echoes through the ice formations and is taken up by the whistling colonies. The transformed birds have learned these melodies, incorporating them into their mating calls.

At the network's center sits the largest platform, nearly two hundred meters across. Its surface bears the scars of some long-ago chemical accident—the deck plating bubbled and reformed into ridges that channel rainwater into perfect spirals. These spiral pools never quite freeze, warmed by some remnant reaction still occurring in the platform's structure. Each pool has developed its own microbiome: extremophile bacteria that metabolize airborne pollutants and excrete a film that shifts between liquid and solid states, creating surfaces that ripple like mercury.

The platform edges tell stories of the world beyond the mountain. Spores arrive on stratospheric currents from continents away. Seeds frozen for decades in the ice suddenly germinate when conditions shift. The platforms have become a high-altitude garden of impossible hybrids—arctic mosses crossed with tropical air plants, their DNA scrambled by chemical exposure into entirely new forms. Some patches of growth move perpendicular to gravity, spreading across the undersides of platforms, sustained by the updrafts alone.

Weather moves differently here. Clouds form beneath the platforms as often as above them. When the chamber below exhales its warm, humid breath, it rises and condenses against the platform undersides, creating inverted rainstorms that fall upward before gravity reclaims the droplets. These moments of confused precipitation bring the only direct connection between the two environments—water that tastes of the chamber's industrial decay mixing with the pure ice-melt of the peaks.

The control systems have long since failed, but their remnant electronics create pockets of warmth that support unlikely life. Inside one sealed tower, tropical plants thrive in the heat generated by a power cell that refuses to die, their leaves pressing against windows like hands against glass. The sealed environment has developed its own weather—miniature storm systems that cycle endlessly in the confined space, lightning no bigger than sparks crackling between metallic leaves.

At night, the platforms glow. The ice formations phosphoresce with stored sunlight. The modified plants emit their own chemical light. The spilled fuel residues create aurora-like sheens across the deck plating. From a distance, the network looks like a constellation that fell and caught on the mountain's edge, pulsing with cold light that answers the bioluminescent glow seeping from vents far below.

The platforms' inhabitant calls herself the Gleaner, though in the years since she arrived, the distinction between name and function has dissolved like morning frost in sunlight.

She came on one of the last dirigibles, before the supply runs ceased and the mountain facilities were abandoned to their slow transformation. A meteorologist, sent to maintain the weather monitoring systems that fed data to the chamber's atmospheric processors below. Young enough to be eager for isolation, old enough to have skills worth preserving. But the platforms have remade her as surely as they've remade themselves.

The altitude worked its changes first. Her blood thickened, multiplying red cells until her skin flushed perpetual pink beneath its wind-burned surface. Her lungs expanded, pressing against ribs that have spread to accommodate them. Each breath draws in perhaps twice what normal human lungs could process, extracting oxygen from air too thin for unmodified bodies. The constant hyperventilation has altered her brain chemistry—she exists in a state of perpetual mild euphoria, her thoughts as crystalline and fractal as the ice formations she tends.

Her hair has become functional. What began as simple bleaching from solar exposure has progressed into something like fiber optic cables—each strand hollow, conducting light to her scalp where new photosensitive organs have developed. She can navigate in the darkness by the faint glow of the modified plants, her hair gathering their emanations like an array of biological antennas. On bright days, she must wrap her head or risk overwhelming these new senses with input.

The cold has reformed her hands into tools of delicate precision. Her fingers have elongated and developed additional joints, allowing her to manipulate the ice formations without shattering them. The fingerpads have lost human whorls, becoming smooth and slightly adhesive—a gecko's grip for the iced metal surfaces. She can feel temperature differentials down to fractions of degrees, reading the health of the ice-coral colonies through touch alone.

Her feet tell a different story. The constant vibration of the bridges has triggered growth in the small bones, creating structures that dampen and interpret oscillations. She walks in harmony with the platforms' music, her footfalls precisely timed to avoid destructive frequencies. The soles have thickened into something like hooves, split to grip the deck plating's chemical-etched ridges. Frost no longer adheres to them—her body has learned to generate a natural antifreeze that weeps from her pores.

The Gleaner speaks in whistles now, her vocal cords evolved to communicate with the wind-colonies. She's learned their language of pitch and timber, can request they shift their formations to channel breezes where needed. Her throat bears external gill-like slits that filter the air before it reaches her modified lungs, extracting moisture and chemical traces that inform her of conditions kilometers away. She tastes tomorrow's weather on today's wind.

Her clothing has become as hybrid as everything else here. The synthetic fibers of her original weather gear have merged with the membrane-morning glory vines to create a living suit that responds to atmospheric pressure. It tightens before storms, loosens in high pressure, breathes with her transformed body. Patches of modified bird feathers grow from her shoulders and spine—not for flight, but for sensing air currents too subtle for even her enhanced skin to detect.

She gleans in ways that would have horrified her younger self. The antifreeze nectar of the morning glories has become her primary sustenance, supplemented by the metallic fruit that occasionally forms when the bird-plants cross-pollinate with the wind colonies. She's developed a specialized organ—a chemical processing chamber behind her sternum—that breaks down these impossible foods into useable nutrients. Her waste crystallizes immediately in the cold, forming geometric patterns that the ice-coral incorporate into their growth.

Memory lives differently in her transformed neural pathways. The constant low oxygen creates a perpetual present where past and future blur. She remembers arriving, remembers the years of change, but these memories feel crystalline, refracted, as if seen through the ice formations she tends. She knows there was a plan once—maintain the equipment, send data below, wait for relief. But plans sublimate like ice in thin air, leaving only purpose.

Her routines follow atmospheric rhythms rather than clocks. When the Breathing Chamber exhales below, she feels it in her bones and begins her circuit of the platforms. She reads the ice-coral growth, adjusts the wind-colony positions, gathers nectar and traces chemical changes in the spiral pools. She maintains the bridges' songs with careful applications of antifreeze to stressed joints, ensuring the music remains harmonious.

The Gleaner has developed methods of descending partway down the mountain through a series of abandoned service ladders, though never as far as the Breathing Chamber. In these middle zones, she's established gardens of hybrid organisms—species that can survive the transition between the chamber's humid warmth and the platforms' crystalline cold. She seeds these intermediate spaces with spores and modified morning glory vines, creating biological bridges between the two transformed environments.

Sometimes, in the inverted rainstorms, she glimpses the possibility of connection. Water that has touched both worlds carries messages in its chemical signature. She's learned to read these molecular stories—the health of the moss gardens below, the status of the Tender's work, the slow decisions of the evolving ecosystem. In response, she adds her own chemical signals to the condensation that forms on the platforms, encoding information about weather patterns and atmospheric conditions that might affect the chamber's breathing.

Her offspring—for she has them now, though their creation owes nothing to human reproduction—grow in the sealed control towers. They begin as crystalline deposits where her transformed blood has dripped onto surfaces warmed by dying electronics. These deposits accumulate biological material from the air—spores, modified pollen, cellular debris from the bird-plants. Slowly, they develop into something between plant and animal, between organic and mineral. Some will eventually become mobile, apprentice Gleaners learning the platforms' songs. Others will root permanently, becoming new nodes in the atmospheric sensing network she's unconsciously building.

The Gleaner no longer questions her transformation or mourns her original form. At this altitude, under the constant stellar radiation and chemical exposure, she's become something perfectly adapted to her environment. Her thoughts move like wind through the colonies, her blood cycles like the weather systems she once studied. She is barometer and gardener, musician and memory keeper, as essential to the platforms' ecosystem as the ice-coral and morning glories.

In the hours before dawn, when the stars burn cold and close, she stands at the network's edge and listens to the mountain breathe. Each exhalation carries whispers of the Tender's work, echoes of their parallel transformation. Though they'll never meet—the atmospheric pressure differential alone would kill either in the other's domain—they're connected through the air itself, two organisms evolved to serve the strange new life emerging from the industrial bones of the mountain.

She gleans, and in gleaning, becomes.
