The Driftmarket sprawls across the limestone shelves where the Underkin's territory meets the sea. No permanent structures mark it—only the worn grooves in stone where tent poles have stood for generations, the fire-circles blackened by countless seasons, the deep ruts carved by wagon wheels following the same paths between the same temporary stalls. When the market forms, it appears overnight like a vast fungal bloom, arriving with the spring tides and dispersing with the autumn storms.

The Saltborne arrive in their reed boats, vessels that look too fragile to survive the open ocean but somehow do. They weave them fresh each journey from the giant kelp forests, the still-living fronds twisted into hulls that flex with the waves rather than fight them. By the time they reach shore, barnacles already crust the underwater portions, beginning the slow transformation from boat to reef. The Saltborne never sail the same vessel twice—they beach them at market's end, let the tide reclaim what the tide gave.

Where the Underkin compress into stone's embrace, the Saltborne expand into water's vastness. Their bodies stretch tall and thin, with elongated limbs that cut through water like oars. Webbing connects their fingers to the second knuckle, their toes to the base. Not enough to call them aquatic, but enough to mark them as people caught between elements. Their skin changes color with the seasons—blue-grey in winter water, green-brown in summer shallows, taking on the hues of whatever sea they've most recently swum.

They dress in layers of translucence. Inner garments of jellyfish membrane, treated with their saliva to neutralize the stinging cells, cling to their skin like second thoughts. Over this, cloaks of woven sea silk, harvested from the byssal threads of deep-dwelling mollusks. The garments shift color in the light, now silver, now gold, now the deep purple of abyssal trenches. Everything they wear can survive total submersion, dry within minutes, fold down to nothing. They trust no possession that can't follow them into the water.

The Driftmarket exists because each people has what the other needs. The Underkin bring up stone tools of impossible precision, fungal medicines that cure the bone-rot that plagues swimmers, beetle-shell containers that preserve food for years. The carved water-vessels they trade are worth fleets of boats—stone worn so thin it's translucent, holding water that stays fresh through any voyage. Most precious are the fire-stones, chunks of rock from the thermal chambers that hold heat for days, allowing the Saltborne to cook food far from shore.

In return, the Saltborne offer the ocean's plenty. Not just food—though they bring that in abundance, smoked fish and fermented kelp and the sweet meat of deep crabs—but materials impossible to find underground. Shark teeth for cutting tools that never dull. Whale bones for construction beams that flex without breaking. Squid ink that the Underkin use to mark their maps on specially prepared lizard leather. Salt itself, in a dozen varieties, each harvested from different pools and carrying different minerals essential for the underground ecosystem.

But the true trade runs deeper than goods. Information flows between the peoples like water finding its level. The Saltborne navigate by reading currents and stars, tracking the movement of fish schools and the patterns of storms. They bring news of the world's edges—which islands have emerged from volcanic birth, which coastlines have crumbled into the hungry sea. The Underkin offer different intelligence: the movement of groundwater that predicts droughts, the tremors that presage earthquakes, the deep sounds that echo up from the earth's core. Together, they assemble a picture of their world no single people could comprehend.

The market has its own language, neither the echo-speech of the warren nor the tonal singing of the sea. Instead, a pidgin of gestures has evolved, elaborate enough to haggle prices but simple enough for children to learn. Hands wave like water, tap like tools on stone. Fingers count in base-eight, matching the legs of the octopi the Saltborne consider sacred. When precision matters, they draw symbols in the sand between them, letting the wind erase each conversation as it ends.

Treaties older than memory govern the interactions. No Underkin may follow the Saltborne to sea; no Saltborne may enter the warren's mouths. The market exists in the liminal space between, neither earth nor ocean but something temporary and necessary. Violence here brings exile from both peoples—the offender marked with phosphorescent paint that glows beneath water and in darkness alike, forever visible to both tribes as one who broke the peace.

Children mix more freely than their parents. Young Underkin dare each other to wade into the shallows, shrieking as waves lap their legs. Saltborne youth explore the tide pools, turning over rocks to find the creatures that live between wetness and air. They trade small treasures—a perfect shell for a glowing fungus, a sharks tooth for a beetle's jeweled wing case. Some learn each other's languages, becoming interpreters when they grow older, valued by both sides but fully trusted by neither.

The rituals that frame the market run deep. On the first night, the Underkin emerge just after sunset, their sensitive eyes able to bear twilight's gentler light. They light fires in the ancient circles, using their hoarded driftwood and dried kelp. The smoke signals the Saltborne, who respond with their own fires on the boats—oil-soaked rags that burn green and blue from copper salts. The colored smoke mingles in the air between them, creating a third color that belongs to neither people alone.

Before any trade, they feast together. The Underkin bring fermented fungi that induce mild euphoria, while the Saltborne offer fermented whale milk that tastes of the deep ocean. Combined, the substances produce visions—shared hallucinations where the drinkers experience both the crushing depths of stone and the infinite expanse of sea. Elders say this practice keeps empathy alive between the peoples, forcing each to feel what the other knows as home.

The actual trading follows patterns refined across generations. Goods are displayed but not touched, each side walking among the other's wares in ritual inspection. The Saltborne sing the virtues of their offerings, voices harmonizing in ways that make even mundane items seem precious. The Underkin respond with the sound-maps of their goods' creation, clicking and tapping the story of each tool's making. Only after this performance can haggling begin, and then it becomes theater—dramatic refusals, elaborate counter-offers, feigned insult and reconciliation.

Marriages happen here, in the space between worlds. Not often, and never easily, but sometimes an Underkin watching the boats arrive feels the pull of horizons, or a Saltborne hearing the warren's echoes understands the appeal of enclosed spaces. These unions are blessed and mourned in equal measure. The couple must choose one world or the other—there is no middle life possible. Children of such marriages often become the go-betweens, able to survive in either element but comfortable in neither, forever translating between their dual heritages.

As the market progresses, temporary structures multiply. The Saltborne erect drying racks for the fish they'll smoke using Underkin fire-stones. The cave dwellers set up grinding stations where they demonstrate their stone-working techniques, shaping custom tools to Saltborne specifications. Cooking fires burn day and night, each people preparing food that the other finds exotic. The smells mingle—roasting beetles and grilling kelp, fungal stews and seafood broths.

Music happens in the evenings. The Saltborne bring instruments of bone and sinew—drums made from whale bladders, flutes carved from albatross wings. The Underkin respond with their stone chimes and water drums, creating rhythms that echo their carved halls. The two musical traditions should clash, but somehow they've evolved to complement—the sea songs flowing around the earth rhythms like water around stone. Young people dance, movements that borrow from both traditions, creating something new in the firelight.

But the Driftmarket is more than celebration. Hard negotiations happen in the shadows. The Saltborne need ever more fire-stones as the seas grow colder, as fish move to deeper waters. The Underkin require more salt as their population grows, as new chambers need their preservation systems. Resources that once seemed infinite show signs of strain. Both peoples face changes that threaten old ways, and the market becomes the place where adaptations are worked out, sometimes painfully.

The weather itself participates. Storms drive the market to early close some years, while perfect conditions extend it beyond traditional bounds. The Underkin read the sky nervously, unused to its moods. The Saltborne test the air with wet fingers, feeling for pressure changes that speak of deep-water movement. Both watch for the subtle signs that mark the market's natural end—the migration of certain birds, the blooming of specific tide pool flowers, the way the moon pulls the highest tides of the season.

When dissolution comes, it happens quickly. The Saltborne load their boats with traded goods wrapped in waterproof bundles. The Underkin pack their burdens into clever baskets that distribute weight for the climb down into darkness. The temporary city vanishes as if it never was, leaving only the eternal marks in stone—the pole holes, the fire circles, the ruts that say here, for a moment, two worlds touched.

On the last morning, a final ritual. Representatives from each people wade into the shallows together, the Underkin fighting their fear of open water, the Saltborne resisting the urge to dive deep. They exchange tokens—usually simple things, a carved stone, a polished shell. These will be displayed in each community until the next gathering, reminders that other ways of living exist, that the world is vast enough for different peoples to thrive in their different ways.

The Saltborne push off with the tide, their living boats already beginning to smell of the sea. They sing as they go, voices carrying back to shore long after their vessels disappear into the horizon's curve. The Underkin watch until their sensitive eyes water from the light, then turn and file back to their breathing mouths, their carved darkness, their stone songs. Each returns to their element, enriched by the exchange, carrying traded goods and traded knowledge back to their waiting communities.

But something lingers in the market grounds after both peoples leave. The worn stone remembers the weight of temporary structures. The sand holds the memory of different footprints—broad and webbed, narrow and calloused. In the tide pools, creatures disturbed by the gathering slowly return to their routines. Seeds dropped from traded food begin to sprout in the cracks, plants from the deep forest mixing with seaweed fragments, creating small hybrid gardens that belong to no single ecosystem.

The Driftmarket is this—a scar and a suture, a wound and its healing, a place where edges meet without merging. It exists because it must, because isolation kills as surely as invasion, because even the most perfectly adapted peoples need what they cannot produce themselves. When the Underkin children ask why they must trade with the strange tall people who smell of salt, the elders respond with a question: what would the warren be without the sea's gifts? When the Saltborne youth wonder why they bother with the pale cave folk, their navigators ask: how would we predict the storms without the earth's wisdom?

Between the breathing mouths of the warren and the breathing waves of the sea, the Driftmarket endures—not as a place but as a practice, not as a building but as a rhythm, ancient as the first meeting between stone and water, necessary as the tide itself.
