The Laterals

Beyond the mountain's breathing reach, where stone remembers its horizontal nature and rain falls in directions everyone agrees upon, the plateau spreads like an exhaled secret. Here, the Laterals have built their sprawling settlements in defiance of altitude, their lives flowing outward rather than up or down, collecting in pools of community wherever the land offers the slightest depression.

They are runners by necessity and nature, their settlements scattered across distances that would dizzy a Sinter. A Lateral thinks nothing of covering thirty miles before noon, their long, loping stride eating ground with an efficiency born from generations of practice. Their legs are distinctly elongated, not grotesquely but noticeably, with a spring-loading quality in their tendons that stores and releases energy with each step. Their feet have spread wide, toes splayed for purchase on the prairie earth, the soles thick with calluses that read the ground like braille.

The plateau itself is deceptive in its simplicity. What appears monotonous from a distance reveals itself as an intricate text of micro-environments—slight rises where different grasses dominate, depressions where water lingers after rain, stone outcroppings that create wind-shadows and thermal variations. The Laterals read this text at speed, their running routes following contours invisible to stationary observation, paths that exist only in movement.

Their architecture spreads low and wide, buildings that seem to pour across the ground rather than rise from it. They build with sod and stone, creating structures that follow the earth's curves, roofed with living grass that makes their settlements nearly invisible until you're upon them. Inside, the spaces flow one into another without clear boundaries, rooms becoming corridors becoming courtyards in an endless horizontal maze that mirrors the plateau's own refusal to be contained.

The Laterals organize their society around Circuits—vast running routes that connect settlements, resources, and sacred sites in patterns maintained by memory and muscle rather than maps. A young Lateral begins running simple circuits as soon as they can maintain pace, gradually learning longer and more complex routes until they can run the Grand Circuit, a three-hundred-mile loop that touches every major settlement and takes even the swiftest runners four days to complete.

They carry messages in their bodies, not just in memory but in their breath and sweat. A runner arriving from another settlement brings chemical news—pheromones that speak of the health of distant communities, the quality of water sources, the emotional state of the places they've passed through. The Laterals have developed a complex olfactory language that operates below consciousness, information transmitted through proximity and shared air.

Their relationship with the Sinters is ancient and essential. Twice each year, during the Vapor Trades, selected runners make the journey to the mountain city. They carry what the vertical world cannot produce—horizontal goods like prairie seeds, sun-dried meat, and most importantly, the crystallized salts extracted from seasonal lakes that appear and disappear across the plateau. These salts are crucial to the Sinters' industrial processes, catalyzing reactions that the mountain's own minerals cannot achieve alone.

In return, the runners descend laden with condensed essences—vials of concentrated vapor that contain trace elements found nowhere on the plateau, metal tools forged in temperatures only the mountain's breath can achieve, and most mysteriously, the Thought Stones. These are minerals that have spent generations absorbing the collective dreams of the Sinters, stones that whisper of vertical possibilities when held to the ear in the plateau's perfect silence.

The journey between worlds is itself a transformation. Runners must slow as they approach the mountain, their horizontal instincts confounded by the increasing verticality. They wrap their splayed feet, bind their running muscles, practice moving in dimensions their bodies resist. Similarly, when a Sinter makes the rare journey to the plateau (always for specific ritual purposes), they must learn to trust the horizon, to move without the constant reference of up and down that defines their existence.

The trade points are neutral spaces, neither fully vertical nor completely horizontal. Platforms that extend from the mountain's base like tongues, where the stone still breathes but faintly, where mist gathers but doesn't dominate. Here, elaborate rituals govern the exchange—not from superstition but from practical necessity. The two peoples' biochemistries have diverged enough that direct contact requires preparation. Runners must acclimate to the mineral-heavy air, while Sinters must adjust to the prairie's intense UV light that their mist-filtered world never experiences.

The goods themselves transform in the exchange. Prairie salt, exposed to the mountain's vapors, develops crystalline structures impossible on the plateau, becoming something new that neither world could produce alone. The condensed essences, released into the horizontal air, create micro-climates around Lateral settlements, tiny zones where mist forms without reason, where the air tastes of deep stone and industrial dreams.

Children are sometimes exchanged—not permanently, but for seasons of learning. Lateral youth who show unusual stillness, who struggle with the constant movement their culture demands, may spend time among the Sinters, learning vertical patience. Similarly, young Sinters who cannot synchronize with the mountain's breathing might run with the Laterals, discovering horizontal freedoms. Most return to their birth-worlds, but changed, carrying in their bodies the memory of other ways of being.

The two peoples share stories that neither fully understands but both cherish. The Laterals tell of the First Runner, who ran so fast they arrived at their starting point before they left, creating the paradox that allows all Circuits to exist simultaneously. The Sinters speak of the Original Vapor, the first exhalation of the mountain that rose so high it forgot to fall, becoming the sky that shelters the plateau. These stories are traded along with goods, told in the pidgin language that has developed in the neutral spaces—a language that includes both harmonic resonances and scent markers, spoken while moving at precisely half the speed of a Lateral's run.

The Vapor Trades have created their own ecology. Plants from both environments have hybridized in the trading zones, creating species that grow horizontally but breathe vertically, spreading across the ground while exhaling mist. Animals, too, have adapted—colonies of creatures that are neither fully bat-lemur nor entirely prairie-dweller, but something between, gliding between worlds on membranes that catch both wind and vapor.

Some few individuals never return from the trades. They remain in the neutral spaces, becoming the Translators—beings who can no longer fully inhabit either world but serve as living bridges between them. Their bodies carry marks of both adaptations—mineral deposits in their lengthened limbs, the ability to run vertically for short distances, lungs that process both clear air and saturated vapor. They maintain the trade protocols, tend the hybrid gardens, and raise the exchanged children during their crossworld seasons.

The mathematics of the trade runs deeper than simple exchange. The Sinters need exactly as much prairie salt as the Laterals need condensed essence—not from negotiated balance but from fundamental complementarity. The mountain's breathing depends partly on reactions catalyzed by plateau minerals, while the prairie's subtle weather patterns require the nucleation sites provided by mountain vapor. The two worlds are organs in a larger body, the trade between them a circulation as necessary as blood.

During the rare Eclipse Trades, when atmospheric conditions allow deeper exchange, the two peoples attempt to share their impossible knowledges. Sinters try to explain the experience of geological time, of feeling stone form inside your bones. Laterals demonstrate the meditation of endless running, the state where distance becomes duration becomes a kind of music. These exchanges fail as language but succeed as experience, each people carrying back incomprehensible gifts that nonetheless enrich their world.

The trade routes themselves are alive with memory. Certain stones at the crossing points have been touched by thousands of runners, worn smooth by horizontal hands, while also being slowly mineralized by Sinter deposits. These marker stones develop properties of both worlds—they hum with harmonic frequencies when the wind is right, emit scents that carry for miles when rain approaches. Following these stones, a trader can navigate between worlds even in the mist that sometimes swallows the border zones.

In their separate ways, both peoples dream of the other's world. Sinters, in their thickest collective dreams, experience the terror and joy of endless horizon. Laterals, in the deep rest after their longest runs, feel the vertical city's crushing embrace, the strange comfort of weight and elevation. These dreams are tradable commodities in their own right—crystallized in stones, carried in sweat, exchanged for other dreams that neither world could generate alone.

The trade makes both worlds more themselves. The mountain city grows ever more vertical, fed by prairie catalysts that accelerate its impossible architectures. The plateau spreads ever wider, its circuits energized by mountain essences that make distance more traversable. What connects them is not just commerce but necessity—each world needs the other to become more fully what it already is.
