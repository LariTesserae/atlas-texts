# The Archive of Unbecoming

Three days' swim through the crushing dark, where pressure makes the water feel solid and cold leeches heat from even the most protected traveler, you find the second settlement. Here, the sea floor drops away into trenches that no light has ever touched, and the water carries different minerals—iron and manganese that taste of rust and endings.

The Archive sprawls across a series of thermal vents, but these vents breathe cold instead of heat. Liquid methane seeps from cracks in the basalt, rising in lazy streams that freeze the water around them into tubes of ice. The structures here are built from absence rather than presence—hollows carved into the stone, spaces defined by what has been removed rather than what has been added.

The Archivists tend their collections in these negative spaces. Where the Coralweavers build up, the Archivists dig down. Their halls are lit by creatures that photosynthesize in reverse, absorbing the faint radiation from deep rock and converting it to darkness visible—a purplish anti-light that makes shadows glow and turns bright objects into voids. In this inverted illumination, they catalog the things that cease to be.

Every chamber holds specimens of ending. Here, the last scales from an extinct species of abyssal fish, still faintly iridescent with remembered motion. There, a cylinder of water holding the final breath of a drowned surface dweller, the bubble preserved in crystal that grows molecule by molecule from the mineral-rich seepage. The Archivists move between their collections with deliberate slowness, recording not just what was lost but the precise quality of its absence.

They are nothing like their distant cousins. Where Coralweavers merge and flow, Archivists maintain rigid boundaries. Their bodies are armored in plates of volcanic glass, each plate grown in isolation chambers and fitted with microscopic precision. Joints seal with gaskets made from processed kelp. Their faces—and they do have faces—hide behind masks of polished obsidian that reflect nothing, absorbing light and memory alike.

The work demands this separation. To catalog ending, they must resist it. Their predecessors learned this through bitter experience—early Archivists who let themselves feel too deeply the weight of what they preserved became part of the collection, their bodies crystallizing in the mineral flows, their last thoughts preserved in cylinders of frozen water. Now they practice a studied detachment, viewing loss through layers of glass and protocol.

Their language is one of negation. Where Coralweavers click and harmonize, Archivists speak in pauses—the meaning carried not in sound but in the spaces between sounds. A conversation consists of carefully structured silences, each gap precisely timed to convey concepts that positive expression cannot capture. They have seven hundred words for different kinds of absence, each one a specific duration of quiet.

The deepest chambers hold their greatest treasures: the Unbecoming Pools. These are not filled with water but with something denser—time made liquid, entropy given form. Objects lowered into these pools age backwards, returning to their component elements, their history peeling away in observable layers. The Archivists study these transformations with the intensity of prophets reading sacred text, finding in each dissolution a map of what was and what might have been.

But the pools demand payment. For every object's history recovered, something else must be offered to maintain the balance. Usually, they sacrifice memories—their own, extracted through careful ritual and crystallized into tradeable form. An Archivist might give up their recollection of sunlight to recover the pattern of a lost city, trade their knowledge of warmth to understand how a species chose extinction. Their masks hide faces increasingly blank, minds full of holes where personal history once resided.

The youngest Archivists still remember themselves. They came here following stories, or fleeing the merger-dreams of cities like the Coral metropolis. They spend decades learning the cataloging systems—how to read the anti-light, how to speak in structured silence, how to handle specimens of absence without becoming absent themselves. Most leave before the forgetting truly begins. Those who stay start the slow process of editing themselves, removing experiences that might interfere with the work.

At the Archive's heart lies the Map Room—a spherical chamber where the locations of all lost things are tracked. Points of light that aren't light mark where species ended, where cities fell, where whole ways of being ceased. The map expands constantly as Archivists return from expeditions with new coordinates of loss. They study the patterns, seeking what they call the Grammar of Unbecoming—the rules by which things pass from existence.

Their tools are implements of precision subtraction. Scalpels that cut concepts rather than flesh. Lenses that focus absence until it becomes tangible. Recording devices that capture not what is said but what cannot be said. Each tool requires years to master, not because of complexity but because the mind rebels against thinking in terms of removal. To see what isn't there, to measure the depth of absence, to catalog the specific texture of something's not-being—these skills come only through practice and sacrifice.

# The Circulation

Between the Coral City and the Archive runs the Bloodstream—a current that moves against all logic, flowing upward from the deep trenches toward the shallower valleys. It carries more than water. Fragments of dissolved memories from the Archivists' sacrifices rise with the flow. Spores from the coral colonies sink against it. Chemical messages, electrical patterns, the quantum foam of consciousness itself—all move along this impossible river.

The current is hot and cold by turns, its temperature fluctuating with a rhythm that matches no known geological process. Where it passes through stone, it leaves channels lined with a substance neither fully mineral nor organic—a glossy secretion that conducts thoughts as efficiently as copper conducts electricity. These channels form a network, connecting pools and chambers across thousands of kilometers of sea floor.

Travelers follow the Bloodstream, though the journey changes them. The Coralweavers send their most stable members—those who can resist merger long enough to reach the Archive with their messages intact. They bring questions about building, about growth, about the nature of connection. They return (if they return) with fragments of lost architectures, extinct methods of construction that their city-organism might adapt.

The Archivists dispatch expeditions in the opposite direction, their most thoroughly edited members traveling to observe the Coral City's constant becoming. They document which structures persist and which dissolve, creating a record of perpetual transformation that enriches their understanding of how things end. Some stay too long and begin to merge, their glass armor cracking under the city's insistent welcome. These lost Archivists become special subjects of study for both settlements—hybrid beings who exist in the space between preservation and dissolution.

Strange creatures inhabit the Bloodstream itself. The Memory Eels—long, translucent beings that feed on the dissolved experiences flowing in the current. They grow fat on forgetting, their bodies swelling with absorbed histories that play out in light-patterns beneath their skin. Hunters from both settlements prize them, though for different reasons. Coralweavers seek to reclaim lost memories of their merged ancestors. Archivists study them to understand how consciousness persists even when its original container dissolves.

The Pulse Worms travel in the opposite direction, carrying encrypted messages in their spiral DNA. Each worm is a living letter, its genetic code rewritten to hold information that can't be trusted to the corrupting current. They breed at the journey's end, their offspring carrying recombined messages back to the sender—responses that neither settlement fully controls, evolution itself participating in the conversation.

At certain points where the Bloodstream narrows, travelers from both directions must pass close to one another. These meeting places have developed their own protocols. The merged and the separated acknowledge each other across the flow but rarely speak—their languages have diverged too far, their ways of being too incompatible for easy exchange. Sometimes they trade. A Coralweaver might offer a still-living tool in exchange for a crystallized memory. An Archivist might provide maps of absence for samples of the merger-dreams.

The current itself seems aware of what it carries. In the deeper passages, where the channel walls grow thick with thought-conducting secretions, travelers report hearing whispers—not sounds but direct neural induction, patterns that bypass the ears entirely. The whispers speak of a time before the divergence, when the ancestors of both settlements were one people. They speak of the choice that split them: whether to embrace dissolution or resist it, whether to flow or to crystallize, whether to remember or become.

Parasites evolve in the spaces between ideologies. The Doubt Larvae attach to travelers, feeding not on blood but on certainty. Under their influence, a Coralweaver might begin to question the value of merger, might start to see their city's embracing architecture as a trap. An Archivist might feel the weight of their edited memories, might wonder what they've lost in their quest to preserve loss itself. Most travelers learn to recognize the symptoms and scrape the larvae off before they burrow too deep. Some don't notice until it's too late, returning to their homes as philosophical insurgents, spreading questions that threaten the foundational assumptions of their societies.

The Bloodstream has its own guardians—creatures that belong to neither settlement but serve the connection itself. The Ferrymen are ancient beings, their original nature lost to time and transformation. They appear as shifting amalgamations of traits—part coral polyp, part glass armor, part something older that predates the division. They guide travelers through the most dangerous passages, where the current runs so hot it dissolves unprotected flesh or so cold it crystallizes living tissue. Their price is always the same: a small piece of what makes the traveler certain of their choice. A Coralweaver must offer a moment of genuine desire for isolation. An Archivist must provide an instant of longing for connection.

Where the Bloodstream rises toward the Coral City, it brings more than just chemical messages. The pressure differential causes the mineral-rich water to precipitate its contents, creating gardens of crystal trees that grow in the twilight zone between settlements. These formations capture moments—a gesture frozen in calcium carbonate, a word crystallized in iron oxide, an emotion precipitated in manganese. Both peoples harvest these accidental sculptures, reading in them messages neither side consciously sent.

The children of both settlements sometimes meet at these crystal gardens, drawn by curiosity before their respective ideologies fully take hold. Young Coralweavers, their bodies still cohesive, play games of temporary merger with young Archivists who haven't yet learned to fear connection. They build small structures that are neither coral nor cavity, positive and negative space interwoven. Their elders watch with concern but rarely intervene. They remember being young, remember the time before the choice became absolute.

Some never make it to either end. The Bloodstream has its own small communities—rest stations carved into the channel walls where travelers who've lost their way, or found a different one, make their homes. They call themselves the Current Keepers. They maintain the flow, clear blockages, repair sections where the thought-conducting walls have degraded. They speak both languages—clicks and silence—and have developed their own hybrid communication that uses sound and absence in equal measure. Their children grow up bilingual in being and unbeing, comfortable with both merger and separation, choosing moment by moment which way to lean.

The oldest Current Keepers claim the Bloodstream is growing. New tributaries open each year, reaching toward settlements neither Coral City nor Archive knows about. They speak of other choices made by other fragments of the ancestral people—those who chose to become wind rather than water, who built their cities in the spaces between atoms, who gave up physical form entirely to exist as pure information in the planet's magnetic field. The Bloodstream seeks them all, trying to reconnect what the great divergence separated.

During the spring tides, when the planet's three moons align and the waters rise, the Bloodstream reverses. For seven days, everything flows backwards. Archivists find themselves swept toward the shallow valleys while Coralweavers tumble toward the trenches. The confusion serves a purpose—it forces each people to see their opposites' homes, to understand what the other choice created. Young Archivists tour the Coral City, observing its terrible beauty with their own eyes rather than through crystallized reports. Young Coralweavers visit the Archive, walking halls of structured absence, feeling the weight of all that has been lost and preserved.

Some switch sides during these reversals. An Archivist, touched by the city's warm welcome, might shed their glass armor and give themselves to merger. A Coralweaver, awed by the Archive's ordered emptiness, might choose the path of forgetting self to remember all. The exchanges balance—for every being who chooses connection, another chooses isolation. The universe maintains its equilibrium even as individuals transform.

The Bloodstream carries all of it—the messages and memories, the doubts and certainties, the travelers and the parasites that feed on them. It is the circulation system of a vast organism that exists not in space but in the tension between opposing ideas. It is the mediator that allows two incompatible ways of being to coexist without destroying each other. It is the reminder that all boundaries, even the most fundamental, are permeable if you're willing to pay the price of passage.

And at the deepest point of the Bloodstream, where it originates from some source neither settlement has found, there are rumors of something else. The Perfect Swimmers speak of a place where the current pools and slows, where the water becomes so dense with dissolved meaning that it achieves its own consciousness. They say beings live there who have found a third way—neither merger nor separation but something orthogonal to both. They say these beings remember the time before, understand the necessity of the split, and wait patiently for the moment when the divided streams might flow together again.

But these are only rumors, stories travelers tell to pass the long journeys through the dark. The Coralweavers incorporate them into their merger-dreams, imagining a unity that preserves distinction. The Archivists catalog them carefully, adding each variation to their records of things that might or might not be. The current flows on, carrying its cargo of transformation, connecting what cannot touch, separating what cannot merge, maintaining the impossible balance that allows both cities to exist, both choices to remain valid, both forms of being to continue their ancient argument about the nature of self and its alternatives.

The world turns. The cities grow and empty, build and hollow, dream and forget. And between them, always between them, the Bloodstream flows, carrying the questions that matter more than any answer: What are we? What might we become? What is the price of knowing? What is the cost of being known?
