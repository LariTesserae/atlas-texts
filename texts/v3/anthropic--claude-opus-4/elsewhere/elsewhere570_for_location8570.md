Far below the mist city, deeper than the thermal springs that warm its bones, deeper even than the oldest foundation stones remember, the mountain is hollow.

The cave system begins as a throat—a vertical shaft that drops through limestone layers like geological epochs, each stratum marking millions of years in bands of grey and white and rust. Water has been patient here, dissolving the mountain from within, following fissures and faults until chambers opened like bubbles in stone. The geology tells a different story than the surface: here the rock remembers when this mountain stood beneath an ancient sea, its limestone pregnant with the shells of creatures that swam before land learned to breathe.

The first chamber spreads wider than the city above, its ceiling lost in darkness that swallows light. Formations cascade from invisible heights—flowstone curtains that ring like bells when struck, columns thicker than the city's towers, pools of water so still they create perfect mirrors of a world turned upside down. The air tastes different here: mineral-sharp, ancient, carrying a charge that makes the skin prickle. Temperature holds steady year-round, warm enough that ice never forms, cool enough that the stone sweats constantly.

But this is merely the anteroom. Beyond, through passages that narrow and widen like breathing, the true depths reveal themselves. Here, bioluminescence paints the darkness in alien hues. Bacterial mats coat the walls in constellations of blue-green light, pulsing in waves that might be communication or mere chemistry. Fungi fruit in impossible colors—violets that hurt the eyes, oranges that seem to generate their own heat. They grow in specific patterns, following veins of minerals through the rock, their mycelia threading through stone like nerves through flesh.

The water here moves differently than above. Rivers flow upward through siphons, pools empty and fill with the mountain's breathing, streams vanish into gravel only to emerge chambers away. In the deepest accessible places, where the pressure makes ears ring and thoughts swim, underground lakes stretch beyond the reach of any torch. Their water is heavy with minerals, dense enough that objects sink slowly, as if through oil.

And in these depths lives something that the city's deep-dwellers might recognize as kin to their quarry-pool creatures, but older, more complete. They move through the dark water with purpose, their bodies elongated for navigating flooded passages, their skin translucent enough to show organs pulsing beneath. They have eyes—huge, complex structures that see in spectrums the upper world has forgotten. Their forelimbs have differentiated into delicate manipulators that can shape stone with secretions that dissolve and rebuild calcium deposits. 

They are builders, these deep ones. Their structures rise from lake floors—not crude nests but deliberate architecture. Spiraling towers of secreted stone mirror the moss patterns in the city above, but with mathematical precision that speaks of intelligence. Channels carved through rock direct water in patterns too complex for chance. In certain chambers, they've cultivated gardens of the luminescent fungi, arranging species by color and intensity to create living murals that shift and change as the colonies grow.

Their language is chemical—pheromones released into water that carry meaning in molecular chains. But they also sing, after a fashion. The chambers resonate with subsonic pulses that travel through water and stone, felt rather than heard. These songs coordinate their building, mark territory, share information about the deep currents and the health of the fungal gardens. Sometimes, in the thermal springs that connect their world to the city above, traces of these chemicals rise. The deep-dwelling priests who claim to understand the quarry creatures are not entirely wrong—they taste echoes of meaning in the water, fragments of a conversation vast and slow.

The deep ones know of the city. Their oldest structures cluster near the thermal vents that humans tap for warmth, though they abandoned these upper reaches generations ago when the drilling began. They taste the city in their water—the runoff from cisterns, the minerals from ground bones, the antifreeze secretions of the salamanders who are, perhaps, their own descendants adapted to a half-land existence. The blind fish in the city's cisterns still respond to their chemical calls, though the fish themselves have forgotten why.

The connection between the worlds is not merely physical. The thermal springs are arteries carrying heat but also information. Water that falls as mist on the city filters through layers of rock, gathering minerals and memories, until it reaches the deep lakes where the builders taste it and know: the patterns of human habitation, the health of the salamander populations, the slow changes in the fungal gardens both above and below.

Sometimes they send messages upward. A particular combination of minerals that makes the thermal water shimmer. Spores of new fungi that appear in the city's lower chambers, glowing in patterns that echo their deep murals. During the spring spawning, some blind fish carry more than eggs—they bear genetic messages, subtle changes that won't manifest for generations but slowly adapt the city's creatures for some future purpose the deep ones perceive.

The young who spend nights by the quarry pools, who return with visions—they've touched the edge of this deeper dreaming. The pools are windows, the almost-creatures that surface there are ambassadors, testing the boundary between worlds. The visions are chemical poetry, pheromone stories dissolved in water, telling of the vast halls below, the patient building, the slow purposes of beings who measure time in stone growth rather than seasons.

And sometimes, rarely, when conditions align—when the mountain breathes just so, when the pressure differentials open usually sealed passages—a deep one rises higher than usual. They surface in the quarry pools in full form, their huge eyes reflecting the grey sky they've never seen, their manipulator limbs tasting the alien touch of air. They observe the city their chemicals helped shape, the humans who build unknowingly in patterns they recognize, the salamanders who might be their children's children's children, adapted to the strange half-life between water and air.

They do not stay. The surface is too thin, too bright, too empty of the pressure that holds their bodies together. They sink back to their lakes, carrying new chemical stories to add to their deep libraries. And the city continues above, unknowing but not unguided, its water cycling between the heights and the depths, carrying messages in molecules, connecting two worlds that share the same stone skeleton, the same ancient water, the same patient mountain that breathes for them both.

This is the greater circulation: not just water cycling from cloud to stone to spring, but information, genetic and chemical, flowing between the city of mist and the city of depth, two civilizations that shape each other without ever truly meeting, partners in the slow dance of adaptation that has made this mountain more than rock—has made it a living system where consciousness exists at every level, from the humans who read weather patterns to the deep ones who taste time in stone, all of it connected by water that remembers everything and forgets nothing.
