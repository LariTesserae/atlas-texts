**The Crystalline Barrens**

Seven days' journey upstream, where the last tributaries that feed the Drowning Works narrow to threads between ancient hills, the land opens into a testament to desiccation. Here, across forty square kilometers of alabaster earth, spreads a landscape that has forgotten water so completely it has transformed the memory into mineral.

The ground cracks in perfect hexagons, each segment lifting at its edges like the scales of some impossible fish, creating a terrain of shallow bowls that catch nothing but light. The soil—if it can be called soil—has a consistency somewhere between chalk and salt, composed of the accumulated deposits from millennia of evaporated lakes. It tastes of absence, leaving your mouth drier than before you tried to wet your lips.

No cloud shadows cross this expanse. The air above the Barrens maintains a curious clarity, as if moisture itself refuses to enter this domain. The sky here appears closer, pressed down by its own emptiness, a blue so intense it vibrates at the edge of perception. When the wind comes—and it always comes in the afternoon—it carries no relief, only the sound of crystal singing against crystal, a high keening that sets teeth on edge.

The formations rise from this fractured plain like thoughts made manifest. They are not quite rock, not quite glass, but something between—mineral structures that grew in the absence of water, feeding on minerals that should have dissolved but instead chose to accumulate. They take forms that suggest architecture: spiraling towers, branching columns, delicate arches that span chasms no wider than a hand. Some have grown together into complexes that resemble cities built for beings who never needed doors, only passages for wind and light.

These crystal formations possess an unsettling property: they grow. Not in any way the eye can follow, but leave a mark on one and return in a season to find it has moved, always upward, always incorporating the scratch into a new whorl or facet. The growth happens in the spaces between observation, in the moments when the Barrens exist without witnesses. Time moves differently here—not faster or slower, but sideways, accumulating in layers like the minerals themselves.

The light becomes a physical presence in this place. It pools in the hexagonal depressions, thickening throughout the day until by noon it has the consistency of honey. It refracts through the crystal growths in patterns that hurt to follow, creating spectrums that include colors for which there are no names. As the sun tracks across the sky, the formations sing—each one resonating at a frequency determined by its internal structure, creating harmonics that build and interfere until the entire Barrens hum with inaudible sound.

What lives here has adapted to existence without water in ways that challenge understanding. The glass beetles are perhaps the most visible—their carapaces transparent, their organs visible as geometric patterns of light-conducting tissue. They feed on the minerals directly, their mandibles evolved to shave microscopic layers from the crystal formations. When threatened, they shatter—not dying but dispersing into a dozen smaller versions that scatter and regroup later, sharing the experience through some silicon-based communication.

Plants exist, after a fashion. The prism flowers grow from cracks in the hexagonal plates, their "petals" actually specialized organs that capture and concentrate light into their crystalline cores. They photosynthesize not with water but with the minerals dissolved in the morning dew that forms once each month when conditions align precisely. Their roots, if examined, prove to be more crystal than vegetable, growing down through the layers of accumulated mineral until they reach the fossil water tables far below—too deep and too ancient to ever rise again.

The wind-weavers drift across this landscape like questions seeking answers. Neither plant nor animal, they exist as colonies of micro-crystals held together by static charge, forming sheets and ribbons that undulate through the air. They feed on the electrical differentials between the ground and sky, growing more active during the rare storms that approach but never quite arrive. When they pass over the formations, they leave traces—etchings that might be writing in a language based on electrical resistance rather than symbol.

But the true inhabitants of the Barrens are the Resonants—creatures so adapted to this waterless existence that their bodies have become living tuning forks. Tall as the crystal spires but thin as reeds, they move by vibration, setting up standing waves in the ground that carry them from formation to formation. Their skin has the texture of frosted glass, and their eyes have evolved into complex arrays of lenses that see not light but the compression waves that move through the crystal substrate.

The Resonants tend the formations with movements that seem random but follow patterns spanning centuries. They strike specific crystals with specialized limbs, setting up vibrations that influence growth in formations kilometers away. Whether they are gardeners or musicians, shepherds or composers, depends entirely on how one chooses to understand their work. They sing constantly—not with voices but with their entire bodies, creating subsonic hymns that travel through the mineral ground to emerge, transformed, from distant spires.

There are the Memory Pools—hexagonal depressions where the light has thickened so much over eons that it has become nearly solid, preserving images from the deep past. Look into one and see the lake that once covered this place, watch the water recede in fast-forward, observe the first crystals precipitating from the hypersaturated solutions. The Resonants avoid these pools with what might be reverence or might be fear.

At the heart of the Barrens, where the formations reach their greatest height and complexity, stands the Cathedral—though no hand built it and no deity claims it. Here the crystals have grown into a space that manages to be both chamber and void, where sound and light interact in ways that create experiences rather than simply phenomena. The Resonants gather here during the solstices, their combined vibrations building until the entire structure rings like a bell made of light, the sound carrying all the way to the Drowning Works where it arrives as a whisper through the pipes, a reminder that somewhere water is only a memory preserved in stone.

The connection between these places lives in that sound, in the minerals dissolved from the Barrens that eventually find their way through deep aquifers to emerge in the springs that feed the Works. The rust that the beetles consume carries trace elements from crystals that knew the sun. The water that drowns the machinery remembers, in its molecular structure, the eons it spent locked in stone.

And sometimes, rarely, when conditions in the Works align—when the steam rises just so and the pressure builds in specific configurations—the droplets that condense on the pipes arrange themselves in perfect hexagons, each one singing for just a moment with the voice of crystals that grow in a place where rain is a fossil and water flows only through the dreams of stone.
