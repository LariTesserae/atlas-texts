Above the Singing Hollow, the surface world spreads like a vast manuscript of stone, its pages separated by canyons so deep their bottoms vanish into heat-shimmer and shadow. This is the Scalar Heights, where altitude determines everything—moisture, temperature, pressure, even the flow of time itself seems to slow as one climbs toward the bone-dry peaks.

The dominant life here has learned to read elevation like a language. The Heights Shepherds, as they call themselves, though they tend no flocks, move their entire existence up and down the cliff faces with the shifting conditions. Their settlements are vertical—long strips of habitation clinging to the canyon walls, designed to be occupied only at certain heights during certain parts of the year.

At the lowest levels, where morning mists still sometimes gather, they maintain their nursery gardens—tiny pockets of green where seeds are coaxed to sprout in carefully hoarded soil. These gardens migrate upward as the season progresses, the Shepherds transplanting each seedling to a slightly higher ledge every few days, following the retreat of moisture up the cliffs. By the time the plants fruit, they may be a thousand feet above where they germinated.

The Shepherds themselves are compact and dense, their bodies adapted for climbing with overdeveloped forearms and fingers that can find purchase on nearly smooth stone. Their feet have evolved secondary thumbs, making them as dexterous climbing backward as forward. Their skin is thick and waxy, sealed against moisture loss, with a texture like polished leather that sheds dust and resists abrasion.

They build their temporary homes from woven stone-silk—a material they harvest from colonies of mineral-eating worms that live in the deepest cliff crevices. These worms excrete threads of processed rock that, when dried and twisted, create ropes stronger than steel yet flexible enough to weave. The Shepherds' hanging homes sway from these cables, entire neighborhoods suspended in space, ready to be reeled up or down as conditions demand.

Water here is memory made manifest. The Shepherds carry their ancestral water-rights in literal form—ancient containers passed down through generations, each holding perhaps a cup of water that has been in the family for centuries. This water is never drunk but used to read the future, a single drop placed on the tongue during important decisions. They say it carries the taste of every choice their lineage has made.

Their sustenance comes not from farming but from processing the air itself. They've developed tools—part chemistry set, part musical instrument—that can condense the sparse humidity into drops of moisture, extracting minerals from dust storms, even converting certain frequencies of sunlight into edible compounds. The work is slow and meticulous, requiring constant attention to barometric pressure and wind patterns.

The connecting space between the Scalar Heights and the Singing Hollow is a vertical labyrinth of natural chimneys and human-carved shafts, some no wider than a shoulders' breadth, others opening into vast intermediate chambers. This network, known as the Throat, extends for miles through the rock, a three-dimensional maze that only the initiated can navigate.

The two peoples discovered each other through vibration. The Singing Hollow's great resonance, traveling through stone, created patterns of dust-fall in certain places on the Heights. The Shepherds, ever attentive to minute changes in their environment, noticed these patterns and began to investigate. It took generations to locate the source, decades more to create stable passages between the worlds.

Now, the Throat serves as more than mere connection—it has become a space unto itself, a vertical borderland with its own ecology and customs. Pressure differentials between the hot Hollow and the variable-temperature Heights create constant winds through these passages. The winds sing different notes depending on the season, the time of day, the weather above and the ceremonies below.

Trading posts cling to the walls of wider shafts, places where the Shepherds' processed air-food is exchanged for the Hollow-dwellers' resonant wood. The traders who work these passages belong fully to neither world, their bodies showing adaptations to both—the elongated frames of the Hollow people but the dense bones of the Heights, voices that can speak in both frequency and word.

The most valued trade is neither material nor food but knowledge. The Shepherds bring down weather patterns encoded in knots, teaching the Hollow-dwellers to predict the pressure changes that will affect their acoustic environment. In return, the Hollow people share their resonance-healing techniques, showing the Shepherds how certain frequencies can mend fractured stone or encourage water-bearing minerals to surface.

Young people from both communities sometimes choose to live in the Throat itself, creating a hybrid culture. They've developed their own language—neither fully verbal nor entirely tonal, but something that uses the echoes of the passages to layer meaning. Their children grow up running vertical marathons through the shafts, as comfortable in perfect darkness as in blazing sun, their bodies adapted to rapid pressure changes.

The Throat-dwellers have discovered certain chambers where the two worlds' influence creates unique phenomena. In one, the mineral-eating worms from the Heights have learned to digest the Hollow's resonant wood, creating new materials with properties of both. In another, condensation from the Heights meets the spore-light from below, forming luminescent pools that the inhabitants cultivate for both light and sustenance.

Religious practices have evolved to incorporate both worldviews. The Shepherds, who once worshipped the sun's journey up and down their cliffs, now include the Hollow's eternal vibration in their cosmology. The Hollow-dwellers have added vertical pilgrimage to their practices, climbing toward a light they cannot see but whose effects they feel in the changing pressure.

Conflicts arise, inevitably. The Shepherds' need for precise atmospheric conditions sometimes clashes with the Hollow-dwellers' sonic ceremonies that can alter air pressure for miles. Water rights—so crucial above—mean nothing below, while the concept of acoustic territory baffles the surface dwellers. These disputes are mediated in the neutral ground of the Throat, where both sides must adapt to the other's communication methods.

The exchange goes deeper than trade or treaty. Intermarriage has created children who can see heat and read stones, who climb by echolocation and sing water from air. Knowledge-systems have merged: the Shepherds now map their vertical territories in acoustic dimensions, while some Hollow-dwellers have learned to cultivate air-plants that grow toward remembered sunlight, following the patterns their hosts learned from surface winds.

Most profoundly, the connection has changed each people's understanding of their own world. The Shepherds, learning of the vast spaces below, no longer see the Heights as the full extent of existence. Their vertical migrations now feel like movement along just one axis of a much larger space. The Hollow-dwellers, discovering that their eternal warmth ultimately derives from the sun so far above, have developed new harmonies that incorporate the rhythms of day and night they never experience directly.

In the deepest accessible chamber where the two worlds nearly meet—a place where the Hollow's heat creates updrafts so powerful they can lift a Shepherd, where the acoustic pressure makes the air itself sing—both peoples have built a joint shrine. Neither fully of one world nor the other, it exists in the margin, the space between breath and song, between falling and flying, between the memory of water and the promise of sound. Here, once each great Interval, representatives of both worlds meet to share stories that can only be told in that liminal space, in languages that exist nowhere else, celebrating the connection that has made them both larger than they were alone.
