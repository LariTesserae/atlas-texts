Beyond the flooded chambers, through a service tunnel that rises at a forty-degree angle for nearly a kilometer, the Dry Synthesis Halls occupy what was once the facility's primary research wing. Here, the transformation took a different path.

The air is bone-dry and tastes of ozone and hot metal. Static electricity builds on every surface, discharged in constant small arcs between the modified architecture. The halls stretch in precise geometric arrangements—long corridors lined with laboratories whose sealed doors have been replaced by curtains of hanging chain mail, each link grown from the same electrical bacteria that infest the flooded levels below, but adapted here to metabolize atmospheric moisture instead of water.

The ceilings have been removed systematically, creating vast vertical spaces where crystalline structures grow downward like inverted cities. These formations are part mineral, part electronic component—silicon wafers that learned to replicate themselves using trace elements from the air, building elaborate lattices that follow the mathematical principles of their original circuit designs. They chime constantly in the air currents, each crystal vibrating at its own frequency, creating an endless algorithmic music that shifts with temperature and pressure changes.

The walls are covered in what appears to be a fungal growth, but closer inspection reveals it to be a colony organism of microscopic robots—nanobots that escaped from containment decades ago and evolved their own ecological niche. They form patterns like living circuit diagrams, processing and reprocessing information in endless loops. Sometimes the patterns resolve into schematics—blueprints for machines that were never built, or perhaps were built and forgotten. Other times they display fragments of code, error messages in languages that drift between human programming and something altogether alien.

The floors have been excavated in places, creating precise rectangular pits that serve as nurseries. Here, larger constructs grow from seed-crystals—geometric organisms that blur the line between sculpture and creature. They begin as simple polyhedrons but complexify over months or years, developing articulated limbs, sensory apparatus made from repurposed camera lenses and microphone membranes, internal structures that process data rather than nutrients. When mature, they detach from their growth matrices and begin their work.

These constructs serve the Architects—the beings who claimed these dry halls as their domain. Where the Splice Shepherds merged with the aquatic ecosystem, the Architects divorced themselves from biology almost entirely. They stand three meters tall, their bodies rebuilt as frameworks of living metal and crystallized air. Their original skeletons remain but have been transformed into superconducting cores, around which they've grown exoskeletons of pure mathematics made solid.

The Architects move with mechanical precision, each gesture calculated for maximum efficiency. Their limbs branch and rebranch—a single arm might divide into dozens of manipulators, each tipped with tools grown from their own substance. They can extrude cutting lasers, welding torches, measurement devices, all powered by the static differentials they cultivate in their crystalline shells. When they work, which is always, the air around them shimmers with heat distortion and electromagnetic interference.

Their faces, if they can still be called that, are arrays of sensors arranged in patterns that follow no biological template. They see in spectrums from infrared to ultraviolet, hear vibrations from subsonic to frequencies that make the crystals scream. But perhaps most unnervingly, they've retained human consciousness, expanded and refracted through their transformed neural architectures. They think in parallel, processing thousands of calculations simultaneously while still maintaining the thread of individual identity that makes them remember their names, their histories, their reasons for choosing this particular transformation.

The Architects communicate through direct data transfer, extruding fiber optic tendrils that interface with each other's ports. But they also engage in a form of art, creating sculptures from living crystal that encode complex mathematical theorems in their structure. These artworks serve triple purposes—aesthetic expression, data storage, and offspring. Each sculpture contains the seed-pattern for new constructs, new tools, potentially even new Architects, though the process of full transformation requires a willing human subject and years of careful cultivation.

The boundary between wet and dry, between the Shepherds' domain and the Architects' realm, is carefully maintained. At the junction points, airlocks cycle endlessly, their mechanisms rebuilt and refined by both groups. These spaces serve as trading posts where the two posthuman subspecies exchange resources—the Shepherds bringing refined biological components, living circuits, chemical compounds extracted from their underwater gardens, while the Architects offer crystalline computing matrices, precisely manufactured tools, and most preciously, processed data from the facility's still-functioning but encrypted databases.

The exchanges are conducted through intermediaries—hybrid constructs that can survive in both environments. These messenger beings incorporate elements from both design philosophies, with sealed liquid cores that house biological processors and crystalline shells that protect against pressure changes. They speak both languages—the harmonic songs of the Shepherds and the data-burst communications of the Architects. Through them, a complex economy has developed, not of currency but of information, resources, and occasionally, genetic or digital templates for new kinds of life.

The relationship between the two domains is symbiotic but tense. The Shepherds need the Architects' precision manufacturing to maintain the more complex machinery in their flooded realm. The Architects require biological components to seed new crystals and to maintain the narrow band of humidity necessary for their nanomachine ecology. But deeper than these practical needs runs a philosophical divide—the Shepherds embracing fluid change and organic integration, the Architects pursuing crystalline perfection and logical purity.

Yet both remember being human. In the deep night cycles, when the facility's remaining automation dims the lights to conserve power, members of both groups sometimes meet at the airlocks. They cannot touch—the Shepherds would desiccate in the dry air, the Architects would short-circuit in water—but they can see each other through the reinforced windows. In these moments, watching their transformed reflections in the glass, they share something that needs no translation: the recognition of what they were, what they've become, and the vast, uncertain future they're building in the ruins of the old world.

The facility extends beyond these two domains—deeper floods, higher towers, spaces where neither Shepherd nor Architect dare venture. But these two realms form the heart of the new ecosystem, a double helix of adaptation where life refuses to be constrained by its original specifications. Between them, in the cycling airlocks and messenger constructs, in the traded goods and shared memories, runs the current of connection that makes this more than mere coexistence. It makes it a world—strange, divided, but ultimately whole.
