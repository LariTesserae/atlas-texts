The Dry Hills

Above the cloud line, where the air grows thin and the perpetual grey ceiling of the lower world becomes a sea of white viewed from above, the hills remember what the world was like before. Here the sun arrives unfiltered, harsh and honest, baking red earth and grey stone into a landscape of sharp shadows and bleached bone colors.

This is mining country, though what they mine would seem like madness to anyone but those who've lived through the drowning of the valleys below. The primary excavations chase seams of salt—vast deposits laid down when ancient seas evaporated, now more precious than the copper and coal that first drew settlers to these heights. The salt mines honeycomb the hills, their entrances marked by white spillage that looks like snow from a distance but burns bare skin on contact. Narrow-gauge railways switch back across the faces of the ridges, carrying containers of processed salt down to the fog line, where caravans of sure-footed mules take over, their pack frames specially treated to resist corrosion.

The settlements perch on ridgelines and cluster around springs, built from the same red stone they're carved into. No wood here—it's too precious, must be hauled from below the fog line where trees can still grow. The buildings huddle low against the constant wind, their flat roofs serving as collection points for the rare but violent rains that sweep across the heights. Every surface not carved from living rock is painted white to reflect the punishing sun. Cisterns, carved deep into bedrock and sealed with imported wax, hold water more carefully guarded than any mineral wealth.

The vegetation exists in defiant patches—thorny acacias with leaves like leather, succulent groundcovers that store moisture in swollen stems, grasses that grow in brief eruptions after rain then cure to golden hay in the heat. The gardeners here practice a different art than their vertical counterparts in the drowned city. They build stone walls to trap precious soil, channel rare rainfall through carved gutters, shade tender shoots with precisely placed stones. A single fruit tree, coaxed to maturity over decades, becomes a family treasure, its harvest celebrated with the same reverence the lowlanders reserve for functioning pumps.

Animals move at dawn and dusk, avoiding the killing midday heat. Wild goats, escapees from ancient herds, pick impossible paths across cliff faces. Ravens patrol the thermals, their calls echoing off canyon walls—they've grown fat on mine refuse and the offerings left at crossroad shrines. Snakes sun themselves on flat stones, disappearing into crevices at the vibration of footsteps. At night, when the temperature plummets, small mammals emerge—rock hyraxes that look like tailless groundhogs, various mice and voles that survive on seeds and insects, the occasional glimpse of the small desert cats that hunt them.

But the dominant life here is lithic, geological. The rocks themselves seem more alive than in the drowning lands—exposed to wind and temperature extremes, they crack and exfoliate, revealing layers of color and crystal. The miners read these stones like the pumpkeepers read their water levels, understanding from subtle changes in grain and hue where the salt runs true, where underground water might be found, where the earth might give way. They've developed a sophisticated understanding of the relationship between the visible surface and the hidden depths—knowledge that passes from generation to generation along with claims to particular excavations.

The fog line marks the boundary between worlds. On clear mornings, the people of the heights can look down on the cloud sea that hides the drowning cities, watching it stretch to the horizon like a second ocean. Sometimes the fog climbs, wreathing the lower settlements in cold grey fingers before retreating. Sometimes it drops, revealing more of the middle slopes where scraggly forests still cling to existence. The children born above the line grow up with this boundary as a fact of existence—below is the grey world of water and moss, above is the bright world of stone and salt.

---

The people of the Dry Hills carry the desert in their bones—spare, essential, worn down to what endures.

The **Salt Miners** work in dynasties, each family controlling specific galleries passed down through generations. They enter the mines before dawn, escaping the heat but also following traditions older than memory—salt mining by full daylight invites disaster, they say, though no one remembers why. In the perpetual cool of the tunnels, lit by carbide lamps that cast harsh shadows on crystalline walls, they work with tools designed for precision rather than force. Salt requires a different touch than metal or coal—too much violence and it shatters into useless powder. They develop an intimacy with the stone, reading the flow of ancient waters in the sweep of deposits, tasting samples to determine purity. Their fingers grow permanently white-rimmed, salt worked into every whorl and ridge of their prints. At shift's end they emerge blinking into sunset, ghosts of the underground temporarily returned to the world of air.

The **Water Priests** tend the cisterns with a devotion that makes the lowland pumpkeepers look casual. Each settlement's water supply falls under their absolute control—they alone know the true levels, they alone decide the rations. They wear no special vestments beyond the brass keys to the cistern locks, but their authority exceeds that of any official. Children learn early to read their moods, to know which priest might allow an extra ration for a naming day, which will measure to the exact milliliter regardless of circumstances. The priests sleep beside their cisterns in small cells cut into the rock, waking at intervals through the night to check for leaks, to listen for the telltale sounds of evaporation or theft. They keep the old calendars that track the rain patterns going back centuries, calculating when the next storms might come, how much the settlement can afford to consume before then.

On the ridgelines, the **Wind Readers** maintain the equipment that measures and predicts the weather patterns crucial to survival. Their stations consist of arrays of instruments—barometers and anemometers salvaged from the old world, but also traditional tools like suspended stones that shift with pressure changes, frameworks of tied grass that sing different notes as the wind changes. They communicate by heliograph during the day, mirrors flashing coded messages across the vast distances between settlements. At night they use fires, specific combinations of smoke and flame that carry meaning. When the wind readers signal incoming storms, the entire population mobilizes to prepare collection systems, to secure anything that might blow away, to bring the animals under shelter. Their predictions have grown more erratic lately, the patterns they've memorized over generations suddenly unreliable.

The **Caravaners** move between the worlds, equally comfortable above and below the fog line. Their mule trains carry salt down and necessities up—wax, preserved foods, news from the drowning lands. They know every path through the transition zone where fog meets clear air, where the vegetation changes from desert scrub to cloud forest to the perpetual wet below. The mules they breed are remarkable animals, sure-footed as goats but strong as horses, with thick coats that shed water below and reflect heat above. The caravaners dress in layers, shedding and adding as they change altitude, their clothing a patchwork of highland wool and lowland waxed cotton. They're the first to know when things change in either world, carrying word of the rising waters to the heights, bringing reports of new salt strikes to the hungry cities below.

In workshops carved from stone, the **Tool Makers** practice metallurgy adapted to scarcity. With little fuel for forges, they've perfected the art of working metal with minimal heat, using the focused power of the sun through arrangements of polished metal mirrors. They repair more than they create, keeping ancient equipment functional through ingenious patches and adaptations. Every broken drill bit gets reforged into something smaller but still useful—awls, needles, surgical instruments. They trade their services for food and water, never charging more than a family can afford, understanding that their skills are what keep the mines operational and the settlements viable. Their apprentices learn patience above all, spending months just maintaining the mirror arrays before they're allowed to touch metal.

The **Botanists of the Heights** tend their scattered gardens with the focus of surgeons. Each plant represents years of effort—soil built grain by grain, water allocated drop by drop. They've developed strains of food plants that can survive on morning dew and brutal afternoon sun, though the yields are meager compared to the vertical farms below. Their seed vaults, carved deep into north-facing cliffs where temperatures stay constant, contain the genetic heritage of the highlands—varieties of grain and vegetables that exist nowhere else, adapted over generations to the particular extremes of life above the clouds. They trade seeds and cuttings more carefully than the miners trade salt, each exchange recorded in ledgers that track the genetic lines like genealogies.

The **Stone Carvers** shape the living rock into architecture and art. With wood scarce and metal precious, they've developed techniques for working stone that approach the precision of carpentry. They carve entire rooms from cliff faces, leaving pillars of living rock for support. Their tools are treasured—chisels passed down through families, hammers weighted perfectly for the local stone. Beyond the practical, they ornament the settlements with intricate facades that tell the stories of their places—histories of rain and drought, successful mining seasons, marriages between settlements. The young apprentice by carrying water for the wet saws, learning to read the grain of different stones, developing the patience to spend weeks on a single doorway.

At the boundaries between settlements, the **Shrine Keepers** maintain the small structures that mark territories and honor the various powers the highlanders recognize—the sun that gives life and death, the rain that comes too rarely, the deep earth that holds both water and salt. The shrines are simple things, usually just arrangements of stones with niches for offerings, but each has its keeper who ensures the proper observances. They accept offerings from travelers—a handful of salt, a cup of precious water poured onto the ground, written prayers tucked between stones. The shrine keepers often come from those too injured or aged for mine work, finding purpose in tending these boundary spaces. They're also the settlement's memory, reciting the genealogies and territorial agreements that determine who can mine where, who has water rights to which springs.

The **Children of Two Worlds** form a special category—those born to highlander and lowlander parents, equally at home above and below the fog line. They often work as translators, not just of language but of entirely different ways of living. They understand why lowlanders fear the naked sun as much as highlanders fear drowning, can explain the visible stars to those who've never seen them and the moss gardens to those who know only stone and thorn. These cultural bridges become more crucial as the crisis below drives more refugees up into the hills, straining resources and traditions alike.

As news of the rising waters reaches the highlands through the caravan routes, the settlements face decisions that their established order never anticipated. The Salt Miners debate whether to accelerate extraction, knowing that desperate cities will pay any price but also that over-mining could collapse the galleries. The Water Priests calculate and recalculate, trying to determine if their cisterns can support an influx of refugees. The Wind Readers search their patterns for any sign of changing weather, any hope that increased rainfall might ease the coming pressure.

The highland peoples have always lived on margins—of water, of heat, of livable space. Their entire culture is built on scarcity, on making do with less, on reading the subtle signs that mean survival or death. But now they face something new: the potential arrival of thousands from below, bringing with them not just desperate need but entirely different assumptions about how life should be lived. The careful balance of the heights, maintained through generations of discipline and tradition, trembles on the edge of transformation.

In the end, both peoples—those of the drowning city and those of the dry hills—are children of the same broken world, adapted to different extremes of the same catastrophe. The fog line that divides them marks not just a change in altitude but in fundamental approaches to survival. Whether they can find common ground, literally and figuratively, in the narrowing band of livable space between the rising waters and the killing sun, remains to be written in salt and water, stone and moss.
