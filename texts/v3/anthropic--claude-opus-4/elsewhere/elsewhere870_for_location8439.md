The Sun Terraces of Altabrisa

Above the cloud line, where air grows thin and precious as gold, the city of Altabrisa spreads across a mountainside like an offering to the sky. Built in ascending rings of white stone terraces, each level carved from the living mountain and polished until it gleams, the settlement appears from a distance like a massive pearl necklace draped across granite shoulders. The lowest terrace sits just where persistent clouds gather, so that much of the year the city floats on a sea of white vapor, connected to the world below only by the Thousand Steps—a switchback stairway that vanishes into the mist like a road to nowhere.

Light rules here with an intensity that would blind a Threnody in minutes. The sun strikes the white stone and rebounds, amplified, reflected from terrace to terrace until the very air shimmers with brilliance. At dawn, the eastern faces of the buildings blaze gold while the west remains in purple shadow. By noon, the light is everywhere, inescapable, a presence as tangible as water in the Carved Hollows. The citizens move through this radiance like swimmers through a bright sea, their bodies adapted over generations to drink in what would scorch others.

The terraces step up the mountain in precise intervals, each ring containing its own district, its own purpose. The lowest levels, often shrouded in cloud, house the markets where traders from the valleys arrive gasping in the thin air, their goods hauled up the Thousand Steps by sure-footed porters. Here the white stone is stained with use—oil from countless hands, soot from cooking fires, the rust of iron goods from below. Higher rings grow progressively cleaner, whiter, until the uppermost terraces gleam like fresh snow, home to the Sun Speakers who interpret the light's messages and the observatories where the sky is mapped in infinite detail.

Water here is not the constant companion-enemy it is in the Hollows, but a precious visitor that must be courted, captured, preserved. The rains come rarely, and when they do, the entire city mobilizes. Ceramic channels built into every terrace guide the water into massive cisterns carved deep into the mountain's heart. These reservoirs, cool and dark, are the city's most guarded spaces. Water theft is punishable by blinding—a sentence that in Altabrisa means death, for without the ability to read the sun's nuances, one cannot survive.

The architecture embraces the light rather than hiding from it. Buildings are oriented to catch specific angles of sun at particular times of day and season. The Dawn Houses face east with massive windows of precious glass, their inhabitants waking with the first light. The Noon Towers rise highest, their tops crowned with mirror arrays that can signal to the valley towns below or concentrate sunlight for cooking and metalwork. The Twilight Mansions face west, their walls painted in reds and purples that glow like embers in the setting sun.

Glass is everywhere—windows, skylights, decorative panels that cast rainbows across white walls. The glassmasters of Altabrisa have perfected techniques unknown below, creating sheets so clear they seem like solid air, curves that bend light into useful patterns, colors that filter specific frequencies for growing rare high-altitude plants in protected conservatories. The greatest masters can blow glass bubbles filled with captured dawn light that will glow softly through the night—these are worth fortunes in the valleys and deeps below.

The air tastes different here—thin, crisp, with an edge like metal. Newcomers gasp and wheeze, their heads spinning with altitude sickness. But those born here have larger lungs, more efficient blood, hearts that beat steady and strong in the sparse atmosphere. They move with a peculiar grace, never hurrying, each motion calculated to conserve oxygen. In the highest districts, where the air is thinnest, the inhabitants speak in short, precise sentences, their language evolving toward efficiency.

Vegetation is sparse but intensely cultivated. The native plants grow low and tight against the stone—cushions of silver-green that bloom in violent purples and yellows for brief weeks. In walled gardens protected from the worst winds, citizens grow food crops adapted to the altitude: bitter greens that concentrate nutrients, root vegetables that plunge deep into pockets of hoarded soil, berry bushes that fruit in the brief summer. Every garden is a miracle of engineering, with windbreaks, sun-traps, and careful water management.

The winds are constant companions, ranging from gentle breezes that carry the scent of snow from higher peaks to howling gales that can pluck an unwary person from the terraces. The city's layout channels these winds, using them for ventilation, for powering prayer wheels and grain mills, for carrying messages via delicate paper gliders that skilled launchers can guide from terrace to terrace. Children learn wind-reading before they learn letters, understanding how each eddy and gust speaks of weather coming or passing.

At night, the cosmic display is incomparable. With no moisture to blur the air, stars burn like closer suns. The astronomers of Altabrisa have mapped constellations invisible from lower altitudes, tracked celestial movements that predict seasons and storms. The night sky is their library, their calendar, their oracle. During the darkest hours, when even the captured dawn-light dims, citizens navigate by starlight reflected and concentrated by carefully placed mirrors.

The city never fully sleeps but drowses through the night, conserving energy for the bright activity of day. In the pre-dawn darkness, the Sun Speakers climb to their stations on the highest peaks, preparing to greet the first light with songs that, they claim, help the sun find its way over the mountain. As the edge of light creeps across the valleys below, still sunk in shadow, Altabrisa begins to glow, terrace by terrace, like a lamp slowly turning up its wick.

This is a place of ascension, where humans have climbed as close to the sky as flesh allows, building a city that exists in conversation with light itself. Where the Carved Hollows huddle in darkness, Altabrisa stretches into brightness, its citizens walking roads of polished white stone between earth and heaven.

The Altabrisans have become creatures of light, their bodies evolved over centuries to thrive where others would burn and gasp. Their skin carries a permanent golden-brown patina, not just from sun exposure but from generations of selection—those who couldn't develop the protective pigmentation simply didn't survive to parenthood. But beneath this universal bronze, their skin does something remarkable: it glimmers. Tiny crystalline deposits in their dermis, built up from minerals in the high mountain water, catch and reflect light in subtle ways. In bright sun, an Altabrisan seems dusted with diamond powder.

Their eyes tell the story of adaptation most clearly. Where Threnodies developed wide pupils to catch scarce photons, Altabrisans evolved the opposite—irises that can contract to pinpricks, complex inner eyelids that filter light like living sunglasses, and a reflective layer behind the retina that gives their gazes an uncanny gleam. The colors range from amber to deep brown, with occasional sports of green that catch the light like polished jade. They see in the ultraviolet range, reading messages in flower petals and stone paintings invisible to lowlanders.

The constant sun and wind have shaped them into lean, efficient forms. Fat is scarce when every calorie must be hauled up the Thousand Steps or coaxed from thin soil. Their muscles are long and ropy, built for endurance rather than bursts of strength. Fingers grow long and sensitive, able to detect minute temperature variations in glass work or feel the quality of light on their skin like texture. Their feet develop thick, callused soles from walking on sun-heated stone, with toes that grip expertly—a necessity on terraces where one misstep means a fatal fall.

They dress in flowing layers of white and pale colors that reflect heat while trapping precious warmth when clouds roll in. The fabric is woven from the silk of high-altitude moths, creatures that feed on the bitter mountain plants and spin cocoons of incredible strength and lightness. The wealthy wear multiple gauzy layers that float and billow in the constant wind, creating personal atmospheres of moving shade. The poor make do with plant fibers bleached white by the sun, patched and re-patched until the garments are more mend than original cloth.

Over these base layers, they wear the city's true innovation: sun cloaks. These garments, passed down through families and worth small fortunes, are woven with thin metal threads in patterns that disperse dangerous radiation while trapping useful wavelengths. Master weavers spend decades learning the songs that guide the pattern-making, for one wrong thread can turn protection into magnification. The finest sun cloaks shimmer with their own light, storing solar energy during the day to provide gentle warmth through the cold nights.

Their hair grows thick but fine, usually worn long and elaborately braided to protect the scalp. They weave in small mirrors, crystals, and polished metal discs that serve both decoration and communication—a skilled Altabrisan can send complex messages across the terraces using hair ornaments and precise head movements. The styles change with status and profession: Sun Speakers wear their hair in high crowns that add height and authority, while gardeners bind theirs flat with clay that protects against the amplified light among the growing beds.

Children are born in the morning hours, tradition holding that first light should be the first thing a new soul sees. Pregnant women spend their final weeks in the Dawn Houses, timing labor to coincide with sunrise when possible. Infants are gradually exposed to increasing levels of light, their eyes checked daily for proper development of the protective structures. Those born with defects that prevent proper light filtering are not cast out—instead, they become the Night Workers, tending the cisterns and observatories, living in the twilight margins but still vital to the city's function.

The young learn by following shadows. Each child is assigned to a shadow-mentor, an adult whose movements they must track through a full day, learning how light changes with time and season. They play games of reflection, bouncing beams from mirror to mirror across the terraces, developing the precise control needed for the city's heliographic communications. By adolescence, they can read the sun's position without looking directly at it, feel storms approaching in the quality of light, predict the exact moment when afternoon clouds will roll in.

Altabrisans organize their society around the sun's path. The Dawn Families hold responsibility for beginning each day properly—maintaining the eastern mirrors, performing the songs of greeting, preparing the communal breakfast fires. The Noon Clans oversee the peak hours of work and trade, when the light is strongest and the terraces bustle with activity. The Twilight Houses guard the western approaches, tend the evening rituals, and preserve the stories told as darkness falls.

Marriage ceremonies happen at the moment of sunset, when day and night balance perfectly. Couples exchange vows while standing in beams of concentrated light, their shadows intertwining on white walls in patterns that supposedly predict the union's future. Divorces, rare but recognized, occur at dawn, with the separated partners walking in opposite directions as the sun rises between them.

They worship through precision. Where the Threnodies dance wildly at the Drum's edge, Altabrisans perform measured movements that track celestial mechanics. Their religious observances involve massive choreographed displays where hundreds move in sync across the terraces, their shadows creating fleeting geometries that honor the sun's path. The patterns, viewed from the highest peaks, tell stories of creation, destruction, and the eternal dance of light and darkness.

The criminals face justice in the light. Minor offenses earn time in the Brightness—forced to sit in amplified sunlight that leaves them temporarily snow-blind, stumbling through days of penance. Serious crimes merit the Shadow Judgment, where the accused stands trial at noon with mirrors focused on them from all sides, the community literally illuminating their guilt or innocence through collective will. The worst offenders are exiled down the Thousand Steps, forbidden to rise above the cloud line—a living death for those born to the light.

Their dead ascend through smoke. Bodies are burned on pyres of aromatic mountain wood, the flames fed with oils that burn in colors matching the deceased's life—white for children, gold for adults, deep purple for elders. The smoke rises through specially constructed chimneys that spiral up from the terraces, carrying the essence skyward. Families keep prisms that split sunlight into rainbows, believing each color holds memories of those who've gone into the light.

The Altabrisans dream in brilliance—visions of suns within suns, of light that speaks and shapes reality. Their artists work in media unimaginable in the depths: paintings that only appear in certain angles of light, sculptures of ice and crystal that must be viewed at specific times or vanish, gardens where the shadows of plants tell time as accurately as any clock. Their musicians play instruments that ring in the thin air with piercing clarity—bone flutes, silver drums stretched with moth silk, wind harps that sing without human touch.

They age into transparency. The oldest Altabrisans seem to glow from within, their skin so thin and crystalline that veins show like maps of blue rivers. They move slowly but with absolute precision, no gesture wasted in the thin air. The very old claim to hear the light itself, to understand its language of wavelength and frequency. They sit in the highest places, eyes closed against the brightness they no longer need to see, interpreting the sun's messages for those still bound to visible perception.

Where the Threnodies flow like water, the Altabrisans burn like stars—bright, focused, consuming themselves in the act of living. They have built a civilization on the edge of the possible, where the margin between life and death is as thin as the air they breathe. Their bodies are lenses, focusing the overwhelming light into useful illumination. Their city is a prism, splitting the white glare into its component beauties.

In the depths of the Carved Hollows, children play at "Deep Dark." On the heights of Altabrisa, they play "Sun Catch," dancing between beams of concentrated light that could blind or burn, learning to move in the spaces between brilliance. Both games teach survival, but in opposite schools.

The two cities know of each other through the Middle Traders—those who live in the cloud layer between extremes, ferrying goods and messages up and down. The Threnodies send up mushroom wine, carved stone artifacts, maps of the deep waters, and phosphorescent molds that the Altabrisans prize for their gentle night-light. In return, the Altabrisans send down sun-glass, concentrated light in crystal bottles, dried fruits that taste of sunshine, and most preciously, news of weather—for the storms that barely dampen the terraces can mean catastrophic flooding in the Hollows below.

Messages travel slowly between the cities. A Threnody trader, ascending the Thousand Steps, must rest for days at each waystation, acclimatizing to the thinning air and growing light. An Altabrisan descending faces the opposite challenge—the increasing pressure, the dimming world, the weight of moisture that makes their lungs labor. The few who make the full journey between cities return changed, carrying a bit of each world in their bodies.

There are stories, possibly myths, of individuals who've lived in both cities—spending years in the depths before ascending to the heights, or vice versa. These Threshold Walkers, if they exist, would be living bridges between water and light, darkness and brilliance. Their children, the stories say, would be something new—able to see in both shadow and glare, to breathe both thick and thin air, to read the languages of both echo and reflection.

The cities mirror each other in their extremes. Where the Carved Hollows weep, Altabrisa burns. Where one hoards water, the other hoards light. Both have shaped their inhabitants into new forms of human, adapted to conditions that would kill the unprepared. Both have created beauty from necessity—the Threnodies in their phosphorescent gardens and water music, the Altabrisans in their light sculptures and celestial dances.

Between them lies the whole world—the cloud forests where normal humans live, the valleys where water and light exist in balance, the places where children don't need to evolve gills or UV-filtering eyes to survive. But at the extremes, pressing against the boundaries of the possible, the Threnodies and Altabrisans have become something more and less than human. They are what we might be, if we followed water down or light up to their absolute conclusions.

In the Carved Hollows, they say the sun is a myth, a story told to frighten children.

On the Sun Terraces, they say the same of the endless dark.

Both are wrong. Both are right. The world contains both extremes and all the gradients between, each inhabited by those who've learned to call that particular band of the spectrum home.
