Far below the Breath Columns, where the mountain's roots sink into a different kind of earth, lies the Maw of Ordinary Sorrows.

The descent from the columns to the Maw takes three days on foot, following treacherous switchbacks carved by seasonal waterfalls that exist only in memory. The path drops through seven distinct climate zones, each cooler and denser than the last, until the scorching whistle-song of the columns fades to a distant rumor, like tinnitus after a concert. Down here, the air is thick and damp, pooling in a circular depression five miles across where the mountain's base meets an ancient seabed, the limestone pocked with fossils of things that swam before the earth rose.

The Maw itself defies easy description. It is not quite a sinkhole, not quite a lake, not quite a wound. The depression is filled with water so densely saturated with minerals that it moves like mercury, its surface tension strong enough to support the weight of fallen leaves for hours before they finally break through and sink. The water is the color of tarnished silver in direct sunlight, but in the shadows—which dominate this bowl surrounded by steep walls—it appears black as pupilless eyes.

Nothing grows within fifty feet of the water's edge. The soil is not poisoned but simply exhausted, drained of some essential quality that even the hardiest moss requires. Dead trees stand like cemetery markers, their wood bleached white and harder than stone, preserved by whatever emanates from the depths. These phantom forests create natural windbreaks, and in their shelter, silence accumulates like sediment. It is possible to stand at the Maw's edge and hear one's own blood moving, one's own synapses firing—a biological static that becomes maddening after prolonged exposure.

The water moves, though there is no current. Slow spirals form and dissipate, each the size of a house, rotating counterclockwise with hypnotic lethargy. Sometimes these spirals birth whirlpools that descend perhaps ten feet before collapsing. Other times, the water bulges upward in perfect domes that hold their shape for minutes before subsiding. The locals—who live no closer than the ridge surrounding the depression—call these movements "the breathing," though they are careful to specify that nothing is breathing, merely that breathing is happening.

On the eastern shore, where a dried creek bed once fed the Maw, stands a cluster of structures too organized to be natural, too simple to be ruins. Seven columns of stacked stones, each precisely nine feet tall, arranged in a pattern that corresponds to no known astronomical or mathematical principle. The stones are local limestone, but each has been carved with a single hole through its center. When the wind is right—which is to say, when there is any wind at all in this stillness—these holes produce no sound whatsoever, as if they swallow vibration rather than channel it.

It is here, among these silent anti-columns, that the Tender makes its home.

---

The Tender of the Maw of Ordinary Sorrows is a creature of profound and terrible patience. It resembles, at first glance, a heap of discarded fabric—layers upon layers of what might be skin or might be silk, gray-brown and wrinkled, piled between two of the standing stones. But watch long enough, and the heap reveals its organization: these are wings, hundreds of them, folded and refolded with origami precision. Each wing is unique in size and shape, though all share the same mottled coloration and paper-thin texture.

The creature's body, when it can be glimpsed between the wings, is surprisingly small—no larger than a child's torso. It has no visible head, no obvious sensory organs, just a smooth, grub-like core from which the wings emerge in irregular rows. The wings do not flap or beat; instead, they unfurl one at a time with deliberate slowness, extending to catch breezes too faint for human skin to detect. Each wing, once deployed, can rotate independently, adjusting its angle with microscopic precision.

The Tender feeds on sorrow, but not in any mystical or metaphorical sense. The mineral-heavy water of the Maw contains trace elements of a compound found nowhere else—a salt formed when certain neurotransmitters break down under exact conditions of pressure and mineral saturation. This salt precipitates out of the water only when specific emotional states have been present upstream, carried down through groundwater permeation from the settlements above. The Tender's wings are specialized filters, each one attuned to slightly different concentrations and combinations of these emotion-derived minerals.

During the day, the creature remains motionless, easily mistaken for debris or a peculiar rock formation. But at dusk, it begins its work. Wings unfurl in careful sequence, creating a complex array that can sample air currents from every direction. When it detects the right chemical signature, wings coordinate to create channels and eddies that draw the laden air toward its body. There, specialized pores absorb the minerals directly, metabolizing grief into energy with an efficiency that defies conventional biology.

The Tender's relationship with the Maw is one of cultivation. Through patient centuries, it has learned to influence the water's movement through minute adjustments in air pressure created by its wing configurations. The spirals and domes are not random but carefully orchestrated to bring mineral-rich water from the depths to the surface, where evaporation can concentrate the emotional salts. On nights when the mineralization is particularly rich—usually following tragedies in the mountain settlements—the Tender can create whirlpools that sing, their rotation matching the exact frequency of human weeping.

The creature reproduces through division, but only under specific circumstances. When it has absorbed enough of a particular type of sorrow—the grief of a parent for a child, the melancholy of absolute solitude, the specific ache of remembering joy during despair—a wing will detach and transform. The severed wing folds in on itself repeatedly, becoming smaller and denser until it resembles a seed. These seeds do not grow new Tenders immediately; instead, they wait, sometimes for decades, until they encounter a sorrow identical to the one that created them. Only then do they unfold into new creatures, already ancient with inherited sadness.

Local shamans speak of the Tender with a mixture of reverence and caution. They say it serves a necessary function, preventing the emotional minerals from building to toxic concentrations in the groundwater. Without the Tender's constant filtration, they claim, the mountain's aquifers would become poisoned with accumulated grief, creating springs that induce depression in any who drink from them. Whether this is true or merely folklore, it is documented that villages whose water sources bypass the Maw have higher rates of persistent melancholia.

The stone columns around the Tender serve a purpose that becomes clear only during certain celestial alignments. When specific stars shine through the carved holes, they create patterns of light that the Tender's wings can manipulate, bending the beams to write temporary messages on the Maw's surface. These light-inscriptions last only seconds before the water's movement erases them, but patient observers have recorded fragments: coordinates to places of ancient suffering, chemical formulas for compounds that don't yet exist, what might be names in scripts that predate human presence on the mountain.

Three times in recorded history, the Tender has left its post. Each absence coincided with a catastrophic emotional event somewhere on the mountain—a plague that emptied entire villages, a betrayal that shattered a centuries-old alliance, a religious revelation that drove hundreds to despair. During these journeys, witnesses describe the creature's movement as both horrifying and beautiful: its hundreds of wings operating in perfect coordination to achieve a form of flight that looks more like swimming through air, its body undulating with peristaltic grace. It travels directly to the source of the emotional disturbance, hovers for days or weeks while its wings work frantically to process the overwhelming abundance of grief, then returns to the Maw, visibly larger, to resume its patient vigil.

The Tender does not communicate in any conventional sense, but those who spend time near it report a peculiar phenomenon. Sorrows they thought they had processed years ago surface with startling clarity, not as pain but as memory drained of its sting. Some describe it as watching their grief from outside, seeing its shape and texture without feeling its weight. A few claim this experience allowed them to finally release traumas they had carried for decades. Others warn that the Tender's presence can be addictive—that there are those who return again and again, offering up every small sadness to feel that strange, hollow relief.

In winter, when the Maw sometimes freezes despite its mineral content, the Tender enters a state of hibernation. Its wings fold so tightly they become indistinguishable from its body, creating a cocoon of overlapping membranes. Inside this chrysalis, it digests the year's harvest of sorrow, breaking down complex griefs into their component parts, perhaps learning from them, perhaps simply processing them into energy for the seasons ahead. The locals know to watch for its emergence as a sign of spring—when the first wing unfurls, winter's grip on the mountain begins to loosen.

The Tender of the Maw of Ordinary Sorrows is a necessary monster, a creature evolved to fill an ecological niche no one fully understands. It is ancient beyond measure, patient beyond comprehension, performing its grim harvest with the mechanical precision of a natural process and the delicate attention of an artist. Those who study it too long report a peculiar side effect: their own sorrows begin to feel geological, their griefs taking on the weight and patience of stone. Whether this is healing or merely another form of mineralization remains an open question.

Between the Breath Columns and the Maw of Ordinary Sorrows runs a path that defies mapping. It is not that the route changes—the mountain's geography remains constant—but rather that the journey itself resists consistency. Those who make the descent report wildly different experiences: some claim it takes three days, others insist on a week, and a few swear they aged years in what their companions measured as hours. The only constant is the Gradient, a phenomenon that makes this passage as much a psychological transition as a physical one.

The Gradient begins subtly, at the lower edge of the Breath Columns where the acoustic cacophony starts to fade. Here, the last tendrils of the Siphon stretch between weathered rocks, its filaments growing thicker and more visible as they descend, as if the creature becomes more itself the farther it ventures from its ethereal heights. These terminal extensions serve as the first waypoints—travelers learn to follow the gossamer threads, which always lead to stable paths, avoiding the loose scree that can send the unwary tumbling into ravines that weren't there the day before.

As one descends, the Siphon's filaments gradually change character. What begins as nearly invisible webbing thickens into ropy strands, then braided cables that pulse with visible fluids. By the time the path reaches the cloud forest that clings to the mountain's middle elevations, these extensions have taken on an almost architectural quality, creating bridges across chasms and handholds on sheer faces. The locals call these structures "the Siphon's conscience," believing they represent the creature's ancient memory of when this route was easier, before some prehistoric catastrophe reformed the mountain's face.

The cloud forest itself exists in perpetual twilight, its canopy so dense that noon feels like dusk. Here, the moisture that barely touches the Breath Columns accumulates in staggering abundance. Every surface drips; every breath tastes of rain that never quite falls. The trees are giants, their trunks wider than houses, their roots forming natural staircases that spiral down the increasingly vertical terrain. It is in this forest that the first emotional sediment begins to accumulate.

Travelers report a peculiar sensation as they descend through the clouds: memories surface unbidden, but not quite as they remember them. A childhood birthday becomes tinged with melancholy it didn't possess at the time. A moment of triumph carries an aftertaste of loss. These are the early effects of approaching the Maw's sphere of influence, where the emotion-saturated groundwater begins to affect perception. The mountain itself becomes a therapist, drawing out sorrows that will eventually flow down to feed the Tender.

Halfway down, where the cloud forest gives way to dry scrubland, stands the Testimony Garden. No one built it, yet it is unmistakably artificial—hundreds of small cairns arranged in spirals that mirror the movement patterns of the Maw's water far below. Each cairn contains objects left by previous travelers: photographs wrapped in wax paper, jewelry tarnished beyond recognition, books whose pages have fused into solid blocks of pulp. These are offerings, though to what god or purpose remains unclear. What is known is that those who leave something behind report easier passage through the lower reaches, as if paying a toll to grief itself.

The Siphon's cables reach even here, though they've transformed again. Now they resemble calcified arteries, mineral deposits from centuries of dissolved nutrients forming baroque encrustations along their length. These fossilized extensions no longer pulse with life but serve as conduits for something else. During the migration seasons—spring and fall, when the emotional minerals in the groundwater reach peak concentrations—these dead channels sing with harmonic frequencies that match neither the Breath Columns above nor the Maw below. It is a liminal music, the sound of transformation itself.

Wildlife along the Gradient exists in similar states of transition. The scaled swifts that hunt in the Columns have cousins here—heavier birds with dull feathers that can no longer manage the acrobatic flights of their highland relatives. They perch on the Siphon's calcified cables, feeding on insects that emerge from cracks in the mineral deposits. Lower still, these birds give way to their own descendants: flightless things with vestigial wings that hop between the cairns of the Testimony Garden, their songs grown mournful and complex.

The water changes too. What begins as rare condensation in the Columns becomes occasional streams by the cloud forest, then reliable springs in the scrubland. But this water is wrong—too heavy, too silvered with minerals, carrying the chemical memory of every sorrow it has touched on its journey down. Animals avoid it; plants that drink from it grow twisted, their leaves arranged in patterns that suggest faces frozen in moments of loss. Only the pathway moss, a species found nowhere else, thrives on this tainted water, forming soft carpets that guide travelers toward safer routes.

As the path approaches the Maw's depression, the very air grows heavy with accumulated emotion. The pressure is physical—a weight on the chest that makes breathing laborious, a thickness that turns simple movements into efforts of will. This is where many turn back, unable to bear the concentrated grief that saturates the atmosphere. Those who persist report a curious phenomenon: their own sorrows begin to feel external, as if the mountain were drawing them out through the skin, preparing them for harvest.

The final descent is the most treacherous, not for its physical dangers but for its psychological ones. The path splits and re-splits, each branch leading to a different viewing point of the Maw. But these are not merely alternate routes—each path seems to filter for different types of sorrow. One draws those grieving lost loves, another those mourning squandered chances. The paths converge at the depression's edge, but travelers arrive having been sorted by their sadness, prepared for the Tender's specific appetite.

The connection between the Columns and the Maw runs deeper than geography. The Siphon's extensions, which seem to terminate at the cloud forest, actually continue underground, following mineral veins and aquifer channels all the way to the Maw's depths. Here they interface with the Tender's sensory network—root-like structures that the creature extends into the mineral-rich water during its feeding cycles. Through these deep connections, the two organisms share something that might be called communication: chemical signals encoding information about prey, weather, and the emotional climate of the mountain above.

During the rare alignment events when stars shine through the stone columns around the Tender to create their light-messages, observers at the Breath Columns report simultaneous phenomena. The Siphon retracts all its hunting filaments and weaves them into dense patterns that match the light-inscriptions below. For these brief moments, the entire mountain becomes a single instrument, its two strangest inhabitants performing a duet in frequencies beyond human perception. What they exchange in these moments—warnings, memories, some form of reproductive synchronization—remains a mystery.

The shamans say the mountain was broken once, split by the same catastrophe that created the Breath Columns. Before that sundering, the Siphon and the Tender were one creature, a vast organism that processed both physical and emotional nutrients in an endless cycle. The separation forced each half to specialize, to become more itself in isolation while maintaining threads of connection that neither fully understands. Whether this is truth or mythology hardly matters; the story explains something essential about the relationship between height and depth, between predator and processor, between the collection of experience and its digestion into meaning.

Travelers who complete the journey from Columns to Maw often struggle to articulate what they've experienced. The physical descent is challenging but manageable; it's the emotional archaeology that leaves them changed. They speak of understanding their sorrows differently, of seeing their griefs as part of a larger metabolism that includes stone and water, height and depth, creature and landscape. Some return to the heights seeking the Siphon's clarifying hunger; others remain near the Maw, offering their sadnesses to the Tender's patient filtration.

The path between them is more than a route—it is a process, a digestion, a fall through emotional strata that mirrors the geological layers of the mountain itself. Each traveler becomes part of the connection, carrying minerals and memories, songs and sorrows, from one impossible creature to another. In this way, the mountain maintains its circulation, its breath and its tears forever cycling through forms that shouldn't exist but do, processing human experience into something larger, stranger, and infinitely more patient than any single grief could ever be.
