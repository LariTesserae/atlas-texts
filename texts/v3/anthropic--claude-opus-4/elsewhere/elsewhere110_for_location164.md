Beyond the vertical ribbons, following the mountain's curve around its eastern face, the stone changes character entirely. Here, the rock refuses its own solidity—it exists in a state of perpetual decision, forever choosing between states of matter. This is the Flux Gardens, though "garden" hardly captures the reality of a place where cultivation and geology engage in constant negotiation.

The stone here ripples. Not metaphorically, but in slow, actual waves that take days to crest and break. The rock behaves like thick honey under summer sun, flowing in terraces that reshape themselves according to principles no Sinter fully understands. Sometimes the stone remembers it should be solid and freezes mid-flow, creating fantastic formations—frozen waterfalls of granite, baroque spirals of basalt caught in the act of becoming. Other times it forgets entirely and pools in temporary lakes of liquid mineral that reflect not the sky but possibilities: what the mountain might become if it chose differently.

The Gardeners who work these spaces are changed by their proximity to indecisive stone. Where the Sinters accumulate minerals in predictable patterns, the Gardeners fluctuate. Their bodies have learned the stone's uncertainty. A Gardener's hand might pass through what appears to be solid rock, finding a pocket of not-quite-matter that yields like thick air. Their bones density shifts—sometimes heavy enough to anchor them against the stone's movement, sometimes light enough that they seem to float just above the surface, tethered by will more than weight.

They wear robes of woven stone-silk, a material that can only be harvested when the rock reaches a particular state between liquid and vapor. The fibers must be pulled from the stone at exactly the right moment, spun while they still remember fluidity, woven before they decide to become brittle. These garments share their wearers' fluctuating nature—sometimes hanging heavy as chain mail, sometimes floating like mist, always moving slightly out of sync with normal physics.

The Gardeners came to this work through exile or calling—it amounts to the same thing. Some were Sinters who descended too deep, touched stone in states that shouldn't exist, and found themselves unable to maintain stable mineralization. Others were born to Gardener families, conceived and raised in proximity to the flux, their bodies never learning the certainty that solid matter should possess. A few claim to have emerged from the stone itself during particularly active periods, though whether these are humans who passed through rock or rock that briefly achieved humanity remains beautifully unclear.

What they cultivate defies simple categorization. In one terrace, crystalline structures grow like plants, branching and budding according to mineral genetics. The Gardeners tend them with tools that phase between states—solid enough to prune crystal branches, liquid enough to flow around delicate growth. These crystal plants feed on uncertainty itself, thriving where the stone can't decide what it wants to be, sending roots deep into zones of maximum flux.

In another section, they grow concepts. Not metaphorically—actual concepts take root in the indecisive stone. The Idea of Ascending grows as a twisted vine of silver-white mineral that phases in and out of visibility. When mature, its fruits can be harvested and processed into a substance that, when consumed, allows one to understand vertical space in ways the mind normally cannot grasp. The Notion of Density spreads as a low groundcover, its leaves teaching lessons about mass and matter to anyone who touches them with the proper intentionality.

The Gardeners communicate through vibration and phase. Words spoken aloud might arrive before they're uttered or after the speaker has forgotten them, depending on the local stone's temporal density. Instead, they've developed a language of induced resonance—touching the stone in particular ways that send vibrations through the flux, creating patterns that carry meaning. A conversation between Gardeners looks like an elaborate dance, their hands and feet creating rhythms in the rock that propagate according to the medium's current state.

Their tools are grown rather than forged. In certain pools of liquid stone, they submerge templates—forms that suggest rather than dictate what a tool might become. The stone considers these suggestions, sometimes for days, before crystallizing around the idea. The resulting implements maintain connection to the flux. A pruning blade might be solid when cutting crystal plants but phase through human flesh without harm. A carrying vessel could hold liquid stone in one moment, abstract concepts the next.

The relationship between the Flux Gardens and the vertical city creates constant tension and exchange. Mineral vapors from the industrial processes drift around the mountain face, and where they encounter the indecisive stone, new phenomena emerge. Sometimes a cloud of factory exhaust will trigger a wave of solidification, freezing acres of flux into temporary stability. Other times, the Gardens' uncertainty creeps back along the vapor trails, causing sections of the city's solid platforms to momentarily question their own existence.

Trade between the two zones happens at the Transition Markets—spaces where the stone can't quite decide whether it belongs to the solid city or the fluctuating gardens. Here, Sinters bring processed minerals, condensation collected in specific atmospheric conditions, and the crystallized remains of their dead. Gardeners offer concept-fruits, phase-silk, and most preciously, bottles of liquid possibility—stone caught in its most undecided state, useful for applications no solid matter could achieve.

The Gardeners organize themselves in Probability Clusters—groups that share not blood or mineral accumulation but statistical likelihood. Members of the same Cluster tend to experience similar fluctuations, their bodies choosing comparable states at corresponding times. This synchronized uncertainty allows them to work together on projects that would otherwise be impossible—one Gardener might hold a tool solid while another phases through stone to place it, their states coordinating through pure probability.

Children in the Flux Gardens exist in superposition until their eighth year. Before then, they flicker between states too rapidly for full manifestation in any one. They're raised in nurseries where the stone has been coaxed into maximum uncertainty, spaces where a child's natural indecision finds harmony with the environment. Only gradually do they learn to hold themselves in one state long enough to interact consistently with others.

The Gardeners' relationship with time differs radically from the Sinters'. Where the city-dwellers mark Accumulations, the Gardeners navigate Probability Waves—periods when the stone's uncertainty crests and all possibilities become briefly more likely. During these waves, past and future gardening projects exist simultaneously. A Gardener might find themselves tending plants they haven't yet decided to grow, or harvesting concepts that were abandoned generations ago but refuse to fully cease.

Their art embraces impermanence to a degree that makes even the Sinters' temporary condensation murals seem fixed. Gardeners create sculptures that exist in probability space—forms that might be seen depending on the viewer's phase state and the local stone's decision matrix. Their music incorporates the sound of stone choosing—the subtle harmonics that emerge in the moment rock decides to flow or freeze. Performances can only happen when the conditions align, making each concert a unique convergence of probability and artistic intent.

The deep spaces of the Flux Gardens, where the stone's indecision reaches maximum, are tended by the Phase Shepherds. These Gardeners have learned to exist in multiple states simultaneously, their consciousness spread across probability space. They guide the formation of new garden terraces, coaxing the stone toward decisions that will create useful growing spaces while maintaining enough uncertainty for cultivation. The Phase Shepherds speak of places deeper still, where the mountain practices unmaking itself, where anti-stone grows in inverse gardens tended by gardeners who might not exist.

Food in the Flux Gardens comes from probability itself. Certain plants exist in multiple states of ripeness simultaneously—a fruit might be green, perfect, and overripe in the same moment. Gardeners have learned to harvest selectively, reaching through probability space to pluck only the perfectly ripe iteration while leaving the others to continue their development. Meals become exercises in phase navigation, with diners shifting their state to match their food's current decision about edibility.

The Gardeners' architecture flows with the stone. Buildings exist as suggestions rather than certainties—frameworks that guide the rock's flow when it chooses liquidity, anchors that hold fast during solid phases. A Gardener's home might be a mansion when the stone feels expansive, a single room when it contracts, or occasionally nothing at all when the local flux decides architecture is too much commitment. They've learned to live with this uncertainty, keeping their essential belongings in phase-locked containers that maintain existence regardless of their surroundings' choices.

Some Gardeners undergo the Dissolution—a practice where they allow their own certainty to erode until they exist in pure probability. These Dissolved Ones can move through the Flux Gardens by choosing different likelihood paths, appearing wherever the stone's uncertainty makes their presence possible. They serve as messengers and guides, though their messages sometimes arrive before being sent or dissolve before reaching their destination.

The boundary between the Flux Gardens and the vertical city blurs during the Convergences—rare events when the mountain's different aspects align. During these times, solid platforms might begin to flow while liquid stone suddenly crystallizes. Sinters find themselves phasing, while Gardeners achieve unprecedented stability. These events are both celebrated and feared, bringing opportunities for exchange and understanding but also the risk that the two realities might contaminate each other irreversibly.

In the highest reaches of the Flux Gardens, where the stone's indecision approaches the philosophical, grows the Garden of Unasked Questions. Here, the Gardeners cultivate problems that haven't yet been formulated, tending to answers for inquiries the mountain is still considering whether to pose. The fruits of these gardens are prized by both communities—eating them doesn't provide knowledge but rather the ability to recognize when a crucial question is about to emerge.

The Gardeners age probabilistically. A young Gardener might have lived twenty years or two hundred, depending on how those years chose to accumulate. The elderly exist in deep time synchronization with the stone, their personal uncertainty matching the mountain's so perfectly that distinguishing between Gardener and geology becomes impossible. These elders don't die in any conventional sense—they simply become increasingly probable in other states, until their presence in the gardens is more memory than matter.

Where the Sinters see their city as a vast organism, the Gardeners understand their realm as a continuous conversation between the possible and the actual. Every terraced garden, every cultivated concept, every managed flux zone represents another exchange in this dialogue. They don't seek to control the stone's uncertainty but to participate in it, adding their own indecision to the mix until the distinction between gardener and garden dissolves like everything else in these spaces of perpetual potential.

The two communities need each other in ways neither fully admits. The city's industrial certainty provides an anchor that keeps the Flux Gardens from dissolving entirely into possibility. The Gardens' uncertainty prevents the city from calcifying into total stasis. Between them, they maintain the mountain in its state of endless becoming—neither fully formed nor fully flux, forever deciding what it wants to be.
