Between the Breathing Terrace and the gardeners' warrens lies a frontier of stone that is anything but empty. This intermediate zone extends for nearly thirty meters of vertical distance—a cathedral of cracks, fissures, and thermal channels where the mountain's internal architecture reveals itself in cross-section.

The heat rises here in visible columns, shimmer-streams that twist through the passages like living things. Where these thermal rivers meet the cooler air filtering down from above, condensation forms—not water, but metallic deposits that plate the rock surfaces in paper-thin layers of copper, zinc, and stranger elements. Over millennia, these deposits have grown into fantastic formations: curtains of metallic crystal that ring like bells when the temperature changes, branching structures that channel and split the heat flow into ever-finer streams.

This is where the deep roots of the terrace plants perform their most crucial work. They descend through this zone not as simple conduits but as active processors, their surfaces studded with specialized organs that strip minerals from the rising gases. Some roots have evolved chamber-like swellings here, biological reaction vessels where the superheated gases pause and cool slightly, precipitating out valuable compounds before the depleted air continues upward. The gardeners maintain these chambers with particular care, clearing accumulated deposits and adjusting the root membranes to optimize extraction efficiency.

The beetle-mechanicals use this zone as their primary highway between the terrace and the warrens. They navigate not by sight but by reading the thermal gradients, their metal shells serving as sensitive thermometers that can detect temperature variations of fractions of degrees. Established routes follow the spaces between root clusters, paths worn smooth by millions of passages. At certain points, the beetles have collectively modified these routes, secreting acids that widen crucial bottlenecks or depositing metal from their own shells to create bridges across unstable sections.

But the intermediate zone hosts its own endemic life as well. Filamentous organisms, part fungus and part wire, stretch across the open spaces in geometric webs. These catch not prey but minerals—their strands accumulating deposits from the passing air until they grow too heavy and break, falling to be consumed by specialized cleaning beetles that process them into the concentrated pellets the ecosystem depends on. The webs regrow within days, their patterns influenced by the thermal flows in ways that create an ever-shifting gallery of crystalline art.

Where the zone meets the gardeners' realm, the architecture changes. Here the random cracks and natural fissures give way to the precisely bored tunnels of the warrens. The transition isn't sharp but gradual—natural passages that show signs of subtle modification, walls that have been smoothed just enough to improve airflow, chambers that might be natural formations or might be the result of patient centuries of guided erosion.

The gardeners have installed various instruments throughout this zone—not machines as humans would recognize them, but living sensors cultivated from modified plant tissue. These grow from the walls like pale fruit, their surfaces sensitive to pressure, temperature, and chemical composition. Through root-like nerves, they connect back to monitoring chambers in the warrens where elderly gardeners too frail for active maintenance work spend their days interpreting the data flows, their thoracic organs pulsing in complex patterns as they track the mountain's vital signs.

The boundary between the zone and the terrace above is marked by the root-flesh substrate—a meter-thick layer of interwoven tissue that serves as both foundation and filter. From below, this looks like a living ceiling, constantly in motion as roots extend and retract, as chemical signals pulse along fiber networks, as waste products are packaged and expelled to fall through the intermediate zone. The gardeners must occasionally traverse this boundary, using special passages where the root-flesh has been trained to part at specific harmonic frequencies, allowing them access to the surface.

During the storms that periodically lash the mountain, the intermediate zone becomes a refuge. The terrace plants retract their most delicate structures and seal their compression chambers. The beetle-mechanicals retreat downward, clustering in sheltered alcoves. The gardeners seal the upper entrances to their warrens and wait. But the zone itself comes alive during these times—the pressure changes and electrical discharges of the storms interact with the metallic formations to create displays of light and sound that the gardeners consider sacred. Young gardeners are brought to witness these storms, to learn that the mountain itself is active, alive, participating in the great work of transformation.

The passage of materials through this zone follows cycles measured in years. A mineral deposit stripped from the rising gases by a root might be processed, concentrated, incorporated into plant tissue, consumed by a beetle, excreted as waste, caught by a filament web, processed again by a cleaning beetle, and finally delivered to the gardeners' processing chambers—a journey that could take decades but ensures that nothing is wasted, that every atom of useful material is extracted from the mountain's exhalations.

This intermediate zone is the circulatory system of the entire community—neither fully of the terrace nor of the warrens but essential to both. It's where the mechanical precision of the plants meets the conscious intention of the gardeners, where evolution and cultivation blend into something indistinguishable. Without this space of process and transformation, neither the miraculous garden above nor the patient workers below could persist. It is the lung through which the mountain breathes, the gap that makes connection possible, the distance that allows two very different forms of life to compose a single, improbable symphony of survival.
