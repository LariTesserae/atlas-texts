The Bleached Terraces

Eight hundred meters above, where the water pulled back like a tide that never returned, the city continues in negative. Here the bones of the old world lie naked under a sun that burns whiter each year, their shadows sharp as glass on the limestone that was once seafloor.

The terraces step down the mountainside in vast shelves—ancient tidal zones written in stone, each marking where the ocean paused in its retreat. The lowest terrace still holds pools of brine so concentrated they shimmer like mercury. Salt crystals bloom from every surface: delicate flowers of halite, twisted spires of gypsum, sheets of calcite thin as paper. When the wind comes, which is always, these formations sing—a high, glassy tinkling that carries for kilometers across the white waste.

Nothing should live here, yet the Dry Ones persist.

They work at night when the stones have given back their heat and the air no longer shimmers. By day they shelter in the reef caves—spaces in the dead coral where ancient fish once hid, now carved deeper by patient hands and lined with salvaged sheets of mica that throw back the killing light. The caves stay cool even at noon, their depths holding shadows like water.

Kell emerges first each evening, her skin the color of salt-bleached wood, eyes permanently squinted against glare that isn't even there in the darkness. She wears wrappings of beaten bark cloth, harvested from the last trees that cling to life in the ravines where morning fog still condenses. Everything here is white or gray or the pale blue of old bones. Even their blood seems thinner, more pink than red, as if the sun has leached the color from their very veins.

She reads the salt gardens by touch—row upon row of shallow pans carved into the terrace stone where they evaporate what little moisture the air holds. Each pan yields different crystals depending on its depth, its angle to the wind, the minerals in its particular patch of stone. They eat salt with everything, need it the way the Woven need air. Their bodies have learned to hoard water so efficiently that they urinate crystals, weep solid tears.

The others emerge as the stars sharpen overhead. Silent Ash, who hasn't spoken since the day he watched the last free water evaporate from the pools. The Twins—not related but born on the same rainless day, their movements synchronized by shared suffering. Old Marine, who remembers when fish swam here, who teaches the young to read the fossil patterns in the stone, to know what plenty looked like frozen in limestone.

Twenty souls, maybe fewer. They don't count anymore because counting means acknowledging the subtraction. When someone dies—of heat, of thirst, of the simple exhaustion of living in this place—they carry the body to the highest terrace and let the sun take it. Within days, only bleached bones remain, which they grind to powder and add to the salt gardens. Nothing is wasted here. Nothing can be.

Their children are born smaller each generation, with larger eyes for the nocturnal life and skin that seems to grow its own salt crust for protection. Little Echo can find moisture in stones that feel desert-dry to adult hands. The boy they call Gecko climbs the sheer terrace walls with fingerpads sticky as a lizard's. They play quiet games in the starlight, hunting the pale scorpions that are their main source of protein, racing through the maze of dead coral with feet that barely whisper on the stone.

The work is water. Always water. They tend the fog nets strung between coral spires, checking them each dawn for the few precious drops that condense from the night air. They maintain the dew channels carved into every sloped surface, guiding what little moisture exists toward collection cisterns sealed with salt-crystal lids. They know every seep in the mountain, every place where the stone sweats at the temperature change of dawn.

But mainly, they trade with the Woven.

The path between the worlds is treacherous—a thousand vertical meters down crumbling switchbacks where one misplaced foot means death. They travel only on the darkest nights, when the moon is new and the stones have cooled enough to touch. Each trader carries salt—pure, refined, shaped into blocks that won't crumble. Salt is the only thing they have that the sea doesn't.

The meeting place is always the same: a tidal pool at the exact boundary where earth meets ocean, fed by underground springs that mix fresh water with salt. Neither group can survive long in the other's element, so they work quickly. Salt blocks passed down, kelp ropes hauled up. Dried fish wrapped in seaweed, medicinal algae that helps heal the sun-blisters. News exchanged in the pidgin they've developed—half hand signs, half spoken words distorted by the different shapes their mouths have taken.

Young Echo makes this journey sometimes, her night vision better than the adults'. She's seen Mira from a distance, watched her move through the water like flying. They are the same age, Echo and Reed, born in the same season to different worlds. Sometimes Echo dreams of water, of moving weightless through green light. Sometimes Reed surfaces in the kelp forests and wonders what it would be like to climb, to have the whole sky instead of just stolen glimpses.

The Dry Ones have their own builders, their own patterns. They construct windbreaks from salvaged coral, channels and funnels that direct the constant breeze to where it's needed—to cool the caves, to power the grinding wheels that turn salt into powder fine enough to trade. Master Bone has learned to read the fossils so well he can predict where the stone is weakest, where to chisel cisterns that won't crack as the mountain settles.

They tried to grow things, in the early years. Carried soil up from the dead valleys, planted seeds saved from the world before. Everything died. Now they cultivate only what was already here—the lichens that grow on north-facing stones, bitter but nutritious. The salt-loving bacteria that bloom pink in the evaporation pans, which they harvest with wooden paddles and dry into protein cakes. A species of tiny succulent that lives in cracks, hoarding a lifetime's water in leaves no bigger than fingernails.

Their art is arrangement. On sacred days—the vernal equinox, the summer solstice, the anniversary of the last rain—they create mandalas from different colored salts and crushed stones. Spirals and stars and patterns that map the fossil reefs below their feet, temporary beauties that the wind erases within hours. Grandmother Marine teaches the young ones these designs, passing down geometries that echo the growth patterns of living coral their ancestors once knew.

They sing, but not like the Woven. Their songs are whispers, shaped by throats too dry for full voice. The melodies catch in the coral caves and resonate, building harmonics that seem to come from the stone itself. They sing while they work, while they wait for dawn, while they carry their dead to the highest places. The songs are maps—each verse locating a water source, a safe path, a place where the stones hold coolness longest.

At the deepest point of their caves, behind a salt-sealed door that takes three people to open, they keep the Memory. Photographs, sealed in glass and wrapped in the last plastic they have. Images of the city when it was whole, neither drowned nor desiccated. Streets busy with people who never thought about water, never counted their tears. The young ones are brought here once, on their naming day, to see what was. Then the door is sealed again, because remembering too much is another kind of drowning.

The truth is written in the terraces themselves. Each level marks a pause in the water's retreat, each pause lasting decades. The ocean didn't leave all at once—it pulled back in stages, like something breathing out over centuries. The Dry Ones know, though they don't speak it, that they live in an exhale. One day the breath will reverse. The water will return, climbing the terraces one by one, reclaiming its territory.

When that happens, the two worlds will meet. The children who are learning to live in both elements—Echo with her night eyes and Reed with her growing gills, all of them shaped by their extremes—will be ready. They're becoming something that can exist in the margin, in the tidal zones between absolute desert and absolute sea.

But for now, the Dry Ones persist. Each night Kell checks the salt gardens, reads the crystal patterns that tell tomorrow's weather. Each dawn they seal themselves back into the coral caves, dreams full of swimming motions they've never made. They are the photographic negative of the Woven, the same adaptation in reverse—life insisting on itself in whatever form the world allows.

When the trade parties meet at the tidal pool, they sometimes pause after the business is done. The Dry Ones crouch at the water's edge, and the Woven surface, and for a moment they regard each other across the boundary. Two branches of the same tree, growing toward different lights. They see themselves reflected and distorted—what they were, what they're becoming, what their children will be.

Then they part, climbing up or diving down, returning to their kingdoms of bone and kelp. The pool remains, mixing fresh and salt in endless circulation, tasting of both worlds and belonging to neither. In its depths, small fish dart—descendants of ancestors who chose neither to follow the sea nor flee to the heights, but to remain in the space between.

This is how a world continues after its ending: not by returning to what was, but by becoming what must be. In the kelp cathedral and the bleached terraces, in the lives shaped by water's presence and absence, the future is being written in salt and cellulose, in held breath and careful movement, in the patient work of survival becoming something larger than itself.
