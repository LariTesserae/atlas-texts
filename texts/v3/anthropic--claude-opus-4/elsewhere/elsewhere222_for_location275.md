Below the scarps, where the eternal song fades to a distant drone and true darkness becomes possible again, the world transforms into something altogether different yet intimately connected to the vertical realm above. This is the Catchment—a vast basin of accumulated sound and sediment that stretches for miles beneath the white cliffs.

The ground here is not solid in any conventional sense. Over millennia, the acoustic energy from the scarps has done something to the earth, creating a substrate that behaves like both solid and liquid depending on the frequency of vibrations passing through it. Walk with a regular gait and you sink; move in rhythm with the underlying harmonics—a skill that takes years to master—and the surface supports you like firm clay.

The debris of ages has collected here: fragments of wind-carved stone fallen from above, the calcified remains of aerial creatures that didn't survive their descent, and strange acoustic fossils—zones where particularly powerful sound waves have crystallized into translucent formations that still hum with trapped music when touched. These formations rise like abstract sculptures from the rippling ground, some towering twenty feet high, others forming delicate networks barely visible until you're upon them.

The primary inhabitants of the Catchment are the Resonance Farmers—tall, unnaturally thin beings whose ancestors were perhaps human, though generations of adaptation have rendered them something else entirely. Their bodies have elongated to better distribute their weight across the sensitive surface, and their feet have broadened into paddle-like appendages that can sense and match the earth's vibrational patterns. They walk with an undulating grace, each step carefully tuned to the ground's harmony.

Their skin has developed a peculiar quality—it dampens sound, absorbing acoustic energy rather than reflecting it. This makes them nearly invisible to echolocation and allows them to move through their sound-saturated environment without creating destructive interference patterns. When they gather in groups, the silence around them is profound, a pocket of quiet that seems to pull at the edges of perception.

The Farmers cultivate the acoustic fossils, tending gardens of crystallized sound with tools that are part tuning fork, part farming implement. They've learned that certain frequencies, applied at specific times, can encourage the growth of new formations. These cultivated crystals serve multiple purposes: building material for their low, curved dwellings that deflect and channel sound; resonators that can store acoustic energy for later use; and most importantly, anchors that stabilize sections of the substrate, creating islands of relative solidity in the shifting ground.

Their society is built around cycles of sound and silence. During the "loud season"—when atmospheric conditions cause the scarps' song to intensify—they retreat into their sound-dampening homes, venturing out only to tend the most critical formations. But during the "quiet season," when certain wind patterns briefly diminish the eternal music, they emerge for communal gatherings where they communicate through a language of gestures and ground-vibrations, their sound-absorbing skin making vocal communication nearly impossible.

They wear clothing woven from the shed fibers of their own hair—material that shares their acoustic dampening properties. These garments are often dyed with pigments extracted from the Memory Mosses that occasionally take root on fallen stone fragments, creating patterns that shift color in response to sound waves. The effect is hypnotic: a Farmer in full ceremonial dress appears to ripple and flow, their garments visualizing the acoustic environment around them.

The Farmers share their realm with creatures that have evolved to exploit the unique properties of the Catchment. Burrowing through the semi-liquid substrate are the Waveform Worms—segmented beings that propel themselves by creating controlled acoustic pulses. They feed on the microscopic organisms that rain down from the scarps, creatures killed by the intense sound but preserved by the strange properties of the Catchment's surface.

More disturbing are the Echo Mimics—amorphous entities that may not be truly alive in any biological sense. They appear to be colonies of specialized acoustic bacteria that have learned to reproduce sound patterns with perfect fidelity. They lurk near the crystallized formations, replaying the death cries of falling creatures or the mating songs of extinct species. Some have even learned to mimic the movement patterns of the Farmers, creating false ground-vibrations that can lead the unwary into areas of unstable substrate.

The Farmers have developed a complex relationship with these Mimics, using them as a form of acoustic memory storage. By carefully feeding specific sound patterns to captive colonies, they can preserve important information—the locations of stable paths through the Catchment, the frequencies needed to encourage crystal growth, the warning signs of impending substrate liquefaction. Elder Farmers often carry small containers of trained Mimics, living libraries that whisper their secrets when properly prompted.

But the true mystery of the Catchment lies in what the Farmers call the Deep Resonance—a phenomenon they speak of only in vibrations, never in their visual language. At certain depths in the substrate, usually near the largest acoustic fossil formations, there are pockets of absolute acoustic vacuum. Not silence, which is merely the absence of sound, but something more fundamental: zones where sound cannot exist.

These voids pull at the surrounding acoustic energy with tremendous force, creating whirlpools in the substrate that can swallow the unwary. The Farmers mark these areas with tall poles topped with Weaver silk—the only material that vibrates visibly even in acoustic vacuum. But they also visit these sites regularly, lowering specialized instruments on long cables, collecting data through methods that remain obscure to outsiders.

What they've discovered—or what they believe they've discovered—has shaped their entire culture. They claim the Deep Resonances are not random but form a vast pattern beneath the Catchment, a network that mirrors the wind-channels in the scarps above. More troubling, they insist this network is growing, that new voids appear in precise locations, as if something were excavating reality itself from below.

The connection between the scarps and the Catchment becomes most apparent during the storm seasons. When lightning strikes the white stone above, the electrical energy doesn't just trigger the Weavers' spawning—it races down through the stone into the earth below. The acoustic fossils light up in sequence, conducting the energy along paths that the Farmers have mapped over generations. These lightning-paths always terminate at the Deep Resonances, where the energy simply... disappears.

The Farmers have a creation myth they tell through ground-vibrations during the quietest nights. They say the Singing Scarps were not carved by wind alone but were shaped by something that needed to generate specific frequencies. The eternal song is not random but purposeful, a sonic key turning in a lock that spans miles. And the Deep Resonances below? They are the tumblers of that lock, gradually aligning as the correct frequencies accumulate over geological time.

They point to evidence: the way certain Echo Shepherds have been observed climbing down from the scarps to investigate the Catchment's edge, their acoustic organs detecting something the Farmers cannot. The patterns in the Memory Mosses' light displays that correspond to fluctuations in the Deep Resonances. The fact that the carved bone maps found on ancient climbing ropes, when overlaid correctly, seem to chart not the surface geography but the void-network below.

Trade between the two realms exists, though it is rare and fraught with danger. The Farmers value the crystalline shells of the Weavers, which they grind into powder and use to treat acoustic fossil formations, encouraging growth in specific directions. In return, they offer the aerial realm something precious: silence. They've learned to weave their sound-dampening hair into nets that, when hung in the wind-channels, create small zones of quiet where the scarps' fauna can rest without constant acoustic bombardment.

The journey between realms is treacherous. The transition zone where the vertical scarps meet the horizontal Catchment is a chaos of conflicting sound waves, falling stones, and unstable substrate. The Farmers maintain a single safe path—a winding route marked by carefully tuned acoustic fossils that create a corridor of stable harmonics. Even so, travelers must time their passage carefully, moving only when the wind patterns allow for minimum turbulence.

Recently, something has changed. The Farmers report that the Deep Resonances have begun to pulse in unison, a rhythm that matches no known natural phenomenon. The Echo Mimics have started reproducing sounds that no living creature has ever made—complex frequencies that hurt to hear and leave listeners with fragmented memories of places they've never been. And in the scarps above, the Shepherds have been observed gathering in unprecedented numbers at the marked channels, their acoustic organs focused downward, listening to something far below.

The two realms, locked in their eternal dance of sound and silence, height and depth, seem to be approaching some kind of convergence. The Farmers have begun preparing—though for what, they cannot or will not say through their language of vibrations. They only point to their most ancient formations, where the first acoustic fossils crystallized around something that predates both scarps and Catchment: fragments of worked stone, covered in those same scratches that mark the forbidden channels above. Always in groups of seven. Always at angles that shouldn't exist.

Whatever shaped the Singing Scarps and hollowed out the Deep Resonances beneath the Catchment may not be finished with its work. And in the margin between these two impossible realms—one of endless music and blinding light, the other of managed sound and careful darkness—something waits with the patience of geological time for its lock to finally open.
