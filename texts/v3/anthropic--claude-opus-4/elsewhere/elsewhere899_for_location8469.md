The Singing Wells of Aethermoor

Three days' journey north, where the limestone gives way to darker stone and the land rises into heath-covered hills, you find them by their voices first. The sound carries impossible distances on certain nights—not water falling into water, but air falling into darkness, each well breathing out its own particular note.

The wells dot the moorland without pattern that any surveyor has discovered. Some cluster close enough that you could throw a stone between them. Others stand solitary, miles from their nearest neighbor. Each is unique in its construction—this one ringed with standing stones so old their carvings have weathered to suggestions, that one collared with precise Victorian brickwork, another barely more than a gap in the earth marked by a ring of darker grass. But all share the same quality: they go down far deeper than wells should go, and they sing.

The songs change with the weather. Before storms, the wells moan in harmonies that make sheep huddle together and birds fly in agitated circles. On clear nights when the moon is dark, they produce whispers that almost resolve into words. But it's during the seasonal inversions—when warm air sits above cold, when mist pools in valleys while hilltops bake in sun—that the wells truly perform. Then they sing in chorus, each well contributing its note to compositions that last for hours, melodies that seem to follow rules no human music obeys.

The local shepherds know to avoid the wells during these concerts. Animals react badly—horses shy and bolt, dogs whine and press close to their masters' legs. But people react worse. Those who listen too long report vertigo that persists for days, dreams of falling through spaces that have too many directions. A few claim to have heard their own voices echoing up from the depths, speaking words they've never said in languages they don't know.

The water in the wells—when there is water—defies expectations. Some wells that should tap the same aquifer yield different waters entirely: one sweet, one salt, one with a metallic tang that stains teeth gray. The levels rise and fall without relation to rainfall. Sometimes a well runs dry for years only to refill in a single night, brimming with water that tests reveal to be older than it should be, carrying minerals from stone layers that shouldn't exist this high above sea level.

Objects dropped into the singing wells behave strangely. Stones fall in silence for too long before the splash. Weighted ropes pay out more length than seems possible, and when hauled back up, come bearing knots no one tied. A Victorian survey team reported lowering a lamp to a depth of 300 feet—already impossibly deep—only to see its light suddenly visible far off to the side, as if the well had branched or bent. The lamp, when retrieved, was covered in a fine white powder that proved to be limestone dust, though no limestone exists in the local geology.

The moor people have their own explanations, passed down in stories that predate written record. They say the wells are breathing holes, that something vast sleeps beneath the heath and these are its airways. They leave offerings at certain wells on certain nights—never food, never coins, but strange specific things: shed snakeskins, children's lost teeth, water collected from seven different springs and mixed in precise proportions. They say the wells remember, that each song is a story, that if you knew how to listen you could hear the history of the world sung from the bottom up.

Attempts to map the wells have failed in curious ways. Survey teams report different numbers each time—not through error but because wells appear and disappear. A well documented and photographed in spring might be absent in autumn, leaving only a depression in the heath. New wells appear after the winter storms, their mouths raw as fresh wounds in the earth. The only consistent pattern anyone has found is this: the wells are most active, most numerous, most musical, along an invisible line that runs south-southwest toward Kellensmere.

The water table here shouldn't connect to the flooded quarries fifty miles away. The geology argues against it—different stone, different depths, a ridge of impermeable rock between. But the shepherds tell of finding white crayfish in their well buckets, creatures that match descriptions of those in the quarry pools. They speak of tasting limestone in water drawn from wells bored through granite. And sometimes, very rarely, objects lost in Kellensmere surface in the wells of Aethermoor: a quarryman's chisel, a child's wooden boat, once a leather journal so waterlogged its pages had fused, though a few words remained visible—something about voices in the stone.

The singers come on the equinoxes.

That's what the moor folk call them, these visitors who arrive when day and night balance. They come walking out of the south, following paths that only they can see. Tall figures wrapped in cloaks that seem wet even in drought, moving with a gait that suggests they're more used to swimming than walking. They visit each well in turn, performing rituals that no one who's watched can adequately describe. They lower things into the depths—not objects but movements, gestures that leave afterimages in the air. They listen with their hands pressed to the earth. They sing back to the wells in voices that carry too many overtones.

After the singers pass through, the wells change their songs. New notes appear in the chorus. Old melodies shift into new configurations. The water levels adjust themselves according to some vast, slow plan. And sometimes, in wells that have been dry for decades, water returns—but water that moves strangely, that reflects light from angles that don't exist, that rises and falls as if tidal though no sea lies within five hundred miles.

The connection between the Drowned Quarries and the Singing Wells remains unproven by any measure science recognizes. But the shepherds know. The moor folk know. The stones know, and the water knows, and somewhere between Kellensmere and Aethermoor, moving through darkness that has too many directions, the Stonekeepers and the singers know.

They are engineering something. Not with tools and plans but with patience and purpose that spans centuries. Each well is a word in a sentence too long for human memory. Each flooded chamber is a note in a song too deep for human ears. The limestone drinks the water and releases it. The granite splits and lets the darkness in. Drop by drop, note by note, the work continues.

On certain nights, if you know where to stand on the high moors, you can hear it: the wells singing in harmony with an echo that comes from the south, from the direction of Kellensmere. It's almost music, almost language, almost like the sound of something vast and patient learning to breathe with a thousand mouths. The sheep huddle. The birds wheel in the darkening sky. And deep beneath the earth, through channels that shouldn't exist, water flows uphill from darkness into darkness, carrying its cargo of whispers and minerals, messages and slow transformation.

The world is porous in ways maps don't show. The Singing Wells of Aethermoor prove it with every breath, every note, every drop of impossible water drawn from depths that connect to other depths. And somewhere between source and surface, in the lightless spaces where stone meets water meets air, the great work continues, patient as erosion, certain as gravity, strange as song.
