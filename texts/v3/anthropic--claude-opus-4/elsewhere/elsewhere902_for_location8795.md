The Reservoir

Three days' growth from the Rootworks, across the calcified deadlands where nothing takes hold, the Reservoir sits in its ancient bowl of stone like a jewel hoarded by the earth. The water here is the last free water in this part of the world — a blasphemy of liquidity in an age that has learned to bind every molecule into living tissue. The great disk of it stretches nearly a kilometer across, perfectly still, reflecting the pale green sky with such clarity that birds diving for its surface sometimes break their necks against the air.

The water is black in its depths — not from pollution but from purity so absolute it swallows light. The original engineers carved this basin from metamorphic rock, fusing the stone at the molecular level to create a surface nothing can penetrate. No algae grows here. No microbe survives. The water exists in a state of perpetual sterility, maintained by mechanisms built into the bedrock that still function after millennia, their power sources as mysterious as their purpose is clear: preserve this water outside the cycle of life.

Around the rim, crystalline formations rise like teeth, each the height of ten humans. These are not natural minerals but accumulated constructs of purification — salt towers built from extracted impurities, growing taller each year as the reservoir's systems pull contamination from the air itself. The crystals hum at frequencies that hurt to hear, vibrating with the tension of keeping their contents stable. Some have developed cracks from the strain, leaking thin streams of hypersaline fluid that kills anything it touches, leaving trails of white death across the stone.

The air here feels different — thinner, older, as if it predates the green sky and remembers blue. It carries no spores, no pollen, no trace of the biological riot that fills the rest of the world. The temperature drops ten degrees at the basin's edge, the water acting as a heat sink that creates its own weather. Clouds form and dissipate in the space of minutes, their moisture pulled back into the reservoir before it can escape.

At the cardinal points, four structures pierce the water's surface — towers of the same fused stone as the basin, each housing machinery that no one fully understands anymore. The northern tower cycles the water, pulling from the depths and releasing at the surface in patterns that prevent stagnation without disturbing the mirror smoothness. The eastern tower maintains the purification fields, its apex crackling with violet energy that tastes like copper in the air. The southern tower runs deepest, its foundations anchored in bedrock chambers where backup systems wait in viral dormancy, ready to rebuild everything if the primary mechanisms fail. The western tower is dark, its purpose lost, its machinery silent, yet the system functions without it — suggesting either redundancy or that its true purpose has yet to trigger.

The Keepers tend this place, though tend is too gentle a word for their vigilance. They are not Grafted — they refused that transformation, choosing instead a different bargain with necessity. Their flesh remains human, but their minds have been shaped by generations of singular purpose. They wear suits of metallic mesh that filter every breath, every excretion, ensuring nothing biological enters the water. The suits are hereditary, passed from parent to child in ceremonies that bind the new Keeper to their duty through ritual scarification — patterns cut into the skin that match the suit's weave, creating a sympathetic resonance that helps the metal remember its wearer.

They speak a language of measurements and prohibitions. Their children learn numbers before words — parts per billion, pH balances, mineral concentrations. They recite the Protocols like other cultures recite prayers: the seventeen steps of morning inspection, the forty-three signs of systemic contamination, the nine hundred and nine forbidden acts that could compromise the reservoir's integrity. Their poetry consists of reading water reports aloud, finding rhythm in the precision of unchanged values, beauty in the persistence of zeros.

The Keepers live in stone chambers carved into the basin's rim, their homes as sterile as operating theaters. They eat food grown in hydroponic gardens that use recycled water — never reservoir water, never, the prohibition against consumption so absolute that even considering it causes physical revulsion in those raised to the duty. They process their waste through crystallization systems that mirror the reservoir's purification towers, adding their extracted minerals to the growing salt formations.

Their tools are ancient and irreplaceable — spectrometers that read contamination in parts per quadrillion, sonic probes that map the reservoir's depth to nanometer precision, field generators that can create localized sterility zones for emergency maintenance. When a tool breaks, they enter mourning periods that last until a replacement can be fabricated using the forge-templates in the deep archives. Some tools have been rebuilt so many times that none of their original components remain, yet they're treated with the reverence due to relics.

The trading happens at night, when the risk of contamination is lowest. The Grafted send emissaries — the most human of their kind, those who can still wear the separation suits without their bark breaking through. They bring payment in the form of compressed nutrients, energy cells charged by the Rootworks' photosynthetic collectors, and most crucially, replacement parts fabricated in the growing forges of the plant-city. In exchange, they receive water.

Not much — never much. The Keepers measure each allotment in milliliters, dispensed through systems that ensure no drop is wasted, no contamination introduced. The water emerges from hidden ports in the basin's rim, flowing through tubes of fused glass that have never known air. The Grafted receive it in containers grown specifically for this purpose — sealed pods that will merge with their bodies, delivering the pure water directly into their systems without exposure to atmosphere.

The negotiations follow forms older than either culture's memory. The Grafted must prove their need through biological readings that show critical dehydration. The Keepers must verify that the previous allotment was fully integrated, not wasted or leaked. Both sides perform this dance knowing it's theater — the Grafted need water to survive, the Keepers need the tools and materials only the Rootworks can provide. Yet the ritual continues, each step a reaffirmation of the boundary between the two worlds: one that hoards water in crystalline stasis, one that has learned to live without it.

Sometimes rogue Grafted approach the reservoir, driven by deep thirsts or the memory of what water once meant. The Keepers' response is swift and without mercy. Static field generators hidden in the crystal formations activate, creating zones of electric death that leave bodies desiccated, their moisture extracted and purified before being returned to the reservoir. These remains are left as warnings, gradually weathering to dust that blows away from the basin, never toward it.

The Keepers maintain histories written on sheets of beaten metal, recording every transaction, every measurement, every anomaly in scripts so precise they approach art. The oldest records speak of times when the reservoir was fuller, when the water level touched marks now meters above the surface. They don't speak of where the water went — only that the rate of loss has slowed over generations, approaching but never reaching zero. The projections suggest thousands of years before the reservoir empties, but the Keepers plan as if apocalypse comes tomorrow.

In the deepest archives, behind locks that require three Keepers to open, lie the Contingencies. Plans for when the water runs out, for when the purification systems finally fail, for when the Grafted grow too powerful to deny. Some involve poisoning the entire reservoir rather than letting it be taken. Others describe transformation processes — ways for the Keepers to preserve themselves in crystalline form, becoming eternal guardians of an empty basin. The darkest propose using the reservoir's water for a final purpose: restarting the hydrological cycle through means so extreme they would remake the world.

The Keepers dream of drowning. Every night, visions of water rising over their heads, filling their lungs, dissolving the boundaries of their bodies. They wake gasping in the dry air of their chambers, checking their suits for breaks, running through the Protocols to calm their racing hearts. They interpret these dreams as reminders — their vigil prevents the world's dissolution back into the chaos of flowing water, uncontrolled and purposeless.

During the clearest days, when the light angles just right, you can see through the water to the reservoir's bottom. There, beneath kilometers of perfect preservation, shapes suggest themselves. Buildings, perhaps. Machinery. The bones of whatever civilization built this place and decided that keeping water from life was worth any price. The Keepers have protocols against looking too long, against wondering what lies beneath. Their duty is to the surface, to the balance, to the measured dispensation of milliliters that keeps both worlds functioning.

The algae arrived last autumn — a single cell somehow surviving the journey across the deadlands, somehow resisting the purification fields, somehow beginning to divide in the reservoir's shallows. The Keepers destroyed it, of course. Scoured the area with fields that killed everything down to the molecular level. But not before some of them saw it bloom — a tiny green star in all that black water, suggesting what the reservoir could become if their vigilance ever faltered.

They've increased the patrols since then. Strengthened the fields. Added new protocols to the recitations. But sometimes, in the space between breaths, between heartbeats, between thoughts, they remember that green star. How it looked almost like hope. Almost like spring.

The trade routes between the Rootworks and the Reservoir are carved by necessity and habit — paths of deadland where nothing grows, corridors of stone scoured clean by generations of careful passage. The emissaries travel these routes in sealed crawlers, vehicles that are themselves hybrid things: chassis of metal and ceramic from the old world, powered by biological fuel cells that the Grafted grow in specialized chambers, wheels that secrete neutralizing enzymes to prevent any stray spore from taking hold in the barren ground.

The journey takes three days because it must. Faster travel would risk contamination, the wake of disturbed air potentially carrying life between the two poles of this divided world. The emissaries stop at prescribed waypoints, structures built at the exact midpoint between organic growth and sterile preservation. Here they perform the cleansing rituals — first shedding the outer layers of their traveling forms, revealing the temporary sterile shells beneath, then undergoing chemical baths that kill any lingering biology while preserving just enough cellular function to survive the return journey.

In these waypoints, the walls hold contracts written in both languages — the flowing script of growth-patterns that the Grafted read through touch, and the precise geometric notation of the Keepers etched in acid on metal plates. These documents record not just water rights and tool exchanges, but the deeper agreement: that these two ways of being will remain separate, will not attempt to convert or contaminate each other, will maintain the balance that keeps both alive.

The irony is written in the very existence of the trade: the Rootworks, for all their independence from free water, still need it for certain critical processes — the initialization of new growth sectors, the cultivation of specific compounds that require aqueous chemistry, the deep-root dreams that guide their expansion. The Reservoir, for all its perfect isolation, depends on materials and energy that only biological systems can efficiently produce. They are locked in a symbiosis of mutual disdain, each considering the other a necessary evil.

Some among both cultures whisper of integration. Young Grafted who dream of swimming rather than rooting. Keeper children who secret away spores, watching them with fascination rather than horror. They meet sometimes in the deadlands, in places where neither surveillance system reaches, and share forbidden knowledge. The Grafted speak of the ecstasy of connection, the joy of belonging to something greater. The Keepers describe the clarity of purpose, the beauty of preservation, the peace of boundaries.

These dissidents imagine impossible futures: reservoirs that grow like cells, dividing and spreading while maintaining purity. Plant-cities that learn to cherish spaces of emptiness, cultivation voids as beautiful as growth. They sketch hybrid philosophies in the dust, proposing treaties that go beyond mere trade to true synthesis. But always, before dawn, they return to their respective homes, washing away the evidence of their meetings, resuming their proper roles.

The world watches this balance with interest that manifests as weather. Storms gather at the boundaries between wet and dry, between growth and sterility, as if the atmosphere itself is trying to reconcile the contradiction. Lightning strikes with unusual frequency along the trade routes, the electric discharge seeking the tension between ways of being. Sometimes it hits the crawlers, forcing emergency protocols, stranding emissaries in the deadlands until rescue arrives.

In these moments of crisis, both sides remember their common origin — the time before the choice, when water flowed freely and humans lived neither as plants nor as guardians but as something more fragile and more whole. The memory is painful, like probing a missing limb, and they quickly retreat back into their certainties: growth is life, preservation is life, there is no other way.

But the trades continue, the milliliters flow, the tools change hands. And in that exchange, despite all protocols and prohibitions, something else transfers. Call it hope. Call it contamination. Call it evolution. The mere fact of contact changes both sides in ways too subtle for immediate detection but too profound for permanent denial.

The Rootworks grows a little more carefully, leaving spaces between structures that might almost be called reserves. The Reservoir's Keepers find themselves measuring not just water levels but also the patterns clouds make on the surface, the beauty of reflection, the way certain angles of light make the black water seem almost alive. Neither side acknowledges these changes, but they accumulate like sediment, like growth rings, like memory.

Somewhere between them, in the deadlands where nothing should survive, tiny crystals form in hidden crevices. They're neither fully mineral nor fully biological — structures that preserve water in matrices that almost live, that almost flow. The emissaries don't report them. To do so would require choosing a category, declaring them either contamination or resource. Instead, they navigate around these anomalies, these suggestions of a third way, these fragments of a future that neither side is ready to imagine.

The world is patient. It has waited through the death of oceans, the binding of rivers, the transformation of rain into memory. It can wait through the careful dance of milliliters and molecules, the precious balance of those who hoard water and those who hoard life. Time moves differently for a world — seasons are seconds, civilizations are songs, the rise and fall of ways of being mere movements in a longer composition.

In that larger music, the Rootworks and the Reservoir are not opposites but harmonies, each holding a note that the other needs to complete the chord. Their trade is commerce but also courtship, their boundaries battle lines but also the space where new things emerge. They are the world teaching itself about choice and consequence, about preservation and transformation, about the terrible necessity of containing multitudes.

The next storm is gathering on the horizon, its clouds heavy with spores and sterile rain, with lightning that tastes of both growth and ending. When it breaks, it will test every protocol, every boundary, every certainty that keeps these two worlds apart and together. But that is still days away, still time for more trades, more measurements, more careful drops of water carried across the deadlands like liquid promises, like the world's patience made manifest, like hope in its most dangerous and necessary form.
