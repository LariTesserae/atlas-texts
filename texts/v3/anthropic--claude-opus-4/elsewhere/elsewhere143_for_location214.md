Below the plateau, where the collected waters of the city finally converge, the world changes character entirely.

The Cascade begins as a thousand waterfalls, ten thousand, uncountable silver threads dropping from the plateau's edge into green darkness. The water falls for nearly a kilometer before it meets anything solid again—not stone but the canopy of trees so vast their crowns merge into a second landscape, an undulating green plateau that mirrors the stone one above.

This is the Gorge Forest, though gorge suggests something narrow, and this chasm spans horizons. The trees here grew to meet the falling water, their highest branches evolved to comb moisture from the air. They are nothing like the stunted vegetation above. These giants wear pelts of moss thicker than a body, support hanging gardens in their crowns, host entire communities of plants that never touch earth. Their trunks disappear into mist below, into darkness so profound it has weight.

The water doesn't simply fall—it transforms. What begins as channeled flow becomes rain, then mist, then something between liquid and air that the forest breathes. On still days, clouds form in the canopy itself, wrapping the great trees in fog that tastes of stone and altitude. When wind blows up the gorge, the waterfalls bend and weave, painting the air with silver that never quite reaches its destination.

Light here is rationed, treasured, stolen. What filters through the canopy arrives green-tinted and swimming, as if the forest exists underwater. Dawn comes late, dusk early. True night lasts sixteen hours. The trees have learned to make their own light—fox-fire runs along certain bark in patterns that might be language, might be purely biological, might be both. Fungi glow in the eternal twilight of the forest floor. Even the water carries luminescence, picked up from some phosphorescent algae that blooms where the falls pool.

The Singers came here following the water down. Not the ones who maintain the city above—those stone-servants fear the green depths as much as they fear dryness. These are different stock, though perhaps related in the way all water-followers are related. They descended in the years after the plateau city emptied, drawn by the sound of falling water that never ceased, by the promise of plenty in the green deep.

They built homes in the trees, though built is too strong a word. They convinced the trees to grow chambers, to thicken bark into walls, to direct branches into bridges. Their architecture is alive, constantly growing, rooms expanding by fingerwidths each year. The largest communities spiral around trunks so vast twenty people holding hands couldn't encompass them, their walkways winding up through the middle canopy where the light is best, where the water-music is loudest.

They are smaller than their possible cousins above, more compact, with eyes evolved for the eternal green twilight. Their skin changes like the forest light—mottled patterns that shift with their movement through different qualities of shadow. They wear the forest as clothing: living moss that clings to their bodies, drawing moisture from their skin and giving back warmth. Vines twine through their hair, not decorative but symbiotic, filtering toxins from their blood in exchange for nutrients.

But it's their voices that named them. They sing the water down.

Stand at the forest edge at dawn and you'll hear it begin—a low humming that might be wind in leaves until you realize it has structure, harmony. The Singers position themselves on platforms throughout the vertical forest, each at a precise point relative to the falling water. They voice the water's path, guide it through the air with sound, ensure it reaches where the forest needs it most.

This is not mysticism but physics, though the boundary blurs. Their songs create standing waves in the mist, pressure differentials that push droplets this way or that. A sustained note can redirect a small waterfall by degrees. A chorus can split a larger fall into precisely aimed streams. They sing rain where drought threatens, sing mist where young trees struggle, sing the water away from places where too much would drown the careful balance.

The songs pass through generations not as memory but as physical inheritance. Young Singers' throats show cartilage structures unknown in other humans, resonating chambers that develop with practice. They begin by learning to feel water on their voices—how a note changes when mist passes through it, how harmony pulls moisture from the air. By adulthood, they can taste the humidity in a pitch, smell the coming rain in a chord progression.

Their calendar follows the water. Seasons here are not temperature but volume—the Rushing, when snowmelt above sends the falls roaring; the Steadying, when flow normalizes; the Thinning, when the falls become veils; the Gathering, when clouds below rise to meet clouds above and everything drowns in fog. Each season requires different songs, different positions, different harmonies between human voice and water voice.

They know every pool in the forest, every stream that runs between the great roots. The water here moves differently than above—not channeled but wild, finding its way through moss and mulch and the bodies of fallen giants. Sometimes it disappears entirely, vanishing into root systems that transport it through living wood to emerge hundreds of meters away. The Singers track these underground rivers by pressing ears to bark, listening for the water's pulse in the wood.

Their enemies are few but significant. The Drying—a fungal blight that desiccates trees from within, turning them to standing kindling. The Drowning—when too much water overwhelms the forest's ability to process it, flooding the root systems, encouraging rot. And the Silence—the times when singers lose their way in the vertical maze, their voices going unheard, their portion of the water-song missing until the forest itself begins to suffer.

They trade with no one, need nothing the forest doesn't provide. The trees fruit in response to their songs—not generously, but sufficiently. The streams carry fish that navigate by sound, drawn to Singer settlements by the constant music. Certain mosses, when dried and eaten, enhance vocal range and endurance. The forest feeds those who feed it water.

At the gorge bottom, below the reach of even their water-guided voices, the river runs. All the falls eventually find their way here, joining into something too large for songs to direct. The Singers rarely descend so far—the darkness is too complete, the pressure too heavy, the silence too profound. But sometimes, during the Gathering season when mist fills everything, they speak of lights moving down there. Not the gentle phosphorescence of the upper forest, but something harder, more purposeful. Lights that move against the current, that pulse in rhythms nothing like organic bioluminescence.

The oldest Singers, their voices worn to whispers by decades of water-song, tell stories. How the builders of the city above weren't the first to live there. How something older retreated downward when the stone was first carved, following the water to its deepest pools. How it waits there still, in the drowned dark, building its own structures from elements the sun never touches. They say this is why the city above emptied—not catastrophe but calling, the deep pulling its children home by paths only water knows.

But these are stories, told in the green twilight when the water-music grows strange and the mist makes shadows of things that shouldn't cast them. The younger Singers laugh, return to their platforms, raise their voices again in the eternal task of singing water through air, guiding the forest's lifeblood from stone heights to green depths.

Still, none of them ever follow the water all the way down.

The forest continues its work regardless. It processes the plateau's water through bodies of wood and moss, through soil that hasn't seen sky in millennia. It exhales clouds that rise to become rain that falls on stone that channels to waterfalls that feed the forest. The cycle continues, mediated by song and stone-servants, by root networks and patient channels, by choices made so long ago their reasons have been forgotten.

In the margins between the two worlds—where the last stone meets the first tree, where channeled water becomes free-fall—you can sometimes see both at once. The city's gray patience above, the forest's green hunger below, and between them the silver thread of water that connects all things. Stand here during the Rushing and you'll understand why both the Singers and the stone-servants serve the water rather than themselves. It is older than both their kinds, more patient than stone, hungrier than trees. It remembers when this was all ocean floor, and it works—through them, through their cities and songs—to make it ocean once again.

The Singers know this, perhaps. It's there in their water-songs if you listen closely—not just guidance but supplication, not just physics but prayer. They sing to something that listens with currents instead of ears, that answers with abundance or withdrawal, that teaches patience in droplets and persistence in streams. They sing because the alternative is silence, and in the silence, you can hear the deep waters moving, planning, remembering.

Far below, where no Singer has ever descended and returned, bioluminescent signals pulse in the absolute darkness. They follow patterns that might be language, might be mathematics, might be music too low for human ears to perceive. The river receives them, considers them, carries them seaward with the rest of its gathered waters.

The message, if it is a message, repeats with the patience of erosion:

*Not yet. Not yet. But soon.*
