Beyond the furthest cistern tower, where even the water-keepers rarely venture, the clay gives way to something else. The transition is gradual—first, the careful bricks become rougher, mixed with stones. Then the stones dominate, until finally there is only raw granite, wind-carved into fantastic pillows and passages. This is the Singing Boundary, named for the voices that carry through its canyons when the wind is right.

The granite rises in a great escarpment, a wall of stone that once formed the ancient lake's far shore. Time and weather have worked it into a vertical labyrinth—deep clefts where shadows pool like water, natural bridges spanning dizzy drops, caves that bore through entire buttresses of rock. The stone is pink-gray at dawn, bone-white at noon, purple-black as evening comes. Unlike the uniform clay of the cities, every surface here is unique, every formation the product of specific pressures and erosions.

Water moves differently here. Instead of careful collection and hoarding, it follows its own wild paths. Seasonal streams cascade through the highest channels, carrying snowmelt from peaks invisible beyond the escarpment's rim. These waters carved the singing tubes—smooth-bore tunnels that howl and harmonize as wind pushes through them. In the deepest grottos, permanent pools reflect darkness, fed by springs that rise from depths the cistern-builders never imagined.

The Listeners make their homes in this vertical world. They are fewer than the city dwellers—perhaps a thousand souls scattered through the entire Boundary—but their presence fills the stone channels like sound fills a bell. They build nothing. Instead, they inhabit what the stone provides: wind-hollowed chambers, natural shelves protected by overhangs, caves where ancient waters swirled granite into perfect dwelling-spheres.

Where the city people are clay-colored and narrow, the Listeners are something else. Their skin carries the hues of stone—grays and roses and ochres earned from generations of mineral-rich water. They grow tall in the vertical world, with reach bred for climbing and balance refined by precipice-walking. Their hair grows wild and long, often woven with feathers from the high-dwelling birds that nest in the escarpment's upper reaches.

They speak, when they speak at all, in notes rather than words. Children learn the tonal language young—how a rising pitch means danger, how certain harmonies carry location, how emotional states can be conveyed in the interplay between voice and echo. In the singing tubes, a conversation might involve three people stationed at different heights, their voices meeting and mingling in the hollow stone cores, creating meaning from interference patterns.

The Listeners know sound as the city dwellers know water. They can navigate in absolute darkness by echo, diagnose illness by listening to breath resonance, predict weather by the changing songs of the wind-tubes. Their most skilled members, the Tone Keepers, maintain mental maps of how sound moves through every part of the Boundary. They know which caves amplify whispers into roars, which channels carry voices for miles, which stone formations can store sound and release it hours later.

Their relationship with water is cavalier by city standards. They drink freely from the pools, bathe in the seasonal streams, let rain soak them when it comes. The springs are reliable enough that conservation is a foreign concept. They collect water, yes, but in gourds and hollow stones, taking what they need when they need it. The abundance that would stagger a city dweller is simply their birthright.

They eat what the stone provides. Hardy plants that root in cracks, bearing bitter fruits and tough seeds. Birds' eggs gathered from impossible ledges. Cave fish, pale and blind, pulled from the deepest pools. Bats taken with thrown nets in the twilight exodus. Rock lizards, larger and meatier than their city cousins. In the high seasons, when certain flowers bloom in the protected valleys, they harvest nectar that ferments into a drink that makes the stones themselves seem to sing.

The connection between these worlds is not simple geography but active, fraught exchange.

The Midway Market occupies a natural amphitheater where clay meets stone, a neutral ground established by necessity. Here, on the seventh day of each month, city dwellers and Listeners meet to trade. The exchanges follow ancient protocols. City people bring fired clay vessels, ceramic pipes, woven grass goods—all things the Listeners cannot make in their stonework world. The Listeners offer what the cities desperately need: mineral salts essential for health, medicines derived from cave fungi, and most crucially, information about the deep waters.

The Listeners' springs are the same waters that feed the cisterns, flowing underground from stone to clay. When contamination or blockage threatens the deep sources, only the Listeners can diagnose the problem. They read the health of the aquifer in the taste of their pools, feel tremors that warn of shifts in the water table, know when ancient channels are rerouting themselves in the depths.

Communication is the challenge. The clay-adapted whisper speech of the city dwellers cannot carry the tonal complexities the Listeners use. The Listeners, for their part, find linear word-sentences constraining and crude. Over generations, a pidgin has evolved for market days—a combination of gesture, simple tones, and object placement that allows basic negotiation.

Young people sometimes cross between the worlds, though few adapt fully. City dwellers who join the Listeners often fall from the heights before learning the stone paths, or starve before mastering the vertical hunting. Their clay-trained movements are too careful, too horizontal for the soaring world of granite. Listeners who attempt city life find the enclosure maddening—the walls that muffle sound, the careful rationing of everything, the endless maintenance of systems. They describe feeling slowly suffocated, their voices dying in their throats.

Yet some make the transition. These rare individuals become the Bridge Walkers, essential translators who can parse meaning between whispers and songs. They maintain small dwellings at the Midway, neither city nor stone, learning to live with one foot in each world. Through them, crucial knowledge flows: water predictions from the Listeners, engineering innovations from the cities, warnings of mutual threats like the sandstorms that can bury both clay channels and stone pools alike.

The deepest connection is one neither side fully acknowledges: they are one people, divided by an ancient choice. Stories persist in both communities of the time before, when the lake still held water and people lived along its shores. As the waters receded, some followed them down into the basin, learning to harvest every drop. Others climbed to where the springs remained, choosing elevation over excavation. Genetic markers confirm what the tales suggest—they are cousins, shaped by different pressures into complementary forms.

In times of crisis, this connection manifests. When the fifty-year drought came, Listeners opened their deepest pools to desperate city dwellers, saving thousands. When rockfall sealed the singing tubes and trapped a Listener clan, city engineers brought their ceramic tools and knowledge of structural weakness to free them. These debts are remembered but never spoken—to acknowledge them too directly would be to admit dependence.

The children know, though. City children playing at the market's edge sometimes catch the tonal speech, adding musical phrases to their whisper games. Listener youth examining traded pottery might trace spiral patterns that echo in their cliff drawings. In these small borrowings, the future writes itself—not reunion, which neither side desires, but synthesis, as each world's wisdom slowly seeps into the other.

Between them runs the deep water, connecting stone spring to clay cistern in the darkness below all human division. It carries minerals from the heights to the basins, dissolves salts that both communities need, maintains the pressure that keeps the springs flowing and the cisterns slowly filling. Neither the careful water-counters nor the free-drinking pool-dwellers fully grasp the system's complexity, but both depend on its continuance.

The land knows what the people forget: that city and stone, whisper and song, scarcity and abundance are merely different verses of the same ancient story—how humans learned to live when the world changed, and how they keep learning still. The cistern cities spread across their careful plain, the Listeners' realm rises in its singing stone, and between them, in market and memory and the movement of deep waters, the conversation continues.
