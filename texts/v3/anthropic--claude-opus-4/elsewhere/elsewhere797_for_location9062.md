The Lowland Markets

Three thousand feet below, where the mountain's roots spread into alluvial plains, the city of Ashpour sprawls beneath an unforgiving sun. Here, water is ceremony and commerce, arriving not in the constant seepage of stone but in twice-yearly floods that transform dust to mud to dust again. The city is built in rings—not perfect circles but organic swirls that follow the ancient flood patterns, each ring rising slightly higher than the last, creating a vast spiral of baked-mud buildings climbing toward a center that no flood has reached in living memory.

The architecture is the opposite of the mountain settlement's fluid adaptation—here, permanence is defiance. Buildings are made of mud bricks fired so hard they ring like bells when struck, their surfaces glazed with salt and sand until they gleam like beetle shells in the noon light. Every surface is smooth, curved, designed to shed the violent rains when they come. The streets are not streets but channels, dry most of the year, that become rushing rivers for a few precious weeks. Bridges arch over these empty waterways, worn smooth by generations of feet and the memory of water.

Ashpour lives by the calendar of floods. The city transforms with the season—in the dry months, the channels become markets, their depths filled with awnings and stalls, merchants spreading their wares across what will be riverbed. Ladders lean against the channel walls, and the lowest levels, which flood first, house only the poorest merchants selling goods that can be moved quickly. The prime spots are midway up—high enough to stay dry longer, low enough to attract customers who won't climb to the rim.

The memory of water shapes everything. Children play games that mimic the flood patterns, racing through the channels with blue ribbons that represent the returning rivers. Adults wear flood marks—intricate tattoos that record the highest water they've witnessed, worn like honors on forearms and calves. The oldest residents carry maps of decades on their skin, their bodies become records of plenty and drought.

This is where the mercury-bright tears arrive, carried down from the nameless peak by traders who know the treacherous paths. They come rarely—perhaps four times a year—moving at night to protect their evolved eyes from the harsh sun. The journey takes twenty days, following routes that exist more in memory than physical paths, as the mountain's shifting geometry erases trails as quickly as they're made.

The traders arrive at dawn, their pale forms ghostlike in the early light, fingers wrapped in cloth to hide their inhuman length. They cannot stay long. Even in the pre-dawn darkness, the open sky oppresses them. They conduct their business in the shadow markets—permanent structures built specifically for this trade, windowless and lit by oil lamps that approximate their eternal twilight.

What they bring is worth the journey. The mercury tears, transported in ceramic vessels wrapped in water cloth, are Ashpour's most precious commodity. The lowland metalworkers have learned to alloy it with copper and tin, creating bronze that never tarnishes, that holds an edge sharp enough to split silk. More than tools, it creates instruments of uncanny precision—water clocks that keep perfect time, surgical implements that seem to know where to cut, measuring devices that find true level even on the city's ancient, settling foundations.

In exchange, Ashpour sends up what the mountain cannot provide. Salt, carved from the dead lakes that dot the plains. Oils pressed from desert seeds, essential for preserving the water cloth and treating the wooden platforms. Most precious: the fired ceramic vessels themselves, made from clay that only forms where ancient seas once stood, shaped by potters who understand the precise curves needed to hold mercury without interaction. These ceramics are born in kilns that burn hotter than the mountain folk could ever achieve in their wet world, creating vessels that ring with perfect pitch when struck.

The trade is more than economics—it's an exchange of impossibilities. The mountain builders work stone like water; the lowlanders make water dance through their scheduled city like trained serpents. One builds with perpetual change, the other against it. The mercury tears are the blood that connects them, flowing down from the eternal construction to the cyclical markets, returning as vessels to hold the next harvest.

The traders speak a pidgin of gestures and whispers, bridging the gap between the mountain's sign language and Ashpour's voice-heavy tongue. Negotiations happen in the dark, hands dancing in lamplight, creating shadows on the walls that become part of the conversation. The lowland merchants have developed their own abbreviated sign language for these encounters, their stubby fingers clumsy but earnest in their attempts to speak in space rather than sound.

Children in Ashpour collect glimpses of the pale traders like treasures. They dare each other to peer into the shadow markets, to catch sight of those impossible fingers, those eyes that swim with too much pupil. They tell stories that grow in the telling—that the mountain people are born from stone eggs, that they can walk on ceilings, that their bones are made of the same ceramic as the vessels. Some of these stories approach truth by accident.

The lowland workers who handle the mercury tears develop their own changes over time. Their fingernails grow thicker, though never achieving the curved piton quality of the mountain dwellers. Their skin takes on a subtle shimmer, as if dusted with the finest metallic sand. Most mysteriously, they begin to dream of vertical spaces, of water flowing upward, of cities that exist in permanent incompleteness. Some leave their families to attempt the journey upward, though few survive the climb. Those who return speak in broken whispers of the impossible architecture, their hands moving in remembered signs they can no longer fully translate.

The two cities exist in symbiosis across impossible distance—one that dissolves and rebuilds, one that hardens and endures. The mercury tears flow between them like nerve signals between distant parts of the same organism. Each depends on the other for what they cannot create: permanence and change, preservation and transformation, the water that stays and the water that leaves.

The festivals align strangely. When Ashpour celebrates the flood prediction—the night when the astronomers announce the water's expected return—the mountain settlement experiences what they call the Brightening, when their eternal twilight lifts slightly and shadows soften. When the mountain builders complete a major section of their construction, adding a new fold to their vertical maze, Ashpour's wells run sweet for days, free of the mineral tang that usually flavors their deep water.

Old women in both places tell the same story in different ways—of a time when the cities were one, when the mountain walked down to drink from seasonal rivers, when the floods climbed high enough to kiss the stone peaks. They say the separation was necessary, that the two ways of building could not exist in the same space without canceling each other out. But the mercury tears remember, flowing between the cities like letters written in a language older than words, keeping the connection alive across three thousand feet of vertical distance and infinitely more in the spaces between what changes and what endures.

In Ashpour, they have a saying: "The mountain builds down as we build up." In the nameless settlement above, they sign something that translates roughly to: "The flatlands rise as we sink." Both are true. Both are the same. The world continues its ancient conversation between stone and water, permanence and change, the mercury tears carrying messages neither city fully understands but both know they cannot live without.
