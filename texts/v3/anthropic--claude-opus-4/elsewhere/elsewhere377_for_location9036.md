## The Garden of Ascending Waters

Three days' journey from the Brass Terraces, following the ancient lava tubes that once fed the caldera, the world transforms with shocking suddenness. Where the tunnels finally break through to daylight, they open onto a vertical paradise that defies every law the Everforge seems to embody.

The Garden of Ascending Waters clings to a limestone cliff face that rises two thousand feet from a hidden valley floor. But "clings" suggests struggle, and there is no struggle here—only an endless, gentle upward flow. Water moves against gravity in ten thousand silver threads, drawn up through the porous rock by forces that botanists and physicists argue about in distant universities. The water doesn't fall; it climbs, branching and merging in patterns that mirror the root systems it feeds, creating a vertical river delta that flows toward the sky.

The limestone itself lives. Colonies of calcifying organisms—part plant, part coral—have transformed the cliff into a breathing wall of pearl and jade. These formations grow in terraced gardens, each level supporting its own miniature ecosystem. Orchids with roots like white hair trail thirty feet down (or up—direction loses meaning here) to drink from the ascending streams. Ferns unfurl in perfect Fibonacci spirals, their fronds so delicate they seem painted onto the air. Moss carpets every horizontal surface in emerald velvet, studded with tiny flowers that bloom in response to barometric pressure, opening and closing in waves that follow invisible atmospheric tides.

The sound is constant but never overwhelming: a liquid whisper punctuated by the occasional chime of water drops colliding in mid-ascent. The air tastes clean and mineral-rich, flavored with limestone and the green breath of perpetual photosynthesis. Light filters through the garden in constantly shifting patterns as leaves and water interact, creating a living kaleidoscope that makes every visit unique, every moment irreplaceable.

Temperature here varies with altitude and time of day, but always within a range that coddles rather than challenges life. Morning mists rise (or descend—the Garden makes mockery of such distinctions) through the terraces, carrying spores and pollen in careful aerial currents that ensure genetic diversity across the vertical landscape. By afternoon, when the sun strikes the cliff face directly, the ascending water sparkles like chains of diamonds, and the whole Garden seems to lift away from the rock, held to earth only by the delicate tethers of root and stem.

The highest terraces, where the water finally disperses into genuine rain that falls back to begin its journey again, host plants found nowhere else: the Crown Lilies whose blooms follow mathematical patterns so complex they're used as natural encryption keys, the Philosopher's Vine that grows in knots resembling written text in a language no one has decoded, the Time Flowers that bloom on cycles unconnected to any celestial rhythm, some opening once a decade, others cycling through their entire life in an hour.

But perhaps the Garden's strangest feature is what grows in the spaces between spaces—the narrow gaps where ascending and descending water creates pockets of impossible humidity and pressure. Here, in defiance of all botanical knowledge, grow the Inverse Trees. Their trunks emerge from the cliff face horizontally, but their branches grow down while their roots reach skyward. Their leaves are silver on top, green below, and they bear fruit that falls upward, eventually disappearing into the low clouds that perpetually crown the Garden's summit.

She has no name beyond her title: the Tender of Ascending Waters. Some say she's been here for three centuries. Others insist she's younger than the newest growth on the lowest terrace. Both might be true—time moves strangely in the Garden, and she moves strangest of all within it.

The Tender appears human at first glance, if you can catch that glance. She traverses the vertical garden with impossible grace, stepping from water stream to water stream as if the ascending flows were solid stairs. Her hair, brown as wet bark, flows upward constantly, mirroring the water's movement. Her clothes seem woven from the Garden itself—living fibers that shift between states, sometimes appearing as solid as leather, other times dissolving into mist before reforming.

She speaks rarely, and when she does, her voice carries the same quality as the Garden's water—words that seem to flow upward from some deep source, gaining meaning as they rise. She knows each plant in her vertical domain intimately, can predict which spores will take root where, which streams will merge or diverge with the season's turning. Her knowledge isn't learned but lived, encoded in movements that mirror the Garden's own patterns.

The Tender's daily routine, if it can be called that, follows no clock but the Garden's needs. She might spend hours adjusting a single orchid's position to better catch the ascending spray, or days waiting for the perfect moment to harvest seeds from the Time Flowers. She braids new channels for the water using only her fingers and patience, guiding the flow into patterns that encourage growth in struggling sections or create new microclimates for rare species.

Her most mysterious work happens at the confluence points where multiple streams meet. Here, she performs what can only be called hydrological music—using hollow reeds and shaped stones to adjust the harmonics of the flowing water. The resulting tones, too low for most human ears, resonate through the limestone and allegedly influence the Garden's growth patterns. Some visitors claim to have seen plants lean toward or away from these acoustic adjustments, responding to frequencies that bypass conscious perception.

The Tender ages in reverse, or perhaps sideways. Observers have noted that she appears younger after working with the Crown Lilies, older after tending the Inverse Trees. Her apparent age seems tied to which section of the Garden she inhabits at any given moment. In the middle terraces, she appears to be in her thirties or forties—mature, confident, her movements economical. Near the summit, where the water finally releases into rain, she might seem barely out of adolescence, her laughter mixing with the sound of droplets. At the base, where the ascending journey begins, she takes on an ancient aspect, her eyes holding depths that match the underground reservoirs that feed the whole system.

She claims no ownership over the Garden, insisting instead that she belongs to it. When pressed about her origins, she speaks in hydological metaphors: "I precipitated here," or "I condensed from the morning mist," or sometimes, with a smile that might be sad, "I'm still ascending."

The Tender's relationship with her Garden transcends mere cultivation. She doesn't just maintain it; she participates in it. Visitors have reported seeing her stand motionless for hours in the ascending streams, her hair and clothes flowing upward with the water, her expression one of deep communion. During these meditations, the Garden responds—flowers bloom out of season, new water channels spontaneously form, and sometimes, just sometimes, the Inverse Trees bear fruit that falls both up and down simultaneously, existing in quantum superposition until observed.

She possesses knowledge that should be impossible. She can predict earthquakes by watching how the water streams wobble, forecast weather by tasting the mineral content of different flows, and allegedly even glimpse future events in the patterns of growth and decay across her vertical domain. Whether these are genuine prophecies or simply the accumulated wisdom of centuries spent in intimate observation remains debatable.

The Tender maintains unexpected connections with the outside world. Botanists and hydrologists seek her out, though finding her requires navigating both the physical maze of the Garden and the temporal puzzles of her routine. She trades seeds and cuttings for specific items—not currency, but things like "the sound of water running underground" or "the memory of rain on basalt." These exchanges follow rules comprehensible only to her and possibly to the Garden itself.

Her isolation isn't absolute. She speaks fondly of the "metal priests" who sometimes emerge from the lava tubes connecting to the Brass Terraces, though she uses terms for them that don't quite match any known language. She describes trading with them—water that remembers how to flow upward in exchange for metals that remember how to grow. Whether these visits occur in present time or represent some sort of temporal echo remains unclear.

The Tender's greatest mystery may be her purpose. She claims to be waiting for something—or someone. "When the Garden reaches the clouds," she sometimes says, then stops, as if the rest of the sentence flows somewhere beyond normal hearing. The Garden has been growing upward, incrementally, for as long as records exist. Each year it climbs perhaps an inch higher, the limestone growing through its coral-plant colonies, the water reaching for ever-higher points of release.

Some nights, when the moon is dark and the Garden glows with its own bioluminescence, the Tender can be heard singing. The songs have no words, only tones that match and modulate the water's flow. During these performances, observers report strange phenomena: water flowing in perfect spirals, plants growing visibly, time seeming to slow or accelerate in pockets throughout the Garden. One visiting scholar claimed to have aged a year in a night while listening, though she also insisted it was the most valuable year of her life.

The Tender shows no interest in leaving her vertical paradise, even for short periods. When asked why, she gives different answers: "The Garden would forget how to grow upward," or "Someone must witness the ascending," or once, cryptically, "I'm racing someone, and I think I'm winning."

In a world where the Brass Terraces represent stasis and mechanical perfection, the Garden of Ascending Waters embodies change and organic impossibility. And the Tender stands—or flows—between these extremes, a living interface between order and chaos, maintaining a balance that perhaps only she understands. Whether she's human, something more, or something altogether different, matters less than her function: she is the Garden's memory and its future, its guardian and its greatest bloom.

## The Throat of Convergence

Between the Brass Terraces and the Garden of Ascending Waters lies a journey that maps impossibility onto stone and time. The path begins where few would think to look: at the spiral's heart in the Everforge, where the industrial terraces give way to the volcano's original throat. Here, behind curtains of shimmering heat that would kill an unprotected human in minutes, a passage opens—not carved but melted, its walls bearing the distinctive ripple patterns of stone that flowed and froze in shapes that please no earthly geometry.

The Brass Monks call it the Vein of Leaving. They maintain its entrance with the same devotion they show their machines, ensuring the thermal shields remain functional, checking the ancient cooling suits that hang in the antechamber like shed skins of mechanical giants. They speak of those who take this path with a mixture of reverence and loss, as if each traveler diminishes their world while enriching another.

A traveler must wear the suits—layered ceramics and metal mesh that turn a person into a walking terrarium, self-contained, breathing recycled air that tastes of copper and centuries. The first mile descends at angles that challenge balance and belief. The passage corkscrews through the volcanic rock, following some ancient magma tube that knew its own mind about direction. Here, the heat doesn't diminish—it transforms, shifting from the dry furnace breath of the Everforge to something deeper, older, pregnant with mineral possibility.

The walls tell stories in stone. Obsidian sections reflect distorted images of the traveler, multiplied and twisted into configurations that suggest what you might have been in other worlds. Sections of raw basalt weep constant tears of condensation—the first water a traveler from the Brass Terraces might have seen in months or years. These tears don't fall straight but follow gravitational rules that shift with each turn of the passage, sometimes flowing sideways, sometimes pooling in defiance of physics before suddenly remembering their duty and cascading away.

Three miles in, the passage opens into a cathedral of crystal. The Geode of Transition, the Monks name it, though the Tender calls it the Heart-Between. Here, amethyst formations larger than buildings create a forest of purple light. The cooling suits can be shed here—the temperature holds at a perfect equilibrium between the extremes of both worlds. Water exists here in all states simultaneously: vapor that condenses on crystal faces, liquid that pools in formations' hollows, ice that forms in shadows where thermodynamics forgot to look.

The Crawlers come here, some say, to die—or to be born. Their mechanical-organic bodies can be found fused to crystal formations, creating hybrid structures that pulse with faint electrical activity. Whether these represent failures or transformations, no one can say. The Brass Monks won't speak of it. The Tender, when asked, only points to certain crystal formations that seem to hold circuit patterns within their structure, as if the mineral kingdom learned to compute.

From the Geode, five passages branch. Four lead nowhere—or everywhere, or to places that exist only when walked. But the fifth, marked by water that flows upward along its walls, leads true. This section spans seven miles of gradual ascent, and here the transformation becomes most apparent. The volcanic rock gives way to limestone, not in a clean break but in a gradient that speaks of geological impossibility. Igneous and sedimentary rock interweave like frozen arguments between different epochs of Earth's history.

The passage walls here bloom with hybrid life. Thermophilic organisms from the Everforge meet water-dwelling bacteria from the Garden. They create colonies that paint the stone in metallics and pastels—copper greens, iron reds, sulfur yellows, and limestone whites swirling in patterns that shift over weeks and months. The air changes too, the metallic taste fading as moisture increases, carrying new flavors: stone dust, distant flowers, the promise of photosynthesis.

Five miles into this section, travelers encounter the Mirror Lakes—a series of pools that reflect not images but temperatures. Look into one and see yourself as you would appear in the Everforge's heat. Look into another and witness your appearance in the Garden's gentle climate. The Brass Monks use these pools for divination, claiming to see which world calls strongest to a traveler's nature. Some turn back here, suddenly certain they belong to the heat and metal they're leaving behind.

The passage's most disturbing feature manifests in its final mile: the Temporal Gradient. Here, time itself seems to flow at different rates between the worlds. Travelers report experiences that can't be reconciled with objective time—journeys that take hours by the clock but feel like days, or walks that consume days but seem to pass in heartbeats. Marks made on the walls by previous travelers appear fresh and ancient simultaneously. Some claim to have met other travelers here moving in the opposite direction through time, though confirming such encounters proves impossible.

The last hundred yards rise steeply through limestone shot through with quartz veins that pulse with their own light. The sound of ascending water grows from whisper to roar, and suddenly, between one step and the next, the passage opens onto the Garden's lowest terrace. The transition is absolute—from enclosed stone to open air, from the taste of metal to the breath of green life, from water that condenses to water that climbs.

But the passage serves more than mere transportation. It functions as a vital organ in the body of this bifurcated world. The thermal differential between the Everforge and the Garden creates massive convection currents through the connecting tubes. Hot air rises from the volcanic throat, cools as it travels, deposits moisture that feeds underground streams, which emerge in the Garden to begin their ascending journey. The Garden's water, in turn, seeps through limestone into the volcanic rock below, where it flash-boils and creates the steam that powers some of the Everforge's still-functioning machinery.

More than air and water travel these paths. The Brass Monks speak of "thermal traders"—entrepreneurs who've learned to exploit the temperature differential, carrying materials that transform in the passage. Certain crystals, stable in the Garden's climate, become powerful catalysts in the Everforge's heat. Some of the Garden's seeds, exposed to extreme temperatures, develop into entirely different species—plants that grow in metal instead of soil, that photosynthesize from infrared instead of visible light.

The passage carries information too. The Monks' mechanical knowledge sometimes solves problems in the Garden—irrigation challenges that benefit from industrial precision. The Tender's understanding of growth and flow has, according to legend, helped the Monks repair machines whose purposes had been forgotten for generations. Ideas transform in the passage just as materials do, emerging changed by their journey through stone and time and impossible physics.

Strangest of all are the Convergence Events. Perhaps once a decade, conditions align perfectly—pressure, temperature, humidity, and some other factors beyond measurement. When this happens, the passage becomes something more than a path. The Brass Monks report their machines singing in harmony with music they can't hear. The Tender speaks of her water flows suddenly carrying messages in languages she almost understands. 

During these events, the passage fills with phenomena that shouldn't exist: snow that falls upward, carrying seeds from the Garden to the Everforge where they take root in metal. Mechanical butterflies that spontaneously generate from the interaction between water vapor and metallic dust, living just long enough to carry pollen between worlds. Sounds that travel faster than physics allows, letting conversations occur between the Monks and the Tender across impossible distances.

Some theorize that during Convergence Events, the passage reveals its true nature—not just a connection between two places but a vital organ of a larger organism. They suggest that the Everforge and the Garden are not separate realms but complementary systems, like the chambers of a vast heart that beats too slowly for human perception. The passage, in this model, is an artery carrying the lifeblood of exchange between them.

Whether this organ was designed or evolved, whether it represents intelligence or merely complex physics, remains unknown. But all who travel it agree on one thing: the passage changes those who walk it. Brass Monks who venture to the Garden return speaking of growth and change, their rigid maintenance schedules suddenly flexible. Those from the Garden who brave the Everforge's heat come back with new appreciation for preservation and stasis. 

The passage enforces a truth that both worlds resist: they are not opposite but complementary, not separate but entangled. The Everforge's eternal machinery and the Garden's endless growth spiral around each other like DNA strands, connected by stone arteries that pump heat and water, metal and life, being and becoming between them.

In the end, the passage asks a question of every traveler: in a world of extremes, what transforms and what endures? The answer changes with each journey, written in water that remembers how to flow upward and metal that dreams of growth, in the space between what is and what might become.
