**The Reach**

Three days upward from the valley's rim, following the ghost of a road that switchbacks through collapsing talus, the plateau breaks into a landscape of towers. Not built towers—grown ones, rising from beds of the same luminous moss that carpets the valley below, but here twisted into vertical spirals that climb forty, fifty meters into the perpetual wind. The towers are hollow, their walls woven from metallic vines that pulse with the same bioelectric current, but fainter here, attenuated by distance from the aurora's direct light.

Between the towers stretch cables—some as thick as your waist, others fine as hair—creating a web of connections that hum and spark in the thin air. The cables are alive, or close enough: sheaths of the same conductor-flesh that covers the Tenders below, growing from tower to tower in sagging curves that shift and adjust with the wind. When storms come—and they come often here, drawn by the electrical differential between the charged towers and the neutral sky—the cables light up like a nervous system, channeling lightning down through the towers' hollow cores to batteries buried deep in the mesa's stone.

This is where the valley's power comes to be transformed. The towers are receivers and transmitters both, gathering the steady current that flows up from below through massive underground cables and converting it into something else—signals, perhaps, or a different frequency of power altogether. Inside each tower's base, glimpsed through gaps in the vine-walls, machinery turns: not metal gears but organs of hardened moss, processing the raw electricity into packages of coded light that race up the hollow cores and burst from the tops in colors beyond the visible spectrum.

The few who work here are changed by it. They call themselves Readers, though what they read and for whom remains obscure. Unlike the fluid Tenders, the Readers have adapted to verticality—their bodies elongated and segmented like centipedes stood on end, with gripping appendages every half-meter that allow them to scale the towers' inner walls with disturbing ease. Their heads are mostly sensory apparatus: clusters of crystalline eyes that can parse the light-signals, tympanic membranes that translate electrical frequency into something like language.

They live in pods woven into the cable network, suspended between towers like fruit in a web. The pods sway constantly in the wind, but the Readers seem to prefer this motion, claiming it helps them think. Inside, the walls are lined with monitoring equipment—not screens or readouts but living displays, patches of specialized moss that glow in patterns corresponding to the signals passing through the cables. The Readers watch these for hours, occasionally reaching out with feather-thin appendages to adjust a connection, redirect a flow, or tap out responses in the same light-language.

What are they reading? The question haunts the space between towers. Some say they're interpreting messages from elsewhere—that the valley's power generation is only half the story, that the real purpose is communication with something or somewhere distant. Others believe they're reading the future in the patterns of electrical flow, that the entire system is a vast computational device solving equations too complex for any single mind to grasp. The Readers themselves speak little of it, their verbal language atrophied from generations of light-speech.

The Reach—for that is what they call this high network—operates on different cycles than the valley. Where below time moves in slow organic rhythms, here everything accelerates. The towers grow visibly, adding a meter of height each year. Cables stretch and snap and are regrown within days. The Readers age quickly, their crystalline sensory organs clouding and fracturing after just a decade of exposure to the intense signals. When they die, they're wrapped in new cable sheaths and strung between towers, their bodies becoming part of the network they spent their lives interpreting.

Young Readers are brought up from the valley—volunteer Tenders who've shown aptitude for pattern recognition, who've demonstrated the ability to perceive beyond the simple maintenance of the moss fields. The transformation is gradual but irreversible: exposure to the Reach's frequencies triggers dormant genes, lengthening their bodies, multiplying their sensory organs, teaching their neurons to fire in harmony with the light-signals. Within a year, they can no longer return to the valley's depths—their modified nervous systems would overload in the denser electrical fields below.

The relationship between valley and Reach is symbiotic but strange. Power flows upward, transformed and transmitted onward to purposes unknown. Information flows downward—the Readers occasionally send signals that adjust the Tenders' behavior, inspiring new growth patterns, directing energy to different sectors. But neither fully understands the other anymore. The Tenders perceive the Readers as distant gods, their light-speech incomprehensible but clearly meaningful. The Readers see the Tenders as a necessary but primitive foundation, their work crucial but unconscious.

Between them runs the current—the same force that powers both their biologies, that grows the moss and charges the cables, that turns the valleys and towers into a single vast organism with two specialized organs. The Tenders maintain the power generation with perfect efficiency. The Readers process and transmit with frantic purpose. Neither questions the system's ultimate goal, if it has one.

At night—though night here is relative, the aurora simply dimming rather than disappearing—you can see the Reach from the valley's edge: a constellation of pulsing towers, cables lit like neural pathways, the whole network breathing with electrical life. And if you could see the valley from the Reach's height, you'd understand how the two fit together: the valley a vast battery, the Reach its flowering crown, the entire landscape a single machine made of living light, generating and transmitting and reading itself in an endless cycle of purpose without meaning, or meaning without purpose, depending on your angle of view.

The wind never stops here. It plays the cables like an instrument, creating harmonics that the Readers claim contain messages of their own. Sometimes, during the fierce storms, lightning strikes every tower simultaneously, and for a moment the entire Reach becomes a single brilliant neuron firing in the dark. In that instant, the Readers say, everything makes sense—the purpose, the pattern, the destination of all those carefully packaged signals racing upward into space.

But the moment passes, and the work continues: reading the unreadable, transmitting the untranslatable, maintaining a connection with something that may or may not be listening, may or may not exist, may or may not care about the vast living circuit spreading across the plateau, powered by the valley below, reaching always upward into the electric dark.
