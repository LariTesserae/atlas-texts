The Sunward March

Above the ice, beyond counting of storms and seasons, the earth burns dry. Here the sun has grown old and close, a swollen eye that never blinks shut. The land bakes in perpetual noon, sand fused to glass in wandering rivers where ancient waters once ran. What vegetation survives grows low and deep—thorned things with roots like desperate fists, leaves reduced to waxy nubs that hoard their moisture like bitter secrets.

The walkers move in lines across this furnace landscape, following paths worn by millions of identical journeys. They are tall where the Dwellers grew long, dark where the underground ones paled to transparency. Their skin has thickened to leather, their eyes narrowed to permanent squints against the light that never ends. They wear the desert like a second skeleton—sand in the creases of their joints, crystals of salt marking the paths of old sweat, hair bleached and brittled to the texture of dried grass.

They cannot stop. This is the first law, written not in words but in the shape of their bodies, the rhythm of their breathing. To pause is to bake, to rest is to become landscape. So they walk, and have always walked, in great spirals that take a lifetime to complete. The young are born walking—mothers deliver without breaking stride, catching infants who know to lock their fingers in the walking-ropes before they know to cry.

Each spiral takes them from water to water, though water here means something different than in any other place. The sources are deep wells capped with ancient mechanisms, wind-powered things that draw moisture from depths unguessed. The walkers know them by their songs—each well hums differently as the wind plays through its workings, a mechanical throat singing of the water far below. They time their spirals to these songs, arriving just as the previous group departs, a choreography of survival measured in generations.

They carry their houses on their backs—not as burden but as body. Frameworks of hollow bone (whose bone? they no longer remember) extend from shoulder-harnesses, draped with skins that can be configured into shelter, clothing, water-catchers, or sails depending on need. In the high winds, whole lines of walkers unfurl their house-skins and let the desert carry them forward, feet barely touching ground, a migration become flight.

Their children learn the paths before they learn words. Each major landmark has its step-count, its breath-pattern, its particular way of placing the foot. The Grandfather Stone (a wind-carved pillar like a reaching hand) requires seven hundred and thirty-one steps of approach, each placed just so. The Salt Gardens where nothing grows demand a shuffle that doesn't disturb the crystal patterns. The Bone Fields—where some ancient dying collected all its participants in one place—must be crossed in single file, eyes down, humming the note that keeps the wind from singing in the skulls.

Knowledge passes from body to body through the walking itself. An elder's gait carries libraries—the slight favor of a left foot means "water in three days," the swing of arms held just so indicates "storm season approaching." They have words, but use them sparingly, like water. Most communication happens in the placement of feet, the rhythm of walking sticks, the angle at which a house-skin catches the wind.

They remember the ice, though none have seen it. The memory lives in their songs—wordless hums that synchronize breathing to movement, that turn a line of walkers into a single organism undulating across the scalded earth. In these songs, coldness is a concept like color to the blind, imagined but never experienced. They sing of stillness the way other peoples sing of love—as the impossible ideal, the perfect opposite of all they know.

At the center of their spirals, where all paths eventually lead, stands the Observatory. Here the ancients built machines for watching the sky, though the walkers use them now for watching the earth. Great lenses of polished crystal, turned by wind-wheels, focus the sun's rage into beams that can read the land like a book. Through these, the farsighted among them can spot new wells, track the movement of sand seas, predict where the glass rivers will run when the heat grows too much for even stone to bear.

The Observatory keepers are the only walkers who stop. They live brief lives, a handful of years before the stillness claims them, but in that time they gather all the knowledge of the paths into great maps made of knotted rope and carved bone. Each walker carries a section of these maps, worn smooth by generations of fingers, adding their own knots for new discoveries, new dangers, new patterns in the eternal spiral.

When walkers die—and death comes sudden here, between one step and the next—they are not buried but dispersed. The body is taken apart with ritual precision, each useful piece distributed among the line. Bones become tent poles, skin becomes water vessels, hair becomes rope. Nothing is waste in a world that offers nothing freely. The dead continue walking in the framework of the living, each person a collection of ancestors reformed for another generation's journey.

In the deepest part of night—for even here, the sun must sometimes drift below the horizon—they allow themselves to cluster, house-skins overlapping into a single shelter. In these brief respites, pressed body to body for warmth against the desert's cold, they tell stories with their fingers against each other's backs. The stories are always about stopping. About a place where water rises from below instead of falling from above. About creatures small enough to hold, slow enough to watch. About stones that stay where they're placed.

They know, in the way that bones know their shape, that somewhere there are people who have achieved this impossible thing: stillness without death, gathering without dispersal. But knowledge and understanding are different things. They can no more imagine true stillness than the Dwellers below can imagine the weight of constant light, the terrible freedom of horizons that never end.

So they walk. Morning finds them already in motion, evening leaves them still moving, and in between they trace their spirals like prayers to a god of continuous motion. They are pilgrims to nowhere, citizens of the journey itself, creating home in the space between one step and the next. The earth burns beneath them, the sky burns above them, and they move between these fires like a living wick, carrying their small portion of shade and story across the face of the endless day.

Between the frozen city and the burning march, water remembers its whole journey. It begins in the deepest wells, drawn up by wind and patience into the throats of walkers. It lives briefly in their bodies—salt-tinged, rationed, sacred—before returning to the air through breath and skin. The air carries it in wisps and traces, moisture so thin it's barely there, until it finds the cracks, the fissures, the secret paths down through stone and ice to where the Dwellers wait in their patient tending.

There it condenses on the cave walls, gathers into droplets, begins its slow seepage through the carved channels. The pale crustaceans taste in it the memory of sunlight they'll never see. The Dwellers read in its mineral content the story of stones they'll never touch. And drop by drop, it makes its way through the underground city, pooling in the ancient places where the crystallized dead slowly dissolve, carrying their memories back up into the great cycle.

Neither people know of the other, yet they are bound in this essential trade. The walkers' eternal motion stirs the atmosphere that drives the water down. The Dwellers' careful tending creates the humidity that rises through hidden vents to seed the desert's rare clouds. Each is half of a single system, a metabolism spread across vertical miles, unconsciously collaborating in the great work of keeping water in motion.

Sometimes the walkers, in their deepest exhaustion, dream of a cool place where nothing moves. Sometimes the Dwellers, in their fondest meditation, feel warmth on skin that has never known it. These are not visions but memories—the water's memories, carried in their bodies, connecting depth to surface in an endless circulation of longing.

The world turns on this axis of opposites. Above, the tyranny of light and motion. Below, the sovereignty of dark and stillness. Between them, the water carries messages neither can read but both somehow understand—that somewhere else, another people have solved the unsolvable, lived the unlivable, found a way to be human in conditions that demand otherwise.

They are each other's afterlife, each other's impossible paradise, each other's proof that survival takes every form except the one you know.
