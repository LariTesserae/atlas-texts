Above, there is still sun.

The Skygarden sprawls across the atmosphere like a living cloud continent, its foundation a mesh of crystallized water vapor and solidified light. Here, plants have learned to feed on radiation directly, their leaves transparent as glass, their roots drinking condensation from the air itself. Everything is pale—bleached by constant exposure, adapted to brightness that would blind ground-dwelling eyes.

The gardens float at varying altitudes, connected by bridges of woven wind. Some drift with the jet streams, others anchor to mountain peaks with tethers of braided aurora. The largest gardens have developed their own weather systems—miniature hurricanes that water the crystal trees, pocket lightning storms that fertilize the sky-soil with nitrogen. At the highest levels, where atmosphere meets vacuum, the gardens wear shells of frozen oxygen, blue as dreams.

The flora here grows in reverse of expectation. Trees hang inverted, crowns spreading downward, roots reaching up to pierce the magnetosphere and feed on solar wind. Vines climb down through empty air, creating vertical forests that sway with atmospheric tides. Flowers bloom larger than clouds, their petals thin enough to see through, casting colored shadows on the earth below that ground-dwellers mistake for divine omens.

The Skykeepers tend these impossible gardens. Unlike their undergrowth cousins, they've evolved toward lightness—hollow bones like birds, skin that photosynthesizes, lungs that process thin air with impossible efficiency. They leap between garden levels in bounds that last hours, their bodies sustained by stored sunlight. When they speak, they exhale glowing mist, their words visible as golden spirals in the clarified air.

They build with calcified sunbeams and compressed weather. Their tools are made of solid lightning, their homes spun from cirrus silk. They've learned to weave wind into fabric, to forge rainbow into metal that holds its spectrum even in darkness. Their architecture defies gravity through pure conviction—towers that grow downward, bridges that arch into nothing, doors that open onto sky.

The greatest structure is the Solar Cathedral, a living building that tracks the sun across the sky. Its walls are grown from a single organism—a hybrid of air-plant and light-coral that feeds on both radiation and worship. Inside, the Skykeepers gather at dawn and dusk to sing photosynthesis into being, their harmonies adding wavelengths to visible light, creating colors that don't exist below.

Between the two gardens runs the Vertican—the impossible space that connects above to below, a churning column of transition where light and dark wage eternal war.

The Vertican has no fixed location. It moves like a tornado made of paradox, touching down wherever the barrier between gardens grows thin. Sometimes it manifests as a perfectly circular hole in the earth, edges lined with crystallizing roots that crack like glass when the light hits them. Sometimes it appears as a cloud that descends past the point of reason, its interior flashing with darkness visible, its edges bleeding shadowlight that stains the sky.

To enter the Vertican is to experience gravity as opinion. Travelers find themselves falling upward and downward simultaneously, their bodies stretched between competing truths. The air here can't decide what it is—one breath might fill lungs with liquid light, the next with solid darkness. Temperature fluctuates between solar furnace and absolute zero, sometimes in the space between heartbeats.

The Vertican is inhabited by creatures that belong to neither garden—the Between-Things, entities that exist only in transition. They have forms like moving contradictions: birds with root-wings, worms made of condensed aurora, flowers that bloom darkness in petals of crystallized sunlight. They feed on the confusion of travelers, growing stronger from paradox, their life cycles measured in the time it takes for certainty to collapse.

Both gardens know about the Vertican but interpret it differently. The Undergardeners see it as a wound that occasionally tears open, letting in the poison of light—they've built underground shelters where they wait out its appearances, tending their darkness-crops behind walls of packed earth and negative space. The Skykeepers view it as a necessary evil, a drainage system that carries away the heaviness that accumulates in their high gardens—they've developed rituals to predict its arrival, evacuating sections of sky before the darkness rises.

Neither knows the truth: the Vertican is where their gardens were born.

Long before the division, there was a single garden that existed in twilight, balanced perfectly between earth and sky. Its gardeners tended both root and crown, darkness and light in equal measure. But perfection is unstable. A philosophical schism developed—some gardeners believed true growth came from burrowing deep, others insisted life's purpose was to climb high. The argument grew into ideology, ideology into transformation.

The garden itself couldn't bear the tension. It split like a cell dividing, each half carrying its own truth to extremes. The Undergarden plunged into earth, embracing absolute darkness. The Skygarden lifted into atmosphere, seeking pure light. The Vertican is what remains of their connection—scar tissue between severed halves, a reminder that they were once one organism.

The Vertican carries messages between gardens, though neither side admits to reading them. Spores from the Undergarden sometimes get caught in its updrafts, carried skyward where they land on cloud-soil and grow into dark spots that the Skykeepers quickly prune away. Light-seeds from above occasionally fall through, taking root in the depths where they sprout into pale cancers that the Undergardeners carefully contain. Each garden pretends these exchanges don't happen, maintaining their purity through selective blindness.

But the exchanges increase. The Vertican appears more frequently, stays longer, grows wider. Some whisper that the gardens are trying to reunite, that the division was always meant to be temporary—a long breath before reconciliation. Others insist the Vertican is a plague, that each appearance weakens the barriers further, that eventually it will swallow both gardens into its spinning paradox.

The wisest of both sides know a deeper truth: the Vertican isn't between the gardens—it IS the garden, the original form they've both forgotten. Every appearance is an invitation to remember wholeness, to accept that growth requires both descent and ascent, that true gardening happens not in extremes but in the tension between opposites.

There are stories of gardeners from both sides meeting in the Vertican—Undergardeners rising, Skykeepers descending, finding each other in the spinning middle where neither's advantages apply. They speak in languages neither fully remembers, share techniques that only work in transition, tend plants that grow in multiple dimensions simultaneously. Some say these meetings are producing new gardeners, adapted to paradox, equally comfortable in blinding light and perfect darkness.

These hybrid gardeners, if they exist, might be the future. Or they might be the past reasserting itself. In the Vertican, time spins like everything else—future and history blend into a single eternal moment of transformation.

The two gardens continue their separate evolutions, each perfecting its own impossible agriculture. The Undergarden grows ever deeper, ever darker, its consciousness spreading through global root systems. The Skygarden climbs ever higher, ever brighter, its presence becoming indistinguishable from weather itself. And between them, the Vertican spins its patient message: division is just another phase of growth, and all gardens, no matter how far they spread, share the same soil, the same sky, the same irrepressible urge to transform light into life into meaning.

The world turns, and with each rotation, the Vertican touches down somewhere new, offering its terrible gift of connection. One day, perhaps, the gardens will be ready to accept it. Until then, they grow apart, each convinced of its own necessity, each secretly yearning for the wholeness they sacrificed for certainty.

Above, the sun burns on, feeding one garden. Below, the darkness breathes, nurturing another. Between them, the truth spins and waits and occasionally opens its mouth to swallow anyone brave enough to remember that once, there was no difference between root and crown, between the breathing dark and the singing light.
