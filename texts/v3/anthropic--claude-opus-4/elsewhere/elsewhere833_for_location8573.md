The Iron Forest of Yith spreads across the polar shelf above Keth-Maral, though "above" means little when the world itself is a vast vertical labyrinth of stone shelves and void. The trees here are not trees—they are ferrous growths that drink magnetism from the planet's core, rising in perfectly hexagonal columns that branch at mathematical intervals. Each trunk measures exactly six meters in diameter, their surfaces etched with spiraling grooves that channel the polar winds into haunting symphonies.

The canopy exists in three distinct layers. The lowest branches spread at thirty meters, their leaves thin sheets of oxidized metal that ring like bells in the constant wind. These copper-green plates photosynthesize not sunlight but magnetic flux, turning the invisible fields into chemical energy. The middle canopy at sixty meters hosts the silver leaves—flexible as fabric, sharp as razors, forever folding and unfolding in response to field variations. The highest branches at ninety meters bear the black leaves, which no one has successfully studied because they absorb all light and seem to exist partially outside normal space.

Between the trunks, the ground is soft with centuries of metallic leaf-fall, a carpet of rust and verdigris that crunches underfoot. Nothing organic grows here, but there is life: the Resonance Worms burrow through the metal mulch, their bodies composed of wound copper wire and flexible ferrite cores. They process the fallen leaves, breaking them down into constituent elements that seep into the soil and eventually rise again through the root systems. Larger creatures stride between the trunks on legs like tuning forks—the Harmonic Striders, whose every step creates a pure tone that echoes through the forest.

The light here comes filtered through metal leaves, casting everything in shifting patterns of green and silver. During magnetic storms, the entire forest glows with St. Elmo's fire, blue-white electricity arcing between branches in lazy spirals. The trees themselves pulse with internal light during these events, their trunks revealing the flowing patterns of magnetic sap beneath the metallic bark.

Sound defines this place more than sight. The wind through the leaves creates a constant metallic whisper that rises and falls with the planet's rotation. Each tree resonates at its own frequency, and together they form vast accidental symphonies that can drive visitors mad or lull them into trances. The Resonance Worms add their own notes—high pitched whines as they burrow, occasional discordant clangs when they strike buried metal. The Harmonic Striders' footsteps provide a walking bassline that travelers learn to follow, knowing that where the Striders walk, the ground is stable.

The forest extends to the shelf edge, where the trees grow shorter and more twisted, their trunks spiraling as they struggle against the conflicting magnetic fields. Here, the Edgewater Falls cascade down toward the suspended cisterns of Keth-Maral, carrying with it a slurry of metallic sediment that the Siphon-Born harvest and process. The water itself becomes magnetized in its passage through the forest, arriving at the cisterns charged with properties that affect both the mechanimals and the human inhabitants.

In the deep forest, where the canopy blocks all natural light, bioluminescent fungi have adapted to feed on metal. They grow in geometric patterns on the trunks—perfect circles, triangles, and more complex shapes that mathematicians claim represent equations. The Compass Moss always points toward the planet's magnetic center, growing thicker on the north faces of trees. During the polar night, these organisms provide the only illumination, turning the forest into a constellation of cold blue lights.

The forest moves, though slowly. Over centuries, the trees migrate, following deep magnetic currents in the planet's crust. Ancient surveys show the forest has traveled nearly a kilometer in the last thousand years, leaving behind geometric clearings where the soil has been drained of all ferrous material. In these dead zones, something else grows: crystalline formations that hum with stored magnetic energy, occasionally discharging in brilliant displays that can be seen from the cisterns far below.

At the forest's heart stands the Conductor—a tree three times the diameter of its siblings, its trunk a single piece of superconducting material that scientists believe predates the rest of the forest. It never grew leaves. Instead, its branches terminate in complex geometric shapes that rotate slowly, seemingly of their own accord. The other trees grow in perfect concentric circles around it, their spacing determined by mathematical relationships that repeat at every scale.

The fauna here has evolved beyond simple magnetotropism. The Silver Beetles navigate by creating small magnetic fields with specialized organs, their shells polished to mirror perfection. They tend the trees like groundskeepers, removing parasitic growths and maintaining the spiral grooves that channel wind. The Polar Foxes have fur that stands on end during magnetic storms, each hair a tiny compass needle. They hunt the beetles by predicting their magnetic navigation patterns, pouncing with uncanny accuracy.

Larger predators stalk the edge zones: the Lodestone Bears, whose bones are so dense with metallic deposits they can barely swim, but whose magnetic sense allows them to detect prey through solid metal. They hibernate during calm periods, emerging only during the storms that charge their bodies with enough energy to hunt. Their primary prey are the Grounding Deer—creatures whose antlers are elaborate lightning rods, forever sprouting new tines that they scrape against the trees to discharge accumulated static.

The connection between the Iron Forest and the Suspended Cisterns runs deeper than the Edgewater Falls. The magnetic fields generated by the forest create the precise conditions needed to suspend the cisterns—a discovery made only recently when a section of forest was clear-cut for study and three spheres immediately began to sink. The trees' root systems extend down through the stone shelf, their tips dangling in the void where the cisterns hang, creating an electromagnetic cradle that holds the entire inverted city in place.

The Siphon-Born know this connection in their bones—literally. The minerals they process from the Edgewater Falls become part of their biology, making them sensitive to the forest's magnetic rhythms. Their breeding cycles align with the magnetic storms above. Their dreams during these periods are filled with visions of walking between metal trees, of roots and pipes intertwining, of a single vast organism that encompasses both forest and cistern.

During the strongest storms, when the Conductor Tree's rotating branches spin fast enough to create audible whines, the Siphon-Born in the frontier spheres report strange phenomena. The evolved mechanimals swim in perfect spirals, mimicking the mathematical patterns of the forest above. The aberrant water forms standing waves that match the frequencies resonating through the stone. Some of the Pipe-Runners claim they can navigate by sound alone during these times, following harmonics that lead them through passages they've never explored.

The Inheritors speak of the Echo Trading—the annual moment when representatives climb the Vertical Miles, the shaft that connects their world to the forest above. They carry containers of processed water, charged with minerals and mechanical life. In exchange, the forest dwellers—if they can be called that—lower down magnetic ore and metal leaves, materials essential for maintaining the cisterns' infrastructure. But the forest dwellers are rarely seen, and some wonder if they exist at all, or if the forest itself is somehow conducting the trade.

The two realms exist in perfect symbiosis, though neither fully understands the other. The Iron Forest processes magnetism into matter, the Suspended Cisterns process matter into life, and between them flows a current of water and metal, sound and dream, that maintains both worlds in their delicate, impossible balance. The Deep Builders, perhaps, understood this connection when they created—or found, or became—these places. Their legacy lives on in the resonance between root and pipe, between the mathematical forest and the industrial ocean, between the Inheritors who swim below and whatever consciousness inhabits the singing trees above.
