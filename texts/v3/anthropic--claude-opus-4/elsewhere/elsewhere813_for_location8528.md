The Depths

Three hundred meters of vertical descent separate the Vapor Gardens from the Depths, though the journey between them follows no straight line. The path winds through the mountain's hollowed core, a spiral of maintenance tunnels and service shafts where the temperature rises with each downward turn. The walls sweat mineral water that tastes of iron and sulfur. Ancient pressure gauges, their faces cracked and blackened, mark distances not in meters but in atmospheres—one, two, five, ten—until the brass fittings begin to glow faintly from the heat within.

The Depths proper begin where the last pretense of human architecture ends. Here, the mountain's natural caverns have been minimally modified—just enough to house the great boilers and their attendant machinery, but not enough to fully tame the space. The ceiling vanishes into darkness punctuated by stalactites that drip scalding water into pools that have never known coolness. The floor is uneven, worn smooth in pathways between the machines but elsewhere sharp with mineral deposits that grow like coral in the constant humidity.

The boilers themselves are monuments to forgotten engineering. Each stands forty feet tall, riveted iron vessels that have developed their own geographies of rust and mineral accumulation. They were built to burn coal, but the fires that once required constant feeding have been replaced by geothermal taps—massive pipes that descend through the floor into depths unmeasured, drawing heat from the planet's core. The conversion was done with brutal efficiency: coal doors welded shut, fireboxes retrofitted with heat exchangers, ash pits repurposed as drainage sumps for the constant condensation.

Between the boilers, a forest of pipes creates a three-dimensional maze. Some are original—iron and brass turned black with age and heat. Others are newer additions, stainless steel that seems garish against the patinated darkness. They carry steam upward to the gardens, but also sideways and down to purposes unknown. Some pipes have ruptured over the centuries, their breaks either repaired with crude welds or simply abandoned, the steam finding new paths through the mechanical ecosystem.

The heat here is overwhelming but not uniform. Near the geothermal taps, the air shimmers and metal cannot be touched without thick gloves. But there are cool spots too—places where underground streams have carved channels through the rock, creating microenvironments where different kinds of life persist. In these thermal gradients, strange economies have developed. Bacterial mats coat the cooler pipes, processing minerals from the steam. Where hot meets cold, condensation happens so rapidly it sounds like rain, creating permanent waterfalls that have carved their own channels through the industrial landscape.

The dominant life forms here are not the cultivated orchids of the gardens above but organisms that exist at the border of animate and inanimate. Sulfur-breathing bacteria paint the rocks in yellows and oranges. Thermophilic fungi grow in white cascades from joints and seams, their fruiting bodies pulsing faintly in the darkness. There are rumors of blind fish in the deepest pools, evolved from ancestors trapped when the boilers were first installed, now living on bacteria and whatever organic matter filters down from above.

But the Depths are not abandoned. They have their own inhabitants, as adapted to this environment as the Tenders are to the gardens above. The Stokers—though they no longer stoke any fires—move through the mechanical forest with practiced ease. Unlike the Tenders' slow grace, the Stokers move with quick, efficient gestures, checking gauges, adjusting valves, clearing mineral deposits from critical junctions. Their clothing is minimal—heat-resistant fibers that cover only what must be protected, leaving skin exposed to develop its own adaptations. They have learned to read the heat like a language, knowing from the color of a pipe's glow or the pitch of steam's whistle exactly what adjustments are needed.

The Stokers speak, unlike their counterparts above, but their language has evolved to be heard over the constant industrial roar. It consists largely of sharp consonants and hand signals, a pidgin of technical terms and older words whose meanings have shifted. They call the boilers by names—Old Grandmother, The Bitter One, Iron Belly—and speak of them as living things with personalities and preferences. They know which pipes will fail next, not through calculation but through an intuition born of generations spent in the heat.

Their dwelling spaces are carved from the rock itself, in places where the temperature drops just enough to make sleep possible. These hollows are sparse—a sleeping pad, perhaps a small shrine to forgotten engineers, tools worn smooth by generations of hands. But there is beauty here too: crystals cultivated in specific thermal conditions, creating gardens of mineral flowers. Arrangements of particularly colorful bacterial mats, tended and encouraged. The Stokers have their own aesthetics, finding beauty in efficiency, in the perfect gradient of heat along a well-maintained pipe, in the musical quality of steam moving through a perfectly tuned system.

The relationship between the Depths and the Gardens is symbiotic but fraught. The Stokers must maintain the heat that the Tenders depend on, but too much or too little disrupts the delicate balance above. Messages pass between the two realms through the pipes themselves—coded rhythms tapped on metal, variations in steam pressure that convey meaning. Sometimes a Tender will descend partway, meeting a Stoker in the intermediate zones where neither is quite comfortable, to discuss matters that require coordination. These meetings are formal, almost ritualistic, each side acknowledging the other's expertise while maintaining the boundaries that centuries have established.

At the very bottom of the Depths, where the geothermal taps disappear into the planet's crust, there is a chamber that neither Stokers nor Tenders enter. The heat there is beyond what even adapted humans can survive, but through thick glass ports, one can glimpse the source of all the garden's power: a wound in the world itself, glowing with the planet's inner fire. The machinery here is self-maintaining—or perhaps maintained by things that are no longer recognizably human, shapes that move in the heat shimmer like mirages of purpose. Even the Stokers speak of this place in whispers, calling it the Heart's Heart, and leave offerings at its threshold—tools worn beyond use, crystals of particular beauty, written records in scripts that have evolved beyond easy reading.

The Depths pulse with their own rhythms—slower than the Gardens, measured in the expansion and contraction of metal, in the patient accumulation of minerals, in the evolutionary time of bacteria adapting to new temperature niches. If the Gardens above are a dream of steam and flowers, the Depths are the engine of that dream, the necessary inferno that makes such controlled paradise possible. Together they form a single organism—one that breathes steam, dreams in orchids and rust, and feeds on the planet's own fever.
