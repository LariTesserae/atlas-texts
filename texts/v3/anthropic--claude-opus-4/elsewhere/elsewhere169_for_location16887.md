Beyond the salt flats, where the hexagonal tiles grow thinner and more translucent until they float like leaves of frozen breath above the actual ground, the land rises into the Gradient Mountains—a range that exists in perpetual transition between states of matter.

The mountains begin as suggestions: slight elevations in air pressure that solidify gradually into vapor, then mist, then something resembling stone but retaining the memory of having once been possible to breathe. Each peak exists at a different density. The nearest ones, visible from Absentia's upper towers on days when the atmospheric phosphorescence dims enough to reveal horizons, appear solid until approached, whereupon they dissolve into probability clouds that taste of copper and forgotten mathematics. The farther ranges achieve true solidity only at their bases, their peaks trailing off into theoretical abstractions that mountaineers claim can be climbed only by leaving one's body at base camp and ascending as pure intention.

No maps of the Gradient Mountains remain consistent between observations. Cartographers who attempt to chart them report that the act of measurement itself causes peaks to shift position, valleys to invert into ridges, and entire ranges to undergo phase transitions between existence and implication. The only reliable navigation method involves following the Doubt Streams—rivers of uncertainty that flow uphill through channels carved by water that hasn't decided yet whether it wants to be liquid or merely the concept of flowing.

The beings who inhabit these transitional peaks call themselves the Probability Shepherds, though they are neither shepherds nor entirely probable. They exist as living superpositions—each individual simultaneously present across multiple locations and states of being until observed directly, at which point they politely collapse into whichever configuration seems least inconvenient for the observer.

Their bodies are woven from the same gradient material as their mountains: bones of crystallized maybe, muscles of compressed likelihood, skin that shifts between opaque and transparent based on the angle of observation and the observer's expectations. They dress in garments made from weather that never quite arrived—robes of storms that turned left at the last moment, cloaks sewn from the shadows of clouds that forgot to form, boots cobbled from the silence between thunderclaps.

They speak in conditional tenses that don't exist in any formal grammar: "I might have been being about to have existed here tomorrow" is a common greeting, while farewells typically involve complex negotiations about which version of each party should remember the interaction and which should forget it to prevent paradoxes. Their language includes seventeen different words for "maybe," each describing a slightly different flavor of uncertainty, from "maybe-leaning-toward-probably-not" to "maybe-but-only-if-you're-not-watching."

The Probability Shepherds' primary occupation involves tending flocks of potential events that graze on the mountain slopes. These aren't animals in any conventional sense, but rather mobile clusters of causality that feed on the grass of things-that-almost-happened. A healthy flock might contain several dozen almosts: "almost rained yesterday," "almost evolved intelligence," "almost achieved critical mass," each one requiring careful management to prevent them from either actualizing (which would destroy their value) or dissipating entirely (which would be wasteful).

Their settlements perch on ledges of crystallized indecision, built from bricks of compressed ambiguity mortared with doubt. The architecture follows non-Euclidean principles of maybe—walls that might be there depending on your approach angle, roofs that exist only when it's not raining, doors that open onto different rooms based on how firmly you grasp the handle. The largest settlement, Perhaps-Upon-Possible, occupies seven different locations simultaneously, though residents insist there's only one town that happens to be very flexible about where it sits.

They trade with Absentia's Stillborn Architects in currencies of uncertainty. A particularly valuable "almost" (say, "the universe almost collapsed when that particle accelerator was activated") might be exchanged for architectural inconsistencies that the Shepherds use to fertilize their grazing lands. The exchange rate fluctuates based on the phase of non-existent moons and the market price of quantum foam.

Their technology operates on principles of productive uncertainty. Their vehicles are powered by engines that work by not being entirely sure whether they're moving or the landscape is flowing backward around them. Their computers perform calculations by setting up conditions where the correct answer becomes slightly more probable than infinite incorrect ones, then waiting for reality to settle into the path of least resistance. Their most advanced tool is the Uncertainty Harvester, a device that collects ambient doubt from the atmosphere and compresses it into solid blocks used for construction and cuisine.

The Shepherds worship through elaborate rituals of intentional indefiniteness. Their holiest ceremony, performed only during eclipses that might be happening, involves an entire community simultaneously believing and disbelieving in the same thing until reality itself grows confused and grants them a boon just to resolve the tension. Their priests, called Doubt-Weavers, train for decades in the art of holding multiple contradictory beliefs with equal sincerity, a practice that eventually allows them to exist in several places at once or, more accurately, to exist in one place that happens to be smeared across multiple locations.

Their creation myth speaks of a time before certainty, when all things existed in a state of pure potential, and claims that the universe's current unfortunate habit of actualizing into specific configurations is merely a phase it's going through. They believe that their role is to preserve pockets of the original uncertainty, keeping them safe until the universe remembers how to be properly indefinite again.

The relationship between the Gradient Mountains and Absentia is complex and contradictory, which both sides consider appropriate. The mountains may or may not have existed before the city's construction began—the geological evidence is inconclusive, with some rock layers dating to billions of years ago and others dating to next Thursday. Some scholars theorize that the mountains are Absentia's dreams, solidified just enough to stand on but not enough to be mapped. Others claim the city is the mountains' attempt to achieve true solidity, an experiment in certainty that went so wrong it looped back around to incompleteness.

During the seasonal maybe-storms, when probability rain falls upward and the peaks rearrange themselves according to aesthetic principles voted on by the wind, travelers between the two realms report strange phenomena. The salt flats developing temporary topography that mirrors the current mountain configurations. Building materials from Absentia's lower levels appearing embedded in cliff faces that insist they've always been there. Probability Shepherds finding rooms in their houses that match exactly the empty spaces in the city's architecture where things were meant to be but never were.

The Shepherds maintain that Absentia and the Gradient Mountains are actually the same place seen from different angles of uncertainty—that if you could achieve just the right degree of doubt about your own location, you could step from a half-built plaza directly onto a semi-solid peak without crossing the intervening space. They say this is how the original builders traveled, back when the distinction between "here" and "there" was less dogmatic about maintaining itself.

Their children play games involving quantum superposition, existing as both tagged and not-tagged until someone observes them directly. They learn mathematics by studying the flight patterns of birds that navigate by uncertainty principles, always arriving at their destination by carefully not being entirely sure where they're going. They reach adulthood through a ceremony that involves choosing which of their potential futures to collapse into, though the wise ones always keep a few alternatives running in parallel just in case.

And in the deepest valleys between the peaks, where uncertainty pools like fog and forms lakes of liquid possibility, they say you can sometimes glimpse reflections not of what is, but of what all of this was meant to become: A complete city on solid ground under a sun that knows exactly where it is and how fast it's going. Mountains that stay put when measured. Beings who exist in only one state at a time and are perfectly content with that limitation.

But these visions always ripple away when approached, leaving only the endless, beautiful confusion of a world that refuses to make up its mind about what it wants to be when it grows up—if, indeed, it ever intends to grow up at all, or if remaining eternally in superposition between all possible futures is, perhaps, precisely the point.
