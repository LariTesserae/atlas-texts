Beyond the terminal flatness of the salt pans, where even the Salters rarely venture, the land rises into the Gradient Reaches—a vast slope that climbs imperceptibly over hundreds of miles toward distant mountains. This is the kingdom of transition, where the salted earth gradually gives way to something else, though that transformation happens so slowly that travelers often walk for days before realizing the ground beneath their feet has changed.

The Reaches begin where the salt crust develops its first imperfections—tiny pockets where windblown seeds have found purchase in accumulated dust. These pioneer plants are different from the halophytes of the pans; they are opportunists that survive on the knife's edge between two worlds. Their roots probe deep, following hairline fractures in the salt layer until they reach the sweeter groundwater flowing down from the highlands. Each plant is an oasis in miniature, its presence gradually altering the chemistry of the soil around it, creating islands of possibility in the mineral waste.

As the elevation increases—though "increases" hardly describes the subtle grade that gains perhaps a foot per mile—these islands multiply and merge. The salt crust breaks up into a maze of hardpan and loose soil, creating a landscape of constant negotiation. Water moves differently here, neither the patient seepage of the pans nor the rushing streams of true highlands, but something intermediate: seasonal threads that appear and vanish according to rhythms no one has fully mapped.

The Drapers make their home in this ambiguous territory. They are weavers of a sort, though what they weave exists somewhere between textile and architecture, between art and survival. Taller than the Salters but more delicate in build, they move on legs that can telescope to twice their resting length, allowing them to step from stable point to stable point across the treacherous mixed terrain. Their bodies are covered in a fine pelt that shifts color with temperature and humidity, ranging from the white of salt crystals to the deep amber of highland soil.

Where the Salters work in mineral and crystal, the Drapers work in fiber and tension. They harvest the tough, silky threads produced by the transitional plants—fibers that form when the plants shift between their two survival modes, salt-tolerant and water-seeking. These threads, no thicker than spider silk but stronger than steel when properly treated, become the material of their calling.

The Drapers create vast networks across the Gradient Reaches, structures that from a distance might be mistaken for morning mist or heat haze. These webs serve multiple functions: they capture the minimal moisture from the air, channel rare rainfall into collection points, and provide highways for the Drapers themselves, who can race across their tensioned networks at speeds that would be impossible on the broken ground below. But perhaps most importantly, the webs are instruments—massive Aeolian harps that sing with every shift of wind, creating a constant, ever-changing music that rolls across the Reaches.

Each Draper family group maintains a territory of several square miles, their web networks overlapping at the edges in carefully negotiated zones. The patterns of their weaving encode complex information: the location of stable ground, the seasonal appearance of water, the movement patterns of the small mammals and birds that navigate between the salt pans and the highlands. Young Drapers spend years learning to read these patterns, running their sensitive finger-pads along the threads to feel the vibrations that tell of distant events.

The Drapers' relationship with their landscape is one of constant adaptation. As the Gradient Reaches shift—soil building up in some areas, salt encroaching in others—they must constantly adjust their networks. A web that has stood for decades might need to be completely reconfigured when a seasonal stream changes course or a section of hardpan finally crumbles. This work is never seen as loss but as opportunity; each reconstruction allows for new patterns, new harmonies in their wind-sung music.

Their shelters cluster where rock outcroppings break the surface, providing anchor points for their larger constructions. These homes are marvels of tension engineering—gossamer structures that look like they might blow away in a strong wind but which have weathered centuries of storms. Inside, the Drapers live surrounded by their craft: walls of woven records that document water patterns going back generations, sleeping nests suspended on networks that respond to the slightest movement, tools and treasures hanging from precisely calculated points where the structural forces balance perfectly.

The Drapers communicate through a language of vibration and tension, plucking their web networks to send messages across vast distances. Their speech can be felt as much as heard—a complex interplay of frequencies that resonate through both air and thread. When multiple Drapers "speak" at once, their overlapping vibrations create interference patterns that add layers of meaning, allowing for a kind of dimensional communication where multiple conversations can occupy the same space without confusion.

They trade with the Salters at the boundary zones where the salt pans meet the Gradient Reaches, exchanging their strong fibers for refined minerals and crystalline tools. These meetings happen at specific times determined by environmental conditions—when the humidity reaches certain levels that prevent the salt-dwelling Salters from venturing too far from their moisture-conserving burrows while allowing the Drapers' organic fibers to remain stable in the mineral-rich air. The two species regard each other with mutual respect tinged with incomprehension, each perfectly adapted to their own world, neither quite understanding how the other survives in theirs.

During the storm seasons, when walls of rain advance across the landscape, the Drapers perform their most spectacular work. They reconfigure their entire territories into vast water-collection systems, channeling the precious rainfall into cisterns carved into the rock. But they must be careful—too much water and the delicate balance of the Gradient Reaches tips toward erosion and chaos. They have learned to read the storms, to take what they need while allowing the rest to pass through to the salt pans below, where the Salters wait for their own precisely calibrated share.

The Drapers age differently than most creatures, their lifespans measured not in years but in tensions maintained and released. An elder Draper's body shows the history of their work—fingers permanently curved from decades of weaving, skin marked with the fine scars of broken threads, eyes that can perceive vibrations in the air that younger ones miss entirely. When they die, their bodies are wrapped in their finest work and suspended high in their web networks, where wind and weather gradually incorporate them into the landscape they spent their lives shaping.

At the farthest reaches of their territory, where the land finally begins its true ascent toward the mountains, the Drapers have discovered something that troubles and fascinates them in equal measure. The threads there vibrate with frequencies they've never felt before, patterns that suggest something vast moving in the highlands above. They've sent expeditions, following their web-lines higher and higher, but none have returned with clear answers—only fragments of description: stone that flows like water, water that builds like stone, and beings that exist at scales their thread-based mathematics cannot quite encompass.

The Gradient Reaches stretch on, a kingdom of eternal transition, where the Drapers continue their patient work of connection and tension, binding the lowlands to the highlands with their singing webs, creating music from the space between worlds. They understand, in their deep, vibrational way, that they are not merely inhabitants of this intermediate zone but its definers—that without their constant work of weaving and reweaving, the gradient itself might cease to exist, leaving only a stark boundary between salt and stone, between the patient Salters below and whatever mysteries await above.
