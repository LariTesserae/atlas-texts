**The Shepherds' Plateau**

Three days' climb from any spring, where the air grows thin enough to show the stars at midday, the world flattens into an impossible meadow. This highland stretches beyond sight in all directions, a sea of silver-green grass that moves with winds born at altitudes where only spirits dwelt—or so the herders say.

The grass here is unlike the coarse mountain vegetation below. Each blade grows exactly as tall as a child's hand is wide, creating a surface so uniform it seems manufactured. But look closer: every stem bears tiny hooks that catch and hold the morning frost, turning the entire plateau into a crystalline sheet at dawn. By noon, when the sun has burned away the ice, these same hooks trap seeds and pollen from the constant wind, creating a self-sustaining feast for the creatures that know how to harvest it.

The soil beneath is ancient in a way the basalt grotto could never be—compressed ash and organic matter from when these heights were lowlands, before the mountain's great uprising. Dig anywhere and find layers: black volcanic ash, white bone meal from herds beyond memory, red clay that speaks of a time when rain fell heavier here. The earth holds its own heat, warmed by decomposition so slow it might as well be geological, creating pockets of warmth that the knowing can find even in the depths of winter.

Wind is the dominant force. It never stops. The herders have ninety-seven names for its variations: the morning knife that cuts straight from the glaciers, the afternoon spiral that brings news from the valleys, the midnight hollow that carries sounds impossibly far. They build nothing permanent here, knowing the wind will claim it. Instead, they arrange stones in temporary patterns—shelters that can be assembled and abandoned as the grazing requires, each configuration known by heart and passed down through generations.

The animals that thrive here have evolved for this specific emptiness. The plateau horses stand barely higher than ponies, their coats triple-layered against the cold, their hooves wide as plates to distribute weight on the sometimes-soft earth. They crop the uniform grass with mechanical precision, their grazing patterns creating temporary mazes that fade as new growth replaces the old. Their manure, dried by wind and sun to chips lighter than wood, provides the only fuel for fires in this treeless expanse.

Smaller beasts make their lives between the grass roots: voles that never surface, creating underground cities connected by tunnels so extensive they change the drainage patterns of the whole plateau. Beetles that feed on the wind-caught seeds, their shells iridescent blue to reflect the intense ultraviolet light. Birds that nest directly on the ground, their eggs mottled to match the precise color of dried grass, their chicks born knowing to freeze at the shadow of a hawk.

But most remarkable are the sky fish—creatures that exist nowhere else, that even the herders struggle to explain. On certain days, when the wind reaches the exact pitch and the pressure drops just so, translucent forms appear in the air above the grass. They swim through the atmosphere as fish swim through water, their bodies catching and bending light into rainbow scales. Whether they are flesh or spirit, whether they exist in the spaces between air molecules or are simply a trick of altitude-addled eyes, no one can say. The herders watch them with the same calm attention they give to clouds, reading omens in their schooling patterns.

The plateau knows no true seasons, only variations in the intensity of cold. Summer brings a brief flowering—two weeks when the grass produces tiny white blooms that carpet the expanse like snow. The flowers open only in full sun, closing at the slightest cloud, creating a living map of the shadows passing overhead. Their nectar, concentrated by the harsh conditions, is so sweet it burns the tongue. The horses seek it obsessively, their muzzles stained white with pollen.

Water exists as rumor here. There are no streams, no springs, no standing pools. Rain falls sideways, driven by wind into a mist that barely wets the ground. Snow sublimates directly from solid to vapor in the dry air. The grass draws moisture from the air itself, its hooked blades condensing what little humidity exists. The herders know where to dig for fossil water—ancient aquifers trapped in gravel lenses, their liquid treasure guarded more carefully than gold.

Navigation requires reading the land's subtle variations. Though the plateau appears uniform, it tilts and folds in ways invisible to casual observation. The herders know these by the way wind patterns change, by minute differences in grass height, by the orientation of vole tunnels. They move across this vastness with the confidence of sailors, reading signs in the earth as others read stars.

Lightning strikes frequently here, drawn by the elevation and the dry air. Where it touches, the grass burns in perfect circles that take exactly seven years to fully heal. These rings become waypoints, their ages known and catalogued. In their centers, where the heat was most intense, the soil fuses to glass. The herders collect these fulgurites, believing them to hold the sky's power, using them in rituals that ensure the plateau's continued fertility.

Sound behaves strangely at this altitude. Voices carry for miles or die within feet, depending on wind and pressure. The herders have developed a language of whistles that cuts through the constant atmospheric noise, each tone calibrated to travel maximum distance. They can conduct entire conversations across valleys of grass, their messages riding the wind currents like the sky fish ride the air.

At night, the temperature drops fast enough to crack stones. The stars crowd close, undimmed by atmosphere, their light almost warm on exposed skin. The Milky Way arches overhead like a second, brighter plateau, and sometimes—when conditions align precisely—the aurora appears, even at this latitude. Sheets of green and gold dance above the grass, and the sky fish, if they are real, breach upward to swim through the colored light.

The herders' shelters cluster in the lee of subtle rises, their stone walls no higher than a sitting person. From a distance, they vanish entirely, just more variations in the plateau's texture. Inside, they are warm with body heat and horse-dung fires, the walls decorated with patterns made from different colored stones gathered across the expanse. Each family's pattern tells their history here—how many generations, which routes they favor, what disasters they've survived.

Their understanding of the grotto far below comes through dreams and the chemical messages in rare uptrafts. When the wind shifts just right, it carries hints of sulfur and wet heat, impossible scents at this elevation. The eldest herders speak of times when the mountain trembled and the grass withered in lines pointing toward the hidden waters. They know something powerful dwells there, something that ensures the mountain's life continues cycling from deep heat to high cold and back again.

The plateau holds its own mysteries. Stone circles older than memory dot the expanse, their purpose forgotten. Some say they mark where sky fish fell to earth and transformed to stone. Others claim they are maps of the tunnel cities below, or calendars tracking cycles longer than human comprehension. The herders maintain them without knowing why, replacing fallen stones, keeping the circles clear of grass, adding their own small marks to patterns begun before their people found this place.

Seasons of the grass itself follow rhythms independent of solar cycles. Sometimes it grows blue-tinted for a year, and the horses' milk tastes of copper. Sometimes it seeds twice in a season, and the voles swarm upward in daylight, drunk on abundance. The herders document these variations in song, their oral histories stretching back far enough to recognize patterns that repeat across centuries.

The wind occasionally brings stranger gifts than pollen or seeds. Perfect spheres of ice, too regular to be hail. Fragments of stone that match nothing on the plateau, some still warm as if fresh from a forge. Once, within living memory, a rain of tiny frogs, all albino, all dead before they touched ground. The herders collected them reverently, reading their patterns of fall like thrown bones.

Trade happens at the plateau's edges, where the grass meets rock and paths descend toward tree line. The herders bring horses, dried milk products hard as stone but nutritious as meat, and the mysterious white honey from the two-week flowering. They trade for metal tools, salt, and news from the lower world. But they always return to the heights, drawn by something more than habit or heritage.

Life here strips away everything nonessential. The wind scours personality down to its core components. The cold makes liars honest—you cannot pretend warmth or strength when survival depends on truth. The vastness either drives people mad or makes them profound; there is no middle ground on the plateau. Those who stay become like the grass itself: uniform in appearance but hooked with hidden complexities, resilient beyond reason, sustaining more life than seems possible.

The herders say the plateau dreams, and its dreams are the sky fish. They say the grass remembers every footstep, and in the patterns of its growth you can read the future. They say that somewhere in the expanse is a place where the ground is thin, where you can hear the mountain's heartbeat and feel the steam from hidden waters rising through miles of stone. But they will not say where, for some knowledge is too dangerous to share.

What is certain is this: the plateau exists in relationship with the grotto, though they never touch. The water that thunders below becomes the ice that whispers above. The heat that crowds life into one chamber dissipates into cold that spreads life thin across vastness. The salamanders' writhing masses balance against the solitary eagles that hunt the plateau edges. Each place holds what the other cannot, and together they form a system, a single breathing entity clothed in stone and grass and the endless cycling of water between states.

The herders, in their wind-carved wisdom, know this without knowing it. They feel the mountain's moods in their bones, sense when the deep heat flares or fades. Their horses know it too, and the voles, and perhaps even the grass—all parts of a conversation too large and slow for any single consciousness to comprehend fully. Only in the songs, passed down and gradually altered like the plateau itself, does the whole pattern begin to emerge: mountain as body, grotto as heart, plateau as breath, and something watching through it all with eyes made of mist and stone and time itself.
