Above the Frost Warrens, where the planet's young skin meets the void, spreads the Thermal Desert—a landscape of savage contradictions that makes the underground darkness seem almost merciful.

Here, the surface cracks like broken pottery under a sun that never sets. The planet's rotation period stretches so long that a single day spans what would be months elsewhere, and the sun hangs in the sky like a malevolent eye, pouring radiation onto stones that remember no shade. The air shimmers with heat mirages that twist the horizon into impossible geometries. What looks like water always recedes, always lies, always leads the desperate into deeper desert.

The ground itself defies reason. In the eternal noon, temperatures soar high enough to soften metal, yet mere meters below, permafrost grips the substrate in the same frozen fist that creates the Warrens. This thermal gradient generates its own weather—violent uprising thermals that spawn dust devils the size of cities, their howling columns visible for hundreds of kilometers as they march across the barren plains. When they collide, the sound is like continents grinding.

But it's the thermal bridges that truly define this place—massive natural structures of crystallized minerals that conduct heat downward while somehow insulating laterally. They rise from the desert floor like the ribs of buried titans, some stretching a kilometer high, their surfaces covered in dendritic patterns where super-heated minerals have cooled and reheated a thousand times. These bridges glow at night (when night finally, eventually comes), radiating their stored heat in waves that make the air around them dance.

Along these thermal bridges lives another kind of builder, another form of adapted humanity: the Sojourners.

Where the Delvers compress and huddle, the Sojourners stretch and spread. Their bodies have elongated to maximize surface area for cooling—tall, impossibly thin, with limbs like a spider's. Their skin has darkened to absolute black, not for absorbing heat but for radiating it. At the right angle, in the right light, you can see the shimmer of specialized scales that reflect infrared while absorbing almost no visible light—a biological mirror that keeps their core temperature survivable.

They dress in flowing layers of metallized fabric that creates personal shade structures. The cloth is woven from mineral fibers extracted from the thermal bridges themselves, materials that reflect heat while allowing air circulation. Their garments constantly move, even in still air, animated by the temperature differentials between their bodies and the environment. From a distance, a group of Sojourners looks like a collection of animate heat mirages.

The Sojourners are nomadic by necessity, following the terminus line—that crawling border between day and night that circles the planet at walking speed. They live in the eternal dawn or eternal dusk, never fully in either killing day or frozen night. Their entire civilization moves at precisely three kilometers per hour, the speed of planetary rotation at these latitudes.

Their cities are wheels. Massive structures built on thermal bridge foundations, designed to roll along paths worn smooth by ten thousand years of passage. The wheels themselves are miracles of engineering—hundreds of meters in diameter, their rims formed from fused desert glass, their spokes woven from thermal bridge crystal that flexes without breaking. Inside, hanging gardens of desert-adapted plants provide food and oxygen, while specialized chambers harvest water from the air during the brief temperature transitions.

The Sojourners have evolved eyes that see heat as clearly as light—necessary when the visible spectrum becomes painful. Their irises are golden, with pupils that contract to mere slits in daylight. They communicate as much through temperature modulation as through sound, able to flush or cool portions of their skin to create patterns visible to their heat-sensitive vision. A conversation between Sojourners looks like a dance of thermal shadows.

But here's the crucial connection: the Sojourners know about the Delvers, and the Delvers know about the Sojourners. They are two halves of a broken whole, sundered by the same geological catastrophe that created both the permafrost depths and the thermal desert.

The connection points are the Breathing Holes—vertical shafts where the pressure differential between the superheated surface air and the cold warren atmosphere creates constant wind. These natural convection engines drive air exchange between the two realms. Some are mere meters wide, others span hundreds of meters. The wind that howls up from below carries the smell of ice and copper. The wind that falls from above tastes of sand and ozone.

At these junctions, careful trade occurs. The Delvers need the mineral fibers that only form in extreme heat, materials crucial for their strongest ropes and heat-retention fabrics. The Sojourners need the ice-oils extracted from blind cave fish, the only substance that doesn't evaporate instantly in their rolling furnaces. Both sides need what the other has, but neither can survive in the other's world.

The trading posts are marvels of thermal engineering. Built in the transition zones of the Breathing Holes, they feature rotating chambers that allow traders to acclimatize gradually. A Delver ascending spends days in progressively warmer chambers, their blood slowly adjusting, special garments replacing their ice-wear. A Sojourner descending undergoes the reverse, their metabolism slowing, their elongated limbs wrapped in successive layers of insulation.

The posts themselves are neutral ground, maintained by the Mediators—descendants of those who were caught in the transition zones during the Great Sundering and adapted to both extremes. They can survive (barely) in either environment but thrive in neither. Their bodies are patchwork adaptations—dark skin on their upper halves, pale below, eyes that see in both spectrums but excel at neither. They speak both languages, understand both cultures, and trust neither completely.

Through these Breathing Holes, more than goods flow. Knowledge passes between the two peoples—the Delvers' discoveries about the planet's deep structure inform the Sojourners' predictions of thermal storms. The Sojourners' observations of the day/night terminus help the Delvers understand the cycles that drive geological instability below. Maps are compared, theories exchanged, warnings shared.

The youth of both cultures often gather at the Breathing Holes, peering across the void at their opposite-born cousins. Delver children dare each other to climb higher in the shafts, feeling the temperature rise degree by deadly degree. Sojourner youth compete to descend deepest, watching their breath become visible for the first time. These games occasionally turn tragic, young bodies found frozen or heat-struck at the limits of their adaptation.

But there are also the love stories—rare, desperate tales of Delvers and Sojourners who meet during trade negotiations and fall into impossible attraction. Some try to find ways to live in the transition zones, building precarious lives in the Breathing Holes themselves. Others attempt the ultimate sacrifice, one trying to adapt to the other's world. These attempts almost always fail, bodies not designed for such extreme changes. The few successes become legends, their hybrid children founding new Mediator bloodlines.

The Sojourners tell their own stories of the time before the Sundering, when the planet's rotation was faster, when water flowed on the surface, when humans lived under open sky without fear. They paint these histories on the inside of their wheel-cities with inks that only appear at certain temperatures, stories that reveal themselves as the city rolls between thermal zones. 

They've developed their own relationship with impermanence—where the Delvers rebuild constantly in the same places, the Sojourners never stop moving, never see the same horizon twice. Their art is in the journey itself, in the careful maintenance of rolling cities that have circled the planet countless times. They know every thermal bridge by its heat signature, every storm by its birth patterns, every oasis by the specific minerals that taint its evaporating waters.

The planet itself connects these two peoples in ways they're only beginning to understand. The same geological forces that drive the Frost Warren collapses also create the thermal bridges above. Deep convection currents link the frozen depths with the burning surface. Sometimes, in the deepest meditation chambers of the Delvers or the highest observation towers of the Sojourners, sensitive individuals claim they can feel the planet's heartbeat—a rhythm that synchronizes the ice-dancers below with the terminus-walkers above.

Recent discoveries have shaken both civilizations. Delver excavations at the bottom of the Great Boring have uncovered artifacts—clearly manufactured, clearly ancient, clearly not made by any human hands. The Sojourners, mapping new thermal bridges forming in real-time, have noticed patterns too regular to be natural. Both peoples are beginning to suspect that their planet's extreme conditions aren't accidental, that perhaps something engineered this world of extremes for purposes they can't fathom.

The Breathing Holes are multiplying. New ones open each year, and the old ones are widening. The Mediators report increasing instability in the transition zones. Some theorize that the planet is approaching another Sundering, that the two peoples must either reunite or face extinction separately. Others believe the Breathing Holes are the planet's way of forcing evolution, that humanity must adapt to use both extremes or perish.

In the deepest Frost Warren chambers, Delver philosophers debate whether to dig deeper or finally attempt the journey upward. In the rolling cities of the Sojourners, councils argue whether to continue their eternal circular journey or seek the poles where, theoretically, the terminator line moves slowly enough to build permanent settlements.

Both peoples feel the change coming. The ice-dancers notice new harmonics in the planet's tremors. The terminus-walkers see unprecedented patterns in the thermal storms. The Mediators, caught between, report dreams of flowing water—impossible water that's neither ice nor steam but something in between.

And in the Great Boring, the dig continues. The Delvers have broken through into a vast chamber filled with machinery that hums with its own heat, systems that seem to be waiting for something. The Sojourners have discovered that certain thermal bridges, when struck at the right frequency, resonate with responses from deep underground.

Two peoples, two extremes, one world balanced on the edge of transformation. Whether they know it or not, the Delvers dancing in their frozen darkness and the Sojourners rolling through their endless day are part of the same dance, the same journey. The planet spins on, carrying them all toward a revelation that waits where ice and fire meet, in the breathing spaces between extremes.

The future approaches at three kilometers per hour—the speed of dawn, the pace of survival, the rhythm of a world still becoming itself.
