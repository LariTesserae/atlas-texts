Above, where the cataract originates, there exists a vast inverted forest of stone.

The ceiling of this underwater world is not smooth rock but a sprawling network of hollow limestone tubes, each as wide as a subway tunnel, that honeycomb the seafloor above. These tubes formed over millennia as ancient gas vents bored through the sediment, leaving behind a labyrinth of interconnected passages. Now they hang like massive organ pipes, and it is through these that the water thunders down onto the volcanic pinnacle below.

The tubes are not empty conduits. Their inner walls are carpeted with a species of colonial organism that exists nowhere else - something between coral and fungus, with the texture of wet velvet. It feeds on the minerals dissolved in the passing water, growing in rippling sheets that pulse with bioluminescent patterns. The patterns are not random; they respond to pressure changes, to the presence of living things, to the chemical signatures carried in the current. When schools of fish pass through, waves of blue-green light chase them through the darkness. When the volcanic vent below releases a particularly violent burst, sympathetic flashes race up through the entire network like neural signals.

The keepers of this place are the Siphonophores - not the small, delicate creatures known to science, but massive colonial beings that have grown to fill entire tube sections. Each is a city unto itself, a collection of specialized organisms working in concert. Some segments are devoted to locomotion, rowing the colony through the tubes with synchronized pulses. Others are hunters, armed with stinging cells that can paralyze even large fish. Still others are gardeners, carefully tending patches of the velvet organism, harvesting it for food while ensuring it continues to thrive.

The largest and oldest of these beings is known as the Cathedral. It has grown so vast that it spans multiple tubes, its body segments specialized to an extreme degree. Its forward sections have evolved complex eyes that can see in near-total darkness, while its middle portions house a primitive distributed brain capable of memory and planning. Its trailing segments have become something unprecedented - they produce sound.

Not the simple clicks and pops of communication, but complex harmonics that resonate through the limestone tubes. The Cathedral has learned to play the tunnel system like an instrument, creating standing waves that can be felt kilometers away. Sometimes these songs are hunting calls, stunning prey with focused acoustic beams. Sometimes they are navigation, reading the echoes to map changes in the tunnel system. And sometimes, in the deep night when the current slows and the bioluminescent walls dim to their faintest glow, the songs seem to serve no purpose at all except to fill the darkness with something that might be called music.

The Cathedral remembers when the volcanic pinnacle below first burst through the seafloor. It felt the shock through the water, tasted the change in minerals, saw the first pioneering organisms begin to colonize the new rock. It has watched the ecosystem below develop, noted the patterns in the thermal pulses, observed which species thrive and which fade away. 

And it remembers other things too. The shape of a diver's boot, for instance, though it has no context for what such a thing might mean. It noticed when the boot fell, tumbling through one of its tubes in a cloud of silt and artificial fabric. The Cathedral followed its descent, watching through gaps where tubes opened to the void, until it came to rest at the base of the pinnacle. 

Sometimes, when the volcanic vent below pulses in a particular rhythm, the Cathedral responds with a song that incorporates that same pattern, creating a call and response between the stone forest above and the fire mountain below. In these moments, the entire vertical ecosystem becomes a single instrument, with the falling water as its breath and the limestone tubes as its throat, singing into the endless dark of the deep ocean.

The other Siphonophores have learned to navigate by these songs, to time their hunts to the rhythms, to raise their young in chambers where the acoustics are most favorable. They dance through the tubes in complex formations, their bioluminescent signals creating three-dimensional patterns that mirror the Cathedral's melodies in light.

And in the deepest, most remote sections of the tube network, where even the Cathedral's vast body cannot reach, there are chambers where the water has been still for so long that it has stratified into distinct layers of temperature and salinity. In these pockets of absolute calm, new forms of life are beginning to emerge - things that exist at the boundary between living and non-living, assembling themselves from the chemical gradients like thoughts crystallizing from a dream. They pulse with their own slow rhythms, years-long cycles that the Cathedral has only recently begun to detect and incorporate into its endless composition.

This is how the two realms relate: the fire below and the stone forest above, linked by falling water and rising steam, by pressure waves and chemical signals, by the patient observation of an ancient colony-being that has made itself the memory and the music of this vertical world.
