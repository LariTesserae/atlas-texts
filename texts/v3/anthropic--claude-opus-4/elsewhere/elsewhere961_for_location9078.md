The Observatory of Rigid Angles

The mountain's opposite face knows a different discipline. Here, at an elevation where the air thins to mathematical precision, the Observatory of Rigid Angles perches on a plateau carved flat as a geometric proof. No natural force shaped this place—it was reasoned into existence, each stone cut to specifications that tolerate no deviation, mortared with a substance that hardens more over time until the joints become stronger than the blocks they bind.

The cold here isn't the wet, living cold of lower altitudes but something absolute and clean. It preserves everything: the edges of the building's thirteen corners (never twelve, never fourteen), the crystalline clearness of thought, the perfect silence that allows for true calculation. Frost forms in fractal patterns on the windows, each branch following rules that the Observatory's occupants have catalogued in volumes that fill entire rooms.

The structure itself defies conventional architecture. It's built according to principles that only make sense when viewed from above—a series of interlocking shapes that create optical illusions at ground level but resolve into perfect clarity from the correct angles. Corridors meet at intersections that shouldn't be possible, stairs ascend in spirals that somehow return to their starting point one floor higher, and certain rooms can only be entered by understanding the mathematical relationship between their doors.

Inside, the walls are covered in proofs. Not written but carved directly into the stone, equations that took decades to complete running from floor to ceiling in unbroken chains of logic. The chisel marks are uniform, each character exactly 4.7 centimeters high, spaced with mechanical precision. Some proofs have been abandoned mid-theorem, their authors having discovered errors fifty years into the carving. These incomplete works are left as warnings, their dead-end logic serving as cautionary tales.

The instruments here measure things that have no common names: the angular velocity of thought, the coefficient of certainty, the precise weight of an unspoken theorem. Great brass devices fill entire chambers, their gears and armatures moving in cycles that complete once every hundred years, tracking patterns in phenomena that only become visible over geological time. The sound they make is less than a whisper—the barely audible tick of metal teeth engaging, the soft rush of sand through hourglasses the size of pillars.

Light enters through windows ground from single crystals, each one a lens that focuses starlight into readable data. The glass itself took centuries to cool, formed in molds that account for the mountain's gradual settling, the infinitesimal drift of continents. During certain alignments, the focused beams converge in the central chamber, creating interference patterns that reveal... something. The Observatory's occupants have been trying to decode these patterns for longer than any of them can remember.

The library exists in seventeen dimensions, though only three are accessible to ordinary perception. Books here aren't read but derived—each volume contains not words but initial conditions, from which the reader must calculate the intended meaning. A simple text might require months of computation; the great works have been under continuous analysis for centuries, their meanings emerging like slowly developing photographs, each generation of scholars adding another layer of understanding.

Water exists here only as ice, forming in perfect crystals that never melt. The observers collect these structures, cataloguing the infinite variations of frozen geometry. Some crystals are kept in special chambers where the temperature never varies by more than a thousandth of a degree, preserved as examples of particular mathematical principles made manifest. They've discovered that no two are truly identical—even those that appear the same contain subtle variations at the molecular level, each one a unique solution to the problem of how water organizes itself in the absence of heat.

The Counter of Infinite Decimals makes her home among these absolutes, though "home" suggests a comfort she's long since calculated away.

Her eyes have evolved past color, seeing instead in spectrums of probability and precision. The irises spiral inward like nautilus shells, each ring demarcating another order of magnitude in her perception. She blinks in patterns—three rapid, two slow—a tic that serves as a continuous calculation, keeping track of some vast equation that runs beneath her conscious thought.

Her fingers have grown an extra joint each, the better to manipulate the Observatory's instruments. She moves them constantly, working through problems in the air, her gestures leaving faint traces of disturbed dust motes that hang in the still air like three-dimensional equations. Her nails grow in spirals, following the golden ratio, and she clips them precisely every twenty-one days, saving the parings in labeled jars—data points in an experiment whose purpose she's forgotten but whose methodology she maintains with religious precision.

She speaks in numbers more often than words. Conversations begin with coordinate positions, establishing exact spatial relationships before communication can proceed. Her voice has developed additional harmonics, allowing her to express mathematical relationships as chords—major thirds for multiplication, diminished fifths for certain irrational numbers, dense cluster chords for expressions that would take hours to write out in conventional notation.

Her hair grows in perfectly straight lines, each strand following a vector determined at the follicle. She's mapped her scalp, knows the equation governing each hair's growth, can predict where every strand will be at any future moment. Sometimes she cuts them to specific lengths, creating interference patterns when she moves her head, visual representations of wave functions that only she can fully interpret.

Her skin has become translucent in places, not from heat but from cold—a crystalline clarity that shows the mathematical precision of her internal structures. Her heartbeat follows a complex rhythm, prime numbers nested within prime numbers, a percussion that serves as her internal clock. She's calibrated it against the Observatory's master timepiece, adjusting her metabolism until the two run in perfect synchronization.

She remembers the Seething Grotto, though "remember" isn't the right word. She calculates it—derives its existence from the necessary principles that govern the mountain. If there is absolute cold and order here, then somewhere else there must be heat and chaos. The equation balances. It must balance.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of her calculations, she finds traces of her sister (not a biological relationship but a mathematical one—two solutions to the same underlying problem). Heat signatures in data that should be cold. Biological rhythms in what should be pure mechanical motion. The bacterial growth patterns from the grotto follow mathematical rules too, chaotic systems with strange attractors that create order from disorder at scales the naked eye can't perceive.

She's tried to map the path between the two places. Not the physical path—that's trivial, a matter of following the mountain's contours—but the mathematical transformation that would take one state to the other. How many iterations to go from ice to steam? What's the function that transforms rigid crystal lattices into flowing bacterial mats? She works on this problem in the moments between other calculations, a background process that's been running for decades.

The Observatory's other occupants (she knows there are exactly seven at any given time, though she's not sure if they're the same seven or if they replace themselves according to some pattern she hasn't yet derived) move around her like planets in perfectly elliptical orbits. They exchange proofs instead of pleasantries, leave theorems for each other in the communal spaces. Sometimes they work together on problems too large for a single mind, their thoughts synchronizing until they become one distributed calculating engine.

In the chamber of converging light, she's building something. A model, perhaps, or a proof made manifest. It involves lenses ground to specifications that push the limits of material science, arrangements of crystals that create interference patterns in seventeen dimensions. She's not sure what it will do when complete—the mathematics suggest several possibilities, all equally probable until the moment of activation.

Late at night (though the distinction is arbitrary here, where artificial light maintains constant illumination), she climbs to the highest tower. Here, where the air is thin enough that consciousness takes effort, she looks out at the stars and calculates their positions backward and forward through time. Somewhere in those calculations, she knows, is the key—the transform function that connects all states to all other states, the equation that binds ice to steam, order to chaos, her to her sister in the grotto below.

The Counter of Infinite Decimals continues her work, each calculation bringing her closer to or further from understanding—distance itself being another variable to solve for in the endless equation of existence. In the perfect silence of the Observatory, her fingers tap out rhythms of increasing complexity, and the brass instruments tick toward revelations that may take centuries more to arrive.

The mountain holds both places in its mass—the fever dream of the grotto and the crystalline clarity of the observatory, each necessary to the other, each calculating or seething toward some unimaginable convergence. Water runs between them in all its states: steam rising, ice forming, liquid flowing through hidden channels that connect what seems separate. And in their respective chambers, two sisters who've never met and always known each other continue their parallel work, transforming and counting, counting and transforming, until the distinction between the two becomes another kind of mathematics altogether.
