**The Underglass**

Three days' descent from the plateau's eastern edge, where the salt rivers finally spend themselves in a series of cascading waterfalls, the world changes. Here, the mineral-rich waters have percolated through countless layers of porous stone, depositing their crystalline cargo in ever-finer gradations until they emerge, nearly pure, into a vast aquifer sealed beneath a ceiling of volcanic glass.

The Underglass exists in eternal twilight. Above, the obsidian sky curves in a perfect dome, formed when ancient lava flows met the limestone caverns and cooled instantly against the subterranean waters. The glass is not uniform—in places it runs thick and black as night, while elsewhere it thins to translucent amber, allowing columns of filtered sunlight to pierce the underground sea. These light shafts move across the water's surface as the sun tracks overhead, creating a shifting geography of illumination.

The water here is impossibly clear. Free of the minerals that clouded it above, it reveals depths that seem to go down forever—though in truth the deepest point is merely forty fathoms. The clarity creates vertigo in those unaccustomed to it; every detail of the bottom is visible, every movement of the creatures below rendered in perfect definition. Schools of pale fish hang in the water column like clouds of silver smoke, their bodies so transparent that their shadows on the sand below are darker than they are.

Islands of black sand rise from the depths where ancient volcanic intrusions pushed through the limestone. These are the only solid ground in the Underglass, connected by chains of partially submerged rocks that create highways for the creatures that move between water and air. The sand is warm to the touch, heated by deep thermal vents that also feed the underwater gardens—forests of white calcite formations that grow like trees, their branches extending in perfect fractals toward the light shafts above.

The calcite trees are not truly vegetation but rather the accumulated work of billions of tiny organisms that extract calcium from the water and deposit it in intricate patterns. They grow so slowly that their shapes record centuries of current patterns, each branch angling toward optimal water flow. In death, they do not decay but simply become substrate for the next generation, creating reefs of breathtaking complexity that rise nearly to the surface in some places.

The dominant inhabitants of the Underglass are the membrane swimmers—creatures that have evolved beyond the distinction between air and water. Their bodies consist of layered films of organic tissue, each no thicker than a soap bubble, stacked in complex arrangements that can trap or release air at will. When they wish to rise, they inflate certain chambers and drift upward like living balloons. To dive, they compress, forcing the air into specialized bladders that maintain their buoyancy at chosen depths.

These swimmers move in three dimensions with perfect grace, using undulating motions that send waves along their membrane surfaces. They feed on the microscopic organisms that bloom where the light shafts strike the water, positioning themselves in the columns of illumination and filtering nutrients through their permeable surfaces. When startled, they can release their trapped air in a burst, shooting away like deflating balloons—though they must surface slowly afterward to avoid damaging their delicate structures.

The most remarkable adaptation belongs to the species known as glass dancers. These creatures have incorporated volcanic glass into their very biology. Their young begin life as simple membrane swimmers, but as they mature, they migrate to the areas where the obsidian ceiling nearly touches the water. There, they secrete special acids that allow them to slowly dissolve the volcanic glass, incorporating the silicates into specialized cells.

Adult glass dancers are living prisms. Their bodies, shot through with microscopic glass fibers, bend and refract light in controlled patterns. They communicate through light displays, sending coded messages in flashes of color that play across their surfaces. During mating seasons, the glass dancers gather beneath the thinnest parts of the obsidian dome, where the light is strongest, and perform elaborate courtship displays that paint rainbow patterns across the water for hundreds of meters in all directions.

On the black sand islands live creatures that have adapted to a dual existence. The thermal crabs navigate by heat, their oversized claws containing organs sensitive to temperature differentials of less than a degree. They locate the warm vents by touch, following gradients in the sand that are invisible to other senses. Their shells are pocked with small holes that whistle in the underground breezes, creating a constant, eerie music that echoes off the glass ceiling.

The amphibious rays are perhaps the strangest residents of the islands. Evolved from aquatic ancestors, they have developed the ability to store water in specialized sacs along their bodies, allowing them to survive out of water for days at a time. They hunt in the shallows but rest on the warm sand, their bodies flattened against the black beaches. Their skin has a peculiar property—it can shift from nearly transparent when in water to opaque black when on land, providing camouflage in both environments.

The ecology of the Underglass is driven by the light. Where the shafts strike consistently, gardens of calcium formations bloom with microscopic life. These become feeding grounds for the filter feeders, which in turn attract the few predators that inhabit these waters. The memorial eels, named for their habit of collecting objects, patrol the edges of the light zones. Their elongated bodies can compress to fit through the narrowest gaps in the calcite forests, where they ambush unwary swimmers.

The eels are compulsive collectors. Their nests, built in caves along the island shores, are decorated with whatever they can find or steal—bits of unusual minerals, shells of mollusks, even the shed membrane layers of the swimmers. Some eels have been observed maintaining their collections for decades, carefully arranging and rearranging their treasures. The oldest nests become archaeological sites, containing objects that tell the history of the Underglass in layers of accumulated artifacts.

In the deepest parts, where no light shafts reach, the water takes on properties that border on the supernatural. Here, the pressure and the isolation have created pockets of water with different densities, invisible lakes beneath the lake where the rules of buoyancy change. Creatures that enter these zones must adapt quickly or sink—or sometimes rise uncontrollably. The deep swimmers have evolved specialized organs that can detect these density changes, allowing them to navigate the invisible landscape of the depths.

The connection between the salt city above and the Underglass below is more than mere drainage. Minerals dissolved from the plateau filter through miles of stone, emerging in the springs that feed the underground sea. But more than minerals make the journey. Occasionally, during the great storms, creatures from above are swept into the sinkholes and caverns that lead down. Most do not survive the transition, but some adapt.

There are colonies here descended from salt city browsers, their mineral shells replaced over generations with calcite, their habits transformed from grazing crystalline formations to filtering the rich waters. Some of the membrane swimmers show traces of salt dweller ancestry in their ability to process minerals, extracting nutrients from seemingly barren water that would starve other species.

The thermal vents that warm the black sand islands are the same geological system that drives the mineral precipitation above. Deep beneath both realms, magma chambers cool slowly, their heat driving convection currents that cycle water between the surface and the depths. This connection is honored by the inhabitants of both realms in their own ways—the salt dwellers in their patient accumulation of minerals, the underglass swimmers in their dances beneath the light.

During certain times, when the conditions are perfect—when storms rage on the plateau above and the water levels rise in the Underglass—you can hear the echo of the salt city's flowing waters in the underground sea. The sound travels through miles of stone, emerging as a low thrumming that makes the glass ceiling vibrate. The creatures of both realms pause in their activities then, acknowledging this reminder of their deep connection, this song of water and stone that links their seemingly separate worlds into one continuous system of life adapting to the extraordinary.
