Above, the world is wire and want.

The canopy here is not leaves but cables—thousands upon thousands of them, strung between towers that rise from the jungle floor like geometric trees. Some cables are ancient, their rubber insulation cracked to reveal copper cores green with verdigris. Others are fresh installations, fiber optic lines that pulse with data-light, their protective sheaths still manufacturer-black. They cross and recross at different heights, creating a three-dimensional web that turns the sky into strata—the telegraph level, the telephone level, the broadband level, each era's infrastructure preserved like geological layers.

The towers themselves are amalgamations of technology's evolution. Their bases might be stone and mortar from the colonial period, their midsections latticed steel from the industrial age, their crowns bristling with cellular equipment and satellite dishes. Vines have learned to follow the guy-wires, and where a cable has snapped and dangles, orchids colonize the exposed copper, their roots turning metallic blue from the absorption.

Between the towers, the cables create their own weather. Morning mist condenses on the wires and drips in curtains. Where electrical current still flows, the air ionizes during storms, creating St. Elmo's fire that dances from line to line. Birds have evolved grounding behaviors—they land with one foot first, testing for live wires, and some species have developed rubber-like pads on their feet. Their nests incorporate wire insulation, woven with traditional materials into structures that are both conductive and insulated.

The sound here is constant communication—not words but signals. The wind through the cables creates an aeolian harp of data transmission. Transformers hum their sixty-cycle mantras. Switching stations click and buzz with automatic relays. And underneath it all, the remnant signals: ghost phone calls bouncing between abandoned exchanges, television broadcasts from stations long silent still propagating through coaxial dreams, the chittering of modems trying to handshake with partners that no longer exist.

This is the domain of the Signalman.

He lives in a crow's nest of his own construction, built where twelve major transmission lines converge in a knot of possibilities. His home is part repair station, part receiving dish—walls made from salvaged satellite panels, a floor of tightly woven coaxial cable, a roof that can iris open to reveal the phosphorescent sky of data streams. He sleeps in a hammock of fiber optic fibers that glow softly with intercepted transmissions, dreams colored by the information passing through.

Where the Tender below has become liquid, the Signalman has become electric. His hair stands perpetually on end, not from static but from alignment with the electromagnetic fields. His fingers have developed extra sensitivity—he can feel the data flowing through a cable by touch, distinguish between video and audio, between encrypted and clear. His eyes catch different spectrums, seeing the infrared of overheated connections, the ultraviolet of arcing shorts.

The Signalman wears a vest made from keyboard keys, each one a different era—mechanical typewriter keys mixed with early computer keyboards, membrane switches, touchscreen fragments. They click and clatter when he moves, a percussive announcement of his presence. His tools hang from a belt of woven ethernet cables: wire strippers that have evolved into surgical instruments, a multimeter old enough to require vacuum tubes, crimping tools modified with additions he can't quite remember making.

He maintains the connections—not out of nostalgia but necessity. The jungle has uses for bandwidth. The trees have learned to communicate through the abandoned networks, their root systems tapping into buried cables, using electrical signals to share nutrients and warnings across distances. Fungal networks have incorporated copper pathways into their hyphal networks. What began as human communication infrastructure has become part of the forest's nervous system.

The Signalman's work is translation. He splices connections between organic and electronic networks, teaching them compatible protocols. A cellular tower becomes a beacon for migrating birds who navigate by electromagnetic fields. A telegraph line carries chemical signals between trees miles apart. Fiber optic cables channel bioluminescent signals from fungal networks, creating light-speed communication between forest patches.

His workshop perches at a major junction, walls lined with equipment spanning two centuries of communication technology. Vacuum tubes glow next to LED arrays. Morse keys share space with touchscreens that have learned to respond to the oils on leaves as well as fingertips. He has charts showing which frequencies the birds avoid, which harmonics the insects use for mating calls, how to modulate signals so they don't interfere with the echolocation of bats that have learned to navigate by the sixty-cycle hum.

The Signalman remembers more of his past than the Tender does—fragments of a life maintaining communication networks for villages, stringing lines through impossible terrain, always believing that connection was sacred. When the networks began to fail, he didn't retreat but climbed higher, following the signals to their sources, learning the languages of electricity and light.

He speaks these languages now. To the remnant AIs still running in server farms powered by hydroelectric dams, he sends status updates in machine code. To the satellites still circling in decay orbits, he broadcasts position data for flocks and weather patterns. To the submarine cables that surface here like ancient sea serpents, he feeds the ocean's temperature data, translated from the reports of deep-diving birds.

Between the Signalman above and the Tender below runs a single connection—a bundle of cables that descends through a natural shaft, waterproofed with resin and wrapped in symbiotic vines. Through this umbilical, they exchange more than words. Water data flows upward: mineral content, flow rates, the dreams of aquatic things translated into electrical impulses. Atmospheric data flows down: barometric pressure, electromagnetic weather, the gossip of satellites converted to chemical signals the salamanders can taste.

They meet rarely in person—perhaps once a season, at the midpoint where wet meets wired. These meetings are wordless communions, each checking the other's transformation. The Tender's skin grows more translucent; the Signalman's more conductive. They exchange gifts: a perfectly preserved vacuum tube for a vial of water that glows with bacterial light, a crystallized circuit board for a mushroom that processes electrical signals.

The space between them is not empty but inhabited. In the vertical jungle between canopy and cavity live the mediators—creatures and constructs that translate between the two domains. Repair drones that have achieved accidental sentience patrol the cable runs, their solar panels evolved into wings. Messenger birds carry data packets in their genetically modified crops, downloading at terminal points the Signalman maintains. The middle heights host relay stations that are part grown, part built—living trees whose heartwood has been replaced with functioning electronics, their leaves solar collectors, their sap a coolant for processing cores.

This vertical ecosystem processes information into meaning, meaning into action. A pH change in the Tender's pools triggers the Signalman to reroute signals away from sensitive nesting areas. Electromagnetic storms detected by the Signalman's instruments warn the Tender to seal certain chambers against electrical intrusion. They are two organs of the same distributed organism, heartbeat and breath synchronized across a hundred feet of elevation and evolution.

The boundary between their domains blurs at the edges. Cables snake down following root paths, carrying signals deep into the earth. Water vapor rises through ventilation shafts, condensing on transmission equipment and creating new channels for data flow. Certain adventurous salamanders have learned to navigate the lower cable runs, their bodies conducting signals between broken connections. Moths that pupated in transformer housings emerge with wings that shimmer with electromagnetic patterns.

At night (and night comes truly here, not the eternal twilight of below), the Signalman's domain becomes spectacular. Data streams visualize as light flowing through fiber optic veins. Corona discharge halos the tower tops. Bioluminescent climbers follow the cables, pulsing in rhythm with the data flow. The Signalman reads these patterns like weather—knowing from a glance which servers are overheating, which connections carry urgent ecological data, where the network needs intervention.

His greatest work spans years—a project to reconnect the global forest, to turn the abandoned telecommunications infrastructure into a planetary nervous system for the non-human world. Each connection he restores or repurposes extends the network's reach. Forests on different continents begin to share chemical signals through submarine cables. Mountain peaks exchange weather data through satellite relays. The ocean speaks to the atmosphere through cellular towers rusting at the margins of the sea.

Meanwhile, below, the Tender's work proceeds at mineral pace. She is teaching water to remember—to carry information in its molecular structure, to preserve and transmit the chemical history of its journey. Each pool becomes a library, each pipe a messenger. The water that rises as vapor to condense on the Signalman's equipment carries with it the archived experiences of stone and salamander, rust and root.

Together, they are creating something unprecedented—a hybrid intelligence that processes information through both electrical and aqueous media, that thinks in timescales from microseconds to centuries. Neither fully conscious nor unconscious, but conscious differently, like the forest itself is conscious—aware without ego, responsive without intention, wise without knowledge.

The work continues. Will continue. Must continue. Because this is what the world is becoming—not natural or artificial but naturofactual, each threshold a membrane through which information and meaning flow in forms we're only beginning to recognize. The Signalman tends his frequencies. The Tender tends her flows. And between them, in the vertical wilderness of wire and water, something new learns to speak.

When storms come, the two domains dance together. Lightning seeks the shortest path between sky and groundwater, and for brilliant moments, the entire system illuminates—every connection visible, every flow revealed. In these moments, the Signalman and the Tender know themselves to be neurons in a vast network, their individual consciousness just brief intensifications in a pattern too large to perceive from within.

But most days are quieter. Data flows. Water drips. Connections are made, maintained, evolved. And in the space between the wire and the wet, between the signal and the spring, the world repairs itself into something stranger and more necessary than its broken parts could ever have imagined becoming.
