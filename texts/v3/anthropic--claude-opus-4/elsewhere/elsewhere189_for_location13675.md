High above the oasis, where the rim of the depression meets the endless expanse of the plain, there exists another world entirely—the Saltwind Reaches, a crystalline desert that stretches to the horizon in every direction. This is not merely arid land, but a living system of its own, one that breathes with the oasis below in an ancient rhythm of exchange.

The ground here is composed of countless hexagonal plates of salt and mineral deposits, fitted together like a vast puzzle. Each plate is unique—some are pure white, others tinted rose or amber or palest blue, depending on the minerals trapped within. When the wind moves across them, they sing—a high, ethereal chiming that rises and falls with the gusts. At dawn and dusk, when the temperature shifts most dramatically, the plates expand and contract, creating a symphony of crystalline notes that can be heard for miles.

This is the domain of the Saltwind Shepherds.

They are beings of impossible delicacy, standing perhaps seven feet tall but weighing no more than a child. Their bodies are translucent, like living glass, filled with a pale, shifting fluid that moves with their thoughts. Their limbs are elongated and jointed in too many places, allowing them to fold themselves against the ground when the storms come. Their faces, if they can be called faces, are smooth expanses broken only by three dark spots—two that might be eyes, and one that opens occasionally to taste the mineral-rich air.

The Shepherds move across the Saltwind Reaches in slow procession, following paths invisible to any eye but theirs. They tend to the salt plates as others might tend gardens, using long, hollow fingers to inject precise amounts of moisture drawn up from deep aquifers. Where they touch, the plates bloom with crystalline formations—delicate structures that trap morning dew and channel it down through hairline cracks to the water veins that feed the oasis below.

They speak in frequencies below human hearing, their communications traveling through the salt plates themselves. A conversation might take days, rippling outward in concentric circles of vibration. They discuss the deep things—the movement of underground rivers, the health of mineral deposits, the proper timing of the rare rains that sweep across their domain every few years.

The Shepherds know of the oasis, of course. They can feel it through their feet—that pocket of riotous life that exists because of their patient work. Sometimes, on still nights when the salt plates are quiet, they gather at the rim and look down at the green darkness below. They see the water that gleams like a jewel, the tangled canopy that shields it, the countless lives that flourish there. They do not envy it. Their existence is one of austere beauty, of purpose stripped to its essence.

Once each year, during the season when certain stars align, the Shepherds perform the Drawing. They position themselves at precise points across the Reaches, their bodies forming a living constellation. Then they begin to move in unison, their steps creating resonances in the salt plates. The chiming builds and builds until it becomes almost unbearable, a wall of sound that seems to shake the very air.

And then the underground rivers respond.

Water rises through the salt plates in thousands of tiny geysers, each no more than a hand's breadth high. The Shepherds dance among them, their movements directing the flow, ensuring that exactly the right amount of water reaches the oasis below while the rest spreads across the Reaches to create temporary pools of brine. In these pools, microscopic life blooms explosively—bacteria and algae that exist nowhere else, that live their entire lifecycles in the few days before the water evaporates.

These microorganisms, when they die, leave behind organic compounds that sink into the salt. Over time, these compounds are compressed and transformed, eventually joining the underground streams as nutrients that will feed the lush growth of the oasis. It is a cycle measured in centuries, tended by beings who think in geological time.

The Shepherds have no names for themselves, no individual identities as humans might understand them. They are more like notes in an endless composition, each playing their part in maintaining the balance between the sterility above and the fecundity below. When one grows old—a process that takes millennia—they simply walk out into the deepest part of the desert and allow themselves to crystallize, becoming a new salt plate that will sing with the wind for eons to come.

There are stories, whispered among the creatures of the oasis, of times when someone has climbed to the rim and glimpsed the Shepherds. The stories never agree on what was seen—some speak of angels made of glass, others of moving mirages that hurt to look upon. But they all agree on one thing: the beings above are as necessary to the oasis as the water itself, guardians of a balance too vast and subtle for any single life to comprehend.

And so the two worlds exist in symbiosis—the explosion of life below, the austere preservation above, each impossible without the other, each perfect in its own necessity.
