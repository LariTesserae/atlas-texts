The Canopy Markets

Three hundred feet above, where the city-tree's crown spreads wide enough to catch clouds, another world turns on different wheels. The bark up here has been worn smooth by generations of wind and rain and the constant traffic of those who make their living in the light. Platforms of woven branches extend from natural crotches in the limbs, some no bigger than a dining table, others vast enough to hold entire neighborhoods of rickety structures that sway with each breeze.

This is where the moth-shepherds work. Their charges are the size of open hands, wings like stained glass windows depicting no saints, only patterns that shift depending on the angle of the sun. The shepherds guide their flocks with long poles tipped with flowers—specific blooms for specific commands, a language of scent that drifts across the platforms in invisible sentences. The moths feed on the tree's highest blossoms, flowers that open only above a certain altitude, their nectar so concentrated it crystallizes on the insects' tongues.

The shepherds themselves are strange to see: burnt brown by the constant sun, their hair bleached white regardless of age, their eyes permanently squinted against the light. They wear cloaks of woven moth-silk that shimmer with the same shifting patterns as their flocks' wings. The silk is worth its weight in silver down below, but up here it's common as cotton, used for everything from rope to roofing.

Between the platforms run bridges of living wood, branches trained and grafted over decades to grow in useful directions. The bridge-keepers maintain these connections, pruning here, encouraging there, whispering to the growth-tips in languages they claim the tree teaches them. Some bridges are wide enough for carts pulled by trained beetles the size of housecats. Others are mere handhold branches, requiring travelers to swing hand over hand across dizzy gaps where the ground is a green memory far below.

The market platforms cluster where multiple bridges meet. Here the moth-shepherds bring their harvest: crystallized nectar broken into amber chunks, cocoons still warm with transformation, scales brushed from wings that catch the light like powdered rainbow. They trade with the seed-collectors who rappel down the tree's flowering faces, with the web-weavers who harvest silk from spiders large as dinner plates, with the rain-readers who predict weather patterns from the way moisture gathers on bark.

Everything here is bright and loud and open. The wind never stops. Conversations are shouted across gaps or signaled with flags made from moth wings. Children born to the platforms learn to walk on swaying surfaces, to judge distance by the paralax of moving branches, to sleep through storms that would terrify ground-dwellers. They play games involving living birds trained to carry messages between platforms, racing to see whose starling can navigate the maze of branches fastest.

The water comes from rain and mist, collected in hollow gourds that grow from the tree itself. These gourd-gardens are tended by specialists who know which varieties will swell to catch the most water, which will filter out the pollen that clogs everything in blooming season, which will ferment their contents into the mild alcohols that make the cold nights bearable. The gourds grow in cascades down the platform edges, their weight a constant calculation in the bridge-keepers' work.

At night, the moths return to roosts in the highest branches, and their wings catch the moonlight like a second sky. The shepherds light fires in braziers of woven root—the smoke keeps away the bats that would decimate their flocks. By firelight, they repair their silk garments, adding new patches that tell stories in the overlapping patterns. They sing work songs that echo across the canopy, harmonizing with the wind in the leaves, the creak of the bridges, the distant hoot of the great owls that hunt in the spaces between trees.

The oldest shepherds tell stories of the time before, when the city-tree was younger and stood alone. They say the first moths came from underground, emerging from deep places carrying their light-patterns like maps. They say the tree itself called them upward, needing their pollination for flowers that bloomed above the reach of bees. They say other things too, stranger things, about what the moths dream in their cocoons, about why certain patterns appear again and again in their wings, about what messages they carry between the high places and the low.

There's a sickness that strikes sometimes, a malady of too much light. The affected see patterns everywhere—in the grain of the wood, in the dance of shadows, in the spaces between leaves. They speak of a great design that connects every living thing in the tree, from the fungi in the roots to the eagles in the crown. They draw maps on bark, on silk, on their own skin, trying to capture something that can't be held. Eventually they leap from the platforms, not to die but to fly, convinced they've decoded the secret of transformation. Some say a few actually make it, becoming something between moth and human, living in the spaces where branch meets sky. Most fall.

The children find chrysalises sometimes in the deep crotches of branches, chrysalises too large for any moth. The shepherds destroy these when they find them, or try to, but the tree protects them, growing bark over them like scabs. Inside, something changes shape in the dark, waiting for its season. The shepherds don't talk about what emerges. They only double their watches during certain moon phases, and keep their longest poles ready, the ones with flowers that smell like fear.

Commerce continues despite these mysteries. The ground-dwellers need moth-silk for their marriages, crystallized nectar for their medicines, wing-scales for their paints. The platform-dwellers need tools the ground provides, metals and ceramics and worked stone. So the trade flows up and down the city-tree's height, following routes older than memory, while between the two worlds—in the middle heights where the tree's girth is widest—live those who climb between, the traders and translators, the message-carriers and medium-dwellers who understand both the language of roots and the vocabulary of wind.

The moth-shepherds' children grow up knowing the weight of air, the texture of light, the way distance collapses when you live at the edge of falling. They learn young that beauty and danger are wing-mates, that transformation is the only constant, that some patterns, once seen, can never be forgotten. They inherit their parents' burnt skin and squinted eyes, their silk-sense and their vertigo-immunity, their way of seeing the world not as solid ground and empty sky but as a vertical maze of possibilities, each branch a choice, each platform a world, each moth a message written in living light, delivered on wings that remember, in their deepest patterns, the darkness from which they emerged.

And far below, so far that even the thought of it is like peering down a well, the termites continue their ancient work, following patterns of their own, building toward something that perhaps—if the moth-shepherds' wildest stories are true—has to do with the chrysalises in the trees, with the transformations the light enables, with the great slow purpose that turns through both darkness and brightness, root and crown, making the city-tree not just a place but a process, not just a home but a becoming.
