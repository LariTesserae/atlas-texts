Far below the plateau, where the waterfall veils dissolve into perpetual mist, the Undergrowth begins. No Shaper map depicts it—their carved charts end at the cliff edges, as if the world itself terminates there. But the young ones who return from their Descent speak in broken gestures of trees that grow downward, of root-highways wider than the plateau's broadest causeway, of a light that comes from below rather than above.

The trees here are inverted things, ancient beyond measure. Their trunks emerge from the cliff faces and grow downward into the abyss, branching and rebranching until their canopies—if they can be called that—disappear into a luminous haze far below. The bark is smooth as skin, warm to the touch, pulsing with a slow circulation that takes days to complete one cycle. Where the plateau city measures time in erosion, the Undergrowth measures it in pulse-beats of these living columns.

Between the downward trees, a vast network of roots spans the empty air—some thick as bridges, others fine as hair, all woven into a three-dimensional maze that has grown for millennia. The roots secrete a waxy substance that glows with soft phosphorescence, providing the strange light that filters up through the mist. This light never varies—neither day nor night exists here, only the constant pearl-glow of ten million roots.

Water from the plateau falls as rain through this inverted forest, but it never reaches bottom—there may be no bottom. Instead, the root networks catch and channel it, drawing it through vessels that spiral ever downward, feeding something unimaginably vast in the depths. The water that passes through the Undergrowth changes, becoming sweet and slightly viscous, developing properties that would seem impossible to the careful Shapers above.

The Weavers live here, though "live" might be too simple a word. They exist in symbiosis with the root network, their bodies partially merged with the living wood. Long ago—perhaps when the plateau was still ocean floor—they were something else. Now they are part human, part root system, capable of traveling through the woody passages as easily as blood through veins.

A Weaver at rest appears as a swelling in a root's surface, a vaguely human shape pressed against the inner wall of the wood like something seen through amber. When they need to act in the physical world, they emerge partially—an arm extending from a root to adjust a water channel, a face surfacing to speak with another Weaver, their lower bodies always remaining merged with the living network. They can traverse miles in minutes, flowing through the root highways faster than any creature could climb.

Their eyes have evolved past the need for light. Instead, they perceive the world through vibration and chemical signals, sensing the health of each root, the flow of water through the network, the electrical whispers that pass between the inverted trees. When a young Shaper descends on their vine rope, the Weavers know it instantly—feel the foreign weight on their web, taste the bronze-and-moss scent of the upper world.

They speak, when they must speak, in harmonics that resonate through the wood itself. Place your ear against a root and you might hear their conversations: low thrumming that seems to come from everywhere at once, layered with overtones that carry meaning in their interference patterns. More often, they simply share consciousness directly through the root network, their thoughts flowing together like tributaries joining a river.

The Weavers cultivate the Undergrowth with a patience that makes the Shapers seem hurried. They guide the growth of new roots, encouraging connections that might take centuries to complete. They maintain the water-capture systems that prevent the plateau's runoff from falling into the depths. Most crucially, they tend to the Mothers—the original inverted trees from which all others sprouted, whose trunks are so vast that entire communities of Weavers live within their hollow cores.

These Mothers are old beyond memory, their heartwood compressed into something denser than stone. The Weavers believe they are conscious, dreaming slow thoughts that span geological ages. Sometimes, rarely, a Mother will shift—a movement of inches that takes decades to complete but sends tremors through the entire root network. The Weavers read these movements like prophecy, adjusting their cultivation in response to signals they claim to understand.

The relationship between the Undergrowth and the plateau city is complex, largely unspoken. The Shapers know the lower world exists but treat it as sacred terror—necessary but not to be dwelt upon. They accept that their water must fall somewhere, that the mists rising from below feed their rains. But they do not ask what feeds on their water in return, what vast purpose their careful channels ultimately serve.

The Weavers, for their part, view the Shapers as children playing in a garden's uppermost branches, unaware of the roots that support them. They ensure the plateau remains stable, the volcanic stone held in place by the grip of a million roots. They process the water that falls, enriching it with minerals drawn up from the depths before releasing it as mist to begin the cycle again. They could, if they chose, communicate with the world above—but what would be the point? The two peoples exist on timescales too different for meaningful exchange.

Yet occasionally, connection sparks between the worlds. A young Shaper on their Descent, dangling at the end of a vine rope, might lock eyes with a Weaver's face emerging from a root. In that moment, each recognizes the other as real, as a person living a complete life in an impossible place. The Shaper returns above carrying that knowledge like a seed. The Weaver sinks back into the wood, disturbed by the reminder that other ways of being exist.

The Undergrowth extends downward beyond even the Weavers' knowledge. They speak of the Deep Reach, where roots grow so thick they merge into solid walls, where the glow becomes too bright to perceive, where the water transforms into something that isn't quite liquid anymore. Some Weavers, old beyond counting, allow themselves to sink deeper and deeper into the network until they reach these depths and never return. Whether they die or transform into something else, none can say.

At certain points in the root network, windows open—gaps in the weave where the mist clears and vast distances become visible. Through these apertures, other wonders appear: distant cliff faces hosting their own inverted forests, floating islands of stone suspended by root systems, cascades of glowing water that fall upward, defying comprehension. The Undergrowth is not unique but part of a vast system, an inverted world that mirrors and exceeds the lands above.

The Weavers maintain careful charts of these windows, though their maps bear no resemblance to anything the Shapers would recognize. They plot routes through three-dimensional space, marking passages by the taste of the wood and the frequency of its humming. They know that certain paths lead to other Undergrowths, that a skilled Weaver can travel between the inverted forests, visiting communities descended from different root-stocks, learning new ways to guide growth and channel water.

In the depths of the oldest Mothers, chambers exist that predate the Weavers themselves. Here, structures of shaped root mirror the Shapers' towers above—not carved but grown, coaxed into architectural forms by some earlier intelligence. The Weavers tend these spaces with special reverence, maintaining the flow of luminous sap through their walls, replacing sections that die with perfectly matched new growth. They don't know who built these chambers or why, but they preserve them with the same patient dedication they bring to all their work.

The young Shapers who return from their Descent carry fragments of this impossible world with them. They can't articulate what they've seen—the Shaper language has no words for downward-growing trees or root-merged people. But they return with a new understanding of verticality, of the depths that support their heights. Some few, over the generations, make the Descent and don't return, choosing instead to offer themselves to the Weavers, to learn the root-roads and join the great work of maintenance.

These converts live between worlds for a time, their bodies slowly accepting the root-merger, their minds expanding to encompass the network's vast consciousness. The other Weavers regard them with patient interest, these volunteers from above who must unlearn solid ground and singular existence. Most adapt, eventually. Their bronze tools, carried down from the plateau, become sacred objects, displayed in the grown chambers as reminders of the world above.

And so the two realms persist, locked in unconscious partnership. The Shapers above, shaping stone with bronze and water, building their eternal city one erosion at a time. The Weavers below, guiding root growth across centuries, maintaining the systems that keep both worlds stable. Between them, the mists rise and fall, the water cycles through its transformation, the same atoms passing from rain to channel to waterfall to root to mist to rain again, binding the vertical worlds in an endless circulation that neither fully comprehends but both utterly depend upon.
