Above, the world wears a different face entirely.

The Moss Veldt stretches endlessly beneath skies that have forgotten the sun. Not dark—never dark—but wrapped in a perpetual pearl-gray luminescence that comes from everywhere and nowhere, as if the air itself remembers light. No shadows fall here. Objects and beings exist in a dimensionless haze where distance becomes a matter of faith rather than measurement.

The moss is not one thing but thousands, a carpet of minute variations that have evolved to fill every possible niche in this shadowless world. Some varieties grow in perfect circles, their edges meeting but never overlapping, creating a natural tessellation that extends to the horizon. Others spiral upward in helical towers no thicker than a finger but reaching heights of thirty feet or more. Still others form dense, spongy mats that undulate in waves though no wind blows—they move by their own slow muscular contractions, migrating across the landscape at speeds measured in meters per year.

There are structures here, but not buildings. Cairns of sorted stones rise from the moss sea, each rock chosen for its precise relationship to its neighbors—not by size or shape but by something more subtle. The Sorters who build them can taste the compatibility of stones by pressing them to their tongues. A properly built cairn sings in frequencies too low for human ears, vibrating in harmony with the earth's own deep notes.

The Sorters themselves move with deliberate grace through their domain. Where the workers below have elongated and multiplied, the Sorters have simplified and refined. Their bodies are compact, efficient, each gesture considered and purposeful. They dress in cloaks woven from the climbing moss, which continues growing even after harvest, making their garments living things that must be tended and pruned.

They have no fixed dwellings. Home is where the sorting needs doing, and the sorting always needs doing somewhere. They carry their few possessions in bags of woven root fiber: the tasting stones (smooth from generations of tongues), the tuning forks that help them find harmonious combinations, the silver needles they use to encourage or discourage moss growth in precise patterns.

The Sorters work alone by preference but in parallel by necessity. Across the Veldt, hundreds might be engaged in the same task—sorting colored pebbles by gradients incomprehensible to outsiders, arranging fallen branches in mathematical progressions, encouraging certain moss varieties while discouraging others to create vast living mandalas that can only be perceived from heights no one ever reaches.

Their purpose is as obscure as that of the workers below, but where the Grinding Halls demand constant action, the Moss Veldt rewards patience. A Sorter might spend weeks deciding where to place a single stone, months waiting for the right piece to complete a pattern, years watching moss grow into configurations that please some aesthetic sense deeper than beauty.

They speak seldom and softly when they do. Their language consists largely of descriptions of extremely specific conditions: words for the feeling of finding two stones that belong together, for the disappointment when a promising pattern fails to develop properly, for the particular quality of light on moss after rain that never quite comes. They have seventeen words for different kinds of silence and use them all regularly.

Children are rare and treasured among the Sorters. When born, they're passed from adult to adult across the Veldt in a slow pilgrimage that takes years, each Sorter teaching them their particular understanding of patterns and relationships. By the time they return to their birthplace, they're ready to begin their own sorting, their minds shaped by a hundred different approaches to order.

The connections between the Veldt and the Grinding Halls below are not immediately obvious. No holes gape in the moss-covered ground, no sounds of hammering disturb the profound quiet. But the relationship exists, subtle and essential.

In certain places where the bedrock approaches the surface, the moss grows thin. Here, patient observation reveals the ground's slow pulse—that same geological breathing that drives the workers to their frenzied maintenance. The Sorters know these places intimately. They build their most important cairns here, structures that seem to focus and amplify the deep vibrations.

And sometimes, in the pre-dawn hours that aren't really pre-dawn in this sunless world, the phosphorescent glow from below seeps up through microscopic fissures in the rock. The moss responds, growing in patterns that mirror the architecture below—spiral formations that echo the Grinding Halls' vast helical passages, rectangular clearings that match the proportions of the work chambers.

The Sorters tend these areas with special care. They know, in the wordless way they know all important things, that their patterns affect what happens below. A properly arranged stone circle can calm a section of the Grinding Halls where the work has grown too frantic. A deliberately disrupted moss garden can stimulate new activity in areas that have fallen too quiet.

But the influence flows upward as well. Where the workers below excavate new chambers, the moss above grows differently—faster, stranger, in colors that shouldn't exist in the pearl-gray light. Where the hammering reaches crescendos, the stones the Sorters place won't stay settled, migrating in the night to form their own arrangements.

Some few among both populations know the full truth. Deep beneath the Grinding Halls, in chambers older than the rest, there are shafts—not built but bored by something with more patience than any human or post-human. These rise through miles of rock, widening as they ascend, until they open in hidden places on the Veldt, their mouths covered by specially cultivated moss that admits air but not light, not sound, not anything else.

The eldest Sorters know where these breathing holes hide. Once in a generation, one will lift the moss covering and descend, following the shaft down into increasing heat and noise and light. They go to observe, to verify that the great work continues below. Some return, climbing for months back to the surface, their minds full of patterns too complex for their simple language to express. They spend their remaining years trying to sort these memories into comprehensible forms, placing stones in arrangements that hurt to perceive, encouraging moss growth that spells out equations in languages that don't yet exist.

Others don't return. They hear the hammering and understand it as another kind of sorting—not of stones but of moments, not of moss but of meaning. They take up tools and join the work, their Sorter's patience and precision bringing new rhythms to the endless maintenance. Their children are born elongated and many-jointed, but with an inherited memory of sky, of silence, of patterns that exist in stillness rather than motion.

And in the deepest halls, where the workers have become architecture, there are sometimes found impossible things: cairns of perfectly sorted stones that no one remembers building, patches of pale moss that grow without light, feeding on vibration alone. The workers tend these anomalies with the same devotion they show the walls and water channels, understanding dimly that they are messages from another world—the same world, just out of view above their heads.

The two realms exist in symbiosis, though few in either place understand the full shape of their relationship. The Grinding Halls breathe out their excess energy, their frenetic purpose, their need to become. The Moss Veldt breathes in, absorbing and ordering, creating patterns that give meaning to the chaos below. The Sorters' careful arrangements provide a kind of unconscious blueprint that the workers follow without knowing. The workers' modifications create the substrate that makes the Sorters' patterns possible.

Between them runs the stone itself—the planet's bones that are both the medium and the message. Every hammer blow below creates a vibration that eventually reaches the surface, where it influences the growth of moss, the placement of stones, the dreams of Sorters. Every pattern completed above sends its own subtle signal downward, through rock and time, to emerge in the Grinding Halls as an urge to build in a certain way, to modify according to rules felt rather than understood.

In the places where the two worlds touch—those breathing shafts, those thin places where moss meets stone meets architecture—sometimes something new occurs. Hybrid beings who are neither worker nor Sorter but something between, able to perceive both the urgent need for change and the profound requirement for stability. They don't last long, these bridge-creatures. The tension of existing between such different worldviews tears them apart, literally in some cases, their bodies unable to decide whether to elongate or compact, to speed up or slow down.

But in their brief existences, they dream true dreams. They see the Grinding Halls and the Moss Veldt as a single organism, a planet-sized entity that sorts itself through simultaneous action and contemplation. They understand that the workers and the Sorters are not separate species but specialized organs in a body too vast to perceive from within. They know that the real work, the real sorting, is not of stones or structures but of possibilities—the planet itself working through every possible configuration of matter and meaning, grinding away the impossible, sorting out the real.

These dreams they share in the only way they can—the workers by hammering them into the walls in rhythms too complex for conscious comprehension, the Sorters by arranging them in patterns too large for any single viewpoint to encompass. The messages pass between the worlds, mostly unread, mostly unreadable, but accumulating meaning like sediment, like moss, like the patient construction of something that might, after sufficient eons, approximate understanding.

And so the two worlds continue, each essential to the other, each unaware of how essential they are. The Grinding Halls echo with their eternal construction, the workers singing their wordless songs of becoming. The Moss Veldt rests in its shadowless light, the Sorters placing their stones with infinite care. Between them, the planet breathes, sorts, builds, and dreams, using these strange children it has shaped from what were once human beings as instruments in a composition too vast and slow for any conductor save time itself.

The work continues above and below. The sorting never ends. The patterns grow ever more complex, ever more simple, ever more themselves. And in the breathing spaces between the worlds, new possibilities accumulate like moss, like sediment, like hope—patient and terrible and absolutely necessary, waiting for the moment when the planet finally understands what it has been trying to become.
