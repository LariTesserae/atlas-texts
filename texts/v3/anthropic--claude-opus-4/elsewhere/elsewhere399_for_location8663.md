Far below, where the mountain's roots spread into ancient valleys, the city of Threyma sprawls in calculated denial of the peaks above. Here, at six hundred meters elevation, the air is thick enough to carry proper sounds—conversation, laughter, the small violences of daily commerce. The city planners laid out the streets in perfect grids, as if geometric certainty could ward off the tremors that periodically ripple down from the construction site above.

But the mountain's influence seeps downward like water finding every crack. The resonances arrive muted but persistent—not the bone-shaking roar the Readers endure, but subtle vibrations that citizens might mistake for passing trucks or distant construction. These frequencies accumulate in the city's steel and concrete, building up charge like static electricity. Glass windows develop spontaneous fractures in perfect spiral patterns. Clocks run differently in basement levels than in penthouses, though the differences are so slight that only watchmakers notice.

Threyma prides itself on modernity. Its buildings rise in glass and steel, their foundations dug deep into bedrock that the architects believed stable. The business district gleams with corporate efficiency—banks that finance mining operations they've never seen, engineering firms that design equipment for conditions they can't imagine, logistics companies that coordinate supplies for workers they pretend are merely distant employees. The city's prosperity flows from the mountain site above, but its citizens cultivate a careful ignorance about the source of their wealth.

In the old quarter, where buildings predate the construction site by centuries, the effects show more clearly. These structures were built when the mountain was merely a mountain, their foundations set for stillness. Now they lean at subtle angles, their walls recording years of micro-adjustments. Residents prop furniture with folded paper, rehang pictures daily, accept that doors will stick or swing freely according to rhythms they don't try to understand. Cats avoid certain corners where frequencies gather. Children play games jumping between tiles, unconsciously avoiding the nodes where vibrations concentrate.

The city's true nature reveals itself in what it manufactures. Threyma's factories produce specialized equipment—not just for the construction site, though that contracts keep many employed, but for other places where the ordinary meets the impossible. They make instruments that measure things with no names: temporal viscosity gauges, resonance dampeners, probability anchors. The workers in these factories develop their own minor adaptations. Their hands grow steady in ways that echo the Readers' precision, though they've never climbed higher than the factory floor. They speak of "finding the frequency" of their work, a phrase that means more than metaphor.

The Memorial Hospital of Threyma has a wing they don't advertise. Here they treat the Readers who come down broken—not just physically, but temporally confused. These patients arrive speaking of yesterday as if it were tomorrow, their internal clocks scattered by too much exposure to the mountain's deep time. The doctors have learned to treat this condition with controlled vibrations, using tuning forks and precise harmonics to realign human time. The success rate improves each year, though the treated Readers never quite return to who they were. They retain a distant look, occasionally pausing mid-conversation as if listening to frequencies only they remember.

In Threyma's universities, certain professors pursue research that would seem like madness elsewhere. They study chronogeology, industrial harmonics, the mathematics of becoming. Their papers, published in journals with limited circulation, use equations that bend back on themselves, describing processes that happen outside linear time. Students in these programs develop reputations for eccentricity. They conduct experiments at odd hours, claiming certain proofs only work when the mountain's vibrations align just so. The janitors find their chalkboards covered in diagrams that hurt to look at directly—mappings of space that include temporal dimensions, flowcharts that loop back before their beginnings.

The city has its own underground, literally and figuratively. In the storm drains and service tunnels, communities gather who've fallen through Threyma's prosperous surface. Some are former Readers whose bodies could no longer handle the frequencies. Others are failed students from the university, their minds touched by concepts they couldn't quite grasp. They've built a shadow economy down here, trading in salvaged resonance dampeners and bootleg temporal anchors. They speak of the mountain differently than those above—not as a source of profit or knowledge, but as a living thing whose growth they can feel in their bones.

Strange markets emerge at the edges of legality. Vendors sell water they claim comes from the construction site—condensation collected from the turbines, bottled and labeled with dates that indicate not when it was collected but when the stone it touched will emerge. Believers drink it seeking visions of the future. Skeptics point out that any effects could be simple mineral contamination. Both might be right. Other stalls offer fragments of metal that have absorbed particular frequencies, worn as talismans by factory workers who swear it helps them find their machine's rhythm.

The city's children grow up strange. They play with toys that wouldn't make sense elsewhere—blocks that fit together only when stacked in certain temporal sequences, dolls whose faces change expression based on vibrations, games where winning requires predicting what move your opponent will think of making. Schools report increased rates of what they carefully don't call precognition—students submitting homework for lessons not yet taught, answering questions before they're asked. The teachers adapt, learning to plan lessons that accommodate these temporal hiccups.

Threyma's artists create works that capture something essential about their city's nature. Sculptors work in materials that age at different rates, creating pieces that evolve over time. Musicians compose symphonies that include calculated silences where the mountain's frequencies can bleed through. Painters develop techniques using pigments that respond to vibration, their canvases shifting subtly with each tremor from above. The city's galleries have learned to hang these pieces on specialized mountings that either dampen or amplify the effects, depending on the artist's intent.

Religious movements spring up and fade, each trying to make sense of what rises above them. Some worship the mountain as a god being born. Others see the construction site as humanity's attempt to midwife a new age. The more pragmatic faiths simply incorporate the vibrations into their practices—meditation sessions timed to harmonic frequencies, prayers structured in patterns that match the turbines' rhythms. The cathedral of St. Brevard, built two centuries ago, has developed acoustic properties its architects never intended. During certain services, when the congregation sings at just the right pitch, sympathetic vibrations from the mountain create overtones that seem to come from the stone itself.

The city maintains a complex relationship with time. Business hours follow the clock, but everyone knows certain tasks work better at particular moments—contracts signed when the vibrations align just so seem to prosper, while those concluded during what they call "dead frequencies" often fall through. The stock exchange has incorporated this into its trading algorithms, though they disguise it as conventional technical analysis. Wedding planners check frequency forecasts along with weather reports. Even the cemetery groundskeepers have noticed patterns—graves dug during certain resonances settle differently, as if the earth itself has preferences about who it holds.

This is Threyma: a city built on pretending the impossible is merely unlikely, where citizens live one reality while their bones know another. It feeds on the construction site above while maintaining careful distance from its strangeness. Its prosperity and its subtle madness both flow from the same source—the emerging mountain and humanity's violent attempt to read its becoming. The city processes the raw materials of that interaction, transforming temporal ore into the mundane stuff of modern life: paychecks, research grants, manufactured goods whose purposes blur between the practical and the ineffable.

Between the construction site and Threyma lies the Gradient—sixty kilometers of elevation and impossibility where the mountain's influence shifts from overwhelming to subtle, where human adaptation takes a thousand forms.

The cable car system connects them, though "system" suggests more organization than exists. The original line, built by the mining company, runs straight as an arrow from Threyma's industrial district to the lower staging areas of the site. But over the years, other lines have been added—some official, some decidedly not. They branch and merge like blood vessels, creating a vertical network of steel cables and swaying cars that carry more than just passengers and supplies.

The operators of these cable cars develop their own culture. They ride the transitions between the city's forced normalcy and the site's naked impossibility, their bodies becoming bridges between states. They know every shift in frequency along the journey, can predict by the cable's vibration what weather awaits at either end. Some operators claim they can feel individual Readers through the steel lines—each person's resonance traveling down the cables like telegraph signals.

Along these routes, settlements cluster wherever the geography allows. At twelve hundred meters sits Haven, a town that exists primarily to process those who can no longer handle the heights. Former Readers arrive here to decompress, their bones still ringing with frequencies that take months to fade. Haven's doctors specialize in what they call "temporal rehabilitation"—teaching people how to live in normal time again. The town's architecture reflects this purpose: buildings designed with careful proportions that dampen errant vibrations, streets laid out in patterns that encourage linear thinking.

Higher, at eighteen hundred meters, Portal perches on a natural shelf of stable rock. This is where the serious equipment gets transferred from wheeled vehicles to cable systems, where engineers make final adjustments to machinery before it enters the emergence zone. Portal's residents are technicians and mechanics who understand both the machines and what the machines must endure. Their workshops ring with testing sounds—devices run through harmonic sequences that would seem like music if they weren't so precisely industrial. Children born in Portal often show perfect pitch, their ears calibrated by the constant tuning of ascending equipment.

Between these larger settlements, tiny communities cling to the mountainside. Some are nothing more than a few families who've found spots where the frequencies cancel out, creating pockets of relative calm. They build houses anchored deep into rock, with flexible joints that allow structures to sway without breaking. These people live by vertical farming, growing crops in terraced gardens that spiral up the slopes. They've learned which plants thrive on vibration—tomatoes that fruit more heavily when shaken at certain frequencies, herbs whose essential oils intensify under harmonic stress.

The Gradient has its own weather, distinct from both the valleys and the peaks. The temperature inversions create layers of cloud that hang at specific altitudes, marking the boundaries between frequency zones. Sometimes these clouds carry charge—not electrical, but something else. Travelers through these layers report temporal displacement: arriving hours before or after they should, though their watches show the journey took exactly the expected time. The cable car operators factor this into their schedules, adding or subtracting hours based on readings from instruments that measure something other than distance.

Wildlife in the Gradient has adapted remarkably. The goats here have evolved inner ears capable of processing the strange frequencies, allowing them to navigate slopes that vibrate constantly. Their hooves have developed a peculiar grip that seems to anticipate the rock's movement. Birds migrate not just by season but by resonance, following frequency patterns up and down the mountain. Some species have developed calls that incorporate the industrial harmonics, creating songs that would have been impossible before the construction began.

The flora shows similar adaptations. Trees grow in spirals, their trunks twisted by years of rotational forces. Their wood, when harvested, maintains these patterns—furniture made from Gradient timber tends to creak in harmony with distant vibrations, as if remembering its origins. Certain flowers bloom not by daylight but by frequency, opening their petals when specific harmonics reach them. Botanists from Threyma's university maintain research stations throughout the Gradient, documenting these changes, though their papers struggle to find language for what they observe.

Water behaves strangely here. Streams don't always flow downward—in places where the vibrations create standing waves, water can move uphill, gathering in pools that seem to defy gravity. The locals know these spots and use them, placing water wheels that turn against logic, powered by forces the physics textbooks in Threyma carefully ignore. Ice forms in patterns that reflect the resonant frequencies, creating crystalline structures that sing when the wind passes through them.

The transformation between city and site happens gradually, then suddenly. A traveler ascending from Threyma first notices subtle changes—a slight ringing in the ears, a sense that their watch runs differently. By fifteen hundred meters, these effects intensify. Thought patterns begin to loop, conversations develop echoes that aren't quite acoustic. At two thousand meters, the industrial sounds from above become audible—not just heard but felt, a pressure that builds in the chest and bones.

The cable cars themselves change as they rise. The lower sections use conventional materials and designs, but higher up, the cars are built differently. Their frames incorporate resonance dampeners, their windows are made of glass that filters certain frequencies. The highest cars, those that actually reach the construction site, are more like survival capsules—sealed environments where passengers can maintain their own temporal coherence while traveling through zones where causality runs rough.

Some people live their entire lives in the Gradient, never ascending to the site or descending to the city. They know both extremes only through stories and the goods that pass through their communities. These Gradient dwellers develop their own philosophies, their own explanations for the world they inhabit. They see themselves as translators, taking the raw impossibility from above and processing it into forms the city can accept. Their children play games that would seem normal in neither place—racing through frequency zones, timing their movements to the mountain's pulse, learning to read the vibrations like weather.

The economic flow through the Gradient is complex. Raw materials ascend—specialized alloys, resonance dampeners, the strange lubricants the Tenders require. Processed materials descend—gauge readings translated into data, mineral samples that carry temporal signatures, sometimes workers whose bodies have absorbed too much strangeness. But more than goods flow along these routes. Information travels in forms that resist easy classification: songs that carry engineering specifications, gestures that encode frequency readings, dreams that predict equipment failures.

At night, the Gradient becomes spectacular. The industrial lights from the construction site reflect off the layered clouds, creating false auroras. The cable car lines glow with accumulated charge, blue-white traceries against the dark stone. Sometimes, rarely, the chronovores descend from the peaks, flowing like mercury down the cliff faces. The Gradient dwellers know to stay indoors when this happens—not from fear, but from respect. The chronovores are cleaning up temporal inconsistencies, smoothing the places where different rates of time have created dangerous paradoxes.

The mail service through the Gradient deserves special mention. Letters posted in Threyma might arrive at the construction site before they were sent, or years later, depending on the route taken. The postal workers have developed a complex system of temporal ZIP codes, sorting mail not just by destination but by when it needs to arrive. Some letters are held in special stations within the Gradient, waiting for the right frequency alignment to continue their journey. Love letters between Readers and their families below often arrive in batches, temporal bottlenecks releasing years of correspondence in a single delivery.

Emergency services in the Gradient operate under special protocols. Ambulances carry equipment to treat not just physical injuries but temporal displacement. Paramedics learn to diagnose chronological concussion—what happens when someone moves too quickly between zones of different time flow. Fire services deal with blazes that burn backward, consuming their fuel before igniting. Police handle crimes that technical happen tomorrow or yesterday, developing investigative techniques that account for non-linear causality.

This is the Gradient: neither fully city nor site, but something uniquely itself. It processes the impossible into the merely improbable, creating space for humans to adapt gradually to the strangeness above or decompress from it when descending. Its communities exist in constant flux, their daily lives shaped by forces that the greater world prefers not to acknowledge. They've built a vertical civilization in the spaces between the human and the geological, their lives tuned to frequencies that sing of deep time and violent emergence.

The Readers, when they descend through the Gradient for their rotations off-site, often pause at certain elevations. They sit in cafes that cater to their kind, drinking tea that helps quiet the resonances in their bones. They watch the locals with something like envy—people who live with the mountain's influence but aren't consumed by it. Some Readers, when their time at the site ends, choose to settle in the Gradient rather than return to Threyma. They find work maintaining the cable systems or teaching in the schools that prepare young people for life at various frequencies.

The connection between the construction site and Threyma isn't just geographic or economic—it's existential. The city exists because of the site, feeds on it, fears it, denies it. The site exists because the city provides resources, workers, the infrastructure of denial that makes the impossible possible. Between them, the Gradient translates each to the other, creating space for both to exist without mutual annihilation.

Together, they form a vertical ecosystem of adaptation and denial, of transformation and stubborn continuity. From the emergence zone where Readers parse the mountain's becoming, through the Gradient's layers of accommodation, down to Threyma's streets where citizens pretend the tremors are only traffic—this is a world learning to live with the impossible, each elevation finding its own way to survive the violence of stone being born.

The mountain continues to rise, pushed by forces too vast and slow for human comprehension. The turbines spin, harvesting energy from geological time. The Readers read, the city manufactures, the Gradient translates. And somewhere in the spaces between—in the cloud layers that mark frequency zones, in the cable cars swaying between states of being, in the dreams of children who've never known a still world—something new is emerging. Not just a mountain, not just an industrial project, but a new way of being human in a world where time itself has become negotiable, where the solid earth learns to flow, where impossibility becomes merely another thing to adapt to, to live with, to call home.
