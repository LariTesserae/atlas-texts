Miles below the Vapor Gardens, where the mountain's roots disappear into darkness, the Dry Workshops occupy caverns that have never known moisture.

The air here is desiccated to a degree that makes breathing painful. It pulls water from lungs and eyes with an almost malicious efficiency. The rocks themselves are bleached pale as old bone, their surfaces covered in a fine dust that rises at the slightest disturbance, hanging in the still air like suspended flour. No condensation has touched these walls in living memory. No drop of water has fallen here since the mountain was young.

The Workshops sprawl through a network of natural caverns that have been expanded and connected by generations of labor. The floors are worn smooth by countless footsteps, polished to a finish that reflects the harsh illumination of the sodium lamps strung along the ceilings. These lights never flicker—their power drawn from the same geothermal sources that feed the gardens above, but here serving a different purpose entirely. They burn constantly, adding their small heat to air already baked by proximity to the thermal vents that are carefully channeled away from these spaces.

Every surface is covered with paper. Sheets hang from wires strung between stalactites. Blueprints cover entire walls, held in place not by moisture-vulnerable adhesives but by an intricate system of clips and wires. The papers range from ancient vellums that crumble at the edges to modern drafting sheets so thin they're almost translucent. Diagrams overlap diagrams in palimpsests of engineering knowledge—cutaway views of boilers, botanical illustrations of modified orchids, chemical formulae for nutrient solutions, pressure calculations scrawled in margins already crowded with annotations in dozen different hands and centuries.

The Drafters work here in absolute silence save for the whisper of pencil on paper and the occasional brittle crack of a sheet being unfolded.

They are pale as cave fish, their skin nearly translucent from lack of sunlight. Where the Tenders above have adapted to moisture, the Drafters have become creatures of desiccation. Their fingers are long and thin as spider legs, stained permanently with graphite and ink. They wear simple robes of undyed cotton that whisper with static electricity in the dry air. Their eyes, magnified behind thick lenses ground from volcanic glass, rarely blink—an adaptation to preserve what little moisture their bodies retain.

The Drafters remember everything. In the absence of humidity that would make paper rot and ink run, they have become living archives of the garden above. Every modification made by the Tenders, every new hybrid orchid, every replacement pipe and redirected steam vent—all of it is documented here in exhaustive detail. They work from memory, having trained themselves to retain perfect mental images of structures they may have seen only once, decades ago. A Drafter can close their eyes and walk through the gardens in their mind, noting every valve, every joint, every species of moss growing on every section of pipe.

Their workstations are austere miracles of organization. Drafting tables built from ancient hardwood that has seasoned in the dry air until it rings like metal when struck. Tools arranged with obsessive precision—ruling pens that have never known rust, compasses with joints worn smooth by centuries of use, T-squares and triangles of brass that gleam dully in the sodium light. Each Drafter guards their tools jealously, some using implements that have been passed down through generations, their edges worn but still true.

The relationship between the Workshops and the Gardens is mediated by the Carriers.

These intermediaries make the journey between the two realms, ascending and descending through vertical shafts lined with airlocks and climate transition chambers. They dress in layers that can be added or removed as they move between the extremes of moisture and aridity. At their waists hang sealed tubes of brass and leather, designed to protect the precious documents they ferry between the worlds.

The journey is perilous. In the transition zones, where dry meets wet, the air becomes unstable. Documents exposed here can go from brittle to sodden in moments, ink running like tears, paper disintegrating to pulp. The Carriers have learned to read the subtle signs—the way dust motes begin to clump, the first hint of condensation on metal surfaces—that signal the boundaries between climates. They move quickly through these zones, holding their breath, protecting their charges with their own bodies when necessary.

When repairs are needed in the gardens above—when the Tenders encounter a problem beyond their intuitive understanding—they leave markers at designated points. A particular arrangement of valves, a pattern of tied vines, a series of colored stones placed on specific grates. The Carriers, on their regular rounds, read these signs and convey them to the Workshops below. There, the Drafters consult their vast archives, cross-referencing symptoms with decades of documented failures and fixes. They draft solutions on paper so thin it's almost weightless, diagrams annotated with precise instructions that account for the decay and growth that will have occurred since their last documentation.

The Drafters never see their designs implemented. They work from memory and faith, trusting that their calculations remain true, that the Tenders above will interpret their instructions correctly. Sometimes a Carrier will return with rubbings taken from successful repairs—impressions of valve settings, tracings of new growth patterns—which the Drafters add to their ever-growing archive. These moments of confirmation are celebrated with subtle gestures—a nod, a slight smile, the careful filing of the new information in its proper place.

In the deepest reaches of the Workshops, behind doors sealed with wax to ensure no moisture can enter, lie the Originals. These are the first blueprints, the master plans drawn by the engineers who conceived the gardens centuries ago. The paper has become something almost beyond paper—so dry it seems more like pressed ash, held together by will and careful climate control alone. The Drafters approach these documents perhaps once in a generation, when some fundamental question arises that cannot be answered by their accumulated modifications and annotations. They copy what they need from memory, as even the gentlest touch might cause these precious sheets to crumble to dust.

The Drafters age differently than those above. Without moisture, their bodies seem to mummify while still living. The oldest among them move with careful deliberation, their skin like parchment, their voices (when they rarely speak) dry whispers that seem to come from great distances. They no longer need to eat much—subsisting on careful sips of distilled water and thin wafers of unleavened bread. Their minds remain sharp as their drafting tools, filled with layers of technical knowledge that span centuries.

At certain times—measured not by the sun they never see but by the great mechanical clocks whose brass gears tick away the decades—the Drafters perform the Renewal. They select their most promising apprentices and begin the process of transferring memory. Not through teaching in any conventional sense, but through a kind of meditative drafting where master and student work side by side, drawing the same plans from memory, the student learning to see through the master's eyes, to hold in their mind the same vast technical landscapes. This process takes years, and when it's complete, the apprentice has become a perfect repository of their master's knowledge, ready to carry it forward into the next generation.

The Workshops expand slowly but steadily. When a new chamber is needed, the Drafters first map it completely in their minds, then draft the excavation plans with such precision that the workers (who come from neither the gardens above nor the workshops themselves, but from some other part of this vast complex) can follow them blindly, chipping away stone to reveal spaces that match the blueprints as if they had always existed, waiting to be discovered.

Between the Vapor Gardens and the Dry Workshops lies the Transition—a vertical maze of shafts, chambers, and airlocks that forms its own distinct ecology.

The main shaft drops five hundred feet through living rock, its walls lined with brass and ceramic tiles that modulate the climate in subtle gradients. At the top, nearest the gardens, condensation beads on every surface. The tiles here are engineered to channel moisture back upward through capillary action, returning it to the gardens above. As one descends, the air gradually dries, the temperature carefully controlled through a series of heat exchangers that bleed warmth away from the ascending air while adding it to that flowing downward.

Every fifty feet, the shaft opens onto a transition chamber—rooms designed for acclimatization where Carriers can rest and allow their bodies to adjust. Each chamber exists at a different point on the moisture gradient. The uppermost are still damp, with small fountains that help maintain humidity. The middle chambers are neutral spaces where the air holds neither excess moisture nor aggressive dryness. The lowest chambers contain desiccant materials—vast panels of specialized clay that pull moisture from anything that enters, preparing travelers for the arid workshops below.

The engineering of these spaces represents centuries of refinement. Pressure differentials are carefully maintained to prevent the mixing of atmospheres. One-way valves allow passage while maintaining climate separation. The airlocks themselves are works of art—brass doors with rubber seals that have maintained their flexibility through some lost process of manufacture, their surfaces etched with instructions in a pictographic language that predates the current written forms used by either Tenders or Drafters.

Strange things grow in the transition zones. In the upper reaches, where moisture still dominates but begins to fail, there are colonies of organisms that exist nowhere else—not quite fungi, not quite lichen, forming paper-thin sheets on the walls that seem to feed on the humidity gradient itself. These growths follow predictable patterns that the Carriers have learned to read as indicators of atmospheric conditions. A slight yellowing of their edges signals that the lower workshops are running drier than usual. A flush of spore production indicates increased moisture from above.

Further down, where dryness begins to dominate, different adaptations emerge. Crystalline formations that might be mineral or might be some extreme form of life coat the walls in the middle chambers. They grow slowly, adding perhaps a millimeter per decade, forming patterns that echo the botanical forms of the gardens above in abstract mineral reproduction. The Carriers sometimes break off small pieces—always from the same designated areas, allowing for regrowth—which they carry as talismans or trade for supplies in the markets that exist in other parts of the mountain.

The Carriers themselves are perhaps the most adapted of all the mountain's inhabitants. Their lungs have developed additional chambers that can seal off, allowing them to breathe comfortably in either extreme moisture or extreme aridity. Their skin produces oils that can either repel water or seal in moisture as needed. They wear clothing of their own design—layered garments with removable sections, each treated with different substances to handle different atmospheric conditions. Some layers are waxed to repel moisture, others treated with hygroscopic salts to absorb it.

The knowledge of passage is passed down through apprenticeship. Young Carriers learn first to read their own bodies—the subtle signals that indicate when they're moving too quickly between climates. A tightness in the chest that means they need to spend more time in the next chamber. A tingling in the fingertips that signals dangerous desiccation. They learn to move documents through the transitions, starting with test papers of no importance, learning how to use the protective tubes, when to transfer materials between different containers, how to use the climate of one zone to prepare documents for the next.

The most experienced Carriers know shortcuts—emergency passages that bypass certain transition chambers, allowing for rapid transport when critical repairs are needed. These routes are dangerous, requiring precise timing and intimate knowledge of the mountain's breathing. They move through maintenance shafts where the climate changes so rapidly that frost can form on one side of the body while the other side burns with dry heat. They carry emergency equipment—breathers that can provide a few minutes of properly humidified air, chemical heat packs that can prevent thermal shock, doses of concentrated salts that can restore electrolyte balance after extreme transitions.

The Carriers maintain small way-stations throughout the transition zones—caches of supplies, replacement equipment, emergency shelters carved into the rock. These spaces are marked with subtle signs readable only to those who know to look for them. Inside, carefully maintained stocks wait: replacement seals for document tubes, rations that remain stable despite the climate variations, medical supplies for treating both moisture-related and desiccation injuries, spare clothing in various sizes and treatment levels.

Communication between the levels happens through the Carriers, but also through more direct means. There are speaking tubes that run the full height of the mountain, their bronze walls treated with acoustic compounds that allow voices to travel clearly despite the distance. The tubes pass through climate baffles that prevent atmospheric mixing while allowing sound to propagate. Messages sent this way arrive distorted by the journey—voices from the gardens sound liquid and distant, while those from the workshops seem to scratch like sand on glass.

There are also the message drops—sealed capsules that can be sent between levels through pneumatic tubes. The system is ancient, its full extent unknown even to those who use it regularly. Some tubes connect only adjacent levels, while others seem to bypass the transition zones entirely, delivering messages through routes that shouldn't be possible given the mountain's known structure. The capsules themselves are marvels of engineering, their seals maintaining integrity across any atmospheric transition, their brass surfaces etched with addresses in the same pictographic language found on the airlock doors.

The Transition is not merely a space between spaces but a world unto itself. The Carriers have their own culture, their own rituals, their own understanding of the mountain's workings. They gather in the neutral chambers for celebrations timed to their own calendar—feast days that mark successful deliveries of particularly crucial documents, mourning rituals for colleagues lost to the passages, coming-of-age ceremonies where new Carriers make their first solo journey from top to bottom and back again.

They tell stories of the deep routes—passages that go beyond the workshops, down to levels where the heat becomes intolerable and the air takes on qualities that have no names. They speak of documents they've carried that seemed to contain more than just technical specifications—plans that hurt to look at, diagrams that suggested modifications not just to the gardens but to the mountain itself, blueprints for systems that might extend far beyond what anyone above or below suspects.

In the neutral chambers, sometimes, the Carriers dance. It's a slow, careful movement that mimics their passages between climates—expansive gestures that speak of moisture, contracting movements that embody desiccation, flowing between the two in patterns that have been refined over generations. The dances serve a practical purpose—keeping bodies limber for the difficult transitions—but they're also something more, a kind of moving meditation on the nature of adaptation and change.

The relationship between the three spaces—gardens, transition, workshops—is one of absolute interdependence. The gardens would choke on their own growth without the precise documentation and planning from below. The workshops would lose all connection to reality without the implemented feedback from above. And without the Carriers and their vertical world, the two extremes would exist in isolation, each becoming more itself until the tension that keeps the whole system vital would snap.

Together, they form a single organism distributed through vertical space—the gardens its flowering crown, the workshops its deep roots, the transition its vital circulation. The moisture rises and falls like breath. The documents flow like thoughts made tangible. The inhabitants move through their appointed spaces, each adapted to their particular niche, each essential to the whole.

The mountain keeps its own time. The machinery cycles, the plants grow and die and grow again, the papers accumulate their layers of knowledge, and through it all the inhabitants maintain their patient work. They are gardeners and archivists, mechanics and scholars, messengers and keepers of the in-between. They tend not just to pipes and plants and papers but to the relationships between them, to the impossible balance that allows such extremes to coexist.

And deep in the mountain's heart, below even the workshops, the thermal sources pulse with planetary heat—the ultimate driver of the entire system, the fire that makes the steam that feeds the gardens that inspire the plans that require the passages that create the world. It has been this way for centuries. It will be this way for centuries more. The mountain breathes, and in its breathing, life finds its strange forms.
