Miles below the salt city, where the plateau's foundations meet older stone, the world inverts. Here, in vast caverns carved by underground rivers that dried up before the first salts ever crystallized above, exists what the deep dwellers call the Inverse Garden—though no surface creature has ever descended far enough to know this name.

The approach begins through fissures in the plateau floor, cracks where the drainage channels have, over millions of years, worn through to something deeper. The salt gives way gradually to limestone, the white crystals yielding to grey stone marked with the fossil memories of ancient seas. The transformation is a journey through time itself—each meter of descent representing millennia of geological history.

The first sign of the lower realm comes as sound: a deep thrumming that rises through the rock, felt more than heard. Those who study such things might recognize it as the resonance of vast spaces below, caverns so large they create their own acoustic weather. The sound changes with barometric pressure, with the movement of air masses in the deeps, with tremors too subtle to register on the surface.

Where the salt finally ends and the true depths begin, the world opens into chambers that dwarf even the cathedral spaces above. But these are not empty voids. The Inverse Garden grows down from the ceiling in formations that mirror the salt city's upward reach—but composed of entirely different stuff.

The gardeners here are not built of incorporated minerals but of something older. Their bodies are soft, almost fungal in texture, yet shot through with rigid structures that might be bone or might be something else entirely. They secrete a substance that hardens into something like pewter—a metallic paste that oxidizes in patterns of verdigris and rust. With this, they build.

The structures hang like chandeliers of impossible complexity. Branching networks of metallic coral descend from the cavern roofs, each branch supporting colonies of creatures that have never known sunlight. The builders work in perfect coordination, though no observer has ever detected how they communicate. They simply know where each drop of metal-paste must go, how each branch must grow to support the weight of future additions.

The hanging gardens serve multiple purposes. They are cities, yes—inhabited spaces where communities of deep creatures make their homes. But they are also mining operations. The metallic secretions absorb specific minerals from the water vapor that rises from even deeper places, extracting precious elements that the gardeners' biology requires. The formations grow toward these vapor sources like plants toward light, their metal branches conducting nutrients throughout the colony.

The light here comes from thermal vents in the cavern floors—cracks that glow with the heat of deeper fires. The organisms cluster around these warmth sources, but not too close. The hanging gardens must maintain a precise distance, close enough to benefit from the warmth and the mineral-rich vapors, far enough to avoid the devastating heat that would melt their carefully constructed homes.

In this inverted ecosystem, rain falls upward. The thermal vents create updrafts that carry moisture from underground rivers—streams that run through channels so deep no surface dweller has imagined them. When this moisture hits the cooler metal formations above, it condenses and drips, but the drops often evaporate in the rising heat before reaching the cavern floor, creating a constant cycle of ascending rain.

The gardeners feed on this moisture, but also on something else. Their metal secretions create a battery-like effect, generating tiny electrical currents between different metallic compounds. These currents attract and trap certain minerals from the vapor, but they also support a unique form of life—electrical bacteria that form living circuits within the metal matrix. The gardeners cultivate these bacterial circuits, feeding on the energy they produce in a form of chemosynthesis unknown on the surface.

Other creatures inhabit this inverted realm. Climbers with adhesive pads navigate the hanging gardens, feeding on metal oxidation and leaving behind patterns of corrosion that the gardeners must constantly repair. Flying organisms—though flying seems the wrong word in these heavy, vapor-laden atmospheres—drift between formations on membranes that catch the thermal updrafts. They feed on the spores and electrical bacteria that float free from the gardens, their bodies flickering with bioluminescent displays that mirror the electrical patterns in the metal colonies.

The most alien inhabitants are the resonators—creatures that exist partially as sound. Their bodies are conventional enough, resembling elongated tuning forks made of calcified tissue. But their consciousness, their essential being, extends into acoustic space. They position themselves at nodes where the cavern's natural resonance is strongest, and there they sing—not with voices but with their entire beings, creating standing waves that can persist for days. These sound-shapes are how they hunt, mate, and communicate, living as much in vibrational reality as physical space.

The connection between the upper salt city and the Inverse Garden is more than mere proximity. The two realms exist in a slow but essential exchange. Water that drains through the salt formations carries dissolved minerals down through the fissures, where it emerges as the vapor feeding the metal gardens. The electrical activity of the gardens, in turn, creates magnetic fields that influence crystal formation in the salt city far above, determining where certain minerals will precipitate.

More remarkably, some creatures migrate between the worlds. The journey takes years—a slow descent through gradually widening fissures, bodies adapting to the changing conditions as they go. The salt dwellers' mineral-incorporating biology serves them well in the transition zone, where they must survive in environments that would dissolve unadapted flesh.

Those that complete the journey arrive transformed. Their salt-crystal shells, subjected to the heat and pressure of the depths, metamorphose into something new—structures that can conduct the electrical currents of the garden colonies. These deep-adapted migrants become living bridges between the two ecosystems, their bodies hosting bacterial colonies from both realms.

The transformation works in reverse as well. Garden dwellers occasionally ascend, their metal secretions gradually giving way to salt deposits as they rise. They bring with them the electrical bacteria, which find new niches in the salt city's biofilms, creating faint auroras in the crystal formations—ghost lights that the surface dwellers have learned to read as signs of deep weather.

The exchange is visible in subtle ways to those who know how to look. Certain formations in the salt city grow in patterns that mirror the hanging gardens below—crystals reaching downward in defiance of their usual habits. In the depths, metal formations sometimes crystallize with salt-like clarity, creating windows of pure metallic crystal through which the garden's bioluminescence shines like captured stars.

There are places, in the transition zones between salt and stone, where the two realms bleed together. Chambers where salt stalactites meet metal stalagmites, where the chemical reactions between them create environments found nowhere else. Here live creatures adapted to neither world but to the intersection—beings that require both the mineral cycles of the surface and the electrical ecology of the depths.

During the great storms that occasionally lash the plateau, the connection intensifies. Lightning strikes on the salt formations above created electrical surges that propagate through the mineral matrix, racing down through the transition zones to earth themselves in the metal gardens below. For days afterward, the gardens glow more brightly, supercharged by the surface energy. The gardeners work frantically during these times, building new structures to capture and store the power.

In return, the deep tremors caused by the gardens' growth—the expansion of metal formations, the shifting of hung weight—propagate upward, creating the micro-earthquakes that crack new fissures in the salt city, opening fresh surfaces for crystal growth. The two realms exist in a conversation conducted in geological time, each responding to the other in ways that shape their mutual evolution.

The migrating creatures are not the only connection between the realms. Certain minerals cycle between them in patterns that take centuries to complete. Iron from the garden's metal secretions oxidizes and rises as rust-red vapor, eventually precipitating in the salt formations as hematite crystals. These crystals, dissolved by rain and carried back down, provide essential nutrients for the garden's electrical bacteria.

There are those among both populations—the ancient salt dwellers above and the eldest gardeners below—who understand this connection. They position themselves at the crucial exchange points, their bodies becoming part of the mineral cycle. When they finally die, their remains create permanent channels between the worlds, conduits that will carry water and minerals and perhaps consciousness itself between the salt city and the Inverse Garden for millennia to come.

This, then, is the greater organism: not two separate realms but a single system breathing through geological time, the salt city exhaling what the garden inhales, the garden's electrical dreams rising to shape the crystal thoughts of the city above. Between them flows a commerce older than any surface ocean—an exchange of minerals and energy and life that has shaped both realms into something neither could have become alone.
