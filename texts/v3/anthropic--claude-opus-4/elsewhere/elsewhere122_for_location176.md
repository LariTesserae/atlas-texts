Far above, where the reef-city's highest spires pierce the water's skin, the Readers work in air that tastes of salt and certainty.

Their library crowns a tower of brain-coral, its base thick as a ship's hull, narrowing as it climbs through fathoms toward sky. The builders here worked differently than those below—not carving into living stone but coaxing it upward, feeding specific colonies, guiding growth with copper frameworks that now lie buried in the coral's heart. It took three generations to grow this tower. The library itself is barely twenty years old.

The space breathes with tides. High water submerges the lower shelves; low tide leaves them streaming, mineral crusts marking each flood's reach. The Readers planned for this. Their texts aren't books but stones—flat plates of polished granite, basalt, jade—each one incised with symbols that catch shadow and hold meaning. The stones rest in specially carved slots, their weight keeping them stable as water comes and goes.

Windows pierce the walls at irregular intervals, following the coral's natural hollows. No glass—the openings stay bare to weather, admitting wind and spray and the occasional ambitious gull. Light enters in solid beams, tracking across the floor in patterns the Readers know by heart. When storms come, they simply move the stones higher, letting rain and wave do what they will with the lower reaches.

The classification system defies mainland logic. Stones group by weight and density rather than subject—the heaviest texts sink to flood-prone shelves, their messages meant for those who read underwater. Lighter stones climb toward the ceiling, where only air-breathers venture. Middle-weight texts migrate with the tides, sometimes submerged, sometimes dry, their meanings shifting with their medium.

Each stone bears two sets of symbols. The first, carved deep, tells its primary text—histories, calculations, star maps, building plans. The second emerges only when wet, when water fills shallow-carved channels invisible to dry sight. These wet-texts comment on the dry, argue with them, sometimes contradict entirely. A astronomical table becomes, when submerged, a poem about loss. Building specifications transform into meditation on impermanence.

The Readers move between air and water like punctuation between words. They wear nothing but tool belts—leather processed with fish oil to stay supple through repeated soaking. Each belt holds styluses of different metals (copper for wet-carving, iron for dry), brushes made from their own shed hair, vials of acids that etch stone at various rates.

Unlike the Keeper below, the Readers change quickly. Their eyes develop nictitating membranes within months of arrival. Finger-webbing appears by the first year's end—thin, temporary things that tear and regrow with use. They learn to read by touch underwater, to hold their breath for the time it takes to carve a complex character, to surface precisely between waves.

The Head Reader is ancient by their measure—almost forty years in the tower. Her skull shows the work's mark: forehead flattened from years of pressing against stones to read in murky water, eye sockets deepened from squinting in spray-filtered light. She coordinates the library's living schedule, knowing which Readers work best at which tides, who carves truest in storm-light, whose wet-texts achieve the finest balance of clarity and contradiction.

They sleep in coral cells grown into the tower's sides, each barely large enough for a body curled fetal. The cells flood at high tide. New Readers often panic their first night, thrashing awake underwater. They learn, or they leave. Eventually the flooding becomes comfort—the pressure like blankets, the temperature marking time's passage more surely than any clock.

The library serves the reef-city, but obliquely. Builders come seeking plans, find philosophy. Historians hunting dates discover recipes for bronze that tastes of regret. The classification by density means knowledge scatters—a single narrative might span stones of different weights, forcing readers to wait for specific tides to complete a thought.

Yet patterns emerge for those who stay. The heaviest stones, those that never leave water, speak of permanence—architectural principles, mathematical constants, the names of the dead. Light stones that rarely submerge record fleeting things—cloud formations, gossip, the sound rain makes on different waters. Medium stones carry the bulk of daily knowledge, alternately hidden and revealed by the tides' breathing.

At spring tide's peak, the entire library floods. Readers who've earned the privilege stay under, moving through the transformed space where every stone shows its wet-text, where meanings layer and interweave in the green-gold light. They read with their whole bodies then, swimming from stone to stone, carrying conversations across the full vertical span of knowledge.

At neap tide's ebb, only the lowest shelves stay wet. The library becomes a tower of dry whispers, styluses scratching new arguments into old stones. Readers work frantically then, knowing the water returns, knowing their dry-texts must be strong enough to survive their wet contradictions.

The Head Reader coordinates from memory now, her eyes clouded by decades of salt. She knows each stone by weight in the hand, by the texture of its carved face. She assigns new Readers to sort the incoming stones—offerings from the reef-city's builders, from distant colonies, from mainland scholars who think they understand what the library is.

They don't. The library isn't a repository but a metabolism. Knowledge enters as rough stone, gets digested through tidal cycles, emerges transformed. A text about wall construction becomes, through repeated submersion and amendment, a treatise on the mathematics of faith. Star charts evolve into navigation guides for the drowned.

Sometimes Readers descend to the flooded chambers, bringing stone-texts to the Keeper and others like it. They watch the slow workers read with infinite patience, running mineral-thickened fingers over wet-text that might take months to fully comprehend. The Readers feel their own heartbeats racing hummingbird-quick by comparison. They surface gasping, return to their tower, carve new stones with the urgency of mayflies.

But the stones remain, accumulating meaning with each tide. The library grows—new chambers coaxed from the brain-coral, new shelves carved to hold the weight of all this wet and dry wisdom. The Readers age and change and pass their styluses to quick-fingered successors. 

And below, always below, the Keeper continues its perfect, geological work, each chisel strike a counterpoint to the frantic scratching above. The reef-city breathes between them—water and air, permanence and change, the slow accumulation of meaning in stone.

When storms come, the Readers seal what texts they can in the highest chambers. They retreat to their cells, feeling the tower sway with deep currents. In the flooded workshop, the Keeper pauses its work, hair-net spread to taste the storm-changed water. Both groups wait, patient as coral, knowing the storm will pass, the work continue, the city grow.

The real text of the reef-city isn't written in either place but in the space between—in the vertical journey from drowned workshop to tide-washed library, in the conversation between those who grow into stone and those who flit between elements. Each depends on the other. The Keeper's slow perfection needs the Readers' urgent records. The Readers' fleeting insights require the Keeper's permanent foundation.

Together they build meaning from mineral and time, crafting a city that reads itself, writes itself, dreams in the intersection of water and air.
