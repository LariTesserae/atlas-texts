The Glass Plains

Three days' descent from the volcanic peaks, where the land levels into vast tableland before plunging toward distant seas, stretches an expanse that cartographers have never agreed how to name. Some maps mark it as the Vitrified Wastes. Others call it the Mirror Desert. The tribes who skirt its edges simply call it the Hungry Glass.

It is a ocean of obsidian frozen mid-surge, its waves caught forever in the act of breaking. The volcanic flow that created it—some catastrophic eruption in the planet's youth—cooled so rapidly that the stone became pure glass, black as the space between stars and sharp enough to flay flesh from bone. The surface ripples and buckles, forming valleys and ridges, frozen splash-patterns where ancient lava fountains fell back to earth, delicate fans where the molten rock spread thin as paper before hardening.

By day, the sun turns this deadland into a furnace of reflected light. The black glass absorbs heat with terrifying efficiency, creating shimmering thermals that make the air itself appear to melt. The temperature at surface level can strip moisture from lungs in minutes. Even the hardiest desert creatures avoid the plains during daylight, leaving them utterly lifeless under the burning sky.

But the glass holds this heat jealously. As night falls, it radiates back into the cooling air, creating a temperature differential so extreme that the obsidian sings. The rapid contraction makes the plains ring like vast bells, each sheet of glass resonating at its own frequency. The sound carries for miles—a choir of stone that rises and falls with the temperature, sometimes harmonious, sometimes discordant, always alien.

This is when the plains come alive.

The first to emerge are the glass dancers—creatures so perfectly adapted to this environment that they seem less evolved than designed. Their bodies are living prisms, translucent flesh that refracts and reflects light in ways that make them nearly invisible against the obsidian. They're flat, their bodies no thicker than a hand's breadth, with edges that taper to molecular sharpness. They move by flexing their crystalline muscles, sending waves through their bodies that propel them across the glass in sliding, skating motions.

The dancers feed on the only thing that grows here—razor lichen, a crystalline organism that spreads across the obsidian in patterns like frozen lightning. The lichen survives by metabolizing the minerals leached from the glass by the extreme temperature changes, growing in microscopic increments over centuries. The dancers scrape it free with specialized mouth parts, their feeding leaving intricate patterns on the glass—temporary artworks that will be colonized by new lichen in another hundred years.

Following the dancers come the deep dwellers. During the furnace days, they retreat into the cave systems that web beneath the plains—obsidian tubes and bubbles formed by gas pockets in the ancient flow. But at night they emerge: pallid things with no eyes, navigating by vibration and the minute temperature differences their heat-sensitive skin can detect. Some are hunters, pursuing the dancers across the treacherous surface. Others are scavengers, seeking the casualties of the glass—creatures that made fatal missteps and opened themselves on the waiting edges.

The most remarkable are the resonance worms. Long as a human is tall but thin as a finger, they're composed of segments that can vibrate independently. They hunt by matching the frequency of the glass they're crossing, essentially becoming invisible to vibration-sensitive prey. When they strike, they shatter their target with precisely tuned sonic pulses, then absorb the liquefied remains through their permeable skin.

Water exists here, but rarely as liquid. The night's temperature differential causes what little moisture the air holds to condense directly onto the glass as frost—delicate crystalline patterns that exist for perhaps an hour before the returning sun sublimates them back to vapor. The plains' creatures have brief, desperate rituals around these frost-blooms, licking the moisture before it vanishes, their bodies pressed flat against the glass to absorb every possible molecule.

At the plains' heart lies something that shouldn't exist—an oasis of sorts, where some geological quirk has created a permanent shadow. Here, the obsidian forms a natural bowl a hundred meters across, its walls angled to trap cool air. The temperature differential is less extreme, the glass here clouded and crazed rather than mirror-smooth. In this slightly more hospitable microclimate, unique species have evolved. Garden worms that cultivate frost-catching webs. Colonial organisms that build structures from shed glass-dancer scales. Even a species of flowering plant—the only one on the plains—that grows a single bloom of living glass, its petals thin enough to flex without shattering.

The Glass Roads are the only safe passages across the plains, and they're safe only by comparison. Ancient travelers discovered that certain routes, where the obsidian flow formed natural channels, create predictable thermal currents. Following these currents, moving only in the brief windows of dawn and dusk when the temperature equalizes, it's possible to cross the plains in relative safety. The paths are marked by cairns of fused glass—monuments built by unknown hands, each one singing its own note when the night winds blow through precisely carved holes.

Storms here are not of rain but of glass dust. When the temperature differentials become extreme enough, whole sections of the plains can shatter spontaneously, sending clouds of obsidian particles into the air. These storms can strip flesh from bone in seconds, but they also serve a purpose—redistributing nutrients across the barren expanse, creating new surfaces for lichen to colonize, opening new caves for the deep dwellers.

The Shattered Throne marks the plains' western edge, where the obsidian flow met some impossible obstacle and reared up into frozen waves fifty meters high. The formation resembles a crown or a breaking wave or a throne, depending on the angle and the light. It's riddled with caves and passages where the glass cooled unevenly, creating a vertical maze that's home to the plains' apex predator—the glass wyverns.

These aren't true wyverns but earned their name from their hunting method. They're colonial organisms, thousands of tiny glass dancers that have learned to move as one. They form wings and teeth and claws from their massed bodies, shape-shifting predators that can disperse into clouds of cutting edges or coalesce into something that can bring down even the largest deep dweller. At rest, they're indistinguishable from any other formation of broken glass. In motion, they're death given form and purpose.

The connection between the Glass Plains and the volcanic chambers isn't immediately obvious. No road links them, no river flows between. But deep beneath the obsidian, the same thermal systems that created both realms still pulse with heat. The deep dwellers' caves eventually connect to lava tubes. The minerals that feed the chamber's stone scarabs have their origins in the weathering of the plains' surface. Even the water follows hidden paths—moisture that condenses on the glass eventually seeps through microscopic cracks, joining the thermal springs that feed the chamber's eternal rain.

But the deeper connection is in the nature of extremes. Both places represent life pushed to its absolute limits, environments so hostile that existence itself becomes an act of defiance. The creatures of each realm have become their environments in the most literal sense—stone scarabs made of volcanic glass, glass dancers with obsidian edges, the Tender of Stones with her mineral skin, the resonance worms that have become living sound.

There are stories, told by the tribes who know these lands, of times when the connections become visible. Nights when the singing of the Glass Plains harmonizes with some deep vibration from the volcanic peaks, creating corridors of sound between the two realms. Times when creatures from one domain appear in the other—glass dancers found in the thermal chambers, their bodies slowly opaquing in the mineral-rich moisture. Stone scarabs discovered on the plains' edge, their obsidian shells allowing them to survive where no other mountain creature could.

And there are whispers of others like the Tender of Stones. Humans who ventured too deep into these hostile places and became them. The Glass Shepherd, who leads the wyvern colonies in their hunts, their body a shifting mass of living glass indistinguishable from their charges. The Frost Gardener, who cultivates the impossible flowers in the plains' heart, their fingers branching into delicate structures that can catch and hold the morning frost. The Echo Walker, who has learned to navigate by sound alone, their bones resonating with the plains' nightly song.

Perhaps they're real. Perhaps they're cautionary tales. Or perhaps they're something more—an acknowledgment that in lands too extreme for normal life, the boundary between alive and inanimate, between creature and environment, becomes meaningless. The Glass Plains don't support life in any conventional sense. Like the volcanic chamber, they are life—a vast organism of stone and glass and the brave things that dance on the knife's edge between existence and extinction.

The plains end where they began—suddenly, a clean line where the obsidian flow stopped and normal stone resumes. Beyond this edge, scrub vegetation immediately takes hold, grateful for ordinary soil and survivable temperatures. Animals graze within sight of the glass but never upon it. Rain falls and collects in natural pools instead of vanishing to vapor.

It's a reminder of how thin the margin is. A few feet separate the possible from the impossible, the living world from the glass desert. But for those who have seen the dancers skating across obsidian beneath the stars, who have heard the plains sing their nightly song, who have witnessed the wyverns rise like death made manifest—the ordinary world seems diminished somehow. Less real than the impossible life that persists in the Hungry Glass, beautiful and terrible and utterly alien.

The glass remembers everything—every step that has crossed it, every drop of moisture that has touched it, every vibration that has run through its substance. In its frozen waves and trapped bubbles live the memory of the violence that created it. And in the delicate patterns left by feeding dancers, in the cairns that sing navigation songs, in the impossible garden at its heart, live the memories of everything that has refused to surrender to it.

The plains wait, patient as stone, hostile as the space between stars. They'll outlast the mountains that birthed them, endure long after the last dancer has skated into extinction. But for now, in this moment between geological ages, they're alive with their own strange vitality—a testament to the stubborn insistence of life to exist wherever existence is possible, and sometimes where it isn't.
