Below the aerial city, where the mountain's eroded teeth give way to forested shoulders and then to gentle foothills, another settlement spreads across the land like spilled water finding every depression. This is the Sprawl of Fens, a town without borders that exists wherever the ground stays wet enough to support its peculiar architecture.

The builders here work not with stone but with living wood. They shape trees from saplings, bending and weaving them into structures that grow rather than stand. A house might take twenty years to complete, its walls formed from the interlocked branches of six or seven trees whose roots share the same boggy soil. Windows are gaps planned decades in advance, doorways carved so gently the trees barely scar. The structures breathe with the seasons—walls thickening with spring growth, spaces opening as winter strips the leaves.

Water defines everything. It seeps up from underground springs, collects in natural hollows, runs in a thousand temporary channels that appear and disappear with the rains. The ground itself is rarely solid—walkways are woven from living willow that floats on the bog's surface, flexing under footsteps. During the wet season, entire districts become archipelagos connected by these floating paths. During drought, the exposed mud cracks into polygonal plates that the inhabitants read like maps, showing where the deep moisture hides.

The Sprawl has no center, no edges—only a gradual thinning where the water table drops and the bog trees can no longer thrive. Families might live a day's journey from their nearest neighbors, or clustered so close their tree-houses grow into each other. The settlement expands and contracts with the water, occupying new territories when springs bubble up, abandoning others when the flow shifts underground.

The inhabitants move through this amphibious world with webbed feet—not born but made, toes bound with water-plant fibers from childhood until the skin grows between them. They pole shallow boats through channels too numerous to map, following routes that exist only in memory and muscle. Their clothing is oiled bark cloth that sheds water, worn loose to dry quickly. They carry nothing that cannot survive repeated soaking.

Where the mountain dwellers cultivate aerial plankton, these people farm the water itself. They grow elaborate gardens of aquatic plants—some for food, some for fiber, some for the psychoactive sap that helps them dream the water's path. Channels are diverted and restored to encourage certain species, discourage others. Fish pens are woven from living reeds that filter water while keeping stock contained. Eel farms occupy the darkest pools, tended by children who learn to grab the slippery bodies without fear.

The bog-dwellers' relationship with water extends beyond the practical. They believe consciousness flows like water, pooling in certain places, running thin in others. Their shamans drink the bog-water mixed with plant saps to "dive" into the collective flow, seeking answers to community problems or tracing underground streams. They emerge from these trances speaking of conversations with water that remembers being cloud, being rain, being the blood in their veins.

Time moves differently here than in the mountain city. Without solid ground for permanent markers, seasons blur into cycles of wet and dry, growth and dormancy. The inhabitants age not in years but in trees—counting their lives by the houses they've started, the boats they've grown, the channels they've opened or closed. An elder might say they are "three houses old," meaning sixty or seventy years have passed since their birth.

Between these two worlds—the aerial city clinging to stone and the sprawling bog settlement—lies a vertical mile of transitional space that belongs fully to neither. This is the domain of the Carriers, the ones who move between.

The journey begins at the Sprawl's upmost reach, where the last bog pools give way to solid ground. Here stands the Traders' Rest, a structure unique in the bog—built from dead wood rather than living trees, its permanence a deliberate signal of its liminal nature. Carriers gather here to prepare for the ascent, trading their webbed foot-wrappings for grip-soled boots, their bark cloth for dense wool that can withstand mountain winds.

The trail itself is ancient, older than either settlement, worn by countless feet into a groove that catches and channels rain. For the first day, it winds through transitional forest where bog species mingle with mountain varieties. The Carriers know every stream crossing, every sheltered overhang, every place where the path forks and the wrong choice leads to impassable cliffs. They move steadily but without hurry—the full journey takes four days up, three days down, and rushing invites disaster.

As the path climbs, the world transforms. Bog plants disappear, replaced by tough mountain shrubs that grab at pack straps. The air grows thinner, forcing lowland lungs to work harder. At night, Carriers sleep in way-stations built into natural caves or beneath overhanging rocks, structures maintained by both communities as neutral ground. The walls bear carved messages—warnings about path conditions, notes about weather patterns, occasionally personal messages between lovers separated by altitude.

The Carriers themselves are apart from either community. Many are children of mixed heritage—born in one world but drawn to the other, never fully comfortable in either. They develop their own culture in the spaces between, their own songs that match the rhythm of climbing, their own stories that weave together the mythologies of both settlements. They know the bog-dwellers' water consciousness and the mountain people's creature wisdom, speaking both languages with accents that mark them as belonging to neither.

What moves between the communities through these human links forms the basis of their mutual survival. The mountain city sends down salamander oil for waterproofing, pressed plankton cakes that provide protein the bog lacks, and most precious of all—certain crystals that form in the high caves which the bog shamans dissolve in their consciousness-expanding brews. In return, the Sprawl sends up fiber ropes stronger than anything the mountain can produce, smoked eels that keep for months, and particular bog plants whose roots help prevent the altitude sickness that can kill lowlanders.

But more than goods travel this vertical road. Knowledge flows both ways—weather patterns observed from the heights warn the bog of incoming storms, while the water-readers below send up predictions about drought cycles that affect the mountain's crucial moisture collection. Young people sometimes journey to the other settlement for fostering, returning years later with hybrid skills that strengthen both communities.

The Carriers also bring news of the world beyond. In their travels to distant markets, they gather rumors and stories, technologies and ideas, filtering what might benefit either community. They are the ones who know that far to the east, another mountain settlement has discovered how to breed larger salamanders, or that southern bog-dwellers have developed new techniques for channel management. This knowledge they parse out carefully, understanding that too much change too quickly could destroy the delicate balance both communities maintain.

There are tensions, inevitably. The mountain dwellers sometimes resent their dependence on bog fibers, while the Sprawl folk envy the security of stone buildings that won't rot. Young people who leave for fostering don't always return, choosing instead the Carrier life or disappearing into the wider world entirely. Resources are sometimes hoarded, prices disputed, agreements broken.

But the connection endures because it must. When avalanches block the mountain passes, trapping the aerial city, it's the bog-dwellers who organize relief climbs. When drought threatens to dry the Sprawl completely, the mountain sends down every drop their cisterns can spare. The two communities are like organs in a single body stretched vertically across the landscape—distinct but interdependent, each ecosystem supporting the other across the gap of altitude and adaptation.

The Carriers understand this best of all. Moving constantly between worlds, they see how the mountain's water eventually feeds the bog's springs, how the bog's evaporation becomes the clouds that bring moisture to the heights. They carry more than goods up and down the ancient path—they carry the knowledge that separation is an illusion, that the aerial city and the Sprawl of Fens are merely different expressions of the same survival imperative, the same human urge to find life in the margins and make it flourish.

In the way-stations between worlds, the Carriers sometimes perform a ritual. They pour water carried from the bog into vessels filled with water from the mountain cistern, then drink the mixture while standing with one foot pointing upslope, one down. It's their way of honoring both sources, of holding in their bodies the truth that water knows no permanent home—only the endless journey between states, between places, between the communities it connects and sustains.

This is how two worlds become one: not through conquest or merger, but through the patient work of those who walk between, carrying what is needed, translating what is foreign, weaving connections that make each settlement not just viable but vital to the other. The mountain city in its stone hollow and the Sprawl threading through the bogs—they are not two places but one system, breathing together across the vertical span of their shared existence.
