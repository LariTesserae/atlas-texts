Several days' journey upward, following the water's source through limestone layers and ancient lava tubes, the mountain opens into a vast circular caldera. Here, where the Weeping Galleries' water begins its patient descent, spreads the Bright Basin—a salt flat so perfectly level it seems like a disk of frozen light pressed against the sky.

The Basin floods with each rain, transforming into a shallow sea perhaps ankle-deep at most. When the water evaporates—which happens quickly in the thin mountain air—it leaves behind a crust of salt crystals that catch and throw back sunlight in ways that hurt to look at directly. The surface reforms constantly: each flooding dissolves the old patterns, each drying creates new ones. Polygonal tiles of salt fit together like a vast puzzle, their edges raised into small ridges where the mineral condensed last.

Nothing grows here except at the very margins, where sweet water from the surrounding peaks meets the salt. Even there, life is sparse—low cushions of adapted plants that can tolerate the mineral burn, their leaves thick and waxy, their roots searching deep for the freshwater lens that floats beneath the brine. When they bloom, which is rarely, their flowers are violently colorful against the white—magentas and ultramarines that seem to vibrate in the clarified air.

The Shattering Years come irregularly. Seven times, or eight, or thirteen times in a century, the rains arrive in such quantity that the Basin becomes a true lake, deep enough to swim in. The salt dissolves completely. Fish appear—somehow, impossibly—silver things that flash like coins tossed in sunlight. Where they come from, no one knows. Perhaps dormant in the salt itself, perhaps washing down from hidden pools in the peaks. They last only as long as the deep water does. When the evaporation begins, they seem to know, schooling frantically in the shrinking pools until the last of them lies gasping on the re-forming crust.

This is when the Walkers come.

They arrive as the water recedes, approaching from settlements beyond the caldera's rim. They wear wooden platforms strapped to their feet—broad planks that distribute their weight across the soft, reforming salt. Their eyes are wrapped in gauze dyed black, with only narrow slits cut for vision. They pull sledges behind them, also on runners, loaded with tools and supplies.

The Walkers are tall where the Vith'arani are compact, their bodies adapted for distance rather than compression. Generations of sun at altitude have darkened their skin to deep brown, with patterns of paler scars where salt crystals have burned them over years of work. They move with a strange gait, sliding rather than stepping, their platforms hissing across the crust.

They come for the salt, but more than that—they come for what the salt captures. As the Basin dries, it forms chambers beneath the crust, pockets where the last water concentrates into brine so dense objects float in it like corks. In these hidden pools, minerals precipitate in forms found nowhere else: branching crystals of halite and gypsum, deposits of borax and lithium, salts of metals that give strange colors—purple, green, a blue so pure it seems borrowed from deep ocean.

The Walkers know how to read the surface, to recognize where these chambers form. They work in the dangerous days when the crust is still soft, before the full drying locks everything in place. Breaking through too early means sinking into brine that can burn skin to blistering. Too late, and the chambers seal permanently until the next Shattering Year. They probe with long poles, listening for the hollow sound that indicates a void below.

When they find one, the extraction is delicate work. They cut through the salt crust in precise patterns, lifting out sections like plates of armor. Below, the chambers reveal their treasures—crystals growing in impossible geometries, brine so saturated it feels like oil between the fingers, sometimes the perfectly preserved bodies of the silver fish, encased in salt like insects in amber.

The Walkers speak a language of few words and many gestures, visible from great distances across the flat. They work in family groups spread across the Basin, each with traditional territories marked by nothing more than memory and respect. At night, they camp at the edges where solid ground begins, their fires visible for miles as points of orange light against the star-silvered salt.

They have their own relationship with water—not the patient acceptance of the Vith'arani, but a fierce attention to its coming and going. They can read clouds like text, knowing which formations bring the flooding rains and which merely tease with virga that evaporates before touching ground. Their calendar is based on evaporation rates. Their holiest ceremonies happen at the balance point—when the last standing water disappears but before the crust fully hardens.

The salt they harvest is not ordinary. The peculiar minerals of the Basin, concentrated by cycles of flooding and drying, create compounds with properties both medicinal and strange. A blue salt that numbs pain but brings vivid dreams. A green crystal that preserves food indefinitely but gives it a metallic taste. The rarest—a violet precipitate found only in the deepest chambers—which the Walkers claim allows one to see heat as color, cold as darkness visible.

Between the Shattering Years, when the Basin lies locked in its armor of hard salt, the Walkers retreat to their rim settlements. But they send watchers—usually the young, earning their place—to monitor the flat. These sentinels live in small shelters built from salt blocks cut from the Basin itself, structures that blur the line between architecture and landscape. They watch for the signs: the way certain peaks gather clouds, how the salt begins to sing in particular winds, the behavior of the high-altitude ravens that seem to know days in advance when the waters will come.

The Walkers also have a saying: "Salt remembers water." They believe the Basin holds the memory of every flooding, that the patterns in the crust encode information about past years, past centuries. The eldest among them claim to read these patterns like a history—this dark line from the flood fifty years ago that lasted three months, this formation from the legendary Shattering that covered even the rim settlements. They teach their children to see time in the laminations of salt, to understand their place in cycles larger than human memory.

When the Vith'arani emerge from their galleries to trade—those rare times when necessity drives them to the surface—it is often the Walkers they seek. The relationship between the two peoples is old and formal, structured by generations of exchange. The Walkers bring salt, of course, but also news of the world beyond, stories of the sky that the gallery dwellers rarely see. In return, the Vith'arani offer what they have: the perfectly polished stones, the dried fungi that help preserve food on long journeys, and most importantly, information about the water.

The Vith'arani know every nuance of the water that flows through their galleries—its temperature, its mineral content, the subtle changes that presage a Shattering Year. This knowledge, whispered in the formal trading language both peoples share, helps the Walkers prepare. Days or weeks before the visible signs appear, the Vith'arani can tell from the taste of their pools that change is coming. They send runners—young people who can bear the anxiety of open sky for a short time—to carry warnings to the rim.

The connection runs deeper than trade. The water that floods the Basin, that creates the environment for the strange salts to form, is the same water that seeps through limestone to become the eternal drip of the galleries. The Walkers' harvest and the Vith'arani's existence are bound by the same hydrological cycle, the same patient conversation between water and stone.

Sometimes, rarely, individuals cross between the worlds. A Walker child born too small for the distances might be given to the Vith'arani, to learn the ways of flowing downward. A gallery dweller who cannot bear the compression might join the Walkers, training their feet for distances and their eyes for the brutal honesty of reflected light. These crossings are traumatic—the body itself must be remade, slowly and painfully, to fit the new world. But those who survive become bridges themselves, carrying understanding between the two realms.

The Walkers mark these exchanges with ceremonies at the exact point where the galleries' outflow emerges into daylight—a series of springs at the base of the caldera's eastern wall. Here, water that has spent months or years in darkness meets its first sun. The ceremony involves salt dissolved in gallery water, creating a solution that belongs fully to neither world. Both peoples drink, grimacing at the mineral burn, acknowledging their connection.

There are tensions, of course. The Walkers sometimes dam the springs to create pools for their own use, slowing the flow to the galleries below. The Vith'arani occasionally harvest minerals from the cave systems that would otherwise wash into the Basin, altering the chemistry of the floods. But these conflicts are managed through the same patient negotiation that characterizes both cultures—slow conversations, formal protocols, the recognition that neither can exist without the other.

The land between their worlds is harsh and transitional. From the Basin's rim to the gallery entrances, the terrain drops through a series of terraces—ancient lake beds from when the caldera was younger and held water permanently. Each terrace has its own character: one dense with thorn scrub that blooms after rain, another bare rock marked only by the trails both peoples have worn over generations. Ravens nest in the cliff faces, and sometimes golden eagles hunt the small mammals that den in the rocks.

Neither people truly inhabit this middle ground, but both use it. The Walkers maintain supply caches in hidden caves. The Vith'arani have carved cisterns where rainwater collects, emergency supplies for their rare surface journeys. Occasionally travelers from each group meet in this neutral space, sharing food and information, their conversations echoing off the rocks in the peculiar acoustic environment where gallery echo meets open-air clarity.

This is how the two worlds connect: through water and salt, through formal trade and rare understanding, through the individuals who dare to cross between. The Bright Basin and the Weeping Galleries exist in the same mountain, sharing the same water, the same geological history, but they have created entirely different answers to the question of how to live. The Walkers expand across their white plain, reading the sky and harvesting the earth's fever dreams. The Vith'arani compress into their stone wombs, finding infinity in the space between drops.

Together, they form a single system—a circulation of water and minerals, people and knowledge, as patient and inevitable as the stone that holds them both. The mountain contains multitudes, and these are two of its songs: one bright and harsh and tasting of salt, the other dark and patient and echoing with the sound of time itself passing, drop by drop, through limestone.
