The Calcified Roots

Eight thousand meters directly below the lowest terrace, where the pressure would crush any air-breathing thing to paste, the water-medium thickens to the consistency of molten glass. Here, temperature gradients reverse—cold seeps up from something deeper still, meeting the machinery's warmth in layers of thermocline that shimmer like aurora through the murk.

The Roots begin here, though "begin" suggests an origin that may never have existed. They are pillars of fossilized intention, each one thirty meters in diameter, rising from depths unmeasured through the crushing dark. Their surfaces appear to be stone—specifically, compressed layers of calcium carbonate and metallic salts—but the stone pulses. Veins of bioluminescent mineral run through them like frozen circulatory systems, glowing amber-gold in patterns that shift over centuries.

These are not supports for the Terrace Mills above, though the Millkeepers once believed this. The Roots predate the terraces by geological ages. Their arrangement follows no architectural logic: some cluster in groups of seven or twelve, others stand solitary for kilometers. They rise at angles that defy the vertical—45 degrees here, nearly horizontal there—as if gravity meant something different when they formed.

The surface of each Root tells a story in mineralized time. The oldest layers, at the base where they disappear into absolute darkness, contain traces of organic material unlike anything that now exists. Spectral analysis—performed by ancient Millkeepers who descended this far only once—revealed protein chains of impossible complexity, crystallized DNA helixes that spiral in five dimensions, the fossilized remains of thoughts made flesh.

Higher up, the Roots bear scars of their abandonment. Great gouges spiral around their circumferences where something tremendous once gripped them. Cavities the size of buildings pocket their surfaces—not damage, but places where something was meant to grow and never did. In these hollows, colonies of pressure-adapted organisms now make their homes: blind things that feed on mineral exudation, living fossils themselves.

The water-medium here carries memory. Not metaphorically—the molecular chains are so long and complex they can store information like synthetic DNA. As the cold current rises past the Roots, it accumulates data: the precise temperature of each Root's core, the frequency of its bioluminescent pulse, the chemical signature of its slow decay. This information spirals upward in helical streams, eventually reaching the Terrace Mills where sensitive Millkeepers can taste it and know something of what lies below.

But the Roots are not passive monuments. At their tips—if they can be said to have tips, since many branch and rebranch into fractal tentacles—they still grow. The rate is infinitesimal: perhaps a millimeter per century. The growth follows patterns that repeat at scales from microscopic to mountainous. New calcium deposits form in spirals that match the spiral scarring, as if the Roots remember what once held them and grow to fill that absence.

The Rootkeepers knew. Before the Millkeepers, before the terraces, before the water-medium itself filled this space, there were the Rootkeepers. Their remains calcify in the depths—beings of living stone that fused themselves to the Roots in some final, desperate symbiosis. You can still find them if you descend far enough: humanoid shapes melded into the Root surfaces, their features weathered but discernible. They have become part of what they tended, mineral nursemaids frozen at their posts.

The few functional Rootkeeper artifacts suggest a civilization built around cultivation of something unimaginable. Tools designed to prune structures in higher dimensions. Resonance chambers that could adjust the frequency of matter itself. Texts inscribed in three-dimensional script that describe cultivation practices for organisms that existed partially outside normal space-time.

Whatever they were growing never matured. The Roots remain—not dead but dormant, not abandoned but waiting. Their bioluminescent veins pulse in patterns that occasionally sync across vast distances, suggesting some deep communication network that predates any current understanding. The patterns correlate weakly with the operations above: when the Terrace Mills increase production, the Roots glow brighter. When new organisms appear in the gardens, new veins of light branch through the stone below.

The relationship between Roots and terraces remains opaque. Did the Mills evolve to tap some resource the Roots provide? Are they parasitic growths on an older system? Or are both parts of some vaster organism whose full shape exists beyond perception? The Millkeepers, despite their ages of observation, have no answers. They only know that damage to the Roots—from the rare tectonic shifts that crack their surfaces—results in cascading failures in the Mills above.

Some things rise from below. Not often—perhaps once in a Millkeeper's lifetime—but when they do, the entire system shudders. Fragments of Root material that maintain their bioluminescence even when severed. Perfectly preserved Rootkeeper tools that still function though their purpose remains obscure. Once, a full Rootkeeper body emerged, not calcified but somehow preserved in the original flesh—stone that bent like rubber, eyes of crystallized time that opened once, looked upward, and dissolved into the water-medium before any could comprehend what they'd seen.

The deepest any current being has descended is twelve thousand meters, where the last visible Roots fade into absolute darkness. The explorer—a specially adapted Millkeeper who compressed their mercury form to withstand the pressure—reported sounds from below: a rhythmic pulsing like a heartbeat in stone, voices in languages that predated speech, the whisper of something vast turning over in its sleep. They also brought back a single artifact: a seed of polished calcium no larger than a pearl, which, when exposed to the warmer waters above, began to sprout tendrils of the same synthetic kelp that fills the terraces.

The connection cannot be denied, though its nature remains mysterious. The Terrace Mills process what the Roots once grew, or failed to grow, or are still growing in timescales beyond comprehension. The synthetic kelp might be a simplified descendant of whatever the Rootkeepers cultivated, evolved to survive in the absence of its original purpose. The Mills themselves might be an autonomous system the Roots generated to maintain basic functions while they wait for conditions to change.

What those conditions might be, none can say. Temperature? Pressure? The return of some absent element or entity? The Roots keep their counsel in stone and light, pulsing their ancient messages through the water-medium while above them the Mills turn and process, the Millkeepers tend and optimize, and the whole system continues its purposeless purpose, a garden growing toward a harvest that may have already passed or may yet come, suspended between the abandoned depths and the endless aqueous sky.
