# The Scarp Cities

Where the Veli Basin ends — though "ends" suggests a boundary more definite than what actually exists — the ground remembers violence. Not recent violence. The basin has no room for anything recent. But violence nonetheless, written in stone that refused to lie flat, that buckled and rose and tore itself into walls of ancient fury. This is the Scarp, and it is where the basin's perfect erosion met something that would not yield gracefully.

The cliffs rise from the basin floor without warning, without the courtesy of foothills or gentle slopes. One step you walk on that endless powder, the next you face a wall that climbs until perspective fails. The rock here is different — darker, denser, shot through with veins of something that catches light and holds it longer than it should. The locals call it memory-stone, though they say this quietly, and never where the stone might hear.

The cities cling to these cliffs like lichen, if lichen could carve and build and dream. From the basin floor, you might miss them entirely — their makers learned long ago to build in the natural folds and shadows of the rock, to follow the logic of erosion rather than impose their own geometry. Only when the light strikes at certain angles do the straight lines become visible, the deliberate terraces, the bridges that span crevasses like spider silk frozen in stone.

They built upward because building outward meant building into the basin, and even they — proud as they were, clever as they were — knew better than to challenge that patient mouth. So they climbed. They carved. They hollowed out the Scarp from within, creating vertical cities that descended as far into the rock as they rose above it. The oldest levels go deep enough that the stone grows warm to the touch, deep enough that the air tastes of minerals that have never known sunlight.

The builders called themselves the Vethara, though this is a translation that loses more than it keeps. They were not from here — nothing that builds cities is ever truly from the basin. They came from somewhere else, somewhere the records carefully do not name, fleeing something the records carefully do not describe. What matters is that they came here, to the edge of erosion, and decided to stay.

They were not like the drifters in the basin. Where those beings had learned to think in geological time, the Vethara burned through their years like fire through paper. They built quickly, desperately, as if they could outrun time by moving fast enough. Their cities rose in decades, their towers in years, their bridges in months of fevered construction that left the stone scarred with tool marks that still show the urgency of their creation.

They were tall and narrow, built for climbing, with hands that could find purchase on holds too small for other species to see. Their bones were hollow like birds' bones, but reinforced with the same mineral that veins the Scarp — they drank it in solution, incorporated it into their bodies, made themselves part of the stone they carved. The oldest Vethara, the records say, rang like bells when struck.

They knew about the drifters. How could they not? On clear days, when the basin's slow winds stilled, they could see the patterns far below — the subtle darkening, the faint geometries of something almost alive. They tried to communicate, in the early years. They built great signal fires, carved messages into the cliff faces that could be read from miles away, even tried to match the drifters' pace, standing still for days at a time while relay teams watched for any response.

The drifters never acknowledged them. Perhaps they couldn't. Perhaps a century was too brief for a drifter to notice, a millennium too short to process as communication. Or perhaps they simply had nothing to say to creatures that insisted on building up instead of wearing down.

The Vethara built anyway. They carved their histories into the stone, though they must have known the basin would eventually claim even the Scarp. They built workshops where they grew crystals that sang in frequencies only they could hear. They built libraries where they stored knowledge in lattices of light trapped in specially prepared stone. They built observatories that looked not up at the stars but down at the basin, tracking the slow patterns of erosion like astronomers tracking the movement of planets.

And they built the Anchors.

No one knows what the Anchors were for — the Vethara's records grow strange when they speak of them, switching from their precise technical language to something that reads more like poetry or fever dreams. The Anchors were not physical structures, or not only physical structures. They were... processes, perhaps. Ceremonies. Machines made of ritual and stone and something else, something the records call "voluntary mineralisation" but never quite explain.

What is known: the Anchors were built at the deepest levels of the cities, where the stone reaches temperatures that should not exist so close to the surface. They required Vethara to operate them, but operation was a one-way journey. Those who entered the Anchor chambers never emerged, though the records insist, with increasing desperation in the later entries, that they were not dead. Simply... anchored.

The cities are empty now, but not abandoned. The Vethara are still there, in a sense. In the deep levels, where the Anchors hum with a vibration too low to hear but not too low to feel, sometimes the walls pulse with a warmth that has nothing to do with geology. Sometimes the crystal workshops still sing, though no one tends them. Sometimes, in the libraries, new knowledge appears in the light-lattices — observations of the basin's erosion that no one could have made, measurements of time in units no Vethara ever used while they still walked their vertical streets.

They found a different way to endure than the drifters. Not by becoming less, but by becoming more — more patient, more permanent, more like the stone they carved. They anchored themselves into the Scarp itself, trading their quick-burning years for something that could think in centuries, dream in millennia, plan in the deep time the basin demanded.

The cities still grow, slowly. New terraces appear over centuries, new bridges span crevasses that didn't exist when the Vethara had bodies. The growth follows no plan any individual mind could devise — it is the plan of the city itself, the stone-slow thoughts of all the Anchored working in a harmony too gradual for observation.

Sometimes travelers from other worlds come to study the cities, to photograph the empty streets and measure the crystal frequencies and catalogue the ever-growing libraries. They never stay long. The cities tolerate visitors, but there is something in the deep hum of the Anchors, in the warmth of the walls, in the way the stone seems to watch, that makes even the most dedicated researchers eager to leave.

They say if you go deep enough, past the levels where even the bravest archaeologists venture, past the heat that makes breathing difficult, down to where the Anchors pulse with their stone-slow heartbeat, you can still talk to the Vethara. They say the stone will speak, if you have years to listen to a single word. They say the Anchored are happy, in their way, or at least not unhappy — that they have found in the Scarp what they fled their unnamed home to find.

They say many things. But few go deep enough to verify.

The basin waits. It has already claimed the lowest terraces, filled them with its patient powder. In another million years, it will claim more. The Vethara know this — knew this when they still had lungs to breathe with. That's why they built upward. That's why they dug deep. That's why they Anchored.

The race between erosion and construction continues, slower than any sport, more patient than any war. The basin says: everything ends. The cities say: not yet.

Both are right.

Both have time to prove it.
