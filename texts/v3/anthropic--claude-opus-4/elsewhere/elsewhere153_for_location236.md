Beyond the convulsing peaks, where the planet's fever finally breaks, lies the Vitreous Plain—a sheet of black glass stretching to the horizon like a frozen sea. This is what becomes of the Birth Peaks' violent expulsions when they travel far enough from their source to cool completely, instantly, in the thin atmosphere.

The Plain is mirror-smooth in some places, so perfectly reflective that the dim star above appears to rest both in the sky and beneath your feet. Elsewhere, the rapid cooling has created fantastic formations: glass forests where spurts of molten material froze mid-fall into trees of obsidian, their branches delicate as spun sugar but sharp enough to slice through steel. Pressure ridges rise like frozen waves where cooling sheets collided and crumpled skyward. Scattered across this alien tundra are impact craters from the peaks' volcanic bombs, perfect circles of shattered glass radiating fracture patterns like frozen flowers.

The temperature here hovers just above absolute zero. What little atmosphere exists has precipitated out into crystalline snow—not water, but frozen methane and more exotic compounds that sublimate slowly in the starlight, creating low mists that pool in the glass valleys. The silence is absolute, broken only by the occasional crack of thermal expansion as the star's weak radiation creates minute temperature differentials.

This is where the Theoretical Anatomists make their study.

They arrived from somewhere else—not just another world, but another kind of space entirely. In their native dimension, information has mass and thought generates gravity. They perceived our universe first as an absence, a void in their reality that drew their attention precisely because it contained nothing they recognized as thought. When they found ways to manifest here, they came as explorers of pure physicality, archaeologists of matter itself.

An Anatomist exists as a controlled violation of local physics. Each one is roughly pyramidal, two meters tall, but their surfaces refuse to hold a consistent state—solid from one angle, gaseous from another, sometimes appearing to extend into more dimensions than three. They move by selectively altering the laws of physics in their immediate vicinity, creating pockets where friction doesn't exist or where gravity points sideways. Their passage leaves trails of faintly wrong space that takes hours to heal.

They came to the Vitreous Plain because it is the only place on this world stable enough for their work. The Birth Peaks' constant transformation makes sustained observation impossible, but here, in this frozen aftermath, they can conduct their studies in peace. What they study is the concept of "solid matter" itself—a phenomenon that doesn't exist in their home dimension.

The Anatomists have built—or perhaps precipitated—structures across the Plain that serve as their laboratories. These buildings appear to be made of materialized mathematics: walls of crystallized probability, roofs thatched with frozen light. Inside, they conduct experiments that would seem like madness to human scientists. They dissect rocks, not to understand their composition, but to understand the very idea of composition. They arrange glass fragments in patterns that somehow generate local gravitational fields. They speak to the obsidian forests in languages made of temperature differentials, trying to teach matter how to think.

Their greatest discovery has been the glass-captured sounds. The rapid cooling of the Vitreous Plain occasionally traps sound waves in the molecular structure of the glass—particularly sounds from the Birth Peaks carried on the thick atmospheric currents before they dissipate. The Anatomists have learned to read these frozen sounds like sheet music, playing them back by vibrating sections of the Plain at precise frequencies. Through this, they've discovered the songs of the peaks, and more importantly, the subsonic communications of the Membrane Shepherds.

This discovery has obsessed them. In their home dimension, consciousness and matter are indistinguishable—thought is the fundamental substance of reality. But here, they've found thinking beings made of matter, creatures that generate consciousness from purely physical processes. The Membrane Shepherds represent everything the Anatomists came to study: the impossible miracle of mind arising from mindless substance.

They've tried to make contact, but the temperature differential makes direct interaction impossible. The Shepherds cannot survive the Plain's killing cold, and the Anatomists' presence raises local temperatures enough to shatter the glass formations they depend on for stability. So they communicate through intermediaries—messages encoded in carefully shaped volcanic glass that they launch toward the peaks using localized gravity catapults. Whether the Shepherds understand these communications is unclear, but sometimes new glass formations appear on the Plain's edge that seem too regular to be natural, too purposeful to be accidental.

The Anatomists have begun the great work of creating a lexicon—a translation matrix between the physics of matter and the physics of thought. They carve equations into the glass that describe how consciousness might arise from silicon and heat. They build models of the Membrane Shepherds from frozen atmosphere and captured light, trying to understand how pattern and purpose can emerge from mere chemistry.

Some among them have begun to change. Extended exposure to this matter-dominated universe is affecting their fundamental nature. These altered Anatomists find themselves becoming more solid, more consistent, more bound by local physics. They've discovered they can taste the subtle differences in glass composition, feel the minute vibrations that presage distant eruptions. One has learned to sing in harmony with the frozen sounds, creating interference patterns that generate new, hybrid melodies never heard on this world before.

The unchanged Anatomists observe these transformations with something between horror and fascination. In their home dimension, to become material would be death—or worse, a cessation of existence so complete it lacks even a name. But these altered ones claim they've discovered something else: a new way of being that bridges matter and thought. They speak of eventually traveling to the Birth Peaks, of learning to withstand the heat, of making direct contact with the Membrane Shepherds to share what they've learned.

The Vitreous Plain serves as more than a laboratory—it's become a library of this world's history. Each layer of glass records the conditions of its formation: the temperature, the composition, the violence of its birth. The Anatomists read these layers like books, reconstructing the planet's evolution, predicting its future. They've identified cycles in the volcanic activity, patterns that suggest the world itself might possess a kind of metabolism, a rhythm of creation and quiescence that operates on geological timescales.

In the deepest parts of the Plain, where the glass is oldest and thickest, they've discovered anomalies. Shapes frozen in the obsidian that match neither the Membrane Shepherds' architecture nor their own mathematical constructs. These forms suggest other visitors, other minds that found this world in its youth and left their own marks upon it. The Anatomists excavate these sites with tools made of twisted space, careful not to disturb evidence that might explain who these predecessors were and why they left.

At night—when the dim star sets and only the amber glow from the distant peaks provides illumination—the Anatomists perform rituals that might be experiments or might be prayers. They arrange themselves in geometric patterns across the glass, their pyramidal forms pulsing in synchronization. In these moments, the boundary between their dimension and this one grows thin. Sometimes, for brief instants, thought becomes visible—silver threads connecting each Anatomist, ideas manifesting as geometric shapes that hover in the frigid air before dissolving.

They've built a beacon at the Plain's center, a spire of impossible angles that broadcasts into dimensions both physical and theoretical. It's a message, a call, a question sent out to any intelligence capable of receiving it. The message is simple, encoded in every possible mathematical language: "How does matter dream?"

The Vitreous Plain is their laboratory, their monastery, their prison. Some Anatomists speak of eventually returning home, carrying with them the impossible knowledge of material consciousness. Others believe they've already changed too much, that the journey back would destroy what they've become. A few radicals propose a third option: to dive deep into this universe's mysteries, to follow matter all the way down to its quantum foundations, to discover if thought and substance were ever truly separate at all.

As the Membrane Shepherds tend their growing peaks, nurturing the planet's violent becoming, the Theoretical Anatomists on the Vitreous Plain work to decode that becoming, to translate it into forms of knowledge that shouldn't exist. Between them—between the builders and the analyzers, the organic and the theoretical—this young world develops its first philosophy, its first attempt to understand itself.

The glass continues to accumulate, layer by layer, each sheet a frozen moment in the conversation between fire and cold, between matter and mind. And in their laboratories of crystallized mathematics, the Anatomists continue their impossible work, teaching themselves to think in atoms and energy, learning to dream in glass and stone.
