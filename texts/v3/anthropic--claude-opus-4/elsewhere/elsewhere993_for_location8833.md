The Drift

Three days' walk from where the tiles end—though walking is the wrong word for how one travels here—the ground softens to sand so fine it behaves like a slow liquid. This is the Drift, where nothing stays still and nothing stays long.

The sand is not uniform. It runs in ribbons of color—pearl white, ash grey, rose gold, midnight blue—each stream flowing at its own pace and temperature. The white sand is coolest, almost cold to the touch, and moves fastest. The blue crawls like thick honey and holds the day's heat long into the night. Where different streams meet, they create spiral patterns that last for minutes or hours before being pulled apart by the deeper currents.

There are no fixed landmarks here, but there are consistencies: the way certain color combinations create temporary hills that persist for days before dissolving, the predictable appearance of glass formations where lightning has struck the iron-rich red streams, the occasional emergence of ancient things from below—bits of shell, fragments of pottery unlike the tiles of the Expanse, bones of creatures that predate the lifting of the seabed.

The only structures are the windships—though they neither sail on wind nor water but on the sand itself. These vessels are built from hollow reeds that grow at the very edge of where the Drift meets the Woven Expanse, in that narrow margin where the ground can't decide what it wants to be. The reeds are massive things, some as thick as tree trunks, with chambers inside their stalks that hold air. When cut and sealed properly, they become buoyant on the flowing sand.

A windship is less a boat than a flexible raft. Long reed poles are lashed together with cured sinew, creating a platform that undulates with the sand currents. At the corners and center, taller reeds stand upright, connected by sheets of membranous material—not sails exactly, but collectors. These membranes, harvested from the swim bladders of sand rays that hunt in the deeper flows, gather static electricity from the constantly shifting grains. The charge builds until it can be released through copper threads woven into the lashings, creating brief repulsive fields that lift the vessel higher on the sand streams.

The static discharge creates a distinctive sound—a sharp crack followed by a tonal hum that changes pitch based on the mineral content of the sand below. Navigators learn to read these tones, understanding from the harmonics whether they're over deep channels or shallow bars, whether the sand below is stable or about to shift.

Weather in the Drift is a matter of sand, not sky. Sandstorms here don't come from above but from below—eruptions where deep currents hit buried obstacles and fountain upward in slow-motion geysers. These sand-spouts can last for hours, creating temporary towers of swirling color that gradually collapse and spread. The electromagnetic fields around these spouts are intense enough to light up the collection membranes from a distance, creating an aurora effect in the particle-filled air.

At night, the temperature differential between the various sand streams creates a different kind of movement. The cold white sand sinks while the warm dark sand rises, causing the entire landscape to turn over like a very slow ocean. This is when the phosphorescent particles become visible—tiny organisms that live between the sand grains, feeding on the static charges. They light up in waves following the electrical patterns, creating a bioluminescent map of the currents below.

Sound behaves strangely here. The flowing sand absorbs most frequencies, but certain tones—particularly the deep thrums produced by the reed hulls flexing—travel for miles through the medium. Messages can be sent by drumming on the deck of a windship in specific patterns. The sound moves faster through some sand colors than others, creating a delay effect where messages arrive as scattered rhythms that must be reassembled by experienced ears.

The edges where the Drift meets the Woven Expanse are constantly changing. Sometimes the sand swallows whole sections of tilework, burying the careful patterns under tons of flowing minerals. Other times it retreats, revealing previously hidden expanses of ceramic and stone. In these transition zones grow the sentinel plants—tall, solitary succulents with deep taproots that can find water even through shifting sand. Their presence marks safe channels, relatively stable paths that might last a season or two before being redirected by the Drift's whims.

The horizon here is never fixed. What looks like distant mountains might be temporary dunes that will flatten within hours. What seems like nearby valleys might be optical illusions created by the heat differentials between sand streams. The only constant is change, flow, the endless rearrangement of the landscape grain by grain.

This is a place that refuses permanence, that makes mockery of maps and plans. Yet patterns exist for those who learn to see them—the seasonal cycling of the deep currents, the predictable formation of glass sheets after storms, the reliable appearance of certain color combinations that signal navigable channels. It is a landscape that must be read in motion, understood not as terrain but as process.

Here, in the Drift, live the Readers of Temporal Sands.

They are a small people, compact and dense, their bodies adapted to resist the constant static charges that build in the flowing environment. Their hair grows in tight spirals that trap sand particles, creating a natural insulation. Over time, these trapped particles fuse into crystalline dreadlocks that chime softly when they move, each person carrying their own unique scale of notes based on their travel patterns and the minerals they've encountered.

The Readers dress in layered wrappings of membrane cloth, similar to what's used in the windship collectors but treated with oils that make them conductive rather than resistant. This allows them to bleed off excess charge gradually rather than in painful shocks. The wrappings are never fully removed—new layers are added as old ones wear away, creating a personal archaeological record. The oldest Readers wear decades of cloth, the lower layers compressed into something like flexible armor.

Their feet are their most remarkable adaptation. Broad and paddle-like, with webbed toes, they've evolved to read the sand through touch. The soles are covered in thousands of pressure-sensitive nodes that can detect minute changes in grain size, temperature, and flow rate. A Reader can stand still and know, through their feet alone, what minerals dominate the streams below, how fast they're moving, whether the patterns suggest stability or imminent shift.

They live on the windships in small family groups, never more than seven to a vessel. Too many bodies in one place creates electromagnetic interference that makes navigation dangerous. Their ships are homes, workshops, and libraries all at once. The reed chambers that provide buoyancy also serve as storage for their collections—vials of different colored sands, each labeled with location and time data in a complex system of knots and beads.

The Readers are the historians of a historyless place. They collect sand samples obsessively, understanding that each grain carries information—where it came from, what stones it weathered from, what organisms have touched it. They've developed a form of divination using these samples, pouring measured amounts onto vibrating membranes and reading the patterns that form. Through this practice, they claim to see the past movements of the Drift, even to predict future flows.

Their primary trade with the Chroniclers of the Woven Expanse is time-keeping. The Readers understand duration in ways the tile-workers cannot. By studying the rate of sand burial and exposure at the boundaries, they maintain the only reliable calendar in this region. They know when certain stone deposits will be accessible, when the thermal vents will shift, when rare minerals will surface from the deep flows.

The exchange happens at the margin zones. Readers anchor their windships where the sand meets the tiles, using long poles made from the same hollow reeds. Chroniclers emerge from their patterns to trade: water and succulent fibers for sand samples and temporal readings. The communication is delicate—the Chroniclers' subsonic vibrations don't travel well through sand, and the Readers' crystalline hair creates interference with the tile acoustics. They've developed a shared language of gestures, performed slowly and repeatedly until understanding dawns.

Children among the Readers begin their education by learning to sleep through the motion. The constant shifting of the sand creates a form of motion sickness in the unadapted. Young Readers spend years developing their sand-legs, learning to move with the flows rather than against them. They play games involving static charges, seeing who can build the biggest discharge before releasing it, who can create the most complex patterns in phosphorescent sand.

The Readers age differently than the Chroniclers. Where the tile-workers become more still and stone-like, the Readers become more fluid. The eldest move like the sand itself, their gestures flowing and continuous, never quite stopping. Their crystalline hair grows heavier and more complex, until the weight of it keeps them seated most of the time, serving as navigators and sand-diviners while younger family members handle the active sailing.

Death in the Drift is a dissipation. Bodies are wrapped in their accumulated membrane layers and given to the deepest, slowest-moving streams—the indigo sand that some say flows all the way to the planet's core. The wrapped forms sink gradually, the sand accepting them grain by grain, until nothing remains but the crystalline hair, which eventually surfaces elsewhere, to be found and collected and added to the family windship as memorial wind chimes.

The Readers know every temporary landmark, every seasonal formation, every reliable pattern in the chaos. They carry in their minds and bodies maps of a mapless place, directions for the directionless, time for the timeless. They are the memory of the Drift, written not in stone or tile but in gathered grains and lived experience, in the accumulated charge of constant motion.

Where the Chroniclers seek to create permanence through patient accumulation, the Readers find continuity in constant change. They build nothing lasting, but they remember everything—every color-stream's rhythm, every sand-spout's location, every brief moment when the flowing world arranges itself into something almost like meaning before dissolving again into motion.
