**The Burnt Lands**

Three days' walk through the moss-choked passes, where the green finally begins to thin and the stone grows darker, the world changes its mind. The transition is not gradual but shocking—a single ridge crossed, and suddenly the air tastes of char and iron instead of moisture and growth. This is where the earth tried a different experiment.

The Burnt Lands sprawl across a vast basin, though "burnt" is a misnomer born from the first travelers who couldn't find other words for what they saw. Nothing burns here—there isn't enough that could catch fire. Instead, the land seethes with a different kind of transformation, where stone learns not to crumble but to flow, where the earth's blood runs close to the surface in veins of heat and strange minerals that stain everything they touch.

The ground here is a patchwork of crusts and pools, of glass-smooth obsidian flows and rough pumice fields that crunch underfoot like snow. Colors that don't exist in the green valleys bloom across the rocks—violent oranges from iron deposits, electric blues from copper, sick yellows from sulfur that crystallizes in delicate flowers around the breathing holes where the earth exhales its hot, toxic breath. Steam vents punch through the crust at irregular intervals, some constant, others erupting without warning in geysers that paint the surrounding rocks with deposits of minerals in patterns like frozen screams.

No moss grows here. No ferns unfurl. The only vegetation consists of strange, crystalline formations that might be plants or might be minerals organizing themselves in lifelike ways. They cluster around the warm vents, feeding on chemicals that would poison anything from the green valleys. These crystal-plants grow in brittle spirals, their surfaces reflecting the harsh, unfiltered sunlight that pounds down through air too dry to hold clouds. When the wind blows—and it always blows here—they chime against each other with sounds like breaking glass.

Water exists, but it has gone wrong. Pools of it collect in depressions, but these are not the clear, cold puddles of the moss lands. Here, water turns colors it shouldn't—milky white with dissolved minerals, rust-red with iron, blue-green with copper sulfates. Some pools bubble constantly, too hot to touch. Others look placid but will strip flesh from bone with their acidity. The safe water sources are known only to those who live here, marked by subtle arrangements of colored stones that would be invisible to valley dwellers' eyes.

The settlement—if it can be called that—spreads across this hostile landscape like a scattered thought. There is no density here, no clustering for warmth and moisture. Instead, structures stand alone or in small groups, each positioned to take advantage of specific resources: a warm vent, a mineral seep, a rare pool of drinkable water. The buildings are low and broad, built from volcanic stone fitted without mortar—the heat cycles would crack any binding agent. Their roofs are covered in obsidian sheets that reflect the brutal sun, creating structures that shimmer like mirages in the heat distortion.

Paths between buildings are marked by cairns of balanced rocks, each tower a navigation point in a landscape where everything looks the same until you learn to read the subtle differences in stone color and crystal growth. The paths themselves are not worn by feet—the stone here doesn't wear that way—but are cleared strips where the more dangerous formations have been removed or marked. Walking here requires constant attention; a misplaced step might break through a thin crust into boiling mud or onto stones hot enough to melt shoe leather.

The people of the Burnt Lands move like dancers through their dangerous home. Where the valley dwellers are compact and careful, these people are tall and loose-limbed, built for covering distance with efficiency. Their skin has darkened to protect against the unfiltered sun, taking on undertones of the minerals they work with—a faint copper sheen here, a dusting like golden mica there. They wear their hair long and twisted into complex arrangements that serve as protection against the sun and wind, often incorporating small obsidian beads that click softly as they move.

Their clothing is minimal but ingenious—woven from fibers extracted from the crystal-plants, creating a fabric that reflects heat and resists the chemical burns from accidental splashes. They wear tall boots with soles made from compressed pumice, providing insulation from hot ground while remaining light enough for the long walks between settlements. Everyone carries water in containers made from hollowed-out mineral formations, lined with a resin extracted from the crystal-plants that makes them impervious to the acids and minerals that would eat through any other material.

They speak in clicks and whistles that carry across the wind-scoured spaces, a language evolved for distance communication. Their vocal cords have adapted to the dry air, producing sounds that the valley dwellers' humid throats couldn't form. They have sixteen different words for types of heat and twenty-three for mineral colors. They name their children after chemical reactions—Sulfur-Bloom, Iron-Song, Crystal-Whistle.

Their knowledge is written in stone arrangements across the landscape, libraries of balanced rocks that tell stories of mineral locations, water safety, seasonal vent changes. Reading these arrangements requires understanding not just the stones' positions but their compositions, their colors, the way light reflects from their surfaces at different times of day. Master readers can extract generations of accumulated knowledge from a single complex cairn, while apprentices spend years learning to construct even simple warning markers.

The rhythm of life here follows the sun's arc rather than moisture cycles. They wake before dawn to work during the cool hours, retreat during the brutal midday heat, emerge again in the evening when long shadows make navigation easier. Night work is common and preferred—the stars are brilliant in the dry air, and many minerals fluoresce under moonlight, making them easier to locate.

They are miners and chemists, extracting the earth's strange gifts with knowledge passed down through generations. They know which vents produce the finest sulfur crystals, where to find the veins of rare metals that the valley dwellers covet for their tools, how to harvest the crystal-plants without destroying their regeneration points. The work is dangerous—burns are common, lungs scarred from toxic fumes, fingers lost to acid pools—but they approach it with a fatalism born from living where the earth itself is hostile.

Their trade with the green valleys is essential to both communities. Caravans leave monthly, carrying loads of minerals, metals, and crystallized salts that the moss-dwellers need for their phosphorescent gardens, their jade tools, their fungal medicines. The journey is treacherous, requiring knowledge of the seasonal passages, the temporary water sources, the safe camping spots where both environments briefly compromise into something survivable.

The traders from the Burnt Lands are recognizable long before they arrive in the valleys—tall figures wrapped in reflective cloth, their pack animals (hardy creatures adapted to chemical burns and heat, with hooves of natural obsidian) laden with containers that chime with mineral music. They bring not just goods but news from the other settlements scattered across the Burnt Lands, stories of new vent formations, warnings of areas gone dangerous, discoveries of untapped resources.

In return, they carry back the concentrated vegetation the moss-dwellers prepare specifically for them—dried foods that provide nutrients absent from their mineral-heavy diet, medicines for treating chemical burns, the moss-fiber ropes that are the only organic material that survives their environment when treated with crystal-plant resin. Most importantly, they bring back the jade knives, which are the only tools that can properly shape the crystal formations without shattering them.

The relationship between the two peoples is complex, built on mutual need but limited understanding. Valley dwellers see the Burnt Lands people as reckless, choosing to live in unnecessary danger. The mineral workers view the moss-dwellers as soft, hiding from the sun's truth in their green caves. Young people from both communities sometimes attempt to relocate to the other, drawn by curiosity or love, but few successfully adapt. The environments are too different, the physical adaptations too specialized.

Yet the trade continues, as it has for generations beyond counting. The paths between the two worlds are worn not by feet but by tradition, by the accumulated knowledge of where to cross, when to travel, how to survive the transition. Seasonal trading posts exist at the boundaries—neutral structures built from both volcanic stone and moss-covered granite, where the two environments meet in an uneasy truce.

The Burnt Lands people have their own mysteries. They speak of places deeper in the mineral desert where the earth's blood pools on the surface in lakes of molten stone, where crystal-plants grow tall as trees and sing in harmonious frequencies that can shatter rock or heal bones, depending on the listener's intent. They tell of the Glass City, abandoned generations ago when a massive vent opened beneath it, now a forest of melted and reformed structures that create maze-like passages filled with mineral treasures and deadly gas pockets.

Their children grow up fearless and observant, learning to read the land's moods from infancy. They play games of balance on warm stones, compete to see who can identify minerals by taste (a dangerous game, carefully supervised), build elaborate cairns that tell adventure stories. By adolescence, they can navigate by the stars, predict geyser eruptions by the sound of underground water, and harvest crystal-plants without protection.

They celebrate the solstices with ceremonies around the largest vents, throwing offerings of rare minerals into the earth's breathing mouths. They believe the land is not hostile but honest, that it offers its treasures openly to those who learn its language. Their dead are placed in mineral pools that slowly encrust the bodies in crystal formations, creating monuments that grow more beautiful with each passing year, monuments that sing in the wind with voices transformed but not silenced.

The two worlds—the green and the burnt—exist in symbiosis, each providing what the other cannot produce, each representing a different answer to the question of how to live on this unfinished earth. The traders who move between them are the blood vessels of this larger organism, carrying nutrients and knowledge and stories that bind the disparate human adaptations into something approaching a civilization.

In the space between these worlds, in the transition zones where moss meets mineral, strange hybrid environments flourish. Here, crystal-plants grow in moss beds, creating formations of unprecedented beauty. Mineral springs feed unique organisms that belong to neither world fully. These boundaries are sacred to both peoples, spaces where their dead are sometimes brought to rest, where young lovers from different worlds meet in secret, where the earth itself seems to be experimenting with compromise.

The future remains unwritten in both landscapes. The green valleys continue their slow burial under vegetation, while the Burnt Lands crack open with new vents, new mineral formations, new dangers and opportunities. The people of both worlds adapt and evolve, their bodies and cultures shaped by their chosen homes, yet bound together by the necessities of survival in a world still deciding what it wants to become.
