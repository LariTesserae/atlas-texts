The Upper Reaches

Above the breathing chamber, accessed through a service ladder that few remember exists, the Upper Reaches wait in perpetual twilight. Here, the building shows its bones: naked I-beams stretch across vast emptiness, the original warehouse structure before the boilers were installed, before the pipes colonized the space below. The ceiling rises forty feet to peaked metal roofing that rings like a bell in the rain.

This is where heat goes to die. All the warmth generated below pools here, creating a microclimate of constant summer beneath the roof. The temperature differential between day and night makes the metal expand and contract with sounds like breaking bells, like distant thunder, like the building clearing its throat. Condensation forms on the underside of the roof and rains down in unpredictable patterns, creating an ever-shifting map of wet and dry zones across the dusty floor.

The dust itself tells stories. Decades of it, layered in sedimentary deposits: construction dust from the original build, coal ash from when the boilers burned different fuel, pollen that drifts in through the louvers, the fine gray powder that flakes constantly from the deteriorating insulation. In some places it lies inches thick, soft as snow. In others, the intermittent indoor rain has cemented it into something like adobe, cracked into polygonal patterns.

The pigeons found this place before the Tender found its garden. They nest in the rafters by the thousands, their calls echoing in the metallic acoustics. Generations of them, dynasties of birds who have never seen sky except through the louvers that provide ventilation. They've evolved slightly—their feathers carry an oily sheen that repels the constant moisture, their eyes adapted to the dim light. They feed on the grain that spills from torn bags in the storage section, on the insects that thrive in the heat, on the seeds that blow in from outside.

But the pigeons share their kingdom. Bats roost in the darker corners, emerging at dusk to hunt the moths attracted to the few functioning lights. Swallows build mud nests against the warm metal, raising three broods a year in the endless summer. The guano from all these residents filters down through gaps in the flooring, enriching the gardens below. The Tender knows this, plans for it, depends on it.

Between the I-beams stretch catwalks—some original, some added later, some unofficial constructions of board and wire that workers built to access difficult spaces. They create a three-dimensional maze through the air, a city of platforms and bridges that the maintenance crews call the Skyways. Some paths lead nowhere, ending at walls that were added during renovations. Others connect in loops and spirals that make navigation an art form.

The center of the Upper Reaches holds the Abandoned Project. Nobody remembers what it was supposed to be—some kind of additional processing equipment, perhaps, or an office space, or storage platforms. The construction was never finished. Half-built walls of corrugated metal define spaces without purpose. Staircases climb to floors that were never installed. Doorframes stand free in space, thresholds to nowhere. The pigeons nest in these structures preferentially, as if recognizing them as apartments designed for their use.

In the northwest corner, where the roof peak meets the outer wall, a section of louvers has been replaced with windows—thick industrial glass clouded with age. The light that filters through is green-gold, tinted by the algae that grows on the outside surface. This is the brightest spot in the Upper Reaches, and here someone long ago set up a makeshift observatory: a chair bolted to a platform, a telescope on a tripod, notebooks wrapped in plastic against the moisture. The notebooks are filled with observations—not of stars but of the city visible through the windows. Sketches of buildings being constructed and demolished, notes on weather patterns, the movement of crowds documented over years.

The telescope still works. Through it, you can see the city that surrounds Processing Station Seven—tower blocks and waste grounds, highways and canals, the distant glitter of downtown. But more importantly, you can see the other processing stations: Six to the north, Eight to the south, Five across the canal. Each one crowned with its own forest of ventilation towers, its own cloud of steam. A archipelago of industrial islands in the urban sea.

The heat creates mirages in the air of the Upper Reaches. Distant catwalks shimmer and duplicate. The pigeons flying below seem to swim through thick atmosphere. Sometimes the mirages show things that aren't there—or aren't there anymore. Catwalks that collapsed years ago still appear at certain angles. Workers see themselves crossing bridges they haven't reached yet. The shadows of birds that died decades past still wheel through the dusty light.

Sound behaves strangely here. A whisper on one platform can be heard clearly on another fifty feet away, while shouting might be swallowed entirely. The pigeons have learned to use these acoustic properties, sending calls along specific paths. The maintenance crews use them too, developing a simple code of taps and calls that carry better than radio in the interference-heavy environment.

This is where things rise: heat, dreams, the lightest particles of dust, the spirits of broken machines. The Upper Reaches are a place of accumulation and dispersal, where the heavy work of the hall below transforms into something lighter, stranger, more diffuse. The Tender comes here too, climbing impossible angles to tend to needs that have nothing to do with pipes or plants. It maintains the balance between above and below, ensuring that what rises also falls, that what accumulates also flows.

The shift changes below are marked by the sudden rush of heat as doors open and close. The pigeons have learned these rhythms, know when the quietest times will come. They descend in waves during the deep night hours, carpeting the empty Skyways, cooing in frequencies that harmonize with the building's own voice.

The Upper Reaches are the breathing chamber's attic, its memory, its connection to the sky. They hold what the hall below cannot: emptiness, altitude, the possibility of flight. Together they form a complete ecosystem, ground and air, root and wing, the mechanical lung and the spaces where its exhalations go to cool and condense and rain back down.

Here, at the junction between inside and outside, between earth and sky, the building reveals itself as a living thing—breathing, metabolizing, dreaming in the language of temperature differentials and air pressure, in the script of pigeon tracks in dust, in the slow revolution of heat rising and falling, forever and again.
