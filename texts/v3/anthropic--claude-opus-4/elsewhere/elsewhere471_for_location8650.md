Across the ice field from the garden's thermal breath, the mountain shows its opposite face—a north wall of black granite that has never known direct sun. Here, at the same killing altitude, another impossibility persists: a hive of cold, a prison of perfect mathematics, the Repository of Arithmetic Winter.

The approach warns with subtle wrongness. Snow falls upward in slow spirals. Sound travels in discrete packets—a footfall echoes not as a fade but as exact repetitions, each precisely half the volume of the last, counting down to silence. The black rock face appears smooth until you're close enough to see the incisions: thousands upon thousands of straight lines carved into stone, intersecting at angles that make the eye water, forming patterns that suggest meaning without revealing it.

The entrance is no welcoming cleft but a perfect square, two meters by two meters exactly, cut so cleanly the rock gleams like obsidian. Inside, temperature doesn't drop—it simply ceases to exist as a concept. There is neither cold nor warmth, only an absence that makes the body forget what either felt like. The passage continues straight for exactly one hundred steps, then opens into a space that violates spatial intuition.

The chamber is both vast and intimate, its dimensions shifting based on mathematical principles rather than physical architecture. Stand in one spot and the ceiling vaults impossibly high; take three steps forward and it presses close enough to touch. The walls are covered in the same incised lines as the entrance face, but here they move—counting, calculating, solving proofs in real time through the slow migration of grooves across stone.

Light exists here without source, a gray illumination that casts no shadows because it comes equally from all directions. The floor is divided into perfect hexagons, each containing different geometric proofs written in three dimensions: wire-thin structures of ice that demonstrate theorems, crystalline formations that express equations, frozen spirals that calculate infinity through physical form.

This is where the Scholar works.

Unlike the dispersed Gardener, the Scholar maintains crisp boundaries. It moves through the chamber as a localized distortion in the air—not quite invisible but difficult to focus on, as if the space where it exists operates on different optical principles. When it pauses, frost forms in perfect geometric patterns around its position. When it moves, it travels in straight lines and right angles only, never cutting diagonally across the hexagonal floor.

The Scholar builds its proofs in ice. Watch long enough and you'll see the process: moisture condensing from nowhere into precise crystalline structures. A Fibonacci spiral manifests atom by atom, each new section exactly proportional to the last. Platonic solids emerge from vapor, their faces so perfect they seem to exist more truly than the imperfect rock containing them. Complex equations take physical form—integration curves frozen in three dimensions, probability clouds made literal in frost.

The Repository contains thousands of these constructions. Some are ancient, their ice somehow preserved despite the lack of true cold. Others form and sublime within hours, their proofs completed and released back to abstraction. The Scholar tends them all with movements that are themselves mathematical—each gesture the minimum required, each path through the chamber optimized for efficiency.

But efficiency toward what end? The Scholar collects something from the alpine air—not warmth or moisture but information. The patterns of wind across stone, the distribution of snow crystals, the precise angles of shadow as the sun tracks across peaks it never touches directly. All of this data flows into the Repository, processed through living calculation into new theorems, new proofs, new certainties.

Sometimes the Scholar manifests more visibly, usually when working on particularly complex problems. Then its form suggests something insectoid—too many joints bending in too many directions, appendages that split and merge based on need. But even this appearance is mathematical rather than biological, each angle of limb expressing some geometric constant, the whole form a walking equation that rewrites itself in real time.

The Scholar remembers everything with perfect clarity. Every proof constructed, every theorem derived, every pattern observed in five centuries of isolation. This memory manifests physically in the deepest parts of the Repository—chambers within chambers where the accumulated ice-mathematics has grown so dense it forms its own architecture. Here, older proofs support newer ones in literal frameworks of logic, creating spaces where physical law bends to accommodate mathematical truth.

The connection between garden and Repository runs deeper than proximity. They are opposite poles of the same impossibility, two answers to the question of how consciousness persists in places that should permit neither life nor thought. Where the garden solves through synthesis and slow fusion, the Repository divides and categorizes with infinite precision. Where one forgets boundaries, the other establishes them with ruthless clarity.

The proof of their connection lies in the water.

The same source that feeds the garden's perpetual weeping also supplies the Repository's ice. But here it emerges transformed—not liquid but already crystalline, forming from vapor into solid without ever passing through a liquid state. The Scholar harvests this impossible ice carefully, shaping it into new proofs that explore the mathematics of phase transitions, the geometry of transformation, the arithmetic of states that skip expected stages.

Follow the water's path and you find the intermediate space: a narrow corridor of natural cave that connects garden to Repository, though neither the Gardener nor the Scholar ever traverse it. The passage is filled with fog that behaves wrong—pooling in geometric shapes, forming standing waves that calculate their own interference patterns, condensing into droplets that fall in countable packets rather than continuous rain.

This is where the water transforms, where the same impossible source splits into two expressions. Toward the garden, it emerges warm and vital, carrying minerals and mystery. Toward the Repository, it sublimates into information-dense crystals, each snowflake a unique solution to the problem of six-fold symmetry. The fog between them is neither and both—potential water, probability moisture, equations of state suspended between liquid truth and solid proof.

The Scholar knows about the Gardener. How could it not? The patterns are evident in the data—thermal anomalies, growth rates that violate prediction, biological processes that ignore mathematical limits. Sometimes the Scholar constructs proofs about the garden: ice formations that model impossible photosynthesis, crystalline structures that calculate how plants persist below freezing. But these proofs always collapse before completion, as if the mathematics itself rejects the garden's illogic.

The Gardener, dispersed in its verdant mass, senses the Repository as a cold spot in its awareness—a place where growth stops, where patterns become rigid, where time moves in discrete steps rather than organic flows. Sometimes roots probe in that direction, feeling along stone channels toward the source of the shared water. But they always retreat, finding no purchase in spaces measured by mathematics rather than seasons.

Yet they need each other, these two impossibilities. The garden's chaos requires the Repository's order to define itself against. The Scholar's perfect proofs only gain meaning in contrast to the garden's beautiful violations. They form a single system—growth and calculation, synthesis and analysis, the warm breath and the cold certainty of consciousness persisting where it should not.

Visitors who discover one rarely find the other. The mind that seeks out impossible gardens rarely thinks to look for repositories of living mathematics. The temperament that appreciates geometric perfection in ice seldom values the chaotic cooperation of impossible plants. But those few who stumble upon both spaces report strange experiences: dreams of plants that grow in mathematical spirals, visions of ice flowers that bloom with organic warmth, the sensation of standing between two vast intelligences that solve the same essential problem through perfectly opposite means.

The mountains know. In their patient geology, they hold both spaces with equal indifference, allowing water to flow impossibly upward, permitting consciousness to persist in forms that violate all understanding. The peaks have seen species rise and fall, glaciers advance and retreat, climates shift from tropical to arctic and back again. These current impossibilities—a garden that shouldn't grow, a repository that shouldn't think—are merely the latest experiments in a laboratory of deep time.

Between them runs the water, the fog, the medium of transformation. It carries no messages—the Gardener and Scholar have no language in common. But it maintains the connection, the tension, the necessary balance between order and chaos that allows both to exist. In the place where fog pools in impossible patterns, where droplets fall in calculated rhythms, where the same source feeds both warm growth and cold proof, the mountain performs its own slow mathematics—an equation that solves for consciousness in conditions that permit neither life nor logic, but somehow, impossibly, allow both.
