The Plateau of Constant Thunder

The Plateau of Constant Thunder spreads five hundred kilometers to the east, where the ancient volcanic chain breaks into mesa country. Here the stone is newer, harder—black basalt that still remembers its molten birth. The plateau rises two thousand meters above the surrounding badlands, its flat top perpetually wrapped in storm clouds that never dissipate, never move on. Lightning strikes the ground once every seven seconds, has done so for as long as anyone can remember. The crashes roll together into a single, endless roar.

The lightning has done strange things to the stone. Where it strikes repeatedly, the basalt transforms into a crystalline material the locals call stormglass—translucent black shot through with frozen fractals of electricity. These formations grow like coral, reaching upward in branching spires that attract more strikes, growing taller, more complex. Some have achieved heights of forty meters, swaying in the constant wind, singing in harmonics just below human hearing.

Between the stormglass forests, the settlements sprawl—low buildings of thick stone, their roofs covered in copper mesh that channels the strikes safely to ground. The mesh glows constantly with coronal discharge, painting everything in blue-white fire. The people here build in circles, leaving the center of each settlement open for the massive capacitor towers that store the endless electricity. The towers hum with power, their surfaces crawling with Jacob's ladders, feeding current to the underground factories where that power is transformed into more tangible goods.

The factories here work in reverse of those in the Cistern Gardens. Instead of using water power for delicate work, they harness raw electricity for transformation. Furnaces that reach temperatures impossible elsewhere. Electrolysis chambers that break down minerals into their component elements. Synthesis workshops where new materials are born from precisely controlled lightning strikes. They make things here that cannot be made anywhere else—metals with memories, ceramics that conduct thought, glass that stores time.

The workers wear suits of rubber and copper, moving like deep-sea divers through the charged air. Their tools are non-conductive ceramic, their movements careful and grounded. They've learned to feel the build-up of charge in their teeth, the taste of copper warning them to seek shelter. During the worst storms—when the seven-second rhythm breaks down into constant barrage—they retreat to the deep bunkers and wait.

But waiting here is productive. The underground spaces are vast, carved by generations of patient work. Libraries of electrostatic recordings, where thoughts are stored in crystalline matrices. Workshops where the stormglass is shaped and tuned. Gardens that grow in the electrical fields—plants that feed on current instead of light, their leaves metallic, their fruits charged with potential.

The fauna of the plateau has adapted to the constant storm. Moths with wings of copper foil flutter between strikes, using the electrical discharge for navigation. Snakes made of what looks like braided cable slither through the rocks, their bodies acting as living grounds. The rabbits here have fur that stands perpetually on end, crackling with static, their eyes glowing with accumulated charge. They bound between the stormglass spires in patterns that seem to predict where the next strike will fall.

Most unsettling are the storm shepherds—whether they're animals or something else remains unclear. They stand three meters tall on legs too thin for their bodies, their forms wrapped in constant coronas of electricity. They tend to the stormglass forests, guiding the growth with gestures that redirect lightning strikes. Sometimes they're solid, leaving footprints in the charged dust. Sometimes they're not, becoming patterns of electrical discharge that move with purpose through the air. The locals leave offerings of copper wire and distilled water at forest edges. The shepherds never acknowledge these gifts, but they always vanish by morning.

The connection between the Plateau and the Cistern Gardens runs deeper than geography. The storm-readers of the plateau have instruments that detect patterns in the lightning—patterns that match the fractal designs on the salamanders' skin, hundreds of kilometers away. When the salamanders in the Cistern congregate in certain formations, storms on the plateau intensify. When the plateau's factories achieve certain harmonics in their production, the water in the Cistern's lake glows brighter.

Traders make the journey between the two settlements, though it takes weeks through the badlands. They carry stormglass shaped into precise forms that affect water flow, and return with distilled essences that stabilize electrical fields. But more than goods flow between the two places. The traders speak of dreams that start in one settlement and finish in the other, of children born in the Cistern with eyes that reflect lightning, of storm shepherds that occasionally appear in the caldera's mists, tending to machines as if they were stormglass trees.

The plateau-dwellers know time differently than those in the Cistern. Where the gardeners move slowly, the storm-workers move in bursts—frantic activity between strikes, perfect stillness as the charge builds. Their language has seventeen different words for the pause between lightning and thunder, each describing a different quality of expectation. They age in surges, children growing to adulthood in seasons, then remaining unchanged for decades.

At the plateau's edge, where the constant storm finally breaks, rain falls upward—the electrical charge so intense it reverses gravity for the droplets. This rain evaporates before it can reach the clouds, creating a perpetual cycle, a wall of inverted weather that marks the boundary of the possible. Sometimes, on very clear days, the people of the Cistern can see this phenomenon from their highest terraces—a distant curtain of impossible water hanging in the eastern sky, reminding them that their own strangeness is not unique, that the world has pockets of wonder connected by threads too subtle to fully grasp.

The storm never stops. The water never stops flowing. And between these two constants, carried by traders and dreams and the underground tremors of ancient volcanic connections, a conversation continues—slow as stone, quick as lightning, strange as the world itself.
