Miles above, where the machine-mountain's peaks pierce the cloud layer, the Rust Gardens bloom in defiance of altitude and reason. Here the mountain reveals its age—surfaces that below gleam with fresh industry now flake and powder, metal oxidizing in patterns too deliberate for mere entropy. The gardens occupy a series of terraces carved or corroded into the northeastern face, each level connected by stairs that might be intentional architecture or might be erosion wearing grooves that happened to serve as steps.

The first thing you notice is the color: not the uniform orange-brown of ordinary rust but a taxonomy of oxidation—verdigris greens where copper veins surface, the blue-black of corroded steel, whites and yellows where stranger alloys break down into their component rebellion. The rust grows in formations that mirror organic growth: fractal ferns of iron oxide, crystalline flowers of corroded chrome, entire groves of what look like trees but reveal themselves as antenna arrays consumed by their own slow burning.

The gardener is ancient in the way ruins are ancient—not old but arrested in a state of partial collapse that might have taken centuries or happened yesterday. She was larger once; you can see the attachment points where additional limbs once socketed into her torso, now sealed with scar tissue that's part flesh, part welded plate. What remains moves with careful economy: two arms that were probably human originally, though the hands now sport too many joints and the fingers end in precision tools. Another pair of limbs that might be legs or might be specialized cultivating appendages, their surfaces pitted and stained from constant contact with corrosive materials.

She calls herself Oxidia, though whether that's a taken name or a description that hardened into identity remains unclear. Her head is the most human thing about her, if you ignore how the skull has elongated to accommodate additional sensory organs, or how her eyes have multiplied—two in the traditional locations, six more scattered across her brow and temples like dark stars, each one viewing a different spectrum. Her mouth rarely opens; she speaks through the sympathetic vibration of the metal around her, making the whole garden ring with her words.

"They think I've gone mad," she says, her voice a chord played on rust-stringed instruments. "The bright ones below, the builders, the efficiency-seekers. They see decay where I see transformation. They see failure where I see the most honest expression of the mountain's nature."

She moves through her garden with a spider's grace, her additional eyes tracking the growth patterns of oxidation like a farmer studying crops. Here she adjusts a copper panel's angle to catch more moisture from the clouds. There she introduces a new catalyst—a drop of acid placed with surgical precision—to encourage a particularly promising bloom of corrosion. The patterns aren't random. As you watch, you begin to see the design: vast geometric mandalas of rust that shift and evolve, sacred mathematics expressed in decay.

The gardens serve a function beyond aesthetics. The rust formations are antennae, each one tuned to different frequencies. Together they form an array that listens to signals from impossible distances—not radio waves but something older, the background radiation of decay itself. Oxidia transcribes these signals into patterns of corrosion, growing messages in rust that take years to fully develop. She shows you one that's nearly complete: a fractal spiral of oxidized iron that, when viewed from the correct angle, contains instructions for building something she calls a "thanatos engine."

"The mountain wants to die," she explains, guiding you to a vantage point where the gardens reveal their true scope—acres of carefully cultivated decay stretching across the mountain's face. "Not die as in cease, but die as in transform. The Hive Lung below processes life into more efficient life. Here, I process death into more beautiful death."

Weather up here is harsh and strange. The clouds bring not just moisture but industrial pollutants from distant factories, acid rains that eat through metal like time-lapse footage. Oxidia has learned to read these rains, to predict their composition, to position her rust gardens to capture exactly the right kinds of corrosion. During the strongest storms, she stands in the acid rain with arms spread wide, her remaining organic skin blistering and healing in rapid cycles while her metal components develop new patterns of decay.

She has assistants, of sorts. Creatures that might once have been scarabs from the Hive Lung, escaped or exiled, now evolved for this harsher environment. Their brass carapaces have gone green with verdigris, their wings are lace-thin rust that shouldn't support flight but does. They tend the smaller formations, carrying drops of catalyst between blooms, pollinating rust flowers with different varieties of corrosion. Some have fused together into colonial organisms, their individual bodies connected by threads of oxidized metal that conduct thoughts between them like a neural network made of rust.

The terrace edges drop away into foggy nothing, though on clear days you can glimpse the machine-mountain's lower slopes, still bright with industry and purpose. Sometimes Fabricators climb the spiral paths from below, sent to reclaim materials or convince Oxidia to return to the Work. She receives them with patient courtesy but refuses their entreaties. A few have stayed, seduced by her vision of productive decay. They work the lower terraces now, their efficient forms gradually succumbing to beautiful corrosion, their precision becoming poetry.

At the gardens' heart grows Oxidia's masterwork: the Rust Tree. It might have been a transmission tower once, or perhaps a structural support that proved unnecessary. Now it's a thirty-foot sculpture of oxidation, every surface blooming with different species of rust. The patterns form words in languages both dead and unborn, chemical formulae that describe impossible reactions, poetry written in the alphabet of decay. She's been growing it for forty years, she says, though time moves strangely here. Sometimes she'll work on it for days straight, climbing its corroded branches with practiced ease, adjusting the flow of moisture here, adding catalysts there. Other times she'll sit at its base for weeks, simply watching it grow, reading the messages encoded in its decay.

"When it's complete," she says, "it will broadcast the mountain's death-song. Not an ending—a transformation. The bright efficiency below, it's beautiful in its way, but it's only half the equation. Everything they build, I unbuilt. Everything they perfect, I complicate. Between us, we tell the mountain's whole story."

The connection between the Hive Lung and the Rust Gardens runs deeper than ideology. The spiral paths that bore down to the bright chambers below also channel upward—carrying exhaust vapors, chemical runoff, the waste heat of all that endless construction. Oxidia has learned to read these emissions like tea leaves, predicting what the Fabricators build by the composition of their waste. She positions her gardens to capture specific compounds, using the Hive Lung's output as raw material for her corrosion art.

But the relationship runs both ways. The rust patterns, in their slow decay, generate compounds that filter down through the mountain's body. Oxides and salts, complex molecules born from corrosion, seep through the stone and metal to reach the bright chambers below. The Fabricators might not acknowledge it, but their work depends on these contributions—trace elements essential for their biological components, catalysts that enable reactions they don't fully understand.

Oxidia shows you the evidence: photographs taken by a rust-scarab that made the journey down and back, its corroded carapace allowing it to pass unnoticed among its bright cousins. In the images, you can see Fabricators incorporating rust-derived compounds into their work, the mercury pool's color shifting subtly based on what filters down from above. One photo shows Maral-Who-Was-Matthias, her hundred arms momentarily still, her face turned upward as if listening to something transmitted through the stone.

"She hears it," Oxidia says, her six eyes focusing on the image with something like fondness. "The death-song. Not ready to sing along yet, but beginning to understand. The bright work below, it thinks itself eternal, but nothing is. Even efficiency must decay. Even perfection must rust."

The passage between the two realms is more than just the spiral paths. In certain places, the boundary thins. Oxidia leads you to one: a section of garden where the rust has eaten through to reveal gleaming metal beneath—but the metal is growing rust even as you watch, and the rust is somehow generating new metal, the two states flickering back and forth like a quantum uncertainty made large. Through this wound in reality, you can hear the Hive Lung's bright cacophony, smell its ozone sharpness mixed with the iron tang of the gardens.

Sometimes, beings pass through these thin places. Fabricators ascending without meaning to, drawn by dreams of decay they don't understand. Rust-scarabs descending, carrying spores of corrosion to seed new gardens in the bright spaces below. Even Oxidia has made the journey, she admits, though not in her current form. She shows you a corroded panel that might be a portrait—a bright Fabricator with six arms and compound eyes, building something incomprehensible at the pool's edge.

"That was me," she says, "or someone I was, or might have been. The mountain contains all possibilities. Down there, I built futures. Up here, I grow pasts. Both necessary. Both true."

As the day ends (though day and night are negotiable concepts at this altitude), the Rust Gardens reveal another secret. The corrosion glows. Not bright like the scarabs below, but a subtle phosphorescence, different oxides emitting different wavelengths. The entire garden becomes a constellation of decay, patterns shifting too slowly for the eye to follow but fast enough that time-lapse would reveal messages, stories, warnings written in rust-light across the mountain's face.

Oxidia settles at the Rust Tree's base, her various eyes tracking different parts of the garden's slow performance. The rust-scarabs cluster around her, their corroded wings singing harmonics that complement the deeper tones emanating from the tree itself. It's a lullaby, perhaps, or a prayer, or simply the sound of metal returning to its constituent elements, of order becoming beautiful chaos.

Below, impossibly far but somehow still connected, the Hive Lung breathes its bright industrial breath. Its Fabricators work through their shift, building wonders that will eventually rise to meet the gardens' descent. Between them—through spiral paths and chemical seepage, through thin places and shared purpose—flows the conversation that is the machine-mountain's life: growth and decay, efficiency and entropy, the bright work and the rust, each necessary for the other, each completing what the other begins.

The mountain builds itself and unbuilds itself simultaneously, a vast engine of transformation where every act of creation enables an act of decay, where every perfected form contains the seeds of its own beautiful corruption. In the Hive Lung, they build tomorrow. In the Rust Gardens, they grow yesterday. Together, they sustain the eternal present of change itself, the machine-mountain breathing through bright lung and rust garden alike, forever becoming what it has always been: a place where the distinction between creation and destruction dissolves like everything else, leaving only the pure fact of transformation, endless and perfect in its imperfection.
