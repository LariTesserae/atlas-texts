Far above the gleaming metropolis, where the last fingers of sunlight fade into perpetual twilight, floats the Driftwood Archipelago—a collection of massive kelp islands tethered by ancient chains to the seafloor below. These living islands, each the size of a city district, bob and sway with the deep currents, their surfaces breaking just above the thermocline where warm and cold waters meet.

The Drifters, as they call themselves, are the descendants of those who refused to follow humanity's great descent into the engineered depths. They live in woven homes nestled within the giant kelp holdfasts, structures that flex and breathe with the ocean's rhythm. Their city is no city at all, but a floating forest of brown and gold, where bioluminescent jellies provide gentle light and schools of surface fish dart between the fronds like birds.

While the deep-dwellers below harvest their sustenance from sterile factories, the Drifters tend wild gardens. They cultivate rare species of floating seaweed, coax shellfish to grow on the dangling roots of their homes, and practice the old art of current-fishing, reading the ocean's moods to know where the schools will run. Their clothing is not manufactured but grown—living cloaks of algae that photosynthesize in the filtered sunlight, changing color with the seasons.

The relationship between the two worlds is complex. The deep city's jets of cooled steam create upwelling currents that bring nutrients to the Driftwood Archipelago, an unintended gift that makes their floating gardens flourish. In return, the Drifters serve as the deep city's memory, maintaining oral histories and star-navigation techniques that the bottom-dwellers have forgotten in their artificial environment. 

Once each month, during the spring tides, Drifter traders descend on kelp-fiber lines, their bodies adapted to pressure changes through generations of practice rather than genetic modification. They bring exotic species, fermented foods that cannot be synthesized, and most precious of all—stories of the sun, which still holds mythical significance to those who live in eternal electric twilight.

The deep-dwellers regard the Drifters with a mixture of fascination and pity, seeing them as primitives who refused progress. The Drifters view their cousins below with a similar ambivalence—amazed by their technology but saddened by their separation from the living ocean. Both societies tell cautionary tales of the other to their children, even as they depend on each other in ways neither fully admits.

In the spaces between—in the middling depths where pressure-suited deep-dwellers sometimes ascend and hardy Drifters sometimes dive—floating market platforms serve as neutral ground. Here, in the blue-black twilight, the two branches of humanity meet, trade, argue, fall in love, and slowly, perhaps, knit themselves back into a single people adapted to all the ocean's depths.
