The Siphon Fields

Three hundred kilometers spinward from where the last Mechanics tend their fused machinery, the Canopy Continent breaks. Not gradually—the transition is absolute, a cliff edge where the wooden world simply stops. Beyond this precipice, the Siphon Fields begin.

The ground here is glass. Not metaphorically—actual glass, smooth and dark, extending to every horizon in rolling dunes and sharp-edged plains. The glass is deep, perhaps kilometers thick, and within its mass hang suspended the shadows of something that might once have been a city. Buildings remain as hollow spaces in the vitrified earth, their forms preserved as absences—negative architecture frozen at the moment of transformation. Streets exist as tunnels of air running through solid glass. The shapes of trees, caught mid-sway, create delicate voids that branch and spread like circulatory systems in the dark mass.

Above this glass landscape, the Siphons stand.

They rise from the glass at irregular intervals—towering structures of metal and ceramic that pierce upward for kilometers. Each is unique in its configuration but identical in purpose. At their bases, where they meet the glass, massive root systems of copper and fiber optic cable spread outward and downward, penetrating the vitrified ground in patterns that mirror the wooden root-floor of the Canopy Continent. These roots glow faintly from within, pulsing with the passage of something that is not quite electricity.

The Siphons themselves are chimeras of technology. Their lower sections are brutally industrial—riveted steel and concrete, access ladders and maintenance platforms suggesting their builders expected to service them. Higher up, the construction becomes more sophisticated. Ceramic panels arranged in fractal patterns. Crystalline windows that look in rather than out. Components that seem grown rather than assembled, their purposes obscure.

At their peaks, each Siphon blooms into a collection array. These take many forms—some are dishes aimed at empty sky, others are forests of antenna, still others unfold into geometric patterns that hurt to perceive directly. All of them pull at something invisible, drawing it down through their structures and into the root networks below. The air around active Siphons shimmers with heat distortion, though the structures themselves remain cold to the touch.

The sound here is different from the industrial drone of the Canopy. It's a pulling sound—a hollow whistle that rises and falls with no predictable pattern. Sometimes it harmonizes across multiple Siphons, creating acoustic phenomena that manifest as visible distortions in the air. The sound seems to come from the glass itself, resonating up through the Siphons and out into the space above them.

The sky over the Siphon Fields is wrong. It's too bright, for one thing—a harsh, colorless light that casts no shadows and seems to come from everywhere at once. There are no clouds, no weather, just an endless expanse of luminous nothing that makes distance impossible to judge. Sometimes, in that bright void, shapes move. They're too far away to resolve clearly—might be debris, might be structures, might be something else entirely. The Siphons' collection arrays track these movements with mechanical precision.

Between the Siphons, the glass landscape tells its frozen story. Here, a building's void shows furniture suspended in mid-fall, each chair and desk a perfect bubble of air in the dark mass. There, the outline of a person remains—not bones or flesh, but simply a human-shaped space where the glass couldn't form. Thousands of these human voids populate the preserved city-shadows, caught in their final moments. Some reach toward each other. Others stand alone, their postures suggesting they saw it coming.

In certain areas, the glass has cracked. These fracture zones spread from impact points—perhaps where Siphons fell or where something struck from above. The cracks create caves and crevices that descend into darkness, and from these depths comes a different kind of light. It pulses in patterns unrelated to the Siphons' rhythm, a deep blue radiance that suggests the glass sea has depths unexplored.

The Siphons affect each other. When one pulls particularly hard at whatever it harvests from the bright sky, others nearby resonate. Their root networks, where they come close underground, create interference patterns visible through the glass—moiré effects that shift and dance as the energy passes between them. Some roots have grown together, creating hybrid Siphons that share resources and synchronize their collection cycles.

The technology becomes more alien with proximity. What look like access panels from a distance reveal themselves as surfaces covered in symbols that shift when observed. Maintenance platforms hold equipment that maintains itself—tools that move when no one watches, diagnostic systems that run checks on the humans who approach them. The boundary between observer and observed breaks down near the Siphons. They pull at more than energy.

At the border where the Siphon Fields meet the Canopy Continent, the interaction creates a unique zone. The wooden cliff-face weeps a constant stream of sap that, when it touches the glass, crystallizes into amber formations. These crystals grow in both directions—down into the glass and up the wooden wall—creating a margin of frozen honey-colored light. The plant's root systems have tried to penetrate the glass here, creating patterns of organic matter fossilized at the moment of contact.

The Siphons never stop their work. Day and night are meaningless under the bright empty sky—time here is measured by the pulse of the roots, the cycle of the collection arrays, the slow growth of cracks in the glass. Whatever they're gathering from above, it flows continuously into the depths, feeding something or going somewhere that the frozen city-shadows below can no longer reveal.

The relationship between the Canopy Continent and the Siphon Fields is not simple. They are the same world but opposite principles—one consuming all matter into biological unity, the other preserving a moment of transformation while endlessly drawing down something from above. The wooden world generates. The glass world collects. Between them, along that amber crystalline margin, exchanges occur that follow no clear pattern.

Sometimes, on the glass plains between the Siphons, patterns appear. They might be frost, or mineral deposits, or something else entirely. They form overnight in perfect geometric designs around the bases of the towers, extending outward in mandalas that can span kilometers. By the next day, they're gone, leaving only the faintest trace in the glass—a difference in refraction that suggests the pattern went deep, that what appears on the surface is only the visible portion of something much larger occurring below.

The distance between the Canopy and the Fields is absolute and yet permeable. No Mechanic has ever walked on glass—the transition would unmake them. No resident of the Fields has touched wood—the plant's chemistry would be poison. Yet trade occurs. Information flows. At the crystalline margin, things are left and things are taken, though by whom and why remains unclear.

The Siphons pull their bright harvest down into darkness. The plant pushes its warm growth up into haze. Between them, along the seam where two impossibilities meet, the remnants of the world that was lie preserved in glass and consumed by wood, transformed but not destroyed, waiting in their different ways for whatever comes next.

This is the same world, but it is not one world. It is a binary system of consumption and collection, growth and preservation, sound and silence, dark and bright. The Canopy Continent and the Siphon Fields exist in necessary opposition, two answers to the same catastrophe, two responses to whatever broke the world that came before. They do not touch except where they must. They do not communicate except through the patterns that appear and disappear, the crystalline margin that grows between them, the things that sometimes cross from one realm to the other and are never seen again.

The industrial drone meets the hollow whistle somewhere in the space between, creating harmonics that belong to neither. This too is part of the design, though whose design and to what end, neither the wooden spaces nor the glass plains can say.
