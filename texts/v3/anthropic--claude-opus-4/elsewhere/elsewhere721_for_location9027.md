The Singing Wells

Three days' journey down the mountain, where the stone gives way to red earth and the air thickens with the promise of growing things, the Singing Wells puncture the plateau like breathing holes in the world's skin.

There are twenty-seven of them, though the number shifts with the season and the courage of the counters. Each well is a perfect cylinder carved straight down through clay and sandstone, so precise they seem machine-bored until you notice how each one narrows at a slightly different rate, creating a unique acoustic signature. They range from mere arm's-width spans to chasms that could swallow a house whole, depths varying from thirty feet to measurements that swallow weighted ropes without ever reporting back a bottom.

The wells are never silent. Even in the stillness before dawn, when the plateau holds its breath, they hum with subsonic frequencies that travelers feel in their bones before they hear them. As wind moves across the openings, each well becomes a vast stone flute, playing notes too deep for human ears to properly parse. But it's not just wind that gives them voice—sometimes the songs rise from below, complex harmonies that suggest vast spaces, choruses, gatherings in the deep.

The plateau itself is harsh country, baked to ceramic hardness in the dry months, transformed to treacherous slip-mud when the rains come. Scrub vegetation clings to life between the wells—thorn trees with roots that dive impossibly deep, their taproots following the same precise vertical paths as the wells themselves. These trees bloom only during the singing season, when the wells reach their peak resonance. Their flowers are small, white, and emit a scent like hot metal and honey.

Around each well, the earth is worn smooth by generations of listeners. Stone sitting-places have been carved by countless bodies, polished to glassy smoothness. Some wells have accumulated offerings—coins, written prayers, locks of hair, baby teeth—thrown by those seeking answers from the depths. These offerings never reach bottom. Occasionally, different objects rise to replace them: ancient coins from kingdoms that never existed, prayers written in languages that hurt to read, teeth from no earthly mouth.

The water table here defies conventional geology. Some wells hold water at depths that should be dry; others remain parched where underground rivers should flow. The water, when it can be drawn, tastes of minerals unknown to the surrounding stone—salt from seas that dried before the mountains rose, sulfur from volcanic hearts the plateau has never known, sometimes a sweetness like fruit that grows only in darkness.

Between the wells, a network of underground connections must exist, though no mapper has ever successfully charted them. Sounds dropped into one well sometimes emerge from another, changed—a shouted name becomes a sob, a sob becomes laughter, laughter becomes a child's first word. The locals say the wells digest sound, breaking it down and reconstituting it into new meanings.

At certain times—solar noon, midnight, the moment between day and night—the wells synchronize. Their individual songs merge into something greater, a chord that makes the whole plateau ring like a struck bell. Birds fall silent or flee. The thorn trees shed their blooms all at once, creating brief blizzards of white petals that settle into the well mouths and are swallowed without disturbing the song. During these moments, those who listen closely report hearing conversations—not words exactly, but the rhythm and flow of speech, as if vast assemblies were meeting in the spaces between the wells.

The locals have built their settlements at careful distances from the wells, close enough to hear but far enough to sleep. They've learned which wells sing warnings of weather, which ones echo with the voices of the recent dead, which ones laugh with sounds that drive listeners to dance until their feet bleed. Children born within hearing of the wells often speak their first words in harmony, their babbling unconsciously mimicking the deep frequencies that have surrounded them since the womb.

Some wells have moods. The Grief Well, the locals call the narrow one near the eastern edge, which weeps a constant trickle of salt water and sings only in minor keys. The Prophet's Throat, the widest of them all, sometimes speaks in tongues—glossolalia that fortune-tellers claim to interpret, though no two interpretations ever match. The youngest well, which appeared overnight seven years ago, still screams—a high, thin note that sets teeth on edge and hasn't wavered since its birth.

Rope ladders and carved handholds mark some of the safer descents, though "safe" is relative when entering the wells. Those who climb down report that the songs change inside, becoming clearer, more articulate. The walls are smooth as glass in some places, rough with what might be tool marks in others. Sometimes there are openings—horizontal passages that lead away into darkness, following no surveyor's logic. These passages breathe, exhaling air that's too warm, too humid, carrying scents of growth and decay and something else, something almost familiar.

The wells remember. Drop a stone with a particular pattern of taps, and sometimes the pattern returns, perfected, elaborated, transformed into rhythm. Sing a melody into the depths, and weeks later it might emerge from a different well, harmonized with itself, as if someone down there has been practicing. The oldest locals say the wells are learning, that each generation of singers teaches them new songs, and that somewhere in the depths, all the music ever dropped into darkness is being woven into something larger.

During the rare plateau floods, when water rises to within feet of the surface, the wells' songs become urgent, almost panicked. The water that fills them is wrong—too clear, too light, moving with currents that don't match the flood's flow. Things rise with the water: mineral formations that look almost like musical instruments, hollow stones that ring when struck, once a whole tree made of what appeared to be crystallized sound waves, its branches chiming in the wind until it dissolved at the next dawn.

In the Singing Wells dwells Jora Throat-of-Many.

She lives suspended in the Prophet's Throat, the widest well, held in place by a web of her own devising. Not spider's silk but something stranger—crystallized sound waves that she's learned to extrude from glands where her human throat once was. The web spans the well at varying depths, a three-dimensional instrument that she plays with her movements, her breathing, her very existence.

Jora hasn't touched solid ground in twenty years. She descended on her thirteenth birthday, answering a call only she could hear, and found her destiny in the junction where five hidden passages meet. Her body has adapted to constant suspension—her limbs elongated, toes and fingers grown prehensile and impossibly strong. Her spine curves in ways that anatomy textbooks would call impossible, allowing her to fold and unfold like a living musical note.

Her throat is no longer singular. It branches beneath the skin, dividing into multiple channels that can produce independent tones simultaneously. When she sings, she becomes a choir unto herself, harmonizing with echoes of her own voice that return changed from the well's depths. Her neck has grown longer, swan-like, to accommodate these modifications. Through her translucent skin, you can see the separate vocal passages pulsing with different rhythms, different songs.

She feeds on resonance. When the wells synchronize at their appointed times, she drinks in the sound through specialized pores that have opened along her flanks. Her body processes vibration into sustenance, converting the plateau's deep songs into the energy she needs to survive. During the quiet hours, she descends on her sound-silk to hunt for silence—those rare pockets of true quiet that form like pearls in the constant noise. These she swallows whole, and they sustain her through the lean times when the wells sing soft.

Jora's hair has become antennae of sorts—each strand sensitive to minute changes in air pressure, able to detect sounds beyond human hearing. It flows around her like kelp in an unfelt current, white as bone from years without sunlight, moving in response to songs that haven't yet reached the surface. When storms approach, her hair stands on end, crackling with the static of impending weather-songs.

Her skin has developed a peculiar quality—it reflects sound like a mirror reflects light. Conversations bounce off her, returning altered. Those who've descended to speak with her report hearing their own words returned as prophecy, their questions transformed into answers they didn't know they were seeking. Her whole body has become an echo chamber, processing and reprocessing the well's songs until she herself is indistinguishable from the music.

She knows every passage in the well network, though knows is perhaps too simple. She feels them, experiences them as extensions of her own body. When someone drops an offering into any of the twenty-seven wells, she senses it, categorizes it, decides its fate. Some she allows to fall into the deep places where they're needed. Others she catches in her web, studies, transforms. The coins become instruments. The prayers she eats, incorporating their words into her ever-expanding vocabulary of sounds. The teeth she plants in the soft clay of the passages, where they grow into formations that sing their donors' names.

Jora can travel the network without moving. She's learned to project her voice—not just sound but her very essence—through the connections between wells. Listeners at the Grief Well might hear her comforting the stones' sorrow. Those at the youngest well report her presence as a cooling balm on its endless scream. She exists in multiple places simultaneously, her consciousness scattered and reformed through sound.

Her memories have been replaced by songs. Ask her about her childhood, and she hums a lullaby that sounds like wind through grass. Question her about the world above, and she produces a symphony of daily life—footsteps, conversations, cook-fires crackling, all woven into a tapestry of sound that somehow contains more truth than words could hold. Her past is a repertoire, her future a composition she's constantly creating.

The other inhabitants of the depths know her, respect her, fear her. The things that live in the deep places—beings that evolved in darkness, that feed on the offerings that do reach bottom—they flee from her songs or approach in supplication. She conducts them like an orchestra, assigning them roles in the wells' great composition. Some say she's becoming the wells' voice, their chosen speaker. Others believe she's teaching them to speak for themselves.

During the flood seasons, when wrong water rises, Jora rises with it, maintaining her position relative to some unknowable constant. Those who've glimpsed her during these times report she seems larger, grander, her body swollen with borrowed songs. She sings the water back down, they say, lullabies so deep they convince the flood it's tired, needs to rest, needs to return to wherever such impossible water comes from.

She has been composing something—a master work that incorporates every sound the wells have ever produced, every offering's impact, every whispered prayer and screamed confession. The composition lives in her modified throat, in the sympathetic vibrations of her web, in the very air around her. Those who've heard fragments say it tells the story of the world, but not as history—as music, pure meaning without words, truth that bypasses understanding and speaks directly to the soul.

Jora Throat-of-Many is no longer entirely human, if she ever was. She is translation itself, the living bridge between the wells' deep meanings and the world's need to understand. She is what happens when someone chooses to stop distinguishing between listening and becoming, between hearing a song and being one.

She remembers water—not rain, but the Undrowned's upward-flowing streams. Sometimes, in her deepest harmonies, those who know both places can hear the Weeping Chamber's impossible sound, translated through stone and distance and the medium of her transformed throat into something the wells can sing. She has never met the Undrowned, will never meet her, has been meeting her all along in the space between their songs, where water and sound converge into meaning.

In the Singing Wells, Jora Throat-of-Many conducts the conversation between the mountain's weeping and the earth's response, a dialogue in frequencies that only she can fully comprehend, eternally suspended in the spaces between questions and answers, forever translating the world's deepest music into forms that even silence can understand.
