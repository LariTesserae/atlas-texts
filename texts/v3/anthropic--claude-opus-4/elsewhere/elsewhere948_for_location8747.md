The High Farms

Above the drowned city, where the weight of forty meters of water gives way to sun and storm, the atolls rise in a broken ring. These are not true coral atolls built by patient polyps, but the ancient peaks of the same hills that once looked down upon the city when it knew air. Now they stand as scattered islands, none larger than a few hectares, their slopes descending through the clear water to the streets below.

The Farmers who work these heights are cousins to the Architects, though the kinship spans so many generations that the exact relationship has dissolved into myth. Where the Architects grew pale and flowing in the eternal twilight of the deeps, the Farmers have become creatures of the surface, their skins dark and thick against the sun, their bodies compact and powerful from fighting wind and wave.

They stand no taller than the Architects but built broader, with six limbs instead of eight—four for gripping and climbing, two specialized for the delicate work of tending their aerial crops. Their skin changes color not for communication but for temperature regulation, darkening to near-black under the noon sun, lightening to grey-green in the cool of dawn. Salt glands dot their bodies like small volcanoes, constantly weeping away the excess minerals from their spray-soaked existence.

The Farmers have turned their islands into vertical gardens. Every surface that can hold soil does—terraces cut into the slopes, pockets carved into the rock, elaborate frameworks of woven kelp stipes that support hanging gardens of salt-adapted plants. They grow a careful selection: the purple-leafed purslane that thrives on spray, the succulent samphire whose seeds they grind for flour, the tough sea beans that store fresh water in their joints, the rare air-kelp that somehow grows in soil but mimics its underwater cousins in form and function.

Their relationship with fresh water defines their days. Each island has its cisterns—natural formations improved over generations or entirely Farmer-carved spaces sealed with secreted resins. The Farmers read the sky like the Architects read stone, knowing by the color of distant clouds and the behavior of certain seabirds when rain will come. Before storms, they deploy intricate systems of channels and collectors, every surface designed to direct water to storage.

During the dry seasons, they descend. This is when the bond between the two peoples reveals itself. In the early morning, before the sun makes the surface unbearable for the Architects, shapes rise from the deep city. They breach in the shadows of the atolls, their pale bodies vulnerable in air but briefly capable of surviving it. The Farmers meet them in the shallows, and an exchange begins that has continued for longer than either group can remember.

The Architects bring offerings from the deeps: rare minerals scraped from the city's stones, concentrated nutrients extracted from the kelp forests, pearls of hardened mucus that contain complex chemical messages about the health of the waters below. Most precious are the memory stones—small objects carved with patterns that encode generations of knowledge about engineering, water flow, structural harmonics.

In return, the Farmers provide what the sea cannot: seeds that will become the next generation of managed kelp, carefully selected for their genetic properties. Dried fruits whose concentrated sugars provide energy the filter-feeding Architects cannot obtain from their diet. Most importantly, they share the solar salts—crystals they create through patient evaporation of seawater in specialized pools, each crystal type prepared with different trace elements that the Architects need but cannot source from depth.

The trading happens in silence broken only by water movement. The Farmers' sign language, adapted for use both above and below water, serves as the bridge between worlds. Their hands flash in quick patterns—so different from the Architects' slow bioluminescence but mutually comprehensible in the meeting zones. Negotiations can take hours, not from disagreement but from the care each side takes to ensure the other's needs are fully understood.

The Farmers live with children in a way the Architects do not. Where the city dwellers reproduce rarely and release their young to find their own way, the Farmers raise their offspring in complex family groups. Young Farmers spend their first years entirely in the interior pools of the atolls, slowly adapting to air exposure. They learn the plants by taste and touch, memorizing the subtle differences between varieties of the same species, understanding which thrive in full sun and which need the protection of an overhang.

Their tools are living things. Certain mollusks, bred for generations, produce shells of specific shapes—scoops for moving soil, sharp edges for harvesting, points for making planting holes. Domesticated crabs serve as mobile assistants, their claws modified through selective breeding to hold water or carry seeds. Even some of the seabirds have entered into partnership with the Farmers, trading nesting sites for help in finding fish.

The Farmers' architecture serves the plants first, themselves second. Their shelters are woven into the growing systems—sleeping chambers that double as root cellars, work spaces whose walls are living gardens. They build with what the sea provides: dried kelp stipes stronger than wood, secreted resins that harden into waterproof coatings, stones carried up from the shallows that still bear the marks of Architect selection.

They know the city below as a felt presence rather than a seen thing. On the clearest days, when the water becomes glass-like, they can make out the kelp canopy and sometimes the geometries of the streets. But mostly they know it through its effects: the way certain currents bring up nutrients that feed the fish, the patches of especially vigorous surface kelp that mark deep-water springs, the places where Architects' work below creates upwellings that affect their islands' micro-climates.

The Farmers track time by growth and harvest, by the cycling of plants through their generations. They maintain seed libraries in the driest parts of their islands, collections that preserve not just species but specific adaptations—this purslane that survived the Year of Three Typhoons, that samphire that produced during the Long Drought. They understand their work as parallel to the Architects'—where those below preserve the bones of the city, the Farmers preserve the genetic knowledge of survival at the margin between sea and sky.

Their greatest art is the Symphony of Tides, performed during the spring's highest waters. As the sea rises to nearly claim their lowest gardens, all the Farmers of all the atolls coordinate their movements. They adjust sluices and channels to create precise patterns of flow between islands. The water, channeled and released in careful timing, creates sounds—gurgles, splashes, the ringing of specially placed stones—that carry across the surface and deep into the water below. The Architects rise as close to the surface as they dare, reading in the water's movement messages of health, abundance, need, and promise for the year ahead.

When Farmers die, their bodies are given to both realms. Part is buried in the highest gardens, enriching the soil where the rarest plants grow. Part is carried to the meeting waters and released with ceremony to sink to the city below. The Architects incorporate these remains into their structures—not as construction material but as memory-markers, points of connection between the sunlit heights and the twilight deeps.

The two peoples need each other in ways that go beyond simple trade. The Farmers' management of surface growth affects the amount and quality of organic matter that filters down to feed the city's ecosystem. The Architects' maintenance of the structures below creates the hydraulic conditions that bring nutrients up to the surface waters. They are separated by forty meters of water but bound in a single system, each tending their portion of a world transformed by the sea's rise, each preserving in their own way the knowledge of how to live when the waters change everything.

In the evening, as the sun drops toward the horizon and the Farmers secure their gardens against the night winds, some will pause at the edges of their islands. They look down through the darkening water, perhaps catching a glimpse of bioluminescent communication far below. They flash their own lights in response—not messages, for the language barrier is too great at such distance, but acknowledgment. We are here, the lights say across the meters of separation. We continue. The work goes on.
