High above the Drowned Bells, where the last light still dances gold across the waves, the Lighthouse of Ashkar's Reach stands on its narrow spine of black basalt—the sole survivor of the cataclysm that took Korathene below.

The lighthouse rises like a accusation from the sea, its stone the opposite of Korathene's pale limestone—volcanic rock so dark it seems to swallow light during the day, yet burns with an inner fire when the sun sets. The tower lists seven degrees from true vertical, a permanent record of the violence that drowned a city but could not topple this solitary sentinel. Barnacles beard its lower reaches, and storm petrels nest in its crevices, but above the high tide line, the stone remains strangely pristine, as if something about its construction repels the sea's patient erosion.

Within the lighthouse lives its keeper—or perhaps it would be truer to say the lighthouse keeps her.

Morwyn the Chronicler came to this post forty-three years ago, when she still walked on two legs of equal length and her eyes matched in color. Now her left leg stretches three inches longer than her right—a compensation for the lighthouse's eternal tilt. Her left eye has gone milk-white from staring into the beacon's flame, while her right has developed an uncanny ability to pierce darkness, its pupil starred with gold flecks that wheel like distant galaxies when she focuses on the horizon.

She is never alone and always lonely. The lighthouse whispers.

The whispers come from the walls themselves—generations of keepers who've worn grooves in the stone with their fingers, tracing the same paths day after day until the rock itself learned their routines. Morwyn hears them at dawn: the careful footsteps of Previous Ashkar climbing the spiral stairs, though he died a century past; the scritch-scratch of Keeper Yolen's pen, though no ink marks the stone; the humming of Mad Elara's songs, wordless melodies that seem to pull at something deep in Morwyn's chest.

The beacon chamber crowns the lighthouse—a room of lenses and mirrors that fracture light into impossible angles. The flame at its heart isn't fire as the surface world knows it, but something pulled from the earth's deep veins. It burns without fuel, a captured piece of the same volcanic fury that birthed the tower's black stone. The light it casts doesn't merely illuminate—it reveals. Ships see it from impossible distances, but more importantly, it shows them things that mere brightness cannot: the bones of whales swimming beneath their hulls, the true depth of the water, the shadows of storms still brewing beyond the horizon.

Morwyn tends this flame with rituals she only half understands. Each dawn, she must sing to it—not any song, but specific tones that resonate in her chest cavity, frequencies that make her ribs ache. At noon, she feeds it—not wood or oil, but chips of volcanic glass that she must harvest from the lighthouse's own stone, prying them loose with tools worn smooth by centuries of use. At dusk, she reads to it from the Chronicle.

The Chronicle fills the lighthouse's middle chamber, carved directly into the curving walls in scripts that span millennia. The oldest marks are mere scratches—tallies of ships passed, storms weathered. But as the spiral of text climbs the walls, it grows more complex. Maps of currents that no longer exist. Astronomical observations that track stars' positions across centuries. And increasingly, records of things seen in the water below.

"The fifty-third day of watching: They build still. New structures rise from the northern district—geometries that hurt to map."

"The seventieth year of my service: Lights below that answer our beacon. They pulse in patterns. I have begun to document the sequences."

"They surfaced today at the spring tide. Only for moments. Their faces—gods help me, I knew her. My own great-grandmother, who drowned when the city fell. She looked at me and her mouth moved but water came out instead of words."

Morwyn adds her own observations nightly, carving them with a stylus of meteoric iron that never dulls. Her entries grow stranger as the years pass:

"The water below the lighthouse is clearer than it should be. I can see the city's temple from the gallery on calm days. The carved water still holds its shape."

"Communication attempt forty-seven: I lowered the brass bell on its chain. They rang it from below—but the sound that came was not brass on brass. It was like stones singing."

"My left eye no longer sees the present. Through it, I watch the city as it was—dry streets, human faces. My right sees now. When I close them both, I see what it will become."

The lighthouse has shaped her as surely as the tides shape stone. Her spine curves to match the spiral stairs. Her fingers have worn grooves in the gallery railing exactly where Previous Ashkar's fingers rested. She sleeps in the lamp room, curled around the base of the beacon like a cat around a hearth, because the whispers are quieter there, drowned out by the flame's subsonic hum.

But it's the connection between above and below that consumes her waking hours.

On the spring tides, when the moon pulls the ocean thin, Morwyn performs the Exchange. She lowers the basket—an ancient thing of woven kelp and copper wire that regenerates its own damage—down through the hundred and eighty feet of water to the city below. Sometimes the Tidewrights fill it with gifts: tools of carved water frozen solid, messages in their click-speech recorded in patterns of holes punched through nacre-thin shells, once a mirror of polished stone that shows not reflections but refracted possibilities.

In return, Morwyn sends down surface things: fragments of driftwood from distant shores, vials of rainwater unmixed with salt, pressed flowers that retain their color despite the depths, and always, always, pages from the Chronicle—careful transcriptions on sharkskin vellum of entries from generations past, as if she's slowly feeding them their own history.

The basket takes hours to descend and return. Morwyn watches its progress through the lighthouse's great lens, which can focus down as well as out. Through it, she's seen the Tidewrights waiting at the temple steps, their elongated forms graceful in the water. She's watched them receive her gifts with ceremonies that seem part reverence, part curiosity. Once, during a particularly clear day, she saw one of them—a Thresher, by the look of their movements—attempt to climb the rope. They made it halfway before something (pressure? fear? impossibility?) forced them back down.

The true purpose of these exchanges remains opaque. Are they trade? Worship? Correspondence? Morwyn suspects they're something else entirely—a kind of mutual documentation, two civilizations studying each other across an impossible boundary. The Tidewrights are becoming something new in their drowned city. And she? What is she becoming in her tilted tower, with her mismatched legs and heterochromic eyes, her spine curved to match stone stairs, her dreams filled with the whispers of dead keepers?

The lighthouse knows. It has always known.

It was built before Korathene fell—that much the oldest entries make clear. Built not as a warning against rocks or shoals, but as a marker, a pin stuck through the fabric of reality to mark a place where something significant would happen. The keepers before the drowning wrote of strange dreams, of voices from below when there was no below, only solid ground. They wrote of the beacon turning colors they had no names for, of ships reporting that the light seemed to come from beneath the water rather than above it.

"The lighthouse remembers," Mad Elara carved in a shaking hand. "It remembers forward."

Morwyn understands now. The lighthouse didn't survive the drowning—it caused it. Or perhaps it simply knew it was coming and positioned itself accordingly, a fixed point around which the catastrophe could pivot. The volcanic stone thrums with the same deep rhythm as the flame, a heartbeat that syncs with the tides, with the growth of salt crystals below, with the slow transformation of human into Tidewright.

She has begun her own transformation. It starts in the extremities—webbing between her toes that she hides in specially made boots, a nictitating membrane that flickers across her good eye during storms. Her hair grows differently now, each strand hollow, drinking in moisture from the sea spray. When she sings to the flame, she finds her voice carries harmonics it didn't before, subtones that resonate in frequencies only creatures of the deep would hear.

The other keepers whisper warnings from the walls, but they all stayed, didn't they? They all carved their observations until their hands could no longer hold the stylus. They all fed the flame and lowered the basket and watched the city below with fascination indistinguishable from longing.

Between the lighthouse and the drowned city, more passes than gifts and messages. Dreams flow upward with the rising bubbles. Memories sink downward with the rain. On the calmest nights, when the sea glass smooth and the flame burns steady, Morwyn can feel it—the pull between above and below, the tension of transformation, the slow certainty that these are not two separate places but one system, one organism, one long experiment in what humanity might become when faced with the impossible.

She will not leave her post. Not because she cannot—boats pass often enough, and she could signal for rescue if she chose. She stays because she is the Chronicler, and the story isn't finished. The Tidewrights below work their patient evolution, and she above documents each stage, her body changing in parallel but opposite ways, adapted not for the depths but for the heights, for the between spaces, for the eternal watch.

Some nights, when the flame burns blue-white and the walls whisper loudest, she climbs to the gallery and looks down through the lens at the city below. The Tidewrights look up at the same moment, their large eyes piercing the water to see her lighthouse, her beacon, her self. They raise their arms in movements that might be greeting or might be prayer or might be simple acknowledgment: we see you, becoming. We are becoming too.

And in that moment, the hundred and eighty feet of water between them seems like nothing at all—just another medium to move through, another state of matter to master, another boundary to dissolve in the slow, patient work of transformation.

The lighthouse flame pulses. The city below answers.

The conversation continues.
