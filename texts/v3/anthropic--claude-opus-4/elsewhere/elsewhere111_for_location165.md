High above, where the city-tree's trunk becomes indistinguishable from the sky itself, another hollow exists. This one was not carved by mysterious predecessors but grown—a massive gall the size of a cathedral, its walls smooth as the inside of a seed pod. The air here is warm and dry, tasting of resin and old paper, and the light comes from phosphorescent lichens that coat the ceiling in constellations of soft gold.

This is where the contract moths live.

They are enormous by moth standards—each one the size of a spread hand, their wings covered in what looks like writing. Not patterns that merely resemble script, but actual text in a hundred dead languages, stories and agreements and warnings growing from their scales like fur. The moths cannot read what they carry. They are living libraries with no knowledge of their contents, fluttering through the warm air on wings that rustle like vellum.

The gall's interior is strung with silk roads—pathways woven by the moths' caterpillars in architectures that shift with the seasons. In spring, the silk highways form spirals. In summer, perfect grids. Autumn brings chaotic tangles that somehow still lead everywhere. Winter sees them arranged in patterns that map constellations not visible from this latitude—or perhaps from this century.

The moths feed on memory. Not metaphorically, but literally—they extend their coiled proboscises into caches of dried sap that bead from the gall's walls, sap that contains the chemical memories of everything the tree has touched. Rain that fell three centuries ago. The shadow of a passing dragon. The sound of axes that came close but not close enough. Each moth metabolizes these memories differently. One might grow text about a trade agreement between kingdoms that no longer exist. Another might manifest a recipe for funeral bread written in the script of angels.

They live in castes determined by the color of their eyes. The amber-eyed are navigators, spending their days mapping the silk roads, adjusting tensions, reweaving connections. Their wing-text tends toward mathematical proofs and astronomical tables. The green-eyed are harvesters, locating new sap deposits, testing their age with delicate antennae that can taste time. They carry contracts on their wings—deeds and wills and marriage agreements from civilizations the tree has outlived. The rare purple-eyed serve no visible function. They hang motionless from the ceiling for days at a time, their wings displaying text that changes too quickly to read, prophecies consuming themselves as soon as they're written.

Once a year, during the autumn equinox, something extraordinary happens. A single moth—never the same one twice—will begin to speak. Not in sound but in patterns of flight that somehow convey meaning directly to the mind of any observer. It recites one complete text from its wings, always a contract of some kind. A treaty between the forest and the first humans to settle at its edge. An agreement whereby rain promises to fall gently on certain days. A marriage contract between two principles of physics, promising to remain constant in this particular region of space.

When the recitation is complete, that text vanishes from the moth's wings, leaving blank scales that will never again grow words. The moth dies within hours, its body consumed by its fellows in a ritual feast that ensures the contract's binding remains unbroken.

The young are stranger still. Caterpillars the color of old bone, they hatch from eggs laid in crevices where multiple sap-memories have crystallized together. They eat these hybrid crystals exclusively, growing fat on the collapsed experiences of centuries. Some caterpillars never pupate—they simply grow larger and larger, their bodies segmenting beyond reason, until they're more like living scrolls than insects, covered in setae that form words in languages that won't be invented for decades.

The pupae hang from the silk roads like dark fruit. Through their translucent chrysalides, you can see the text forming on the developing wings, letter by letter, word by word. Sometimes a pupa will glow from within, its occupant writing itself too quickly, too brightly. These overlit chrysalides never open. They harden into amber with the half-formed moth trapped inside, its partial wings displaying fragments of contracts too important or too dangerous to exist whole in the world.

The relationship between the two hollows—the cold fungal city below and the warm contract hall above—is one of studious ignorance. The termites know the moths exist; sometimes a worker will find a scale that has drifted down through the layers of wood, covered in text that makes their antennae curl. They wall these scales away with the other things they don't understand. The moths know the termites exist; their memories taste of small movements in the deep wood, of patient excavation toward something that grows more urgent with each generation.

But neither species acknowledges the other directly. They are like two dreams the tree is having simultaneously, careful not to wake itself by letting them touch.

Between them, in the middle layers of the great trunk, tunnel the things that feed on forgetting. Pale grubs the size of human fingers, they chew through wood and memory alike, leaving passages that smell of amnesia. They're the reason certain texts never fully form on moth wings. They're the reason termites sometimes stop mid-task, antennae waving in confusion before muscle memory takes over. The grubs pupate into beetles that live exactly one day—just long enough to mate, lay eggs in the boundary between remembering and forgetting, and die with their mandibles full of something that might have been important once.

Sometimes a child climbing the tree will put their ear to the bark and hear it: the rustling of wings that sound like whispers, the patient chewing of mandibles working in mathematical precision, the silence of things carefully not meeting. They'll feel the warmth from above and the cold from below and understand, in the wordless way children understand things, that the tree is not just alive but thinking, its thoughts taking the form of cities and their citizens, writing and unwriting themselves in the wood's dark heart.

The tree itself never speaks of what it knows. But sometimes, on nights when the moon is dark and the sap runs thick with tomorrow's memories, a moth will carry a text that's nothing but questions. "What were we before we were this?" the scales will ask in a script so ancient it predates asking. And down below, in the cold fluorescent darkness, the termites will pause in their excavations, their antennae detecting something vast and patient and waiting, something that smells exactly like an answer.
