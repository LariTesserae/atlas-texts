The Bone Market

Three days' journey upstream from the Woven Depths, where the mountain lake narrows into the rushing Vethra River, the water runs white with sediment. Not clay or sand, but the ground remnants of ancient calcium—bone dust so fine it turns the rapids into flowing milk. The living follow this pale current to its source: a canyon where the walls are made entirely of the dead.

The Bone Market fills a natural cleft in the mountains, though what's natural here has been reimagined by generations of traders. Enormous skeletons—whales that swam in seas now lifted into mountaintops, ground sloths the size of houses, things with too many ribs or too few legs—have been assembled into a vertical city. Skulls become shops, ribcages form market stalls, and vertebrae stack into winding staircases that climb the canyon walls. Every surface has been carved, scrimshawed, etched with prices and promises and ownership marks stretching back centuries.

The merchants here deal exclusively in calcium and its mysteries. They'll buy bones, certainly—fresh or ancient, whole or ground, identified or mysterious. But they also trade in the stranger commerce that bones enable: memories pulled from marrow, futures read in fracture patterns, the peculiar energy that runs through skeletal remains like electricity through copper. The richest traders work from inside mammoth skulls, their wares displayed on tables of fused shoulder blades. The poor squat in the gaps between ribs, selling finger bones and teeth from baskets woven of sinew.

Water runs everywhere through the market, channeled by grooves carved into ancient bone. The Vethra has been split into a hundred streams that cascade from level to level, powering bone-grinding mills and washing the endless dust into the river below. The sound is constant: water on calcium, pestle on mortar, the click of beads on abacus frames made from vertebrae. In the wet season, the entire market floods to the third level, and commerce continues by boat between the giant ribs, leaving high-water marks of mineral white on every surface.

The bones arrive by various means. Funeral barges drift down from the mountain villages, carrying the year's dead to be sold or interred in the canyon walls. Prospectors haul fossil discoveries up from the valley—sometimes just a single precious tooth, sometimes entire creatures still locked in stone. The Woven Depths themselves occasionally disgorge their secrets: Threadkeepers will appear at the market's edge, pushing the skeletal remains of lake giants that died in the flooded chambers, their bones green with algae and riddled with roots. These fetch the highest prices, as bones that have marinated in that strange architecture carry unique properties.

The primary currency is marrowcoin—smooth discs cut from fresh bone and sealed with lacquer to preserve the living essence inside. The older the marrow, the more valuable the coin, though freshness has its own worth for certain transactions. Skilled evaluators can taste a coin and tell you the species, age at death, diet, and sometimes even the temperament of its source. Fortunes are made and lost on the ability to distinguish real ancient marrow from clever forgeries.

The three Great Houses control most of the significant trade. House Ossuary specializes in human remains, maintaining vast catacombs where the bones of notable individuals are stored like wine, aging and increasing in value. They sell ancestry, literally—a fingerbone from a famous artist might grant temporary skill, while the skull of a philosopher could provide moments of clarity. House Menagerie deals in the exotic: dragons if they can find them, certainly, but also chimeras, failed magical experiments, and the bones of creatures from other planes of existence. House Calcium works in volume, grinding tons of bulk bone into powder for agriculture, alchemy, and construction. Their mills never stop running.

Between the Great Houses, smaller traders ply specialized crafts. The Readers interpret the cracks in burned shoulder blades, claiming to see possible futures in the fracture patterns. Bone musicians play flutes carved from femurs and drums stretched with preserved skin, their haunting melodies echoing off the canyon walls. The Whispers claim they can extract the last thoughts of the dead from skull fragments, selling these final moments to grieving relatives or curious scholars.

The living inhabitants of the Bone Market have adapted to their calcium-rich environment in disturbing ways. Generations of exposure to bone dust has left many with milk-white eyes and teeth that grow too fast, requiring constant filing. Their own bones are dense and heavy, making them slow on land but surprisingly capable swimmers—they sink like stones and walk along river bottoms, harvesting freshwater mollusks whose shells add to their stock. Children born here often exhibit peculiar talents: the ability to smell death approaching, fingers that can feel the memories in old bones, or an uncanny knowledge of anatomy across all species.

At the market's heart stands the Throne of Tides, assembled from the skeleton of something that might have been a god—or at least something worshipped as one. Its skull alone is the size of a cottage, and its ribcage forms a natural amphitheater where the most important auctions are held. The Throne is neutral ground, administered by the Pale Council, whose members are chosen for their inability to be bribed—usually because they've already traded away whatever desires might make them corruptible.

The relationship between the Bone Market and the Woven Depths runs deeper than mere geography. Bones that have spent time in those flooded chambers return changed—sometimes still bearing traces of the strange flora, sometimes hollowed out by roots into intricate patterns. The Threadkeepers understand something about death that the merchants can only guess at. They bring not just bones but knowledge: which remains will disturb the growth-patterns if left in place, which deaths were peaceful enough to leave useful memories, which creatures died with unfinished business that makes their bones restless.

In return, the Bone Market sends things downstream. Not bones—the Threadkeepers have no use for such things—but the living consequences of their trade. Apprentice merchants who've inhaled too much bone dust and begun to cough white. Failed experiments in reanimation that escape into the river. The runoff from the grinding mills, rich with calcium and stranger minerals, which feeds the explosive growth in the Woven Depths. Some say this is intentional, that the ancient builders designed their architecture to process whatever flows down from the canyon. Others insist it's coincidence, that the symbiosis between death-market and life-maze arose naturally from proximity.

The busiest season is winter, when the mountain passes close and the dead must be stored rather than transported. The Great Houses prepare all year for these months, emptying their warehouses to make room for the fresh arrivals. Prices drop as supply increases, and even the poor can afford a knucklebone of fortune or a tooth of wisdom. The grinding mills work day and night, filling the air with white dust that falls like snow on the market below.

But the strangest time is the spring flood, when meltwwater rushes down the canyon and the market must retreat to its upper levels. Then the bones below are submerged, and for weeks they soak in the mineral-rich runoff from a hundred mountain streams. When the waters recede, the bones have changed—colors shifted, textures altered, some even showing signs of new growth, as if death were just another state that could be negotiated. These flood-touched bones command special prices, especially from customers who arrive from downstream, their bodies twisted by years of tending impossible gardens, their fingers branching like roots, seeking something in death that mirrors their own threshold existence.

The Bone Market never closes. Even in the deepest night, lanterns burn in skull-windows and the mills continue their grinding. Somewhere, a transaction is always taking place—memories bartered for marrowcoin, futures read in fracture lines, the dead parceled out to serve the living. The white river runs on, carrying its cargo of calcium to the lake below, where it settles into the soil of underwater gardens, feeding the endless growth, connecting death and life in an economy older than either city, as natural and strange as the mountains themselves.
