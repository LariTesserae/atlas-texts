The Carillon Heights

Seven miles upwind, where the city climbs toward cleaner air, the Carillon Heights maintains its vigil. The district was built for those who managed the foundries, not those who worked them—a calculated distance that kept smoke from drifting into manicured gardens while keeping the industrial fires always in sight from upper windows. Now those windows are sealed with photovoltaic glass, turning the old anxiety of watching into the new anxiety of harvesting.

The houses here never succumbed to green. Their architects built them to resist—deep foundations anchored in bedrock, walls of imported granite that refuses moss, copper gutters that poison climbing vines with verdigris runoff. The residents who remained after the collapse turned this defensive architecture into a fortress against the transforming world below. They live in the clean spaces between solar panel and rain collector, measuring their days in kilowatts stored and liters filtered.

The air tastes different here—thinner, sharper, with none of the foundry district's vegetal dampness. Wind scours the heights, carrying away spores before they can settle. The Heightkeepers, as they've named themselves, maintain this sterility with religious dedication. They wear filtered masks when venturing onto their rooftop gardens, where food grows in hydroponic towers fed by precisely measured solutions. Nothing touches soil. Nothing roots beyond its container.

Their leader, Castellan Vance, occupies the old Commandant's mansion at the highest point. His great-grandfather built the foundry; he now oversees its containment. From his tower room, he watches the green sprawl through military-grade binoculars, tracking its advance like a general monitoring enemy positions. His maps, updated weekly, show the creeping border where civilization ends and the Green begins. Red pins mark where they've had to burn back incursions. Blue pins indicate successful firebreaks.

The Heightkeepers trade with the foundry dwellers, though they'd never admit it directly. At the border markets—neutral zones established in old parking structures—they leave caches of medical supplies, battery cells, and water purification tablets. In return, they find packages of the pigments that only grow in contaminated soil, the psychoactive fungi that ease their isolated anxiety, the strange hybrid fruits that taste of metals no longer mined. The exchanges happen without contact, mediated by the Runners.

The Runners are children of both worlds, born from brief unions between desperate Heightkeepers and adapted foundry folk. They live in the vertical space between, scaling the cliff faces where defensive architecture meets wild growth. Their bodies show their dual heritage—the lean efficiency of the Heights, but with the subtle luminescence that marks exposure to the foundry's chemistry. They've built eyries in the zone where neither community claims control, hollows carved into the cliff where wind and moisture meet.

In the Heights, water is currency. Every surface channels rain into collectors. Gray water cycles through three stages of purification before release. The old swimming pools have been converted into covered reservoirs, their art deco tiles hidden beneath steel lids. The Heightkeepers have learned to read the sky for rain the way their ancestors read stock tickers—obsessively, desperately, their whole economy balanced on precipitation patterns.

They generate their own mythology of purity. Children learn the Litany of Filtration, the Seven Stages of Decontamination, the proper protocols for maintaining the sterile barrier. Their coming-of-age ceremony involves a solo descent to the border—close enough to see the Green, to smell its alien fertility, before returning to take their oath of separation. Some don't return. The Heightkeepers burn their names from the records and redistribute their water shares.

At night, the Heights glow with hoarded electricity while the foundry below pulses with bioluminescence. The contrast creates a vertical gradient of light—harsh white LEDs above, soft green-gold below, and between them a zone of mixing where the Runners have learned to move unseen. They carry more than trade goods. They carry genetic material, stories, adaptations. Despite all efforts at separation, life finds ways to intermingle.

The Castellan's daughter, Serra, maintains the seed vault in what was once a wine cellar. She catalogs the genetic material of the world before—heirloom varieties that grew in soil, that needed bees and seasons and all the messy business of natural reproduction. She's never seen most of them grow beyond the sterile medium of her test gardens. Sometimes she trades seeds with the Runners, who claim the foundry folk have developed new cultivars that thrive in contamination. She labels these corrupted and stores them separately, but keeps them nonetheless.

The Heights runs on rigid scheduling. Morning water allocation. Midday production reports. Evening perimeter checks. The Heightkeepers move through their routines with mechanical precision, maintaining the infrastructure of survival. But the routines have developed cracks. Some residents have begun sleeping on their roofs, claiming the filtered indoor air gives them nightmares. Others have been caught cultivating small patches of actual soil in hidden corners, yearning for something that grows without hydroponic solution.

Dr. Merchant maintains the community's health with a combination of pre-collapse medications and new synthetics derived from traded fungal compounds. She's noticed changes in the long-term residents—not illness exactly, but adaptation. Their eyes have grown more sensitive to the blue spectrum, optimized for LED light. Their digestion has become so efficient on processed nutrition that natural food causes distress. They're evolving for their artificial environment as surely as the foundry folk have adapted to theirs.

The border between Heights and foundry isn't a wall but a gradient—a zone where the manicured meets the wild, where control dissolves into chaos. Old Copper Mary sends messages up with the Runners sometimes, written in the industrial pidgin both communities half-understand. She warns of deep changes, of root systems that have found the aquifer both communities depend on, of new growth that doesn't respect the careful boundaries the Heightkeepers maintain.

The Castellan files these messages in his tower room, next to his maps and evacuation plans. He knows the Heights is not sustainable—it's a closed system slowly running down, entropy dressed in solar panels and water recyclers. His great-grandfather built an empire of iron and smoke. His daughter will inherit only the illusion of control, the fiction that humans can live separate from the world they've transformed.

But for now, the Heights maintains its watch. The foundry spreads below like a green sea, and the Heightkeepers tend their sterile gardens, and between them the Runners trace impossible paths, carrying the future in their hybrid blood and contraband exchanges. Both communities persist in their different ways of being human, neither fully right nor wrong, each becoming what their environment demands.

The wind carries spores upward despite all precaution. In the corner of a rooftop garden, unnoticed, a strange vine has taken root in a hydroponic tower. Its leaves show the telltale metallic sheen of foundry adaptation. By the time anyone notices, it will be too late—not for catastrophe, but for the slow negotiation between human control and life's insistence on connection, contamination, survival in all its messy glory.
