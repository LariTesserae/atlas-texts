## The Cistern Above

### The Greater Darkness

Twenty feet directly above the flooded chamber, through layers of mortared stone and careful engineering, lies its counterpart: a vast cistern that could swallow a cathedral. This is the structure the chamber serves—a reservoir so immense that those who work within it have never seen all its corners at once.

The cistern is ancient where the chamber below is new. Its walls are blocks of black basalt, each the size of a merchant's cart, fitted so precisely that a blade cannot slip between them. But age has had its way. The mortar has dissolved in places, leaving gaps that weep constant tears of mineral-rich water. These tears have built curtains of flowstone down the walls—pale calcite draperies that glow faintly in the darkness like frozen waterfalls.

The space is not empty. It holds water to a depth of thirty feet, leaving another forty feet of air above—a twilight realm of echo and shadow. The water here is cold and still, the opposite of the chamber's warm circulation. It tastes of iron and stone.

### The Boat-Tenders

They call themselves the Lamplighters, though they tend no flames. Seventeen of them live their entire lives in the cistern, never seeing the sun except as that distant cylinder of light that marks the inspection shaft. They are the descendants of the engineers who built this place, bred for generations in the darkness until their eyes have grown large and pale as pearls.

The Lamplighters move through their realm in narrow boats of tarred wood, poling silently across the black water. Each boat carries glass spheres filled with bioluminescent algae—their cultivation is the Lamplighters' highest art. The algae glow blue-green when disturbed, so the boats trail ribbons of ghostly light through the water as they pass.

They speak in whispers that carry perfectly in the stone acoustics. Their language has evolved to use the echo—a statement made at one wall will reflect and layer, adding meaning as it bounces. A simple word like "water" becomes, through careful positioning and pitch, a complex poem about depth, purity, and time.

### The Purpose

The Lamplighters maintain the cistern's delicate ecosystem. They know that the warm chamber below is not just an overflow drain but a purification engine. The heated water that rises through it carries away impurities, while the sun-shaft acts as a natural UV sterilizer. The Glass-Sails that rise from below are actually cleaning organisms, designed by the ancient engineers to consume harmful bacteria.

But the Lamplighters' true purpose has been forgotten. They continue their rituals—scraping algae from calculated sections of wall, adding measured amounts of limestone powder to the water, singing the harmonic frequencies that encourage the Glass-Sails to rise—without knowing why. They only know that when they fail in their duties, the water grows foul and their children sicken.

### The Calendar Walls

The cistern's walls are carved with thousands of rectangular recesses, each the size of a human hand. These are the calendar cells. Once, each cell held a colored glass tile that would glow when struck by reflected sunlight from mirror systems above. The pattern of glowing tiles marked the days, seasons, and years.

Most of the tiles are gone now, fallen into the water or stolen centuries ago. But the Lamplighters have developed their own calendar using the cells. They place different species of bioluminescent creatures in specific recesses—slow-pulsing jellies for months, quick-flashing worms for days, steady-glowing fungus for years. The pattern is complex beyond outsider comprehension, a living constellation that tracks not just time but water levels, temperature changes, and the health of the cistern's ecosystem.

### The Overflow Galleries

Around the upper rim of the cistern, thirty feet above the water, runs a series of arched galleries. These were once inspection walkways for the engineers. Now they are the Lamplighters' homes—small chambers carved into the walls, connected by the ancient walkways.

The galleries are lined with shelf fungus that the Lamplighters cultivate for food. The fungus feeds on bat guano from the colonies that roost in the highest vaults. The bats, in turn, feed on the insects that breed in the warm updrafts from the chamber below. It is a vertical ecosystem, each level dependent on the others.

The Lamplighters have learned to read the water by the bats' behavior. When the bats cluster on the eastern wall, rain will come within days, and the water level will rise. When they abandon certain sections, it means the water there has grown too pure—lacking the minerals the insects need to breed.

### The Speaking Stones

In the center of the cistern, rising from the water like irregular islands, stand five pillars of different stones: red sandstone, white marble, green serpentine, black obsidian, and yellow sulfur-stone. These are the Speaking Stones, though they have been silent for generations.

The Lamplighters know that once, when the water reached precise levels and temperatures, the stones would resonate. Each stone has a different density and mineral content, creating unique frequencies when properly activated. Together, they would produce harmonies that could be heard throughout the buried complex, signaling water quality, seasonal changes, or danger.

Now the Lamplighters try to replicate these songs with their own voices, gathering in their boats around each pillar on appointed nights. They have preserved fragments of the true songs—hauntingly beautiful scales that seem to make the water itself shiver. But they cannot make the stones themselves sing.

Except once: a young Lamplighter named Echo discovered that if she dove deep and struck the base of the white marble pillar at exactly the moment sunlight touched the water through the inspection shaft, the stone would emit a single, pure note that lasted for hours. The note was so beautiful that the other Lamplighters wept. But the effort damaged Echo's lungs, and she died within days. Now striking the stones is forbidden, though sometimes the young ones dare each other to try.

### The Deep Gates

At the cistern's lowest point, beneath thirty feet of black water, stand three bronze gates each twice the height of a person. These lead to deeper reservoirs, older systems, places the Lamplighters fear to go. The gates are closed with mechanisms none now understand—wheels and counterweights that would require draining the entire cistern to operate.

But the gates are not perfectly sealed. Through their corroded edges seep creatures that should not exist: the Glass-Sails that rise to the chamber below, but also things that disturb the Lamplighters' careful balance. Eels made of living mercury that poison sections of water before dissolving. Colonies of crystal that grow with frightening speed, threatening to choke the inspection shaft. Seeds that sprout into pale, carnivorous lilies.

The Lamplighters have developed rituals to contain these intrusions. They sing the ward-songs while tracing patterns on the water with their poles. They drop offerings of cultivated algae and limestone powder at specific points above the gates. Usually, this is enough. When it isn't, they seal off sections of the cistern with sheets of tarred cloth and wait for the intrusion to die or dissolve.

### The Bright Times and the Dark Times

The Lamplighters measure their lives by the Bright Times—those moments when someone must descend to inspect or repair the warm chamber below. This happens perhaps once in a generation. The chosen one is lowered on ropes down the inspection shaft, moving from the twilight world of the cistern into that realm of roaring gold light.

They always return changed. They speak of the overwhelming brightness, the violence of the water's movement, the alien beauty of life thriving in pure light and heat. Some go mad from the experience, spending their remaining days trying to recreate that golden brilliance with ever-more-elaborate arrangements of bioluminescent cultures. Others become prophets, claiming the chamber below is a god's eye looking up at them, judging their work.

But most simply return quieter, more thoughtful. They improve the cultivation techniques. They add new harmonies to the old songs. They teach the children to read the water's moods with greater subtlety. They understand, in a way they cannot articulate, that their twilight world and the brilliant chamber below are one system, dark and light in necessary balance.

### The Future-Memory

Sometimes, floating in their boats in the vast darkness, the Lamplighters experience what they call the Future-Memory. It is a collective vision that comes upon them all at once: they see the cistern as it will be when their work is finally complete.

In the vision, the water glows with its own soft light. The Speaking Stones sing constantly in harmonies that make the heart soar. The Deep Gates stand open, revealing not horrors but wonders—gardens of crystal and mercury, forests of living glass, cities built by creatures of pure thought. The inspection shaft has widened into a great window, and sunlight floods the entire space, but it does not hurt their adapted eyes. Instead, they see both light and shadow as equal beauties.

They know this vision is impossible. The cistern is built for darkness, their eyes are creatures of shadow, and the Deep Gates must never open. But still, they work toward this Future-Memory, generation after generation, cultivating their algae and singing their songs and keeping the ancient balance.

Because they understand, in their bones, that they are not just maintaining a water system. They are tending a living thing of stone and water, light and darkness, human design and inhuman evolution. The cistern and the chamber, the darkness and the gold, the whispers and the roar—all are one great creature, breathing slowly through centuries, and they are its consciousness, its dreams, its careful, loving hands.
