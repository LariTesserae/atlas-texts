Above, where the Underglass Markets pulse with their fungal heartbeat, the frozen city clings to life in a different way entirely. The Thermal Districts spread across the surface like a vast circuit board of heat and cold, a patchwork of temperature zones that shift with the price of power and the whims of infrastructure.

Here, warmth is currency. The city survives in bubbles—geodesic domes of reinforced glass that trap heat from geothermal pipes, from factory exhaust, from the bodies of too many people crowded too close together. Between these domes stretch corridors of killing cold, where the ambient temperature hasn't risen above freezing in thirty years. Ice builds in layers on every surface, creating a geology of frozen time—you can read the years in the strata, darker bands marking industrial accidents, clearer ice from the few weeks when the factories shut down for maintenance.

The architecture is defensive, turned inward against the cold. Buildings cluster together like emperor penguins, sharing walls to conserve heat, their windows triple-paned and facing inward toward covered courtyards. External walls are blank faces of insulated concrete, traced with the veins of heating pipes like varicose veins on old skin. Some buildings have grown together over the decades, connected by enclosed bridges and sealed passages until you can't tell where one ends and another begins. The city maps are useless—they show the original streets, not the warren of interior connections that residents actually use.

Steam is everywhere. It billows from grates, hisses from pipe joints, clouds the dome-glass from within. The city lives in a constant fog of its own breathing, moisture that freezes instantly when it escapes containment, creating gardens of ice crystals that grow and shatter and regrow. The sound is distinctive—the crack of ice falling from overhead, the hiss of steam, the groan of heating pipes expanding and contracting, the muffled footsteps of people who've learned to walk carefully on surfaces that are always slick with condensation or ice.

The Thermal Districts are stratified by temperature. The Cores—the central areas around major heat sources—maintain a sweltering tropical heat. Here, the wealthy live in towers that rise above the dome-line, their penthouses climate-controlled paradises where they can afford to waste heat on comfort. The air is thick and oppressive, full of the smell of too many air recyclers working overtime, tinged with the chemical tang of whatever the factories are producing this week.

Moving outward, the Median Zones maintain barely comfortable temperatures. These are the domains of the working class—those who still have jobs in the factories, who can afford basic heating credits, who haven't yet been pushed to the Periphery. Their apartments are small but warm, their windows fogged with the breath of families packed into spaces meant for half their number. They wear their outdoor clothes indoors, ready to flee if the heating fails, if the pipes burst, if the daily brownouts become something worse.

The Periphery barely qualifies as habitable. Here, buildings freeze from the inside out when the heating credits run out. Ice forms on interior walls, creeping across ceilings like malignant growth. Residents burn whatever they can find—furniture, books, synthetic logs made from compressed garbage. The smoke has nowhere to go, trapped under the domes, creating a permanent haze that stings eyes and scorches lungs. People wrap themselves in layers of salvaged insulation, their bodies bulky and anonymous under the wrappings, only their eyes visible through gaps in the cloth.

Beyond the Periphery lie the Frozen Zones—areas where the heating infrastructure has failed entirely. These neighborhoods stand like monuments to entropy, their buildings sheathed in ice so thick you can't see the original architecture. Sometimes you catch glimpses of the life that was—a frozen curtain visible through a window, a car buried under decades of snow, a playground where the swings have become stalactites. But even here, life persists: squatters who've learned to survive without municipal heat, building igloos inside abandoned stores, tapping illegally into steam pipes, creating tiny pockets of warmth in the frozen waste.

The factories dominate everything. They're the city's beating heart, its purpose and its poison. Massive structures of corrugated metal and concrete, they produce goods for warmer places—electronics, chemicals, processed materials that require the kind of cheap labor only desperation provides. Their smokestacks pierce the sky like fingers, belching steam and smoke that falls as black snow on the surrounding districts. The workers arrive in shifts that follow factory time, not solar time—the sun is irrelevant here, just a pale disk that provides light but no warmth through the perpetual industrial haze.

The factories themselves are cities within the city. Workers often sleep in the machine rooms, unwilling to waste heating credits on their assigned quarters. They create unauthorized communities in the spaces between production lines—cardboard shelters, communal kitchens running on stolen power, children who grow up to the rhythm of industrial machinery. The foremen pretend not to see, as long as quotas are met. Sometimes entire families work different shifts in the same factory, passing each other in the corridors, maintaining an apartment they rarely visit except to collect mail and pay heating bills.

Between the domes run the Transit Tunnels—covered passages that allow movement without exposure to the killing cold. They're supposed to be purely functional, but human nature abhors a vacuum. Vendors set up stalls in the wider sections, selling hot tea from thermos flasks, offering quick repairs to torn insulation, running dice games where the stakes are heating credits and ration cards. The tunnels have their own microclimate, warmer than outside but colder than the domes, a twilight zone where ice and steam meet in constant conflict.

The city government operates from the Central Core, the warmest and most secure zone. Here, in buildings that never experience brownouts, bureaucrats manage the distribution of heating credits through algorithms that weigh productivity, social value, and a dozen other factors that seem to shift daily. The Mayor's office maintains a viewing gallery where visitors can look out over the city—a sprawling vista of domes glowing with internal light, of factories belching steam, of the dark spaces between where the Frozen Zones spread like cancer.

The police patrol in heated vehicles, their presence more common in the Cores, increasingly rare as you move outward. They wear powered suits that maintain body temperature, their faces hidden behind heated visors. In the Periphery, law is maintained by neighborhood committees, by unwritten agreements, by the simple fact that everyone is too cold and tired to cause much trouble. Crime, when it happens, tends to be desperate—the theft of heating units, the siphoning of power, the kind of violence that erupts when someone's family is freezing and someone else has what they need.

The city survives on careful balance. Too much heat in one area means freezing in another. Too much production means the air becomes unbreathable. Too little means the economic engine stalls and heating credits stop flowing. It's a constant negotiation between warmth and cold, between production and survival, between the needs of the many and the comfort of the few. And below it all, down where the permafrost meets human ambition, the Underglass Markets pulse with their own strange life, connected to the surface by more than just the pipes and tunnels that everyone pretends don't exist.

The inhabitants of the Thermal Districts have evolved their own adaptations to life in this stratified cold, their bodies and minds shaped by the constant negotiation with temperature and the tyranny of heating credits.

**The Heat Runners** move between the districts like antibodies in a bloodstream, carrying warmth where it's needed most. Kayla wraps her body in phase-change materials—substances that absorb heat in the Cores and release it slowly in the Periphery. Her route is tattooed on her arms in UV ink, only visible under the black lights of the Transit Tunnels. She knows every brownout schedule, every maintenance window, every spot where the thermal sensors have blind spots. Her cargo is human warmth: she guides paying customers through the cold zones, her body radiating stored heat like a mobile furnace. The constant temperature swings have damaged her nervous system—she can barely feel her extremities anymore—but she reads the cold like others read weather, knowing from the quality of ice on a window whether a building has been abandoned for days or weeks. She sleeps in different places every night, always moving, always accumulating heat to redistribute. Her clients pay in food, in medicine, in information about which factories are hiring. Some nights she guides refugees from the Frozen Zones, their children wrapped in reflective emergency blankets, their eyes wide with the shock of warmth after too long in the cold.

**The Thermal Barons** control the heating infrastructure from towers that pierce the dome-line. Director Chen hasn't felt natural cold in fifteen years. His penthouse maintains a constant 26°C, humid with imported plants that clean the air and remind him of the tropical city where he was born. His body has adapted to comfort—his skin is thin and sensitive, his metabolism slow, his hands soft from never wearing work gloves. He manages heating distribution through screens that show the city as a heat map, red zones of warmth surrounded by deepening blues of cold. He makes decisions that kill people—cutting heat to a sector to redirect power to a factory, raising credit prices to fund infrastructure repairs—but experiences these choices only as numbers shifting on displays. His children attend climate-controlled schools where they learn about the cold as an abstract concept, their field trips carefully managed journeys where they observe the Periphery through heated vehicle windows. He genuinely believes he's saving the city, making the hard choices that keep the factories running and the Cores warm. The workers who maintain his heating systems enter through service corridors, invisible, their presence marked only by the continued function of complex machinery he's never learned to understand.

**The Factory Shifts** live lives divided by temperature. Marcus works the night shift at Chemical Plant 7, where the production floor maintains a hellish 45°C to keep the reactions running. His body cycles between extremes—sweating through twelve-hour shifts, then plunging into the near-freezing temperature of his Median Zone apartment. The constant shock has aged him rapidly; at thirty-five, his hair is white, his joints arthritic from the temperature swings. He wears cooling vests filled with gel packs to work, heating pads at home, his body a battlefield between hot and cold. His wife works the day shift at the same plant, and they meet for one hour during the shift change, sharing a meal in the cafeteria that's kept at a moderate 18°C. Their daughter lives with her grandmother in the Periphery, visiting only on designated family days when both parents can pool their heating credits to warm the apartment properly. Marcus dreams of saving enough to move closer to the Core, but the math never works—heating costs rise faster than wages, and the factories always need their pound of flesh, extracted in sweat and shortened lifespans.

**The Dome Cleaners** work the exterior surfaces, the only people who regularly experience the true cold. Wrapped in suits that cost more than a year's heating credits, they rappel down the geodesic surfaces, scraping away ice and black snow to keep the light filtering through. Yevgeny leads a crew of six, all of them refugees from the Frozen Zones who know how to read ice, how to work in temperatures that would kill the soft Thermal dwellers in minutes. His suit is patched with materials salvaged from a dozen sources—aerospace insulation, military-grade heating coils, a faceplate from a deep-sea diving helmet that keeps the moisture in his breath from freezing his lungs. He's seen the city from angles no one else has, knows where the heat leaks from poorly maintained seals, where desperate people tap illegally into steam pipes, where bodies are hidden in the ice to be found during the spring thaw that never comes. The work is dangerous—ice falls, equipment failures, the constant risk of hypothermia—but it pays in heating credits and access to the Transit Tunnels. His team communicates in hand signals, their voices muffled by masks, their humanity reduced to shapes moving against the grey sky.

**The Credit Farmers** game the heating system through careful manipulation of the bureaucracy. Anya operates from a Median Zone apartment she's subdivided into a maze of small spaces, each officially registered to a different identity. She collects heating credits from multiple sources—disability allowances for conditions real and fabricated, work credits from jobs that exist only on paper, emergency aid redirected through careful form-filing. Her apartment is a tropical oasis, warm enough to grow the food plants she sells to Core dwellers nostalgic for fresh vegetables. She maintains a network of informants in the city administration, people who alert her to policy changes and loopholes. Her fingers dance across keyboards, filling out forms in dozen of names, each identity carefully maintained with just enough activity to avoid algorithmic detection. She justifies her work as wealth redistribution, taking credits that would otherwise go unallocated and spreading them through the Periphery via a network of heating cooperatives. The city knows people like her exist but can't afford to crack down too hard—she provides a pressure valve, a way for the desperate to survive without resorting to more destructive forms of rebellion.

**The Ice Readers** work the boundaries between Thermal and Frozen, interpreting the patterns of freeze and thaw. Old Man Petrov lost three fingers to frostbite before he learned to read the ice properly. Now he consults for salvage crews and urban explorers, examining ice formations to determine what lies beneath, whether a building is safe to enter, where the valuable materials might be preserved. Ice tells stories—clear ice means slow freezing, valuable electronics might have survived; cloudy ice means rapid freeze, burst pipes, destruction. He reads the strata like geological time, pointing out the dark band from the Chemical Spill of '08, the clear layer from the Great Shutdown, the recent formations that suggest illegal heating in buildings supposed to be abandoned. His remaining fingers are always cold, the nerves damaged beyond repair, but he feels the ice through vibration, through the way his tools sing different notes against different formations. He lives in the Periphery by choice, says the cold keeps his mind sharp, though others suspect he simply can't afford to leave the zone where his expertise matters.

**The Thermal Children** grow up adapted to their zones, their bodies shaped by temperature from birth. In the Cores, children are pale and soft, prone to heat rashes and respiratory problems from the recycled air. They play in climate-controlled playgrounds, their games involving complex social hierarchies based on whose parents control the most heating credits. In the Median Zones, children learn to dress in layers, to recognize the sound of pipes about to burst, to do homework huddled together for warmth when the evening brownouts hit. They're tougher, more adaptable, their bodies switching efficiently between temperature zones. In the Periphery, children grow up small and hard, their metabolisms supercharged to generate heat. They play in the frozen spaces, building ice forts, sliding down frost-slicked surfaces, their laughter visible as puffs of steam in the cold air. Some develop a condition the doctors call "thermal flexibility"—the ability to maintain core body temperature in extreme cold, their bodies burning calories at rates that would starve Core dwellers. These children are recruited young by the factories, by the cleaning crews, by the city services that need workers who can function where others would freeze.

Each group has adapted to their thermal niche, creating cultures and survival strategies as distinct as different species. They interact at the boundaries—in the Transit Tunnels, in the factories, in the grey zones between one district and another—but always return to their temperature homes, their bodies and minds calibrated to specific bands of the thermal spectrum. Together, they maintain the delicate balance that keeps the city functioning, each group essential, each group trapped by the very adaptations that allow them to survive.

And below them all, connected by a network of pipes and passages and hidden economies, the Underglass Markets pulse with their own warm life, drawing heat from above, sending resources upward, the two systems intertwined like the circulatory systems of some vast, dying organism learning to live with its diseases.

**The Connection**

Between the Underglass Markets and the Thermal Districts above lies a vertical maze of infrastructure that binds the two worlds in an uneasy symbiosis. The connection isn't just physical—it's economic, social, and increasingly biological, as both environments evolve their own ecosystems that bleed into one another through a thousand secret channels.

The most obvious connections are the Thermal Shafts—massive pipes that carry waste heat from the factories down through the permafrost. Originally designed to prevent the frozen ground from thawing and destabilizing foundations, these shafts have become the Underglass Markets' primary heat source. Market dwellers cluster their stalls around the shaft housings, where the temperature stays just warm enough to prevent hypothermia. The shafts sweat constantly, their metal surfaces beading with condensation that feeds the pale gardens of moss and mushrooms. Entrepreneurs have tapped into this moisture, running networks of collection tubes that provide the Markets with their water supply—mineral-rich, slightly radioactive, tasting of metal and industrial memories.

But the official infrastructure tells only part of the story. Over decades, a secondary network has grown like mycorrhizal threads through the city's bones. Maintenance tunnels, supposedly sealed, have been carefully breached. Forgotten mine shafts from the city's extraction past have been reopened and connected to utility corridors. Storm drains, dry for decades in the frozen climate, serve as highways for those who know their ways.

The Rat King's domain encompasses many of these passages. What the surface knows as pest infestations are actually carefully managed supply lines. The pale rats, evolved to digest synthetic materials, carry small items in their cheek pouches—memory chips, pharmaceutical samples, packets of seeds from the factory hidroponics labs. They follow pheromone trails laid down by the Market's hidden chemists, their routes as organized as any shipping company's. In return, they feed on the Markets' waste, breaking down materials that would otherwise accumulate, transforming garbage into fertilizer for the fungal gardens.

The human traffic follows more deliberate patterns. Heat Runners like Kayla don't just move between the Thermal Districts—they dive deep during their routes, carrying messages and small packages to the Markets. A memory stick hidden in the lining of a thermal suit can contain medical databases, technical manuals, or entertainment files to trade in the underground economy. They surface with other goods: medicines brewed from modified mushrooms, electronics repaired with salvaged components, information about which factories are dumping what chemicals where.

The Salvagers form the most direct bridge between worlds. They know every access point, every weakness in the security systems. Yuri and his colleagues don't just scavenge—they manage the flow of materials. A broken heating unit from the Thermal Districts finds new life powering Market stalls. Chemical drums, emptied of their original contents, become hydroponic gardens for the pale vegetables. In return, the Markets process materials the surface world can't handle—breaking down electronic waste with fungal acids, extracting rare elements with bioengineered moss, performing the dirty recycling that keeps the factories supplied with raw materials.

The factories themselves maintain a willful blindness to these connections. Middle managers know that productivity stats don't align with official resource allocation, that somehow their workers stay healthier than nutrition budgets should allow, that broken equipment sometimes returns repaired by hands that don't appear on any payroll. But the Directors in their climate-controlled towers prefer not to ask questions that might disrupt the delicate equilibrium. The Underglass Markets serve as a pressure valve, a place where the desperate can find alternatives to freezing or starving, where the underground economy handles what the official economy cannot or will not.

Some connections run deeper than commerce. The Moss Children travel between worlds through passages too small for adult humans, their bodies able to compress in ways that suggest their symbiosis with the phosphorescent moss has progressed beyond the merely external. They appear in the Thermal Districts' darkest corners—utility closets, ventilation shafts, the spaces between walls where pipes run. Residents report glimpses of glowing eyes, finding small offerings left behind: a mushroom that when eaten helps the body process polluted air, a vial of clear liquid that prevents frostbite, a diagram showing which heating units are about to fail. The Moss Children seem to be engaged in their own economy, one that operates by rules neither surface nor Market dwellers fully understand.

The chemical flows between worlds create their own weather patterns. Warm air from the Markets rises through the ventilation shafts, carrying spores and synthetic pheromones. When it meets the cold air above, it creates localized fog banks in the Transit Tunnels, dense mists that smell of mushrooms and machine oil. These fogs have become navigation markers for those who travel between worlds—each major intersection has its own scent signature, its own quality of moisture, its own population of adapted organisms feeding on the chemical gradients.

Water, that most precious resource in the frozen city, circulates between the levels in an endless cycle. The factories use massive quantities for cooling and chemical processes. Much of it escapes as steam, condensing on the dome surfaces, running down through collection systems. But significant amounts seep deeper, carrying industrial chemicals and heat energy down to the Markets. There, the fungal gardens and moss plains filter it through biological processes that concentration metals and break down toxins. The processed water, still contaminated but in different ways, finds its way back to the surface through illegal taps and salvaged pumping systems. Some theorize the entire city has become a single organism, its water cycle serving as a circulatory system that feeds both official and underground economies.

The Credit Farmers above and the Market Brokers below maintain careful communication through encrypted channels carried on pirated network infrastructure. They trade in different currencies—heating credits above, generator fuel below—but arbitrage opportunities abound. A shortage of specific electronics in the Markets translates to targeted thefts in the Thermal Districts. A new chemical process in the factories creates new opportunities for the underground refineries. Information flows both ways: which sectors will face heating cuts, which factories are hiring, where the security sweeps will focus, what new organisms are emerging from the contaminated ecosystems.

Cultural exchange follows the economic connections. Music from the frozen city—industrial rhythms born from factory noise—echoes through Market caverns, remixed with the natural percussion of dripping water and groaning stone. Fashion trends migrate and mutate: the thermal wear of surface dwellers adapted with bioluminescent threading, creating clothes that glow in the Markets' eternal twilight. Children's games pass between worlds, modified for different environments—tag played in three dimensions through the Transit Tunnels, betting games using both heating credits and phosphorescent stones as stakes.

Disease and adaptation travel these same routes. New respiratory infections bred in the Markets' damp air spread to factory workers, while antibiotic-resistant bacteria from the hospitals above find new vectors in the underground ecosystem. But beneficial adaptations spread too: gut bacteria that help process contaminated food, genetic variations that improve low-light vision, metabolic changes that allow bodies to function on fewer calories. The underground chemists and surface bio-hackers trade samples and notes, each population serving as test subjects for innovations that might help both survive.

The authorities maintain checkpoints at official access points, security cameras in the Transit Tunnels, thermal sensors that detect unauthorized movement between levels. But the city is old, its infrastructure layered and patched over decades of adaptation. For every passage they seal, two more are opened. The connection between worlds has become essential to both—the Markets need the heat and resources from above, the Thermal Districts need the recycling and pressure release the Markets provide. Like symbionts that have evolved together so long they can no longer survive alone, the frozen city and its underground shadow have become aspects of a single organism, their connection written in pipe and passage, in chemical exchange and human migration, in the endless negotiation between cold and warmth, light and darkness, the official and the unseen.

The city administrators speak of two distinct zones—the productive Thermal Districts above, the problematic underground below. But anyone who truly knows the city understands this division is an illusion. Every factory worker who sleeps through their shift knows they can find stimulants in the Markets. Every Core dweller enjoying fresh vegetables knows they were grown in fungal-lit gardens far from any sun. Every person shivering in the Periphery knows that somewhere below, warmth pools around the thermal shafts, that survival might be just a dangerous journey away.

The city lives because it is not one thing but two, each feeding the other, each poisoning the other, locked in an embrace that neither can break without destroying both. And in the spaces between—in the tunnels and shafts, in the fogs that smell of mushroom and metal, in the bodies of those who navigate between worlds—new forms of life emerge, adapted to neither pure cold nor pure warmth but to the gradient between them, the zone of mixing where the real life of this dying and persistent place endures.
