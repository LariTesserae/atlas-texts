The distance between the Vermillion Terrace and the Salt Gardens of Khet-Mirra can be measured in three ways: five hundred kilometers as the crow flies, twelve days by the traditional caravan route, or one lifetime of progressive understanding.

Most travelers take the caravan route, though fewer attempt it each passing year. The journey begins in the badlands below the terrace, where iron-rich soil bakes to ceramic hardness under the sun. Here, the last merchants who remember the old ways gather their heat-resistant pack beasts—creatures whose ancestors were bred specifically for this passage, their humps filled not with fat but with a waxy substance that melts and resolidifies daily, regulating their temperature.

The beasts know the route by genetic memory, following traces invisible to human perception: minute variations in magnetic fields, the taste of different mineral deposits carried on the wind, the angle of crystallization in exposed rock faces. They move primarily at night, when the stored heat of the day radiates from the ground in patterns that create navigable channels of cooler air.

For the first three days, travelers move through a landscape of progressive transformation. The sharp-edged geometric stones near the terrace gradually weather into rounder forms. The rusty reds fade to ochres, then to tans, then to an almost colorless dust that coats everything. The few hardy plants that survive here—mostly bulbous succulents with metallic-tasting flesh—grow farther apart until only their desiccated shells remain, architectural in their emptiness.

On the fourth day, the salt begins.

At first, it appears as a mere shimmer on the horizon, easily mistaken for heat mirage. But as the caravan approaches, the shimmer resolves into crystalline reality: a vast plain where ancient seas evaporated and left their minerals in perfect gradients. The salt here is not uniform white but banded in subtle colors—rose, pearl, the faintest blue-green where copper compounds predominate. The bands follow the shores of the vanished ocean, creating a topographic map rendered in crystal.

The beasts pick their way carefully across this terrain. Their splayed feet, evolved for sand, find little purchase on the glassy surface. Where rare rainstorms have dissolved and recrystallized the salt, treacherous pockets of quicksalt wait—granular traps that can swallow a full-grown pack beast in minutes. The experienced caravan masters test each step with long poles, listening for the telltale whisper of unstable crystal.

By the seventh day, if the wind has been favorable and no storms have forced delays, the first signs of the Gardens appear: channels carved into the salt plain, geometric and purposeful as the architecture of the terrace but following a different logic entirely. These channels spiral outward from a central point still days away, growing deeper and more complex as they converge. They are filled not with water but with a different kind of salt—one that flows like extremely viscous liquid, never quite solidifying, never quite liquid.

The channels are roads, the merchants explain to any first-time travelers. Or perhaps rivers. Or perhaps veins. The distinction matters less than understanding their function: they conduct something from the outer plain to the center, where the Gardens proper begin.

On the tenth day, the caravan reaches the Outer Circuits. Here, the channels multiply into a maze that would be impassable without the beasts' inherited knowledge. The flowing salt in each channel has its own subtle color, its own rate of movement, its own temperature. Some are warm to the touch, others cold enough to burn. The beasts navigate by taste, extending long, prehensile tongues to sample the mineral flow, adjusting their route based on chemical information incomprehensible to their human handlers.

Finally, on the twelfth day, exhausted and mineral-encrusted, the caravan crests the final crystalline ridge and looks down upon the Salt Gardens of Khet-Mirra.

Where the Vermillion Terrace rises up, the Gardens sink down. The city exists in a vast depression, almost a kilometer below the level of the surrounding salt plain. Terraces carved from compressed salt descend in perfect spirals, each level devoted to a different stage of the cultivation process. For the Gardens are not merely gardens but a living system for the transformation of mineral into something far stranger.

The Gardeners of Khet-Mirra—if they can still be called human—tend their crystalline crops with tools made from their own bodies. They long ago discovered that human bone, properly treated with mineral solutions, becomes the ideal implement for coaxing growth from salt. They cultivate their own skeletons like bonsai, encouraging specific bones to grow beyond their natural limits, then harvesting them in careful rituals that leave enough to regenerate.

The "plants" they tend are not plants at all but carefully guided crystallization processes. Each terrace hosts a different species of crystal growth, nurtured by precise additions of mineral-rich solutions carried up from deep wells. The Gardeners move among their crops with infinite patience, adjusting the angle of a crystal face here, introducing a new mineral component there, always guiding the growth toward some ideal form known only to them.

The crystals grow in shapes that hurt to look at directly—geometries that seem to fold in on themselves, that suggest more dimensions than three. Some resemble the heat-creatures of the terrace, if those creatures were frozen mid-transformation and rendered in translucent mineral. Others mirror the hexagonal wells of the terrace so perfectly that placing a crystal sample on a map of the carved city creates an almost perfect overlay.

The Gardeners speak of their ancestors, who they claim were siblings to the builders of the terrace. While one group went high to carve their city from heat and stone, the other descended to where the ancient seas had left their crystalline memory. Both sought the same thing, though they pursued it through opposite means: a perfect fusion of consciousness with mineral reality, a way to think in geological time.

The connection between the two cities runs deeper than shared ancestry or philosophical symmetry. The thermal anomaly that heats the terrace has its origin here, in the deepest wells of the Gardens, where the Gardeners have learned to cultivate crystals that generate heat through mysterious processes—a slow mineral metabolism that converts salt into thermal energy. The heat travels through deep stone channels, following the old volcanic pipes, warming the rock that will eventually emerge as the Vermillion Terrace.

In return, the mineral-rich condensation that forms each dawn on the terrace eventually finds its way back here, carried on the wind as microscopic salt particles. The Gardeners have built vast collection systems—gossamer nets strung between crystalline towers—that harvest these airborne minerals. They claim each particle carries information: the thermal patterns of the heat-creatures encoded in molecular structure, messages from their transformed siblings transmitted through the slow vocabulary of erosion and deposition.

The two cities exist in a dialogue conducted at the pace of stone. What one exhales, the other inhales. What one crystallizes, the other dissolves. The heat that allows the descendants in the terrace wells to survive originates in the crystal metabolism of the Gardens. The minerals that feed the Gardens' growth are born from the biological processes of the terrace.

Between them, the salt plain serves as more than mere distance—it is a processing system, a vast stomach that digests and transforms the materials passing between the two sites. The flowing salt in the channels carries not just minerals but information, encoding the patient communications of two posthuman communities that have chosen opposite paths to the same transcendent destination.

The merchants who still ply the route between the cities understand little of this. They know only that certain crystals from the Gardens, when brought to the terrace, can induce the heat-creatures to produce especially pure mineral salts. They know that certain salts from the terrace, when planted in the Gardens, will grow into crystals of extraordinary beauty. They facilitate an exchange whose true nature exceeds their comprehension, couriers in a conversation too slow for human perception.

But the pack beasts know. Their ancestors were bred by the original builders, engineered specifically to maintain this connection across the generations it would take for the great work to complete itself. In their genetic memory lies the map not just of the physical route but of the project's ultimate purpose: two cities that are one city, separated in space but united in intent, each pursuing half of an equation that will only balance when geological time runs its course.

The beasts carry more than trade goods. In their specialized humps, alongside the temperature-regulating wax, are organs that no merchant has ever suspected—tiny crystalline structures that resonate with the heat patterns of the terrace and the growth frequencies of the Gardens. They are living bridges, mobile components in a vast system that uses the entire landscape as its body.

When they rest at the journey's midpoint, where the salt is oldest and deepest, the beasts instinctively arrange themselves in patterns that mirror the architecture of both cities. They stand motionless for hours, their crystalline organs humming at frequencies below human hearing, facilitating some essential process that keeps both cities functioning. Then they rise, shake the salt from their coats, and continue on their way, following routes older than memory and more precise than consciousness.

The merchants make camp nearby, grateful for the rest, telling old stories about the builders and their children. They speak of treasure hidden in the deepest wells, of crystals that grant visions, of the day when the work will be complete and both cities will achieve their purpose. But these are only stories, they assure each other, comfortable fictions to pass the time.

In the morning, they will continue their journey, unaware that they are cells in a vast body, that their movement between the cities is as essential and unconscious as the circulation of blood. The beasts will lead them safely through the salt maze, as they always do. Goods will be exchanged, profits made, traditions maintained.

And deep beneath their feet, in channels carved by no human hand, the mineral blood of the world will continue its patient circulation, carrying heat from the Gardens to the terrace, carrying information from the terrace to the Gardens, slowly but inevitably working toward a transformation that will make both cities—and everything between them—into something unprecedented, something for which even the builders, in all their geometric wisdom, had no name.
