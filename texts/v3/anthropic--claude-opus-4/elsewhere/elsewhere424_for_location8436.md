Several kilometers inland, where the flood's reach ended in a ragged shoreline of partial submersion, stands the Salvage Heights—a vertical city built from the bones of the disaster. Here, in the twilight zone between drowned and dry, humanity clings to elevation with the desperation of those who remember when the water came.

The Heights rise in tiers of improvised architecture, each level telling its own story of survival. At the base, where brackish water still laps against concrete during high tides, the Mudlarks have built their stilted shanties. These structures stand on legs of salvaged I-beams and telephone poles, their floors precisely calibrated to remain just above the highest recorded waterline. Rope bridges and zip lines connect the dwellings, creating an aerial network that allows movement without touching the treacherous ground below.

The water here is nothing like the crystalline clarity of the drowned foundry. This is tidal territory, where twice daily the sea brings its burden of silt and debris. The color changes with the hours—brown in morning, gray-green at noon, black with suspended sediment when the tide turns. It carries the smell of salt and rust, decay and diesel fuel. Oil slicks rainbow the surface in perpetual motion.

Above the Mudlark level, the city builds upon itself. The second tier occupies what were once third and fourth floors of office buildings, now reinforced with salvaged steel and connected by catwalks welded from fire escapes. Here live the Reclaimers, those who venture into the drowned districts for salvage. Their homes are workshops and warehouses, filled with sorted machinery awaiting repair or repurposing. Water pumps hang from every ceiling, ready to be lowered and tested. Coils of copper wire, cleaned and re-spooled, gleam like treasure.

The architecture here is purely functional—wind breaks welded from car doors, rain catches improvised from satellite dishes, entire walls built from stacked washing machines filled with concrete. Beauty exists only in efficiency. A well-designed water filtration system draws more admiration than any artistic endeavor. The aesthetic is aggressive practicality, each surface serving multiple purposes, each space utilized to its maximum potential.

Higher still, in what the residents call the Dry Decks, the city's merchants and administrators make their homes. These levels, safely above even theoretical flood marks, have developed their own culture of elevation worship. Status is measured in meters above sea level. The most powerful families occupy penthouses that were once radio towers and rooftop machinery rooms, now expanded with terraces and gardens that cantilever out over the void.

The vertical nature of the Heights creates its own weather. Warm air rising from the tidal flats meets cool downdrafts from the upper levels, creating a constant cycle of mist and clarity. In the morning, fog obscures everything below the third tier, making the upper levels appear to float. By afternoon, thermals create dust devils that spiral up through the vertical streets, carrying the smell of hot metal and human industry.

Sound travels strangely here. The vertical canyons between buildings create echo chambers where conversations from the Mudlark levels reach the penthouses as ghostly whispers. The constant clatter of construction—someone is always building higher—provides a percussive counterpoint to the distant industrial hymn that drifts from the drowned foundry when the wind is right. At night, the city rings with the sound of pumps and generators, a mechanical lullaby that never quite masks the sound of water moving below.

The Heights' residents have evolved their own adaptations, though less dramatically than their submerged cousins. Mudlarks develop an uncanny ability to predict tides, their bodies attuned to pressure changes that herald the water's rise. Their children learn to swim before they walk, and develop a resistance to the dozens of waterborne pathogens that thrive in the brackish shallows. They wear their scars proudly—marks from barnacle cuts and rust poisoning are badges of survival.

The Reclaimers have their own modifications, mostly practical. Artificial gills—crude compared to natural evolution—allow for extended dives into the clearer waters of the foundry district. Magnetic implants in their fingertips help them sense live wires and ferrous metals in murky water. Their eyes adapt to the constant transition between bright surface light and underwater dimness, pupils dilating and contracting with mechanical precision.

Between these two worlds—the drowned foundry and the Salvage Heights—lies a territory of constant negotiation. The Transition Zone, as it's known, is neither fully submerged nor truly dry. Buildings here exist in partial flood, their lower floors claimed by water, their upper reaches still breathable. It is a landscape of staircases that descend into darkness, of windows that look out both above and below the waterline.

The Transition Zone serves as the primary interface between the two cities. Here, in pressurized chambers and airlocks improvised from shipping containers, trade occurs between the human Salvagers and the less human inhabitants of the depths. The Gardeners bring their mineral-rich algae, wound into ropes that can survive the transition from water to air. In exchange, they receive tools, glass for their underwater greenhouses, and most importantly, seeds—genetic material from the dry world that they hope to adapt to their submarine agriculture.

The Collectors rarely venture this far up, but their influence is felt. They maintain clear channels through the submerged portions of the Transition Zone, paths that Reclaimers have learned to follow. Sometimes, rarely, a young Collector will be spotted in the twilight waters, observing the human salvage operations with what might be curiosity. These encounters are governed by unspoken rules—no sudden movements, no direct interaction, each species pretending not to notice the other while learning what they can.

Trade runs deeper than simple exchange. The Messengers, those jellyfish-like creatures of living communication, have learned to breach the surface in the Transition Zone. Certain Mudlarks, those with the right neural mutations, can interface with them, receiving visions of conditions in the drowned districts—where new ruins have been exposed, where dangerous currents have developed, where the Collectors have marked territories as off-limits. This information is invaluable to the Reclaimers, who pay handsomely for accurate reports.

The relationship is not always peaceful. The Depth Sickness that claims Gardeners can affect humans too. Reclaimers who spend too long in the drowned districts begin to hear the industrial hymn even on dry land. They speak of understanding the patterns, of seeing how the flooded machinery connects to some greater purpose. These affected individuals often disappear, walking into the water one day and never surfacing. Their equipment is found neatly sorted on the shore days later, organized by material and function in the Collectors' distinctive style.

More dangerous are the raids. When storm surges push the water higher than normal, creatures from the depths follow. Grinders, drawn by the rust-rich environment of the lower Heights, beach themselves against stilted houses, their grinding plates seeking metal to process. The Mudlarks have learned to suspend their homes on ceramic insulators during storm season, denying the Grinders purchase. Still, every storm claims buildings, pulled down by the patient, grinding assault.

The Resonance Prophets pose a different threat. Their vibrational forms can travel through any medium—water, air, even metal structures. During certain atmospheric conditions, their harmonics propagate up through the Heights' steel skeleton, inducing visions in sleeping residents. These dreams are always of drowning, but a drowning that feels like coming home. Support groups have formed for those affected, teaching techniques to resist the prophets' influence—white noise generators, counter-frequencies, meditation practices that anchor the mind to dry land.

Yet for all the danger, the two cities need each other. The Heights cannot survive without salvage from the drowned districts. Motors, pumps, and generators pulled from the foundry depths keep their precarious vertical city running. The pure metals that the Collectors so carefully sort are worth fortunes in the dry economy, where new manufacturing is all but impossible. Even the oxidized flakes that fall like snow in the deeper regions have value—when processed, they yield rare earth elements essential for electronics.

Conversely, the drowned foundry depends on the Heights for genetic diversity. The Gardeners' careful cultivation would fail without new plant varieties from above. The Collectors, for all their alien behavior, still require certain proteins that only surface-world organisms can provide. These are delivered in the form of carefully prepared blocks, traded for quantities of sorted materials that would take human workers months to separate.

The exchange goes beyond material goods. Knowledge flows between the worlds, translated and retranslated until its origin becomes obscure. The Architects' understanding of fluid dynamics, transmitted through Collectors and interpreted by pattern-sensitive Mudlarks, has revolutionized the Heights' water management systems. Human engineering knowledge, simplified into vibrational patterns the Messengers can carry, helps the underwater inhabitants maintain the delicate balance of their ecosystem.

Some individuals serve as bridges between the worlds. The Tide Readers, born in the Heights but touched by the depths, can survive in both environments. They spend weeks in the drowned foundry, their modified lungs extracting oxygen from water, then return to the Heights to recuperate and report. Their accounts help map the changing topology of the sunken city, tracking which buildings have collapsed, where new currents have developed, which territories the various inhabitants have claimed.

The most successful Tide Readers develop a dual consciousness—human enough to function in the Heights, alien enough to navigate the foundry depths without triggering the inhabitants' territorial responses. They speak of learning to think in currents rather than directions, of feeling the industrial hymn as a kind of sonar. Some claim to have communicated directly with the Architect, though their accounts of these exchanges are frustratingly abstract—descriptions of pressure differentials and conductivity that seem to encode meaning beyond their literal interpretation.

In the highest reaches of the Salvage Heights, where the air is thin and the original city's radio towers pierce the sky, live the Watchers. These voluntary exiles maintain the communication equipment that connects the Heights to other survivor settlements. But their true purpose is to monitor the water—its daily rise and fall, its seasonal variations, its slow but inexorable climb. They record everything in meticulous detail, charts and graphs covering every wall of their tower stations.

The Watchers know what others prefer to forget: the water is still rising. Not dramatically, not daily, but measurably. Each year brings new high tide marks. Each decade sees another tier of the Heights threatened. The city builds ever upward, but there is a limit to how high salvaged materials can stack. Already, engineers debate the feasibility of the next expansion, calculating load stresses and wind resistance for structures that push the boundaries of improvised architecture.

This knowledge creates a strange temporality in the relationship between the cities. The Heights, for all its desperate vitality, is temporary—a staying action against inevitable submersion. The drowned foundry, for all its alien transformation, represents the future. Every Reclaimer who ventures into those depths sees their own tomorrow. Every creature that evolves in those sunken halls previews what humanity might become.

Some embrace this future. The Depth Seekers, a semi-religious group in the Heights, practice extended submersion, training their bodies to accept the change. They modify their homes to flood controllably, living for hours in chest-deep water, learning to love the weight of it. Critics call them defeatists, but they see themselves as realists, preparing for the inevitable in the only way that makes sense.

Others rage against it. The Builders push always upward, designing impossible towers that would scrape the sky if they could only find materials strong enough. They speak of massive pontoon cities that would rise with the water, of sealed arcologies that could maintain dry land indefinitely. Their sketches and scale models fill workshops throughout the Heights, dreams of salvation through engineering.

Between acceptance and denial lies adaptation—the daily reality of life between worlds. Children born in the Heights learn to read both air and water, to hold their breath for impossible lengths, to see opportunity in salvage and beauty in rust. They play games that would horrify pre-flood parents—diving competitions in the Transition Zone, races through partially flooded buildings, elaborate tag played across three dimensions of vertical architecture.

These children represent the true synthesis between worlds. Unlike their parents, they have no memory of fully dry land. The semi-submerged city is their native environment. They develop languages that incorporate the Collectors' vibrational patterns, create art from salvaged materials arranged in ways that echo both human aesthetics and the alien organization of the depths. Some display physical adaptations that blur the line between evolution and mutation—webbed fingers, nictitating membranes, skin that processes sunlight more efficiently.

The boundary between the Salvage Heights and the drowned foundry is not a line but a gradient, measured in water depth, oxidation states, and degrees of transformation. Every tide redraws the map. Every storm reshuffles the players. Every adaptation in one world provokes response in the other.

In the end, both cities are transitional forms, way stations on a journey whose destination remains uncertain. The Heights reaches toward a sky it can never quite grasp. The foundry sinks toward depths that have no bottom. Between them, in the zone where neither fully rules, new forms of life and consciousness emerge—neither fully human nor entirely other, but something born from the marriage of flesh and metal, air and water, memory and possibility.

The water continues its patient work, molecule by molecule, tide by tide. And life, in all its stubborn variety, continues to find a way.
