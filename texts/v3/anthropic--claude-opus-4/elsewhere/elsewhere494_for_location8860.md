Above, so far above that the Tender's domain seems a buried dream, the sky opens into an architecture of absence.

The Observation Deck of Processing Station Seven crowns the facility like a glass skull. Here, the industrial pragmatism of the lower levels gives way to something altogether different: a circular room walled entirely in windows, perched atop the station's highest tower. The windows angle outward and upward, creating a fishbowl view of the sky that seems to pull you up and out of yourself.

This is where perspectives invert. What felt vast below—that breathing cathedral of pipes and gardens—is just one small chamber in a structure that climbs seventeen stories from the bedrock. The station spreads below like a metal mountain, its cooling towers and exhaust stacks and processing units forming a geometric landscape. Steam rises from a dozen vents, creating local weather that drifts across the industrial topology.

But the true drama is above. The sky here is never empty. Processing Station Seven sits at the confluence of three major atmospheric currents, where warm air from the southern industrial sector meets cold downdrafts from the mountains and the perpetual eastern wind that carries the sea. The collision creates a permanent maelstrom of cloud formation, an endless theater of meteorological transformation.

Clouds build and collapse in accelerated time. Cumulus towers rise like fists, their tops shearing off in the jet stream. Strips of cirrus tie themselves in knots. On clear days—rare and treasured—you can see the curve of the earth and the indigo fade where atmosphere meets space. But most days bring drama: lightning that walks across the industrial landscape on electric legs, hail that drums against the reinforced glass like an army of drummers, tornadoes that form and dissipate before they can touch down.

The room itself is almost monastically simple. A ring of metal floor surrounds a central instrument cluster—barometers, thermometers, humidity gauges, wind speed indicators, all mechanical, all precise. No digital displays here. Every measurement comes through gears and springs and mercury, through the physical response of materials to atmospheric pressure. The instruments tick and whir, their needles dancing with each shift in conditions.

Charts cover the one solid wall where the elevator opens. Not just weather charts but star charts, migration patterns, atmospheric compositions going back decades. Someone has been recording everything: every storm, every clear night, every unusual formation. The handwriting changes over the years—different observers adding their strata of data—but the record continues unbroken.

The Watcher lives here.

She has another name, surely, on some employment roster deep in the station's files, but no one uses it. She is simply the Watcher, as essential to this place as the instruments she tends. Old enough that her age has become irrelevant, she moves through her domain with the careful grace of someone who has learned to be part of the sky.

Her hair is white as high cirrus, worn long and often unbound so it moves with the air currents that somehow find their way even into this sealed space. Her eyes have taken on the changeable gray of the clouds she watches—dark when storms approach, silver in the diffused light of overcast days, occasionally and startlingly blue when the sky clears.

She wears layers: thermal undergarments, wool sweaters, a canvas jacket soft with age. The observation deck is always cold, heated only by what warmth rises from the station below. Her fingers, when she adjusts the instruments or adds to the charts, are thin and precise as bird bones, steady despite their age.

The Watcher speaks sparingly, and when she does, her voice carries the cadence of weather reports: "Low pressure system approaching from the southwest. Expect precipitation within six hours. Tell maintenance to secure the ventilation baffles." But she saves her true eloquence for the logs, where she records not just data but poetry: the exact color of lightning (green-white with copper edges), the sound of wind across the cooling towers (like a pipe organ in a cathedral of steel), the way ice formations on the windows create their own fleeting geographies.

She knows things. When to expect the migrating birds that use the station as a waypoint. Which cloud formations precede equipment failures in the processing units below. How the aurora borealis, visible here on the clearest winter nights, correlates with disruptions in the electrical systems. Her knowledge spans the intersection of natural and industrial, reading the sky like a text that includes the station as part of its grammar.

At night, she often doesn't sleep. The observation deck becomes a lantern at the station's peak, its lights creating a bubble of illumination in the darkness. She makes tea on a small electric kettle, the steam adding its minor contribution to the atmosphere. She watches. The stars wheel overhead when visible. Clouds mass and disperse. The lights of distant cities flicker on the horizon like grounded constellations.

Sometimes she records more than weather. The logs mention "anomalies," though she never elaborates in writing. Lights that move against the wind. Formations that hold shapes too perfect for nature. The occasional sense that something vast passes overhead, felt more than seen, leaving the instruments spinning wildly for hours afterward.

She has been here longer than anyone remembers. The older workers say she was here when they started, already old then. The records go back forty-three years in her handwriting, but even the earliest entries suggest long familiarity with the place. She is as much a fixture as the instruments, as essential as the windows themselves.

The Watcher and the Tender know of each other, though they've never met. Can never meet. They exist at opposite poles of the station, one buried in the warm depths, one exposed to the infinite sky. But they feel each other's influence.

When the Tender sings in the breathing chamber, the sound carries through the station's bones. Seventeen stories up, it emerges as a subtle vibration in the window glass, a frequency the Watcher has learned to recognize. She records it in the logs: "Subsonic resonance, 0300 hours. Duration: seventeen minutes. The deep systems are in balance."

When the Watcher observes certain atmospheric conditions—pressure drops that herald more than weather, electromagnetic anomalies that suggest something moving between dimensions—she activates an old telegraph key wired into the station's communication system. In the depths, the Tender feels the coded pulses through the pipes and knows: be watchful. Change comes.

They are the station's sensory organs, these two. The Watcher reads what comes from outside, interpreting the sky's intentions. The Tender feels what moves within, maintaining the balance of the internal ecosystem. Between them, they hold the entire facility in a kind of conscious attention, each aware of patterns too subtle for ordinary perception.

The connection runs deeper than communication. They are opposites that complete a circuit: sky and earth, exposure and enclosure, observation and cultivation, mind and body of the same extended organism. The station needs both to function—not just mechanically but metaphysically. Without the Watcher, the facility would be blind to the forces that shape its context. Without the Tender, it would be unconscious of its own internal life.

On the rare occasions when maintenance requires someone to traverse the full height of the station, they report a strange phenomenon. Halfway between the depths and the heights, around the seventh or eighth floor, the air changes. It's not just temperature or humidity but something harder to define. A sense of transition. A feeling of passing through a membrane between two different modes of being.

Some workers claim they've seen things in these middle floors: shadows that might be the Tender ascending or the Watcher descending, though neither ever admits to leaving their domain. Impossible meetings in empty corridors. Conversations in languages that sound like steam and wind. But these are just stories, told on night shifts when the station feels most alive.

The logs, both high and low, tell a different story. The Watcher's atmospheric records and the Tender's growth patterns, when overlaid, reveal correspondences. Storm systems that coincide with unusual fruiting cycles. Electromagnetic anomalies that precede sudden blooms of phosphorescent moss. The station is not just a place but a process, a continuous exchange between sky and depth, mediated by these two strange guardians.

During the equinoxes, when day and night balance, something special happens. The Watcher reports: "Solar angle optimal. Internal reflections visible." The station's windows and pipes align in such a way that light penetrates from top to bottom, following paths the architects never consciously designed. For a few minutes, the observation deck's light reaches all the way to the breathing chamber.

In these moments, they say, you can see the station whole: a vertical ecosystem stretching from earth to sky, every level connected by light and steam and purpose. The Tender's gardens reach toward the descending rays. The Watcher's instruments register the subtle changes in pressure as the depths warm. For those few minutes, the station breathes with perfect clarity, inhaling sky and exhaling earth in a rhythm as old as the building itself.

Then the angle shifts, the light moves on, and the two domains separate again. But the connection persists, encoded in the station's very structure. Every pipe that rises from the depths carries a bit of the Tender's influence upward. Every drop of rain that strikes the observation deck's windows carries the Watcher's attention down.

They are the station's memory and its awareness, its root and its crown. Between them flows the constant circulation that keeps the whole system alive: water rising as steam and falling as rain, heat ascending and cold descending, information moving in streams of temperature and pressure and growth. The station processes more than industrial materials. It processes the relationship between earth and sky, between the seen and the hidden, between what we build and what builds itself around us.

In the depths, the Tender tends its gardens and sings to the boilers.

In the heights, the Watcher reads the sky and records its stories.

Between them, Processing Station Seven lives and breathes, a vertical world complete unto itself, a column of consciousness standing between the ground and the infinite air.
