Beyond the breathing darkness, past where the fungal towers thin to stumps and the moss-carpets give way to bare stone, the world transforms. Here, the permanent cloud ceiling cracks open in places, allowing shafts of brutal sunlight to pierce through. This is the Scar—a vast plateau where the planet's crust split during its violent birth, creating a landscape of crystal and glass.

The ground itself is the corpse of a cataclysm. When the planet was young, internal pressures built until the surface ruptured, spewing forth not lava but superheated silicates that cooled instantly in the planet's then-thin atmosphere. The result: fields of volcanic glass in formations that defy understanding. Obsidian spires twist skyward in helical patterns, their surfaces so smooth they seem liquid. Between them, prairies of glass needles grow like grass, each blade thin as paper and sharp enough to slice through steel. The needles chime in the wind, creating harmonics that travel for miles.

But it's the crystals that dominate. They erupted later, growing through the glass fields like infections. These are not the crystals of Earth—they grow and shift, expanding during the day and contracting at night, their lattices constantly reorganizing. Some are massive, building-sized aggregations of purple and gold that hum with stored solar energy. Others spread in delicate webs, each node no larger than a fist but connected by threads of crystallized minerals that conduct vibrations between them.

The boundary between the breathing darkness and the Scar is absolute. On one side, perpetual twilight and teeming life. On the other, a sterile brilliance that kills most organisms within minutes. The transition zone is perhaps a hundred meters wide—a place where the last fungal towers lean away from the light, their sides burned black, and where rivers of condensation from the humid lowlands evaporate into steam that rises in permanent columns.

Yet life, as always, finds a way.

The Weavers came from the darkness originally, perhaps ancestors of the Chorus who were caught in a mass migration when climate patterns shifted. But what emerged from that evolutionary crucible bears little resemblance to their fluid cousins. Where the Chorus flow, the Weavers crystallize.

A Weaver colony looks like a cancer of glass and protein. Each individual member is a tiny thing, smaller even than a Chorus-body, but armored in excretions that harden into something between shell and crystal. They cannot survive the raw light individually—it would cook them in seconds. Instead, they build.

Their cities rise from the glass fields like geometric nightmares. Using secretions that somehow bond with both obsidian and crystal, Weavers construct elaborate structures that are part architecture, part organism. The outer layers are purely defensive—dead members whose shells have been reinforced and fused into barriers that can withstand the brutal day-cycle. But pierce through (if you could pierce through something harder than diamond), and you'd find a honeycomb of passages where the living members scuttle in permanent shadow.

They've evolved past sound. In the Scar, where the air is thin and the acoustic landscape is dominated by the singing of glass needles, chemical communication would dissipate too quickly. Instead, Weavers communicate through vibration patterns conducted through their structures. A message can travel from one end of a city to another in milliseconds, propagating through crystal threads they've incorporated into their architecture.

But their true genius lies in their relationship with light. Somehow—and no one who might study them understands how—Weavers can metabolize the energy stored in the living crystals. They cultivate them like gardens, encouraging growth in specific patterns that channel and concentrate solar energy. Deep in their cities, in chambers no larger than a closed fist, they conduct processes that transform light into forms of matter that shouldn't exist.

Their tools aren't wielded—they're grown. A Weaver colony needing to expand will spend months growing a crystal-boring apparatus, a fusion of biological secretions and mineral accumulation that can slowly push through even the toughest obsidian. They shape the glass fields around their cities, creating mazes of reflected light that confuse and trap the few predators that hunt the transition zones.

The individual Weavers themselves are barely recognizable as relatives of the Chorus. Their soft bodies have atrophied, becoming little more than nerve clusters and digestive systems. Their shells, by contrast, have become everything—locomotion, manipulation, sensation. They move on legs that are actually crystalline projections from their shells, controlled through some process of electrical conduction. When they die, their soft parts desiccate in hours, leaving only the shell—which their colony-mates will either incorporate into architecture or grind into paste for the young.

Reproduction among Weavers is an architectural project. When a colony decides to reproduce, it begins constructing a special chamber at its heart—a perfect sphere lined with mirrors of polished obsidian and focusing crystals. This takes years. When complete, select members enter the chamber and shed their shells entirely, becoming vulnerable in way they never are otherwise. What happens next is hidden from outside observation, but the chamber fills with light—not from outside but generated by chemical processes unknown elsewhere on the planet. After days of this radiance, new Weavers emerge, their infant shells already beginning to harden, carrying with them fragments of crystal that will seed a new city elsewhere in the glass fields.

The Weavers war with light itself. During certain seasons, solar flares wash across the Scar with radiation that can crack even their reinforced structures. The colonies have learned to predict these events through vibrations in the planetary crust that precede the flares by days. When detection comes, entire cities reconfigure—outer walls thicken, passages seal, and the colonies retreat to their deepest chambers to wait. Some cities don't survive. Their ruins dot the glass fields, slowly being reclaimed by crystal growth, their empty passages singing in harmonics that warn other colonies of danger.

There is exchange between the worlds, though rare and strange. Sometimes, during the fierce storms that sweep across the transition zone, Chorus colonies are blown into the Scar. Most die instantly. But occasionally, a few members survive long enough to encounter a Weaver city. What happens next varies—sometimes the Weavers incorporate the dying Chorus into their structures, creating hybrid passages that pulse with biological rhythms. Other times, chemical exchanges occur that neither species seems to fully control, resulting in offspring that belong to neither world—creatures that build shells of hardened spore-silk and communicate through bioluminescent flashes.

The Weavers know the darkness exists. In their deepest chambers, they sometimes create models of it—small spaces where they exclude all light and cultivate fungi brought from the transition zone. They sing to these spaces (if vibration through crystal can be called singing), patterns that echo the ancient memories of flowing water and soft moss. But they can never return. Evolution has taken them too far from their origins, made them too much creatures of crystal and light.

In the Scar, where glass needles chime eternally and crystals grow like living things, the Weavers build their impossible cities. They are proof that life can adapt to anything, even if that adaptation requires becoming something that life, as originally conceived, was never meant to be. They are builders and chemists, architects and light-farmers, as alien to their Chorus cousins as the Scar is to the breathing darkness.

Yet on quiet nights, when the wind dies and the glass fields fall silent, vibrations travel through the planet's crust—messages in no language either species consciously knows. The Chorus feel them through the moss, the Weavers through their crystal foundations. For brief moments, the two worlds touch, and something deeper than consciousness remembers when they were one.
