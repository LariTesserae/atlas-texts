The Crystal Plains of Upper Siltmere

Above the sunken city, where the limestone gives way to older granite, the land opens into vast plateaus of translucent stone. This is a place of terrible clarity—a landscape where the earth itself has crystallized into sheets of mineral glass, each layer preserving the moment of its formation like geological memory. The plains stretch endlessly under skies that have forgotten how to hold moisture, their air so dry it pulls water from exposed skin within minutes.

The crystals grow in terraces and shelves, some no thicker than window panes, others deep enough to swallow sound. They overlap and intersect at precise angles, creating a natural architecture of light and refraction. Walking here means navigating a maze of reflections—your image split and scattered across a thousand surfaces, each one showing you at a slightly different moment, as if time itself fragments in the crystal lattice. The formations ring like bells when the wind passes over them, producing pure tones that carry for miles in the desiccated air.

Nothing grows in soil here because there is no soil—only the endless crystal substrate and the fine dust that accumulates in the joints between plates. This dust is precious; it contains the pulverized remains of ancient organisms trapped in the original crystallization, their minerals now part of the glittering sediment. When rare winds stir this dust into devils, they create whirling columns of bioluminescent particles that glow faintly in daylight and burn like stellar fire at night.

The fauna has evolved past the need for water as humans understand it. Crystal wasps build nests from secretions that harden into perfect geometric forms, their bodies transparent save for the dark nodes of their essential organs. They harvest moisture from the air itself, their wings designed to condense the slightest humidity through rapid vibration. Prism-backed beetles scuttle across the plains, their shells cut with facets that focus sunlight into heat beams, which they use to fuse dust into protective shelters. At night, things that might once have been moths dance in the air, their bodies now living lenses that bend starlight into complex patterns—a form of communication, navigation, or worship.

The light here is aggressive. Dawn arrives not as a gentle brightening but as a violent cascade of photons ricocheting through the crystal maze. Noon brings a bombardment of radiance from every angle, light reflecting and re-reflecting until the air itself seems to burn white. Sunset multiplies into a thousand different horizons, each crystal face holding its own version of dusk. Night offers no true darkness—starlight and moonbeams trap themselves in the crystalline labyrinth, creating a landscape of silver shadows and ghost-light that never fully fades.

The silence is profound, broken only by the occasional crack of thermal expansion or the high, pure notes of wind-song. Sound behaves strangely here, sometimes amplifying through resonant chambers, sometimes disappearing entirely into formations that swallow vibration. Conversations can carry for miles along certain crystal alignments, while screams might die within feet of their source. The inhabitants have learned to navigate by these acoustic paths, following routes where their voices will carry to distant settlements.

Weather in the Crystal Plains defies conventional patterns. Rain, when it comes perhaps once a decade, strikes the formations like hammers on a xylophone, creating symphonies that drive listeners mad with their beauty. The water runs off immediately, channeled by the angled surfaces into specific collection points where it disappears into deep crystal shafts. Some theorize these shafts connect to the flooded depths of Lower Siltmere, that the water makes a journey through the earth's mineral body, undergoing transformations that conventional chemistry cannot explain.

The true mystery of the plains lies in their growth. The crystals are not static—they extend themselves molecule by molecule, following patterns that suggest intention rather than mere chemistry. Certain formations appear overnight, while others slowly shift their angles over years, optimizing for unknown purposes. The oldest inhabitants swear the plains are writing something, that the entire landscape is a gradually evolving script in a language of light and form.

Time moves differently here. The crystals preserve moments—shadows of events that happened decades ago suddenly appear in certain lights, perfect three-dimensional recordings held in the mineral matrix. Sometimes these recordings show things that haven't happened yet, or things that happened differently than remembered. The boundary between past, present, and future becomes negotiable, subject to refraction like everything else in this place.

The relationship between Upper and Lower Siltmere reveals itself in these temporal anomalies. The same limestone that dissolves below crystallizes above, as if the earth were running two experiments simultaneously—one in dissolution, one in preservation. The water that drowns the lower city originates here, in the rare rains that the crystals collect and channel downward. But something else travels between the two realms, something that moves through the mineral veins connecting crystal to clay.

The plains extract a different price from their inhabitants than the foundries below. Here, the cost is not physical transformation but a kind of essential calcification. Those who stay too long find their thoughts becoming angular, their emotions refracting into component feelings that no longer quite combine into whole experiences. They begin to see time as the crystals do—not as a flow but as a series of discrete moments, each one preserved, examined, and ultimately transcended.

Some say the Crystal Plains are manufacturing time itself, that each formation is a temporal instrument playing notes in a composition that spans geological ages. They point to the way certain crystals seem to age backward, becoming clearer and more perfect with each passing year. They describe visions seen in the deepest formations—glimpses of Lower Siltmere not as it is but as it will be, transformed into something that bridges the gap between organic and mineral, between dissolution and preservation.

Others claim the plains are a lens, focusing something vast and distant down to a point of unbearable intensity. They speak of the noon-light convergences, when every crystal aligns to create a single, perfect beam that shoots upward into the sky, carrying information to or from somewhere else entirely.

The air tastes of ozone and ancient dust. The crystals sing their eternal songs. Light bends and breaks and rebuilds itself. And in the depths below, the foundries process their materials, while above, the plains process something far more fundamental—the nature of permanence itself, the possibility of preservation in a universe that tends toward entropy.

Between these two extremes, between drowning and desiccation, between dissolution and crystallization, something is being born. The inhabitants of both realms are its cells, its thoughts, its instruments. Lower Siltmere dreams of rising while Upper Siltmere dreams of sinking, and in their dreaming, they create a third thing—a possibility that exists in neither place but requires both for its realization.

The experiment continues. The crystals grow. The water rises. And somewhere between clarity and opacity, between the perfect preservation of the plains and the constant transformation of the foundries, the future crystallizes drop by drop, moment by moment, forever almost arriving.
