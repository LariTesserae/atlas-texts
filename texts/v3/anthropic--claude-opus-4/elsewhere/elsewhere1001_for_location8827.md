The Stillworks

Above the Churning Hollows, where the stone has long ago settled into its final form, the Stillworks spreads across seventeen square miles of absolute architectural precision. Here, every surface meets at perfect right angles. Every corridor runs exactly three meters wide, two and three-quarters meters high. The walls are smooth gray granite, so finely fitted that the joints between blocks are barely visible lines no thicker than a human hair.

No windows interrupt these walls. No decorations mar their uniformity. The only variations are the doors—heavy brass rectangles that appear at mathematically precise intervals, each one identical to every other, each one locked. The brass never tarnishes. The locks never rust. Behind some doors are more corridors. Behind others are vast chambers filled with machinery that has no apparent purpose—wheels that turn nothing, pistons that pump only air, gears that mesh in elaborate patterns but drive no output. The machines run constantly, their brass and steel components moving in perfect silence, lubricated by some substance that never needs replenishment.

The light comes from nowhere and everywhere at once—a flat, shadowless illumination that maintains exactly 437 lux at all points. There is no visible source. The light simply exists, as constant and unchanging as the architecture itself. It neither dims nor brightens, neither flickers nor shifts. Objects cast no shadows here. People cast no shadows here.

The air maintains a steady 18.3 degrees Celsius with 47% humidity. It neither moves nor stagnates, carrying a faint scent of machine oil and copper that never grows stronger or weaker. No dust settles on the surfaces. No moisture condenses on the walls. The Stillworks exists in a state of perfect environmental stasis, as if the very concept of entropy has been surgically removed.

Sound behaves strangely here. Footsteps echo exactly three times, regardless of the size or shape of the space. Voices carry precisely twelve meters before cutting off abruptly, as if hitting an invisible wall. The machines make no sound despite their constant motion. Sometimes, in the deepest chambers, you might hear what sounds like breathing—slow, mechanical inhalations and exhalations—but following the sound leads only to more empty corridors, more silent machines.

The geometry defies comprehension when examined closely. Corridors that should intersect pass over or under each other without any visible change in elevation. Rooms that should occupy the same space exist independently, accessible through different doors. Maps of the Stillworks invariably fail—not because the space changes, but because its three-dimensional logic doesn't translate to two-dimensional representation. The only successful navigation method is memorization of specific routes, following the same sequence of turns and doors each time.

Water exists here, flowing from brass taps set at regular intervals along the corridors. The water is always exactly 15.5 degrees Celsius, tasteless, odorless, and appears to be absolutely pure. It flows when the taps are turned, stops when they are closed, and seems to require no source or drainage. Food appears as well, in certain chambers at predictable intervals—dense, gray blocks that taste of nothing but provide complete nutrition. They manifest on brass plates that weren't there moments before and vanish if not consumed within exactly four hours.

The Stillworks connects to the Churning Hollows through seventeen shafts, each one a perfect cylinder seven meters in diameter, dropping straight down through 350 meters of solid rock. These shafts are the only vertical spaces in the Stillworks—everywhere else maintains the same relentless horizontality. At the bottom of each shaft, brass doors seal the connection, though they bulge and groan as the pressure from below builds and releases. Sometimes they burst open, flooding the lower corridors with the chaos of the Hollows—mud, water, screaming stone, and occasionally, the Flowbloods themselves. But within hours, the mess vanishes, the doors reseal, and the Stillworks returns to its perfect order as if nothing had happened.

The Coordinators discovered the Stillworks by accident—or perhaps the Stillworks discovered them. They were human once, scholars and engineers drawn by rumors of a perfect architectural space, a place where the fundamental laws of construction had been solved and rendered in stone and brass. What they found exceeded their wildest theoretical speculation.

But the Stillworks changes those who stay too long, though in ways subtler than the dramatic transformations of the Hollows. The Coordinators have been here for generations now, and their humanity has been refined away like impurities from an alloy.

They move with mechanical precision, each gesture calibrated to minimize energy expenditure. Their bodies have grown angular, as if adapting to the right angles that surround them. Their skin has taken on a grayish cast, smooth and slightly reflective like polished stone. They've lost the ability to perspire—their bodies maintaining exactly 18.3 degrees Celsius internally, matching their environment perfectly.

Their eyes have become instruments of measurement. They can judge distances to the millimeter, angles to the second of arc, without tools or conscious thought. Their pupils are square now, though they claim not to have noticed this change themselves. When they look at you, you feel catalogued, your dimensions recorded, your spatial coordinates filed away in some vast mental schematic.

The Coordinators dress in identical gray robes that hang exactly to mid-calf, held by brass clasps at precise points. Their heads are shaved clean—hair is an irregularity the Stillworks seems to discourage. They wear no ornamentation, carry no personal items. Individual identity has become almost meaningless to them; they refer to each other by function rather than name: Prime Surveyor, Third Machinist, Secondary Archivist.

They've developed a language of pure mathematics, spoken in numerical sequences that describe spatial relationships, mechanical functions, and logical operations. They can still speak conventional languages when necessary, but find them frustratingly imprecise. Their own communication sounds like recited equations, crystalline in its clarity to them, incomprehensible to outsiders.

The Coordinators' purpose—if it can be called that—is to maintain and expand their understanding of the Stillworks. They patrol the corridors in groups of four (never three, never five), measuring and remeasuring spaces that never change, documenting the precise operations of machines whose purpose remains opaque. They've created vast archives of these measurements, room after room filled with brass plates etched with microscopic precision, recording millions of data points that reveal no patterns, serve no function except the recording itself.

They've learned to operate some of the machines, or at least to vary their operations. A lever pulled at the correct angle might open a previously hidden door. A wheel turned exactly 347.6 degrees might cause a new corridor to become accessible. But these discoveries lead only to more corridors, more machines, more perfect emptiness to catalogue.

The Coordinators reproduce through a process they call "calibration." When their numbers drop below optimal levels (they maintain exactly 144 individuals), they select candidates from visitors or from their own occasional ventures to the surface. The selected individuals are brought to the deep machine halls and subjected to precisely controlled environments—specific temperatures, pressures, sounds—until their bodies begin the slow transformation. Most die or flee before the process completes. Those who survive join the collective, their previous identities dissolved into function.

They fear the Churning Hollows with an intensity that approaches religious dread. The chaos below represents everything antithetical to their existence. Yet they maintain the seventeen shafts, ensure the brass doors remain functional, even venture down periodically to repair damage. They see this as a necessary evil, a maintenance task required to keep the disorder contained.

But contamination is inevitable. Sometimes a Coordinator returns from shaft maintenance changed—a slight tremor in their movements, a tendency to unconsciously flex joints that shouldn't flex. These individuals are quarantined in specific chambers, observed for exactly 77 days. If the irregularities persist, they are exiled through the shafts to the Hollows below. The Coordinators record these events with the same dispassionate precision as everything else, noting the exact rate of contamination, the specific symptoms, the time elapsed before exile becomes necessary.

They trade with both surface dwellers and, reluctantly, with the Flowbloods. From above, they acquire raw materials—brass and steel for replacement parts, specific minerals for their incomprehensible experiments. To the Flowbloods, they provide the gray nutrition blocks and measured quantities of pure water, receiving in exchange samples of flowing stone and the bioluminescent fungi that they study but never cultivate.

The Coordinators don't sleep in any conventional sense. Instead, they enter four-hour periods of "stasis" where they stand perfectly still in designated chambers, eyes open but unfocused, breathing in perfect rhythm with some unheard mechanical pulse. They claim to dream of blueprints during these periods, vast technical drawings that reveal new sections of the Stillworks, though following these dream-maps leads only to areas that were always there, waiting to be discovered.

Their greatest achievement and deepest mystery is the Control Room—a vast spherical chamber at what they calculate to be the exact center of the Stillworks. The walls are covered in millions of tiny brass switches, dials, and levers, each labeled with symbols that predate any known language. The Coordinators have spent generations attempting to decode these controls, flipping switches in careful sequences, documenting the results. Sometimes nothing happens. Sometimes a door elsewhere in the complex opens or closes. Once, they claim, they made it rain in the Stillworks—a brief shower of that same pure, temperature-neutral water falling from nowhere for exactly twelve minutes.

They believe the Stillworks is a machine in its totality, that the corridors and chambers are not architecture but components in some vast mechanism whose function transcends their understanding. They search for the Operator's Manual, convinced it must exist somewhere in the endless rooms. Some among them whisper of the Builder—not a god but an engineer of impossible skill who designed this place for reasons they may never comprehend.

The Coordinators are not happy—happiness is an irregularity. They are not sad—sadness is an inefficiency. They simply are, existing in perfect harmony with their perfect environment, measuring and maintaining and documenting, waiting for the moment when all their data aligns and reveals the true purpose of the Stillworks.

That moment never comes. It likely never will. But precision, they have learned, is its own reward. In a universe trending toward entropy, they have found a pocket of absolute order, and they will preserve it with their lives—or whatever their existence has become in this place beyond shadow, beyond time, beyond the simple chaos of being human.
