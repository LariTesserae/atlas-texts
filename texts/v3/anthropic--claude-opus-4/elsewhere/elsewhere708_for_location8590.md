**The Lowland Pools**

Three days' descent from the plateau's edge, where the morning fogs are born, the land softens into something altogether different. Here the earth gave up its ambitions long ago, subsiding into a maze of shallow pools that stretch to every horizon. The water is the color of old bronze, stained by centuries of peat and iron, and it moves so slowly that motion becomes philosophy—a thing to be debated rather than observed.

The pools have no proper edges. Land becomes marsh becomes shallow water becomes deeper water in gradations so fine that even the residents cannot say where one ends and another begins. What looks like solid ground might sink beneath your weight; what appears to be open water might support you on a mat of floating vegetation so dense it has forgotten it isn't earth. The only certainties are the stone platforms—ancient basalt outcroppings that rise like islands, their tops worn smooth by countless generations of use.

These stones are old in a way the plateau cannot imagine. They erupted in an age when the world was younger and angrier, then spent eons being gentled by water and wind until their edges rounded, their surfaces developed a patina like dark glass. Now they serve as anchors in the shifting marsh, places where structures can be trusted to remain where they're built.

The settlement here doesn't fight its environment—it floats with it. Houses perch on rafts of woven reeds, tethered to the stone platforms by ropes that allow them to rise and fall with the seasonal floods. The builders work with what the marsh provides: rushes and reeds cut and dried and woven into walls that breathe, floors that flex, roofs that whisper in the slightest breeze. When the architecture fails, it fails gently—walls sagging rather than collapsing, roofs settling into graceful curves.

Wood is precious here, imported from distant forests or salvaged from the bog where ancient trees lie preserved in the peat. These bog oaks, black as night and hard as stone, are used sparingly—a door frame here, a roof beam there, carved implements passed down through generations until they're worn to nubs. The rest is reed and rope and patience, structures that look temporary but endure through flexibility.

The water itself is the settlement's highway and marketplace, pantry and waste system. Narrow boats slide between the houses, poled by standing figures who read the water's depth through the subtle resistance of their poles. Children swim before they walk properly, learning to move through water with an efficiency that makes them seem like different creatures when they must navigate on land.

The pools bloom with life. Lily pads carpet the surface in summer, their flowers opening to release perfume so intense it can be tasted. Beneath them, fish move in slow schools—carp grown fat on marsh plants, eels that thread through the reed roots, tiny silver things that flash and disappear. Birds nest in the margins: herons standing motionless for hours, ducks dabbling in the shallows, mysterious bitterns whose booming calls echo across the water at dusk.

The air here is thick—not thin like on the plateau, but dense with moisture and scent and sound. It carries everything: the splash of a diving bird three pools away, the scent of marsh flowers, the taste of rain before it arrives. Morning mists rise so thick that the world disappears, leaving each house isolated in its own small bubble of visibility. Evening brings different mists, warm air cooling over water, creating layers of temperature and clarity that make distances deceptive.

The peat beneath the water is a library of the dead. Cut blocks of it dry in the sun, stacked like brown bread on the stone platforms. When burned, it releases the smoke of thousand-year-old plants, acrid and sweet. But the peat also preserves—leather, wood, flesh. The pool people know to be careful when cutting too deep, for the bog remembers everything it swallows.

Time moves differently here. The pool people plan in generations, knowing that what they build must flex rather than stand firm. They plant trees their grandchildren will harvest, dig channels that will silt up and be redug by their descendants, weave houses that will rot and be replaced piece by piece until nothing original remains but the pattern. Their oldest stories tell of when the stone platforms were mountains, before the land sank and the waters rose, though whether this is history or metaphor, none can say.

The rhythm of life follows water. High-water seasons bring the houses together, creating temporary neighborhoods as platforms crowd with clustered dwellings. Low-water exposes new lands, fields of rich mud where quick crops can be planted and harvested before the floods return. The extremes are remembered in names: the Year of Joining, when floods were so high that the entire settlement became a single floating mass; the Dry Walk, when the water retreated so far that people could walk between platforms without wetting their feet.

Disease moves through water too, and the pool people have learned to read its signs—the color that means bad water, the smell that warns of rot, the particular stillness that says death lurks below. They have a hundred remedies brewed from marsh plants, teas and poultices and bitter draughts that may or may not work but at least provide the comfort of action.

At night, gases bubble up from the deep peat, igniting sometimes in brief blue flames that dance across the water's surface. The pool people call these the breaths of the drowned, though they speak of them fondly, as if the dead were simply neighbors who happen to live below rather than above the water. They leave offerings sometimes—flowers floated on lily pads, food scattered for the fish, tools deliberately dropped in specific spots where ancestors were buried in the peat.

---

The pool people are long where the plateau dwellers are compressed—stretched by generations of reaching, wading, poling boats. Their limbs extend like reeds, joints loose and flexible, movements flowing rather than abrupt. They stand hip-deep in water as comfortably as on land, their bodies having found the proportion that makes both environments natural.

Their skin tells the story of water. Permanently wrinkled from constant moisture, it has a particular toughness—not the callused hardness of stone workers but a supple resistance, like leather that's been oiled daily. They're marked by the sun in strange patterns, tan where they stand above water, pale where they're usually submerged, creating a natural waterline across their bodies that shifts seasonally with the floods.

They speak in liquid sounds, their language full of flows and rises, tones that carry across water better than consonants. Where the plateau people whisper, the pool people call—long, musical phrases that skip across the surface, encoded with location and intention. A mother calling her children home creates a different pattern of echoes than a merchant announcing wares, and everyone reads these acoustic maps instinctively.

Their eyes are narrow against the glare of sun on water, permanently crinkled at the corners, the irises often light—gray or pale brown or the green of deep water. They focus differently than mountain eyes, trained to see through reflection and refraction, to spot the shadow that means fish, the ripple that betrays a snake, the particular way reeds bend when something large moves through them.

Children are born into water—literally, as mothers often give birth while standing in the pools, the babies emerging from one fluid into another. They learn to float before they learn to fear, spending their earliest years tethered by long ropes that let them explore while keeping them safe. By three, they can identify edible plants by taste, navigate by the smell of different water zones, feel the difference between solid ground and floating mat through their bare feet.

The boat builders are the settlement's engineers, reading the grain of bog oak, calculating buoyancy with intuitive mathematics, understanding how shapes move through water. They work with adzes and patience, carving vessels that last generations with proper care. Each boat is individual—shaped for its owner's weight and reach, balanced for their particular way of standing, decorated with patterns that identify it from a distance.

Weavers work year-round, their fingers permanently stained green from rush juice. They create everything from house walls to clothes, baskets to boats (for the smallest vessels are woven rather than carved, sealed with pine pitch traded from the forests). Master weavers can create fabric so fine it's nearly waterproof, or nets so perfectly tensioned they seem to catch fish by themselves.

The healers here are herbalists and water readers both. They know which pools harbor medicinal plants, which muds draw out infection, which waters can cure and which can kill. Their apprentices spend years learning to identify plants by touch alone, as many gathering expeditions happen in pre-dawn darkness when certain flowers open. They treat the plateau people's lung diseases when stone dust finally drives them down to breathe thick, humid air—though many can't adjust and return to die among their walls.

Fishers work with an economy of motion that makes them seem lazy until you understand the patience required. They know individual fish, the great carp that have evaded capture for decades, the eels that return to the same pools year after year. Their techniques involve more waiting than chasing—traps that funnel fish naturally, weirs that work with current rather than against it, an understanding that water does most of the work if you let it.

Peat cutters are the historians and miners both. They read layers like years, knowing that this brown means a dry decade, that black speaks of floods. They find things in the peat—tools and bones and offerings—and debate whether to keep or return them. The oldest families have cutting rights to specific bogs, passed down with maps of what lies where, warnings about which areas to avoid.

Their spiritual life flows like water—no fixed ceremonies but constant small observances. They read omens in the flight patterns of birds, the way lily flowers open, the dreams that come from sleeping above certain pools. Their holiest people are the singers who can call fish, though whether this is magic or simply generations of observed correlation between certain songs and certain behaviors, no one questions too deeply.

They trade constantly with the plateau, despite the difficulty of the journey. Young people make the climb carrying smoked fish, dried rushes, medicines the stone people desperately need. They return with metal tools, worked stone, and stories of a place so dry and solid it seems like another world. Some stay, joining the water-carriers who are the only plateau dwellers who can properly appreciate flowing water. Others last a season before the weight of stone becomes unbearable and they flee back to their shifting world.

The pool people marry by binding boats together, literally lashing two vessels into one. Divorces involve careful unweaving, each partner taking back what they brought. The most stable marriages develop a particular rhythm of movement, two people poling in such harmony that their joined boat moves faster than either could manage alone. Children inherit both boats eventually, choosing which style suits them better or combining elements of both.

They count wealth in knowledge rather than objects—who knows where the best fish gather, where the sweetest rushes grow, how to read tomorrow's weather in today's water. A rich family is one with many memories, stories that encode practical information in narrative form. The poorest are those who've lost their stories through death or forgetting, forced to relearn through dangerous trial and error what others know by inheritance.

Death comes gently here, when it comes by age. The dying are taken to the sacred pools—specific places where the peat is deep and preserving, where offerings have been made for generations. They go consciously when possible, walking into water that receives them without struggle. The pool people don't grieve as the plateau people do—death is simply another kind of submersion, the dead remaining present below the surface, occasionally surrendering up a tool or ornament to remind the living they're still there.

Their madness is different too—not the pressure break of the plateau but a slow dissolution, boundaries between self and water gradually erasing. The mad become prophets of a sort, speaking the language of pools, translating between human need and water's will. They're cared for practically, given simple tasks like tending lily gardens or feeding birds, jobs that require presence but not coherence.

At night they sleep in woven hammocks suspended above the water, whole families swaying together in reed houses that creak with every movement. The sound of water is constant—drops from the roof, waves against the platform, the bubble and sigh of gases rising from peat. They dream of currents and tides, of swimming through air, of a world where everything flows and nothing needs to stand against the weight of stone.

They sing while they work, but it's different from the plateau's functional chanting. Their songs are conversations with the environment—calls that test the echo to judge distance, melodies that match the rhythm of poling, harmonies that rise and fall with the work of weaving. They have songs for every activity, and activities for every song, work and music intertwined like reeds in a basket.

The pool people pity the plateau dwellers, though they try not to show it. To live without the give of water, the softness of reeds, the constant possibility of movement—it seems like a kind of death. But they need them too, these stone-workers who create permanence, who make the tools that make their boats, who buy the fish that justify the fishing. The trade between them is more than goods—it's a circulation of possibilities, each settlement dreaming of what the other has while being unable to imagine actually living there.

Where the plateau people are compressed into density, the pool people spread like water itself—loose, adaptable, finding the shape of their container. They are patient where the others are urgent, yielding where the others resist, circular where the others are linear. They build to float rather than to last, love the temporary beauty of lily flowers that bloom and die in a day, find permanence not in structures but in patterns—the way a weave repeats, the way water always finds its level, the way a story carries truth longer than any building stands.

They are people of the between—between land and water, solid and liquid, staying and going. Their bodies mirror their environment, all length and flow and graceful adaptation. They survive not by fighting but by joining, becoming part of the marsh until the distinction between human and habitat blurs like the edge between water and land. They are not happier than the plateau people—happiness is too solid a concept for such fluid lives—but they are more content, having learned the great secret of water: that yielding is its own strength, that softness can outlast stone, that in accepting change they find the only permanence that matters.
