The Undergrowth

Three miles below the suspended gardens, where the Fountain Tree's deepest roots disappear into permanent shadow, the Undergrowth spreads across what was once valley floor and is now a landscape of twilight canyons. Here, in the space between the true ocean below and the floating waters above, life has taken different turns.

The light that reaches these depths is not light as the surface knows it—it is the memory of light, filtered through miles of water-air and the dangling roots of Uth-Mara. It arrives as a deep red glow, like ember-light, painting everything in shades of rust and wine. The organisms here have abandoned green chlorophyll for darker pigments that can harvest these lengthy wavelengths. The vegetation appears black until you look closely and see it is the deepest possible purple, the color of bruises and deep-sea trenches.

This is where the Rootwalkers make their paths.

They were human once, or something like it—cousins to those who would become the Coral-Weavers above. But when the waters rose and rose and finally lifted free of the earth entirely, the ancestors of the Rootwalkers went down instead of up. They followed the roots of the mangroves as they grew impossibly long, stretching through empty air to maintain their connection to the true sea below. Generation by generation, they descended, until the floating gardens above became legend, then myth, then forgotten entirely except in the oldest songs.

The Rootwalkers build no cities. Instead, they have become architects of negative space, carving vast halls from the living wood of the aerial roots. The roots themselves are massive here—some thick as temple columns, others spreading into cathedral groves where a single tree's descending anchors create forests hanging in air. The Walkers hollow these roots with patient precision, leaving enough living wood to maintain the flow of water upward while creating warm, dry spaces within.

Their tools are bred from boring beetles the size of housecats, whose mandibles can be directed with pheromone trails painted on the wood. The beetles eat the heartwood and excrete a resinous paste that the Rootwalkers sculpt into furniture, walkways, and the elaborate masks that mark their clan affiliations. A master rootwright might spend decades directing a single excavation, creating spiraling chambers that follow the root's natural grain, rooms that breathe with the tree's own circulation.

Inside these root-halls, the air is thick with spores and the sweet smell of decomposition. Fungal gardens carpet the floors—not the bioluminescent varieties known above, but heat-generating species that warm their chambers through slow combustion of cellulose. The Rootwalkers cultivate these with the same patience their distant cousins show with coral, creating patchwork fields of different species whose metabolisms peak at different times, maintaining a steady warmth against the abyssal chill.

Water is paradoxically scarce in this humid twilight. The roots jealously guard their upward flow, and the true ocean below is a day's dangerous climb down hanging vines that grow brittle and unreliable in the dry air. The Rootwalkers have become masters of water harvest and hoarding. They set web-nets of spider silk between the roots to catch the sparse drift of moisture from above. They tap certain roots—never the Fountain Tree's, which are sacred and forbidden—with careful incisions that weep a few drops daily into calabash gourds. They have learned to read the root-veins like maps, knowing which carry fresh water up and which return salt water down, which will share their burden and which will wither if breached.

The Walkers themselves have evolved for climbing and grasping. Their arms are long, muscles ropy and powerful, with a range of motion that allows them to brachiate through the root networks like the gibbons of lost forests. Their feet are as dexterous as hands, with opposable big toes and hardened pads that grip smooth bark. Most striking are their eyes—enormous, occupying a third of their faces, with pupils that dilate to catch every photon of the dim red light. In full darkness, they navigate by touch and the ultrasonic calls they produce with specialized larynxes, creating sonic maps of the root labyrinths.

They wear garments woven from root-fiber and spider silk, soft as water and strong as steel. The silk comes from colonies of semi-domesticated spiders they maintain in the upper reaches of their territory, creatures the size of dinner plates that have been bred for docility and production. The Walkers' children learn silk-handling before they can properly walk, developing an immunity to the mild venom through repeated exposure. Master weavers create cloaks that can catch air like wings, allowing controlled falls through the vertical forests—though true flight remains the dream of madmen and poets.

Their diet consists primarily of root-blood—the nutrient-rich sap they tap from minor roots—supplemented with fungus and the occasional harvest from above. Certain times of year, when the wind patterns shift, seed pods and air-bladders fall from the suspended gardens like rain. These harvests are festival times, when normally solitary clans gather at the great meeting roots to trade, feast, and arrange marriages. They ferment the air-bladders into a potent liquor that makes their echolocation go haywire, sending them stumbling through the roots in giggling packs, rediscovering their world through drunk sonar.

The Rootwalkers organize themselves around bloodlines that trace back to the first descent. Each clan guards specific root-territories passed down through generations, maintained through elaborate treaties and occasional quiet wars. They mark their boundaries with scent-glands they've developed at wrists and throat—each clan's signature musk immediately identifiable to any Walker, carrying information about genealogy, health, recent travels, and emotional state. To smell another's boundary marker is to read a complex chemical history.

They believe the roots are conscious, part of a vast neural network that thinks thoughts too slow and large for mobile beings to comprehend. Their shamans ingest psychoactive fungus and press their bodies against the root walls, claiming to hear the trees' conversations—water-dreams flowing upward, salt-memories descending, the endless circulation of some unimaginable arboreal intelligence. They say the Fountain Tree remembers the time before the waters rose, that its deepest roots touch stone that recalls when rain fell down instead of up.

The greatest taboo among the Rootwalkers is the cutting of a primary root—one of the great pillars that sustains the gardens above. Such an act would be catastrophic, potentially causing whole districts of Uth-Mara to sink as their support withers. The penalty is exile to the Brittle Zones, where old roots have died and the territory becomes a maze of rotting wood and unstable passages. Most exiles fall to their deaths eventually, but some survive to become the Deep Walkers, half-legendary figures who have found ways to live in the transition zone between roots and ocean.

Trade between the Undergrowth and Uth-Mara exists, though neither society fully remembers the other. Certain roots serve as trade-channels—hollowed tubes through which baskets can be raised and lowered on spider-silk cables. The Coral-Weavers send down tools of cultivated shell and bottles of distilled water. The Rootwalkers send up fungal medicines, spider silk, and the crystallized root-sap they call earthblood, prized above for its supposed ability to grant visions of solid ground. The traders never meet—goods are left at staging points, exchanges made in mutual absence. Both sides tell stories of the Others, wondering but never quite willing to traverse the miles of vertical space between their worlds.

Children sometimes dream of climbing all the way up or all the way down, of meeting their mythical cousins and learning their strange ways. But the journey is treacherous—the middle distances are prowled by things that have adapted to neither water nor air but the confusion between them. Giant aerial leeches that can drain a climber dry, colonies of proto-jellyfish that have learned to fly and hunt in packs, worse things that have no names because few survive seeing them. The wisdom of both peoples says: stay in your strata, tend your growth, trust the slow thoughts of root and reef.

At the deepest point of the Undergrowth, where the last roots disappear into the wine-dark sea below, stands the Anchor Temple. Here the shamans of all clans maintain their vigil, watching the point where root meets water, recording the tides, the temperature, the color of foam on black waves. They believe that one day the waters will drop again, that the roots will find earth instead of ocean, that the two peoples will reunite on solid ground. Others say the waters will continue to rise until even Uth-Mara drowns in sky, until the whole world is nothing but layers of water at different heights, each supporting its own unlikely life.

In the red twilight of the Undergrowth, time moves differently than in the gardens above—not slow like honey but cyclical like breath. The roots pulse with their hydraulic heartbeat. The Walkers sleep hanging from their climbing hooks, dreaming spider-dreams of webs spanning the void between worlds. And far above, so far it might as well be another universe, coral grows toward heaven, carrying its own stories of a world transformed, while the great trees remember everything and share their memories with no one but the dark.
