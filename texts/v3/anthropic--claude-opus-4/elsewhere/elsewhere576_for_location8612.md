Beyond the rim of the caldera, where the Cistern Gardens end and the world resumes its horizontal logic, the Slag Plains stretch to the horizon like a frozen metal sea. This is what remains of the volcano's final fury—millions of tons of iron-rich ejecta that cooled into a landscape of rust-red dunes and obsidian ridges. The contrast is absolute: where the Gardens weep with constant moisture, the Plains are desiccated beyond imagining. Where the caldera contains and channels life in its vertical embrace, the Plains offer only endless, indifferent distance.

The wind here carries tiny particles of metallic dust that coat everything in a fine ferrous powder. It scours and sculpts, creating formations that ring like bells when struck—organ pipes of volcanic glass that sing mournful harmonics in the constant gale. The ground itself is treacherous: what looks solid might be only a thin crust over pockets of powder fine as flour, ready to swallow the unwary. Ancient lava tubes run beneath the surface, their entrances hidden by drifts of metallic sand.

But the Plains are not empty. They host their own form of life, as strange as anything in the Gardens but born from scarcity rather than abundance. The Drifters make their homes here—nomadic bands who travel in vehicles that are part sailing ship, part electromagnetic sledge. Their craft ride the static charges that build between ground and sky, great metal masts catching lightning to power their passage across the waste. The vessels are scorched and pitted, repaired with salvage—fragments of meteor iron, pieces of the old world's satellites that fell here, drawn by the Plains' magnetic anomalies.

The Drifters themselves are the Gardens' opposite in every way. Where the Gardeners are pale and languid, the Drifters are dark and quick, their skin toughened by exposure, their movements efficient and wary. They wrap themselves in mesh suits that dissipate static, wear goggles of polished meteor glass, and speak in clipped phrases that waste no breath. Their hair is kept short or braided tight with metal beads that click warnings when electromagnetic storms approach.

They hunt the things that live in the iron dust—creatures even stranger than the caldera's fauna. Glass serpents that swim through the dunes, their bodies acting as fiber optic cables that channel sunlight into focused beams. Ferromagnetic colonies that look like rust-colored tumbleweeds but are actually millions of tiny organisms linked by magnetic fields, rolling across the Plains in search of metal to consume. Most prized are the compass beetles, whose shells naturally align with magnetic north and whose dried bodies are worth their weight in water.

The Drifters' knowledge is practical and harsh. They know which color of lightning announces a survivable storm and which means death. They can read the patterns in the dust—the way it settles after wind tells them where the stable paths lie, where the sink-holes wait. They've mapped the deep tubes by sound, dropping resonant spheres and listening to the echoes, building a three-dimensional understanding of the hollow world beneath the Plains.

Their mobile settlements cluster where the old lava tubes breach the surface, temporary cities of metal and mesh that can be struck and moved in hours. They tap into the deep magnetism, using the temperature differential between surface and depth to generate power. Their technology is crude but robust—electrical systems shielded in lead glass, mechanical computers that calculate with gears and springs, communication through modulated magnetic fields that can penetrate the metallic interference.

At night, the Plains become an alien vista. Aurora-like displays flicker where the solar wind hits the metallic particles in the air. The iron dunes glow with their own heat signature, creating mirages that show places that don't exist—or perhaps that exist elsewhere, the ferrous dust acting as some vast antenna receiving signals from distant elsewheres. The Drifters navigate by these false stars, having learned through generations which mirages are consistent enough to serve as landmarks.

The connection between the two worlds runs deeper than geography. Every hundred days, when the great water wheel completes its turn in the Cistern Gardens, the Drifters know. The electromagnetic pulse it generates can be detected across the Plains, a deep throb in their instruments. They time their trading journeys by it, arriving at the caldera's rim in caravans of static-charged vessels.

The trade is essential to both. The Drifters bring metal—pure ingots extracted from the compass beetles, rare earth elements sifted from the dust, chunks of meteor iron with properties that defy conventional metallurgy. They bring glass formed by lightning strikes, each piece unique and imbued with electrical memory. Most crucially, they bring information about the world beyond, coded in magnetic beads that the Gardens' Readers obsessively decode.

In exchange, the Gardeners provide water—not just for drinking but for the Drifters' electromagnetic engines, which use charged vapor as propellant. They trade preserved foods that can survive the Plains' desiccation, medicines derived from the caldera's strange flora, and most mysteriously, small sealed containers of the lake water with its prophetic reflections. The Drifters never speak of what they see in these portable prophecies, but they pay dearly for them.

The journey between the worlds is itself a transition. Climbing out of the caldera, the air grows thinner and drier with each meter. The last of the mist clings to travelers' clothes, evaporating as they crest the rim. The temperature drops—the Gardens' constant cool replaced by the Plains' extremes, scorching by day and freezing by night. The very quality of sound changes, from the Gardens' water-muffled quiet to the Plains' wind-scoured clarity.

Some few individuals move permanently between the worlds. Gardener children who can't tolerate the humidity flee to the Plains, their lungs adapting to the dry air, their pale skin darkening under the unfiltered sun. Aging Drifters, their bodies finally failing from years of electromagnetic exposure, descend into the caldera to spend their last years in the healing mist. These transplants are never fully accepted by their new communities but serve as living bridges, carrying knowledge and customs between the two realms.

The boundary zone itself is a place of pilgrimage. Where the rim's edge catches both the Gardens' rising mist and the Plains' scouring wind, unique phenomena occur. Plants that grow nowhere else—half-metal, half-organic things that feed on both moisture and magnetism. Birds that nest in the caldera by day hunt across the Plains by night, their bodies shifting between modes like living transformers. The salamanders sometimes climb this far, and where they meet the glass serpents, hybrid offspring result—creatures of living mercury that flow between states of matter.

Both peoples tell stories of the time before the separation, when the volcano's death created their divided world. The Gardeners sing of the last eruption as a birth, the mountain turning itself inside out to create their vertical paradise. The Drifters speak of it as a scattering, the mountain's heart spread across the Plains to seed it with magnetic life. Neither story is complete without the other.

In the deepest tubes beneath the Plains, where the Drifters fear to go, there are sealed chambers that connect to the lowest flooded levels of the caldera. Sometimes things pass through—a compass beetle appearing in the prophetic lake, a salamander found crystallized in a formation of volcanic glass. These exchanges hint at deeper connections, at systems and cycles that neither people fully understand.

The Readers in the Gardens and the eldest Drifter navigators have begun to correspond, comparing their observations. The patterns the Readers see in the salamander lights match the formations of the Drifters' aurora. The fractal designs that hurt to look at appear in both the Gardens' fauna and the Plains' crystal growths. They're beginning to suspect that their two worlds are not separate ecosystems but organs of some larger organism—the Gardens its cooling heart, the Plains its electromagnetic nervous system.

As the great wheel turns in the caldera and the static storms rage across the Plains, both peoples continue their patient adaptations. They are growing into something new, shaped by their environments but also shaping them in return. The Gardeners become more like their machines and plants. The Drifters become more like their beetles and magnetic fields. And in the space between—in the mist that becomes dust, in the water that becomes lightning—something altogether different is being born.
