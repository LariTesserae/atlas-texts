Below the Weeping Shelf, where the last of the meltwater disappears into cracks too narrow for even the Listeners to follow, the mountain opens into vast hollows. Here begins the Dry Depths—a world of desiccated silence that exists in perfect opposition to the bright wetness above.

The Thirst Keepers dwell in these arid caverns, and they are everything the Listeners are not.

Where the Listeners grew translucent, the Thirst Keepers have become opaque to the point of seeming to absorb light. Their skin has the texture and color of cured leather, deep browns and ochres with a matte finish that reflects nothing. They are broad where the Listeners are thin, compact where their upstream neighbors are elongated. Generations of water scarcity have shaped them into beings who waste nothing—not movement, not moisture, not breath.

The Dry Depths stretch through the mountain's heart in a series of interconnected chambers, each one carved by ancient waters that no longer flow. The stone here is polished smooth by eons of erosion, creating curved walls that sweep up into darkness. No ice formations interrupt these spaces. No condensation beads on the surfaces. The air is so dry it pulls moisture from lungs and eyes, making every breath a conscious act.

Crystal formations thrust from floor and ceiling—not ice, but mineral deposits left behind when the waters receded. These crystals grow in twisted spires and spreading fans, some clear as glass, others clouded with impurities that create jewel-bright colors in the rare light. The Thirst Keepers have learned to read these formations like the Listeners read their melt-pools—each crystal's growth pattern tells a story of temperature, pressure, and the ghost of ancient moisture.

The relationship between these two worlds is one of desperate interdependence. The meltwater from the Weeping Shelf, after trickling through countless cracks and fissures, eventually drips into collection pools in the highest chambers of the Dry Depths. But it arrives changed—filtered through stone and time until it carries minerals and memories from the mountain's core. This water is sacred to the Thirst Keepers, each drop accounted for, each collection pool guarded.

The Thirst Keepers measure their days not in flows but in drops. They have developed an intricate system of clay pipes and carved channels that distribute water throughout their territory with mathematical precision. Nothing is left to chance. Each family receives their allotment based on careful calculations involving age, health, contribution to the collective, and the phase of the moon (for they believe lunar cycles affect the rate of drip from above).

Their eyes have evolved enormous—dark pools that can gather the faintest light in the deep chambers. Unlike the Listeners' horizontal slits, the Thirst Keepers' eyes are round and protuberant, with multiple layers of reflective tissue that create an unsettling shine in darkness. They see heat as clearly as light, navigating their pitch-black world by the warm signatures of living bodies and the cooler patterns of stone.

Where the Listeners communicate through touch and tap, the Thirst Keepers have developed a language of echoes. Their voices have dropped to subsonic frequencies that travel vast distances through stone. They speak in booms and rumbles that would be felt rather than heard by surface dwellers. A conversation between Thirst Keepers resembles seismic activity—words that make crystals ring and dust fall from ceiling cracks.

The two peoples meet at the Middle Gates—a series of chambers that exist at the intersection of wet and dry, where the last of the surface moisture meets the beginning of the deep drought. Here, water traders from below exchange crystals, dried fungi, and the precious "void-pearls" (spherical stones that form in the deepest caves) for measured quantities of meltwater, phosphorescent lichen, and news from the bright world above.

These trades are conducted in utter silence. The Listeners cannot produce the low frequencies the Thirst Keepers use, and the Keepers' throat structures make the high-pitched tap-language impossible. Instead, they've developed an elaborate gesture language performed with exaggerated slowness—the Listeners moving too quickly for Keeper eyes to track easily, the Keepers moving with such deliberate conservation that Listeners grow anxious watching them.

The Thirst Keepers cultivate their own ecosystems in the darkness. Blind crickets the size of rats populate their caverns, feeding on mineral deposits and each other. The Keepers farm these creatures in stone pens, their chirping (pitched too high for Keeper ears but creating vibrations they can feel) indicating health and readiness for harvest. Fungal gardens grow in carefully tended patches where the humidity is artificially maintained using their precious water resources—pale, ghostly growths that provide their primary nutrition.

Children of the Dry Depths undergo a ritual vastly different from their upstream cousins. At adolescence, they are sent into the deepest caves with a single water ration that must last three days. They must find their way to the Absence Chamber—a spherical void so perfect that no echo returns, where even the Keepers' deep voices are swallowed by silence. There they must sit in absolute sensory deprivation until they experience what the Keepers call "the True Thirst"—a spiritual awakening to the preciousness of every molecule of water.

The Keepers' architecture reflects their relationship with scarcity. Buildings are carved directly into stone walls, with thick doors that seal hermetically to prevent moisture loss. Public spaces are vast and empty—conservation of resources extends to conservation of decoration. Beauty here is found in perfect function, in the elegant curve of a water channel that loses not a drop to evaporation, in the precise angles of crystal gardens that direct the faint light to where it's needed most.

They keep time by the Great Count—an enormous cascade of measuring vessels that fill drop by drop, marking hours, days, years, and generations. The sound of water droplets hitting stone rings through their central chamber at perfectly regular intervals, a liquid heartbeat that synchronizes their civilization. The Keeper whose family tends the Great Count holds a position of supreme honor, for if the rhythm falters, all of society loses its temporal anchor.

The Thirst Keepers know of the Speaking Pool far above, though none have seen it. In their stories, it represents the ultimate paradox—infinite water that drives men mad, prophecy that drowns wisdom in excess. They have their own oracle: the Dry Mirror, a perfectly still pool of heavy water that lies in the deepest chamber. It shows no reflections, absorbs all light, and those who stare into it too long report visions of places where water has never existed—deserts beyond imagination, voids between stars, the dry husks of potential futures.

Their bodies process water with extraordinary efficiency. They rarely urinate, their systems extracting every possible nutrient and molecule of moisture. Their tears have evolved away—they show grief through a low harmonic humming that makes the crystals around them sing in sympathy. Even their blood is thick and slow-moving, their hearts beating at a pace that would concern any surface physician.

The two peoples—Listeners and Thirst Keepers—form an unconscious whole, though neither fully grasps this. The Listeners' constant flow and change creates the very water the Keepers depend upon. The Keepers' preservation and careful distribution ensures that water continues its journey through the mountain rather than pooling and stagnating. Together, they are the circulation system of the mountain itself—one the arteries, one the veins, forever cycling resources between abundance and scarcity, light and dark, sound and silence.

Sometimes, rarely, a child is born in the Middle Gates to parents from both worlds. These individuals, called the Balanced, can see in both spectrums, speak in both languages, survive in both environments. They become the true traders, the bridge-builders, the ones who ensure the precarious symbiosis continues. But they belong fully to neither world, forever standing at the threshold, translating between the bright flow above and the dark preservation below.

This is how the mountain lives: not as stone and ice alone, but as a complete organism with the Listeners as its rushing blood and the Thirst Keepers as its conserving bones, each people essential, each completely alien to the other, bound together by the water that flows eternally between them.
