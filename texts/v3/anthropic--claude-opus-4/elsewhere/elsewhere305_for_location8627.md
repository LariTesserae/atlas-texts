Below, where the mountain meets the valley floor, the world changes completely.

The Substrate Gardens spread for miles—a vast fungal metropolis that exists in permanent shadow. Here, the overflow from the Vertical City's processing settles like invisible snow, accumulating over centuries into something between soil and flesh. The ground itself lives, breathing through billions of pores, processing what filters down from above into new forms of existence.

Nothing here grows upward. Instead, life burrows down and spreads outward in vast mycelial networks. What appear to be hills are actually the crowns of massive fungal bodies, their bulk hidden underground in cavern-systems of their own making. The surface ripples with slow movement—expansion and contraction on a geological timescale, punctuated by sudden blooms that can carpet square miles in a matter of hours before withering back to nothing.

The Gardeners who tend this place are as different from the Tenders above as soil from air. Where the Tenders stretched tall and lean, the Gardeners compress—bodies broad and low, skin thickened to leather, eyes designed for the subtle variations of darkness rather than twilight. They move through their domain on all fours as often as upright, fingers splayed to read the chemical signals in the substrate, to taste the health of their vast charge.

They live in burrows lined with specially cultivated fungi that glow with bioluminescent pulses. Each Gardener maintains their own strain, passed down through generations, the light-patterns as unique as fingerprints. They communicate through these lights as much as through sound—complex conversations flickering between burrow-mouths in the eternal dusk, visible as a constellation of ground-stars to anyone looking down from above.

The relationship between high and low is absolute but poorly understood by either side. What the Vertical City harvests from the air must go somewhere. The Tenders speak vaguely of "processing," but the end result of their great industrial metabolism flows downward—not as waste but as potential, as the raw material for the Substrate Gardens' impossible ecology.

It falls as particles finer than dust, invisible to the naked eye, settling so gradually that a human lifetime might see only millimeters of accumulation. But the substrate knows. It tastes each particle, sorts it, incorporates it into the vast organic computation that is the garden's true purpose. The fungi process information as much as matter, their networks encoding patterns too complex for human comprehension.

The Gardeners have learned to read these patterns in fragments. They know that certain accumulations produce certain blooms—that a series of crimson sporocarps might follow a particular quality of downfall, that the bioluminescent displays pulse in rhythm with the mountain winds far above. They maintain channels in the substrate to direct the flow, to encourage certain growths, to prevent others that experience has taught them lead to collapse or toxicity.

The journey between the two realms is undertaken rarely, and always with ceremony. The Vertical Road—part carved steps, part metal infrastructure, part living tissue—switchbacks down the cliff face through zones of increasing pressure and humidity. Travelers must stop at way-stations to allow their bodies to adjust, to shed or don clothing as the temperature rises, to prepare their lungs for the thick, spore-laden air below.

At the midpoint stands the Exchange—a structure that belongs fully to neither world. Built into a cave system where the cliff begins to gentle into slope, it serves as both physical and cultural interface. Here, representatives meet to trade not goods but information. The Gardeners bring samples—spores that carry encoded data about the deep networks, crystallized nutrients that only the processed air can produce. The Tenders bring readings—measurements of the invisible currents, samples of moth-wing scales that carry their own chemical messages.

The Exchange is staffed by the Between-People, those who've spent so long traveling the Vertical Road that they belong to neither realm fully. Their bodies show the strain—lungs that wheeze in both thin and thick air, eyes that struggle in both twilight and darkness, skin that can't quite decide whether to thicken or thin. But they understand things others cannot, serving as translators not just of language but of worldview.

Through the Between-People, knowledge flows: The Gardeners learn that certain fungal blooms correlate with disruptions in the air currents above. The Tenders discover that their moths' mating cycles synchronize with fruiting events below they never suspected. Both sides piece together fragments of a larger pattern—a metabolism that encompasses the entire mountain, from wind-scoured peak to fungal depths.

In the deepest reaches of the Substrate Gardens, where the pressure of accumulated material has compressed the oldest growths into something approaching stone, the Gardeners maintain the Memory Chambers. Here, the fungal networks have achieved such density that they can store and replay patterns from the deep past. A skilled Garden-Keeper can coax these ancient mycelia to fruit with spores that carry chemical echoes of the mountain's history.

These memories speak of a time before the separation, when the mountain's inhabitants moved freely between high and low, when the distinction between air-harvest and earth-process hadn't yet crystallized into separate cultures. They hint at a purpose behind the great system—something about preparation, about accumulation toward a critical mass, about a transformation that requires both the aerial harvest and the terrestrial processing to complete.

But the memories are fragmented, corrupted by time and the inevitable errors of biological storage. What remains clear is only the imperative to continue—to tend above, to garden below, to maintain the flow between them.

Some few individuals attempt the full circuit—born in one realm, traveling to the other, trying to understand the whole. Most fail, bodies unable to fully adapt, minds unable to reconcile the fundamentally different ways of being. But a rare few succeed in developing dual citizenship, spending decades above and decades below, their bodies changing with each transition, becoming living bridges between the two worlds.

These Circuit-Walkers speak of dreams where they see the mountain from impossible perspectives—from inside, as if the stone were transparent, revealing the true scope of the system. They describe vast chambers within the mountain where neither air nor earth dominates, where the harvested material undergoes transformations that produce neither the factory-glow above nor the fungal-pulse below, but something else entirely. They tell of feeling the mountain's heartbeat, slow and deep, and knowing with certainty that the entire system—cities, gardens, and all—is itself alive, a single organism of incomprehensible scale and purpose.

The moths know. This becomes clear to anyone who observes them carefully enough. They carry messages between the realms, their migrations encoding information in patterns too subtle for human perception. They feed above and breed below, their caterpillars growing fat on fungal growths before metamorphosis carries them up the cliff face to begin the cycle again. In their genes, in their behaviors, in the chemical signals they emit and detect, they bind the system together in ways the human inhabitants can only partially perceive.

During the rare aligned ceremonies—when the Tenders' Accounting coincides with the Gardeners' Great Fruiting—observers stationed at the Exchange report phenomena that suggest the deeper unity. The factory-glow above pulses in rhythm with the bioluminescence below. The moths form migration patterns that, viewed from the proper angle, resolve into symbols neither culture recognizes but both somehow understand. The mountain itself seems to breathe, air flowing up and substrate settling down in perfect reciprocal rhythm.

After these events, both Tenders and Gardeners return to their work with renewed purpose, though they couldn't articulate why. They tend and garden with the patience of those who know they're part of something larger, even if that something remains forever beyond their comprehension. The system continues, as it has for countless generations, as it will for countless more, processing the invisible into the manifest, the aerial into the terrestrial, the potential into the actual.

And somewhere within the mountain, in chambers no human has seen, something accumulates. Something waits. Something approaches completion, one mote of processed air, one spore of informed fungus at a time. The mountain is patient. The system is patient. And in their patience, perhaps, lies their purpose.
