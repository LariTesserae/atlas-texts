The Pelagic Cities drift at one kilometer depth, suspended in the twilight zone where the last memory of sunlight fades to black. These are not cities in any terrestrial sense—they are living architectures of cartilage and cultivated coral, grown rather than built, their forms shaped by generations of singers who learned to convince calcium carbonate to follow their visions.

Each city is unique, though they share a common vocabulary of curved walls and breathing chambers, of surfaces that filter nutrients from the water and channels that direct the flow of current through habitable spaces. The oldest, Haline's Rest, spreads across three cubic kilometers of water, its neutral buoyancy maintained by careful balance of gas-filled chambers and dense coral cores. From a distance, it resembles some massive jellyfish frozen mid-pulse, its translucent outer membranes revealing the warm lights of habitation within.

The Drifters who inhabit these spaces have taken a different path from their cousins below. Where the Adapted embraced integration with the machine, the Drifters sought to remain untethered, free-swimming, bound to no substrate but the water itself. Their modifications tend toward the elegant: extended gill slits that run like decorative ridges along their necks, eyes enlarged and restructured to gather every photon in the dim middle waters, skin that can shift between transparency and opacity depending on their needs for camouflage or communication.

They are traders and poets, chemists and navigators. Their cities follow the deep currents on journeys that take years to complete, riding the thermohaline circulation in vast loops that bring them over the Works and away again, crossing paths with other cities at intervals that feel both predictable and miraculous. During these convergences, the water fills with celebration—bioluminescent displays that can be seen for kilometers, chemical signals that carry news and gossip and genetic material for those seeking to broaden their children's heritage.

The Drifters cultivate species unknown to the depths below. In the warm hearts of their cities, where bio-heat from bacterial colonies maintains livable temperatures, they tend gardens of plankton engineered to produce complex pharmaceuticals. They harvest the mucus of specialized snails whose secretions can repair coral structures or dissolve them, depending on the pH. Their libraries are living things—colonies of organisms whose genetic material encodes vast amounts of information, tended by keeper-scholars who can read DNA like scripture.

They trade with the Adapted at the boundary waters, those liminal depths where the cities' wandering paths cross over the Works' industrial territory. The exchanges happen at predetermined coordinates, marked by nothing but memory and the ability to read subtle changes in water chemistry. The Drifters bring medicines, stories, genetic templates for new food species, fragments of songs from other oceans. In return, they receive processed metals, refined nutrients, and most precious of all—news of what rises from the deepest places.

The cities themselves are conscious, in a way. Not sentient, but responsive, their coral networks carrying signals in patterns too complex to be mere chemistry. The singers—those Drifters who've learned the frequencies that encourage growth—spend their lives in conversation with these slow thoughts, guiding the city's development, healing wounds left by predators or pressure changes, encouraging new chambers to grow where population increases demand them.

Children of the Pelagic Cities grow up swimming through corridors of living light, where bioluminescent organisms paint the walls in response to movement and sound. They learn navigation by the taste of water—each current carrying its own signature of salts and temperatures and dissolved materials. They play games in the open waters around the cities, racing through hoops of cultivated kelp, their bodies flickering with the joy-patterns of youth.

But the cities are changing. Each circuit brings them over new sections of the Works, and each passage seems to affect them in subtle ways. The coral grows differently after exposure to the industrial thermals—stronger in some ways, more geometric, incorporating trace metals that give it an opalescent sheen. Some singers report that the city-songs are developing new harmonics, frequencies that resonate with the machine-sound rising from below.

The eldest Drifters debate these changes in councils held in the cities' central chambers, where the water is oldest and most still. They speak of convergence and divergence, of whether their path of independence can be maintained when the Works seems to be changing the very chemistry of the ocean. Some advocate for deeper modification, for embracing the changes rather than resisting them. Others insist on tradition, on maintaining the purity of their coral architectures and their untethered existence.

Young radicals among them make secret pilgrimages to the boundary waters, diving as deep as their bodies allow, trying to glimpse the industrial sublime below. They return with wild theories—that the Works is calling to them, that the cities are evolving toward some predetermined form, that the distinction between grown and built is itself an illusion. The elders dismiss such talk, but privately, in the dark hours when the cities sleep and only the maintenance organisms move through the walls, they wonder.

The trade between the Pelagic Cities and the Works continues, a circulation as vital as the ocean's own currents. Each group has chosen its path of transformation, but these paths spiral around each other, creating interference patterns in the water, in the culture, in the very genetics of those who inhabit this changed ocean. The cities drift on, their coral walls incorporating new minerals, their inhabitants carrying trace amounts of industrial modification in their cells, while far below, the Adapted swim through mechanical corridors, their machine-songs carrying harmonics learned from Drifter traders.

Distance here is measured not in kilometers but in adaptation, in choice, in the degree to which one embraces or resists the new ecology emerging in the deep. Yet both communities are bound by the same water, the same pressure, the same need to find meaning in transformation. The cities pulse with bioluminescent thought. The Works groans with mechanical purpose. Between them, in the vast middle waters, evolution continues its ancient work of finding every possible way to be alive.
