The Meridian Exchange floats between the seventy-third and seventy-fourth floors.

This is not imprecision but fact: the building's elevators rise smooth as thought through seventy-two levels of commerce and calculation, then shudder to a halt. The buttons for 73 and 74 exist, worn smooth by generations of fingers, but pressing them achieves nothing. To reach the Exchange requires knowing that between 19:17 and 19:44 each evening, pressing both simultaneously while holding a valid transit token against the emergency speaker grille will cause the elevator to slip sideways out of ordinary space, arriving at a platform that exists in the gap between what was built and what was planned.

The Exchange occupies a floor that was designed but never constructed, a phantom limb of architecture that persists in the building's autonomous nervous system. The original blueprints, water-stained and forgotten in a sub-basement archive, show it clearly: a full floor dedicated to "pneumatic infrastructure and vertical transit interchange." The developers, running over budget, simply skipped it, building floor 74 directly atop 72, trusting that no one would notice the missing numbers in a building so tall. They were mostly right. But the building's automated systems, programmed according to the original plans, continue to allocate power, climate control, and even elevator service to a floor that exists only as an absence made manifest.

Into this architectural lacuna, the traders came.

They deal not in stocks or bonds but in something far more fundamental: the buying and selling of distance itself. In the Exchange's vast, twilight-lit floor—the windows open onto a view of the city that exists at no single height, a parallax smear of all possible seventy-third floor vistas—traders huddle around terminals that glow with equations describing the relationship between here and there. Markets exist for the distance between lovers separated by quarantine. Futures can be purchased on the gap between what was said and what was meant. Complex derivatives track the separation between the cistern far below and the sky it will never see.

The traders themselves are difficult to perceive directly. They wear the standard uniforms of their profession—crisp suits that seem to exist at a slight remove from the bodies within them, as if the fabric occupies a parallel plane precisely one millimeter to the left of reality. Their faces are temporally smeared, showing all possible expressions simultaneously: the focused squint of calculation, the wide eyes of sudden realization, the slack jaw of devastating loss, superimposed into a blur of human feeling. To look at one directly induces a kind of vertigo, so most business is conducted through peripheral vision and implication.

The commodity they trade—distance—arrives through pneumatic tubes that riddle the space like a brass cardiovascular system. These tubes connect to elsewhere: to the gaps between floorboards in penthouses, to the spaces between words in love letters, to the precise interval separating the cistern's steam from the condensation it becomes. Capsules shoot through the tubes at irregular intervals, containing not physical objects but carefully measured abstractions: the seventeen centimeters between a mother's reaching hand and her child's face at the moment she decided to leave, the three-day gulf between sending a message and regretting it, the immeasurable yet finite distance between what the steam-creature is and what it might imagine itself to be.

These distances are refined, processed, packaged into standard units. A "meter" on the Exchange floor has nothing to do with physical measurement—it refers to the standard unit of emotional separation, calibrated against the original distance between the builders' dreams and their budget reality. Traders shout orders in a pidgin of mathematics and longing: "Selling thirty meters of anticipation at seven-point-three!" "Buy order for sixteen units of parallel lives, standard divergence!"

The trading floor never closes because time moves differently in architectural non-space. The clocks mounted on pillars throughout the Exchange show every possible time between 19:17 and 19:44, the eternal twilight of access hours. Traders work in shifts that last subjectively for days but occupy no time at all in the floors above and below. They survive on vending machine coffee from machines that were never installed, eating sandwiches from a cafeteria that exists only as an allocation in the building's phantom budget.

At the center of the floor stands the primary board, a massive display showing the aggregate flows of distance through the city. Lines of light trace connections between every gap, every separation, every measured absence. The cistern appears as a steady pulse of deep red—a reliable producer of the distance between intention and abandonment, between design and entropy. The Exchange itself shows as a knot of silver lines, trading in its own impossibility, packaging and repackaging the distance between what is and what was meant to be.

The traders work with desperate efficiency, knowing that their entire market depends on a clerical error, a skipped floor, a system that believes in something that was never built. They are archaeologists of intention, harvesting the gaps between plan and execution that riddle every human construction. The pneumatic tubes sing with commerce as distances are bought, sold, bundled, derived into instruments so complex that no single trader understands their full implications.

In the depths below, the steam-creature drifts through its prescribed patterns, unaware that each pulse of its attention, each moment of almost-communication, is being packaged and sold seventy floors above. The distance between what it is and what it might want to be—if it could want—is a steady commodity, reliable as breath.

And between them, in the building's electrical bones, messages flow: service requests for floors that don't exist, climate readings from rooms that were never built, maintenance schedules for the eternal servicing of absence. The building dreams of its missing piece, and in dreaming, makes it real enough to trade in, real enough to float between what is and what should have been, forever conducting business in the gap between intention and form.
