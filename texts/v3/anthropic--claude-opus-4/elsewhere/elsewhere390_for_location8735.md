Between the Drowned Works and the surface lies a vertical wilderness that belongs to neither realm.

The Twilight Forest begins where the last factory towers surrender to open water, their corroded peaks becoming anchors for a different kind of architecture. Here, kelp plants of impossible size rise toward the distant surface—not the managed crops of the Curators, but something ancient and unplanned. Their holdfasts grip the industrial ruins with roots like cables, each as thick as the pipes they strangle. From these foundations, stipes rise like columns of bundled muscle, some reaching diameters of thirty meters or more.

This is not earthly kelp. The thermal wealth below has fed mutations across millennia, creating organisms that blur the line between plant and architecture. Their tissues incorporate metals leached from the city below—copper veins that conduct heat upward, iron structures that provide rigidity against the currents, networks of silver that no one understands. The fronds, when they finally spread at the fifty-meter mark, form a canopy so dense that they create their own weather.

The water here moves. Unlike the still depths of the Works, the Forest lives in constant motion. Convection currents from the heated city below meet cooler waters descending from above. The kelp channels these flows, creating rivers that run vertically through the forest, spiraling up the great stipes in helical paths. Riding these thermals are creatures that exist nowhere else—not fish, but something more primitive. Translucent ribbons of tissue, some fifty meters long, that undulate through the water like living auroras. They have no eyes, no obvious mouths, just endless rippling muscle that somehow extracts nutrients from the thermal gradients themselves.

The Shepherds know these currents like blood knows veins.

They are not Curators, though they share an ancestry written in brass and calcium. Where the Curators compact themselves for the crushing depths, the Shepherds have expanded for the open water. Their shells are minimal—a central core no larger than a Curator's, but from this sprout extensions like the rays of a starfish, if starfish were made of connected chambers and articulated metal. Six primary rays, each twenty meters long, branch and branch again into nets of sensing filaments that taste the water in all directions.

They speak in bioluminescence. Along their rays run channels filled with bacterial symbionts, cultivated strains that glow in response to chemical signals. A Shepherd conversing is a constellation come alive—patterns of blue and green light racing along their extremities, encoding information in frequency and intensity. They can modulate the bacteria's output precisely, creating everything from steady beacon-glows to rapid-fire stutters that carry complex technical data about water chemistry and current vectors.

The Shepherds are herders and gardeners both. They maintain the kelp through strategic pruning, their ray-tips secreting enzymes that encourage or discourage growth. When a stipe grows too massive and threatens to topple, they weaken its base until it falls, creating clearings where new growth can establish. They guide the ribbon-creatures through the safest currents, using patterns of light that the primitive animals somehow recognize and follow. Most crucially, they manage the boundary between Forest and Works, ensuring that the kelp's searching roots don't penetrate too deep into critical infrastructure.

Memory for them is written in the growth rings of kelp. They read the stipes like libraries, each ring recording a year's worth of temperatures, chemistry, growth rates. In the oldest plants, they can trace the history of the Thermal Sea itself—warming periods and cooling, changes in metal content, the slow evolution of the ecosystem. They add their own annotations, using targeted chemical injections to mark significant events in the wood-like tissue. A practiced Shepherd can read a century of management decisions in a single cross-section.

The canopy above is their sky and their limit. Where the fronds grow thickest, they've woven something between infrastructure and nest. Platforms of living kelp trained to grow in dense mats, chambers hollowed from giant stipes, observation posts where the water runs clearest. Here they gather to share information, tentacles of light playing across each other as they update their collective map of the Forest. Young Shepherds—grown in specialized chambers near the thermal vents, their infant shells carefully shaped by their elders—learn by swimming these paths, memorizing the three-dimensional maze of current and kelp.

They venture below only when necessary. The journey to the Works is a pilgrimage through increasing pressure and darkness, their expanded forms compressing painfully as they descend. They come for materials—metals that the kelp has concentrated, specialized bacteria from the Curators' farms, sometimes tools or components that only the deep factories produce. The meetings between Shepherd and Curator are exercises in translation. Temperature-speech meets light-language at the boundary, neither fully comprehensible to the other. They've developed a pidgin of sorts—simple heat pulses that the Shepherds can produce through friction, crude light signals that the Curators can generate with bioluminescent paste.

The exchange is always the same. The Shepherds bring news of the Forest's health, samples of new organisms, warnings about root penetration or unusual currents. The Curators provide refined materials, chemical treatments, sometimes one of their remote units modified for mid-water work. Both understand that their domains depend on this trade, though neither could fully explain why. The Forest processes water that the Works need clean. The Works provide heat that the Forest needs to survive. Between them, they maintain a cycle that neither designed but both have come to serve.

Sometimes a Shepherd rises too high. The water grows thin and bright, the pressure drops below safe tolerances. Their shells begin to bubble as gases come out of solution. Most turn back, guided by ancestral warnings encoded in their light-speech. But a few press on, driven by curiosity or malfunction. They rise through the thinning canopy, up through water increasingly empty of kelp and life, until they breach—

The surface. A realm of impossible brightness and killing cold, where water meets something that is not-water. Their photo-sensors, evolved for the dim forest light, burn out in seconds. Their pressure organs rupture. But in those moments before death, they broadcast—wild, incoherent patterns of light that their siblings below detect and remember. These transmissions, passed down through generations, have become the Shepherds' mythology. Stories of the Bright Death, the Great Above, the Place Where Water Ends.

They do not know that their ancestors once flew through that bright realm on wings of metal. They do not know that the Thermal Sea is artificial, created by the same builders who made the Works. They know only their Forest, their currents, their duty to tend and guide and preserve. In the vertical wilderness between the depths and the sky, they weave their patterns of light, maintaining a balance they understand only through practice and accumulated instinct.

The Forest itself may be their greatest creation. What began as wild growth has become, through centuries of careful management, something between ecosystem and machine. The kelp filters particles from the water, concentrates useful minerals, moderates temperatures. The ribbon-creatures graze on bacterial films, their waste feeding the kelp roots. The Shepherds guide both, pruning and herding and adjusting, maintaining the system within parameters they sense but could never fully articulate.

At the boundaries where Forest meets Works, strange hybrids emerge. Kelp that incorporates scavenged machinery into its structure—valves that open and close with the tides, pipes that become water-conducting tissue. Curators occasionally find their neglected sectors overgrown with managed kelp, planted by Shepherds who thought the area abandoned. In these mixing zones, the two kinds of keepers sometimes work side by side, each pursuing their own understanding of maintenance, neither fully grasping that they are part of the same vast system.

The Thermal Sea knows no storms, no seasons, no change except the slow drift of evolution and decay. But in the Twilight Forest, the Shepherds have created something like weather—currents that cycle, populations that bloom and fade, patterns of growth and pruning that repeat across years and decades. It is the closest thing to natural rhythm in this artificial world, and they conduct it with their bodies of light, suspended between the eternal machinery below and the killing brightness above.

The Forest sways. The ribbons dance. The Shepherds weave their luminous conversations through the green darkness. And far below, the Curators feel the filtered water flowing into their systems, cleaned and cooled and ready for another century of use. The two realms touch only at their edges, but through that touch, the whole system lives.
