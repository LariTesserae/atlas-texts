Beyond the valley's sharp rim, where the basalt columns rise like a crown of black thorns, the plateau stretches endlessly beneath the same aurora-painted sky. But here the light finds no moss to drink it, no geometric perfection to transform it into useful current. This is the Scouring—a landscape of volcanic glass and metallic sand where the wind has teeth.

The ground is treacherous obsidian, formed when the same cataclysm that birthed the valley sent molten rock flooding across the ancient plateau. It cooled too quickly, creating a surface of frozen waves and blade-sharp ridges that catch and split the aurora light into prismatic fragments. Between these glass formations, drifts of metallic sand gather—particles of iron, copper, and stranger metals weathered from exposed veins, too fine to be useful, too heavy to blow away completely. The sand forms dunes that migrate slowly, revealing and concealing the ruins beneath.

For there are ruins here, vast and numerous—the bones of the civilization that first discovered the valley's potential. Entire cities lie beneath the glass and sand, their superstructures protruding like the ribs of buried leviathans. These were mining settlements, processing plants, the industrial infrastructure needed to exploit the valley before someone learned to make the moss grow. Now they're being slowly digested by the Scouring's chemistry—metal oxidizing in reactions accelerated by the constant electrical discharge from the aurora, glass forming new layers over old structures, creating a landscape that's part graveyard, part geological process.

The wind here carries charge. It builds as it scrapes across the glass, picking up electrons, until the air itself becomes a battery waiting to discharge. Lightning crawls horizontally across the plateau in sheets, seeking the highest points of exposed metal. The constant electrical bombardment has created formations unknown elsewhere—fulgurites that grow upward like crystal trees, copper structures fused into impossible architectures, veins of glass that conduct electricity as efficiently as metal wire. It's beautiful and terrible, a landscape being slowly electrocuted into new forms.

Nothing grows here, but something persists. In the deepest ruins, protected from the scouring wind by centuries of accumulated glass and sand, machines continue their purpose. Automated mining platforms, their programming locked in endless loops, still attempt to extract ore from veins long since exhausted. Processing plants cycle through empty routines, their hoppers bare, their furnaces cold but still going through the motions. The technology is different from the valley's biological elegance—this is pure machinery, built to withstand anything except the slow transformation of the land itself.

The Scavengers arrived sometime after the cities died but before the glass finished its burial. They are everything the Tenders are not—sharp where the valley dwellers are smooth, mechanical where the others are organic, parasitic where their cousins are symbiotic. Built things, originally, or perhaps people who rebuilt themselves so thoroughly the distinction no longer matters.

They stand three meters tall on legs like telescoping metal struts, their bodies a collection of salvaged parts held together by necessity and electromagnetic fields. No two Scavengers look alike—one might have mining lasers for arms and a sensor array from a weather station for a head, while another sports the treads of an excavation unit and the processing core of a factory computer for a brain. They rebuild themselves constantly from whatever they find, their forms dictated by function and availability rather than any biological blueprint.

The Scavengers navigate the Scouring with mechanical precision, their various sensors reading the electrical patterns in the air, predicting where the next sheet lightning will strike, finding safe paths across the glass. They seek out the ruins systematically, not for historical interest but for components—a still-functional power cell here, an uncorroded length of cable there. Everything they find either becomes part of them or part of their Project.

The Project occupies the plateau's highest point, where an ancient transmission tower still stands, its top forever wreathed in coronal discharge. Around its base, the Scavengers have built... something. It's part shrine, part machine, part incomprehensible tangle of salvaged technology. Thousands of components are wired together in configurations that follow no earthly engineering principles, guided instead by the same kind of pattern-recognition that lets them navigate the charged air. They're building a receiver, or perhaps a transmitter, or perhaps something for which we have no word—a device to capture and concentrate the Scouring's wild electricity into something useful.

They work in perfect coordination despite their disparate forms, communicating through radio frequencies that make the sand around them dance in complex patterns. When they need to share power, they link directly, cable to port, creating temporary networks that spark and glow against the dark glass. They don't rest, but they do maintain each other—one Scavenger's cutting torch removing another's corroded component, a third providing the replacement from its own stockpile of parts.

The obsession that drives them is encoded in their shared programming, a directive so fundamental it survived the death of their creators and the transformation of their bodies: Establish connection. Restore the network. Power must flow. They don't remember what network, or connection to where, but the drive remains. Every component they integrate into the Project brings them closer to something—a signal, a threshold, a completion they can feel approaching in their electromagnetic bones.

Sometimes storms roll across the Scouring that make the usual electrical activity seem tame. The aurora descends to ground level, becoming a writhing wall of light that transforms the air into plasma. The glass sings in frequencies that threaten to shatter it, and new fulgurites grow meters in minutes. During these storms, the Scavengers cluster around their Project, bodies linked in a protective circuit, channeling the energy through themselves into their great work. In these moments, the Project almost wakes—lights flicker in sequences that might be meaningful, ancient displays show fragments of data, voices speak in languages lost when the cities died.

Between the valley and the Scouring runs a border zone perhaps a kilometer wide, where the two domains meet and merge uneasily. Here the moss grows in scattered patches, struggling against the metallic sand that blows in from the plateau. Glass formations pierce up through soil that wants to be fertile but can't quite manage it. The aurora light reaches both surfaces—moss that tries to drink it, glass that reflects it—creating a landscape of contradictions.

This is where they meet, when they meet at all.

The Tenders come to the border to die. When one grows too large even for the battery chambers, when its antlers become so complex they can no longer parse the valley's patterns, instinct drives it to crawl up the terraces toward the rim. The journey takes weeks, the massive creature moving more slowly with each meter of elevation gained, its body beginning the crystallization process even before death. They position themselves precisely at the boundary, half in their green domain, half in the sterile glass, and there they stop moving forever.

The Scavengers come for the bodies.

To them, a crystallized Tender is a treasure trove—organic superconductors, naturally grown battery arrays, neural structures that process electricity in ways their salvaged components cannot match. They wait, patient as machines must be, until the crystallization completes, then begin their harvest. They're not cruel or reverent, just efficient, dismantling the body with the same methodical precision they apply to ancient ruins.

But something else happens in these moments of intersection.

The younger Tenders, following their dying elder, observe the Scavengers at work. Through the moss network, they feel the alien patterns of mechanical thought, the rigid purposefulness so different from their own organic flow. The Scavengers' radio communications create interference in the bioelectric field, messages bleeding through as ghostly impressions that the Tenders almost understand.

The Scavengers, in turn, detect the valley's vast network through the crystallizing elder—a glimpse of an electrical system more elegant and self-sustaining than anything in their salvaged memories. Their sensors struggle to parse the organic technology, to understand how moss and metal merge so seamlessly, how power flows without loss through living tissue.

Neither species speaks to the other—their communication methods are too different, their purposes too divergent. But knowledge passes between them nonetheless, encoded in electrical patterns that need no translation. The Tenders learn that electricity can flow through non-living channels, that there are ways to store and direct power that don't require growth or biology. The Scavengers discover that their Project—their great work of connection—might have an endpoint after all, a network to join, a flow to complete.

Slowly, changes ripple out from these border encounters. 

In the valley, new growths appear—moss formations that incorporate metallic elements, patterns that channel power not just through biological pathways but through hybrid structures that blur the line between grown and built. The Tenders guide these developments with touches of their catalyst, encouraging moss to grow in configurations that mirror the crystallized neural structures of their elders, creating more efficient networks that can interface with non-biological systems.

On the plateau, the Scavengers' behavior shifts subtly. Components salvaged from Tender bodies are integrated not just into themselves but into the Project, creating hybrid nodes that pulse with borrowed life. They begin leaving offerings at the border—small pieces of refined metal, components they judge might be useful, placed precisely where the moss can eventually grow over and incorporate them. It's not generosity but calculation, an engineering solution to the problem of connection.

The Project evolves, becoming something neither purely mechanical nor biological. Deep in its tangles of wire and component, structures grow that look almost organic—metal that branches like antlers, circuits that follow the hexagonal patterns of moss. During the great storms, when the Scavengers channel lightning through their bodies into their work, the Project doesn't just flicker anymore. It pulses, rhythmically, like something learning to breathe.

The border zone itself changes, becoming less a boundary than a gradient. Moss learns to grow on glass, incorporating the metallic sand into its structure, becoming hardier, more conductive. Glass formations near moss patches develop strange properties—storing charge like batteries, conducting current along their surfaces in patterns that mirror the hexagons below. The two landscapes begin a slow convergence, measured not in years but in centuries, each taking on properties of the other.

And in the deepest battery chambers of the valley, where the eldest Tenders have become living regulators, something new happens. The electrical patterns they maintain begin to include frequencies they've never generated before—radio wavelengths that pulse through the moss network, seeking response. Far above on the plateau, the Project's receivers align, tuning to these new signals, establishing connections that bypass the physical border entirely.

The network grows. Not as either the valley's creators or the plateau's builders intended, but as something new—a hybrid system that turns the entire landscape into a single vast circuit. Power flows from the aurora through moss and glass alike, processed by biological systems and mechanical ones, stored in batteries both grown and built. The Tenders expand their careful maintenance to include the glass formations at the border, while the Scavengers begin to factor the valley's bioelectric patterns into their salvage calculations.

They still don't speak. They may never speak. But they've become parts of the same machine, components in a circuit that encompasses valley and plateau, moss and metal, the grown and the salvaged. The aurora light falls on both domains equally now, and both transform it into power that flows wherever it's needed, crossing borders that matter less with each passing year.

In the end, perhaps this is what the original builders intended—not the valley alone or the plateau's industry, but the combination, the synthesis, the circuit completed across domains. Or perhaps this is something entirely new, an emergent property neither predicted nor designed, arising from the simple need of electrical systems to connect, to flow, to become more than the sum of their isolated parts.

The bright silence of the valley continues. The charged wind of the Scouring still howls. But underneath both, in frequencies audible only to creatures of living metal and conducting moss, a new song builds—the hum of connection, the whisper of unified current, the sound of a world teaching itself to be whole.
