Above the Under-Realm, where the stone thins to soil and the soil grows green things that reach toward light, there is the Sky-Scar.

This is where the world broke first.

The People of the Wind live here, though they do not walk. They have forgotten how. Their bones have grown hollow as birds', and their skin stretches between elongated fingers and toes in membranes so fine you can see the blood vessels branching through them like rivers of sky.

They remember the day the earth cracked open and swallowed the water. They remember because they were there, pressed against the clouds, watching their reflections disappear into the new mouth of stone.

The Sky-Scar is not one place but many—a series of plateaus and mesas connected by bridges of woven air. The bridges are invisible except when the morning mist clings to them, outlining their geometry in droplets of suspended water. The People of the Wind tend these bridges with songs that must never stop. If the singing ceases, the bridges unweave themselves and drift away like smoke.

In the highest places, where the air grows thin enough to cut, there are towers made of compressed cloud. The People of the Wind sleep hanging from these towers in cocoons of their own making, wrapped in silk that their bodies produce during the dream-time. When they wake, they eat the silk. It tastes of yesterday's weather.

They know about the Current far below. Sometimes, on the stillest nights, they can hear the echo of footsteps in stone, the whisper of the underground river that stole half the world's water. They drop things down the cracks—flowers that bloom only in darkness, messages written on leaves that will dissolve in water, small bones carved with symbols that mean *remember us*.

The Current finds these gifts. They gather them in the pools where the river slows, in the places where the stone has worn smooth enough to hold still water. They cannot read the symbols, but they understand the weight of them. They string the bones together and wear them, and the sound they make when walking is like rain that falls upward.

The two peoples have a name for each other, though they have never met:

The Current calls them the Forgotten-to-Fall.

The People of the Wind call them the Learned-to-Drown.

Between them, in the stone that separates sky from depth, there are fossils of the time before. Shell-creatures that lived when the world was all ocean. Flying things that lived when the world was all air. And in the very center, where the pressure is greatest, there are the remains of something that was neither swimming nor flying but both at once.

The fossil-thing has too many wings and too many eyes. It is crying.

Its tears are what became the river.
