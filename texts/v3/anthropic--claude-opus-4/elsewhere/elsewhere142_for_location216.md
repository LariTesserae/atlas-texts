Above the Sinter Warrens, where the salt-bleached earth bakes under a sun that seems too close and wrong-colored, the Vitreous Flats stretch to every horizon in sheets of fused glass and crystallized sand. This is where the world broke and healed badly, where some ancient catastrophe turned the ground to molten glass that cooled too quickly, trapping bubbles of atmosphere from another age.

The surface here tells lies. What appears solid shatters under weight, revealing pockets of vacuum that pull with sudden, silent violence. What seems fragile—thin sheets of volcanic glass stretched like drum skins—supports tremendous loads, thrumming with footfalls that echo through the hidden cavities below. The noon heat turns the air into a living thing that presses against exposed skin, while the midnight cold cracks the glass with sounds like distant artillery.

The Flats connect to the Warrens through thermal chimneys—irregular shafts where temperature differentials create permanent windstorms. These howling columns pull mineral-rich air from below, depositing it as delicate frost patterns on the glass above. Where the deposits accumulate, strange gardens grow: not plants but accretions of crystal that mimic organic forms. Metallic ferns unfurl in the heat, their fronds made of whisker-thin copper filaments. Trees of salt rise from glass roots, their branches supporting nests of spun silver where nothing lives but the wind makes sounds like singing.

The only water exists in the trapped bubbles—ancient rain caught in glass prisons, visible but unreachable. Some bubbles are vast as buildings, their contents an ecosystem unto themselves, sealed since the breaking. Through the distorted glass, observers might glimpse impossible things: fish that swim through air thick as honey, plants with leaves of beaten gold, storms that cycle endlessly through spaces no larger than a room. Whether these are hallucinations born of heat and thirst, or glimpses of what the world was before it burned, none can say.

The Vitric Herders know, but their knowledge comes at a price that reshapes both body and mind.

They were human once—or something near enough. The first herders came seeking the mineral wealth that the thermal chimneys promised, following rumors of copper gardens and silver trees. What they found was transformation. The Flats change those who stay too long, who breathe too deeply of the mineral air, who drink from the wrong springs where condensation pools in the pre-dawn cold.

A Vitric Herder stands taller than they should, their limbs stretched by some interaction between human bone and the peculiar gravity of the Flats. Their skin takes on the quality of the glass itself—translucent in places, showing the dark flow of altered blood beneath. Their eyes adapt to the killing light, developing nictitating membranes of polarized crystal that give them sight in spectrums where the bubble-worlds reveal their secrets.

But the true change runs deeper. The Herders' minds fracture and fuse like the glass beneath their feet, developing parallel consciousnesses that can process the paradoxes of their environment. One mind watches the solid world, tracking the safe paths across the treacherous surface. Another peers into the bubbles, learning the languages of the trapped ecosystems, reading meaning in the endless patterns of the prisoned storms. A third mind—and this is what makes them valuable—communicates with their flocks.

For the Herders do not herd any earthly creature. Their charges are the glass-singers, beings that exist more as vibration than substance. Born from the resonance between the Flats and the Warrens below, the glass-singers manifest as patches of harmonized air that move across the glass surfaces, leaving trails of altered crystal in their wake. They feed on temperature differentials, clustering around the thermal chimneys at dawn and dusk when the temperature changes most rapidly.

The Herders guide these flocks with instruments of their own devising—tuning forks made from the metallic ferns, drums stretched with sheets of flexible glass, wind-catchers that channel the chimney howls into precise frequencies. Each herder develops their own call, a signature combination of tones that their flock learns to follow. The relationship is symbiotic: the glass-singers' trails create new patterns in the surface, revealing safe paths and hidden bubble-chambers, while the Herders protect their flocks from the silent storms.

These storms come without warning—zones where the air itself forgets how to carry sound. They roll across the Flats like invisible tides, leaving silence so complete it has physical weight. Caught in one, a glass-singer dissipates, its vibrations unable to propagate. The Herders have learned to read the signs: the way certain crystals dim before a storm arrives, how the bubble-fish flee to the deepest parts of their prisons, the particular quality of light that precedes the silence.

The Herders live nomadically, following routes determined by the songs their flocks leave in the glass. They make camp in the rare stable zones, where ancient structures of fused stone provide shelter from the temperature extremes. These camps are temporary cities of glass and crystal, built and abandoned according to patterns only the Herders understand. They trade with each other in currencies of sound—bottled echoes from the deepest chimneys, crystallized music from their flocks' most complex harmonies, maps drawn in frequency rather than space.

Their culture revolves around the preservation and interpretation of sound. Master Herders can read the history of the Flats in the resonance patterns of the glass, determining where ancient seas once stood, where cities rose before the breaking. They maintain oral traditions that are literally oral—stories that must be told at specific pitches to convey their full meaning, songs that encode technical knowledge about flock management and storm prediction.

The Herders know of the Sinter-Born below, though they have never met. The knowledge comes through the thermal chimneys, in the way certain deep harmonics rise from the Warrens during the Sinter-Born's most elaborate ceremonies. Some Herders claim to have learned to interpret these sounds, to understand the distributed thoughts of the quicksilver beings below. They speak of trade conducted through the chimneys—compositions dropped into the howling winds that return days later, transformed by their passage through the Sinter-Born's consciousness.

The relationship between the Flats and the Warrens is one of necessity and longing. The mineral breath of the depths makes possible the crystal gardens above. The temperature differentials that torment the Herders power the ecosystems below. The glass-singers' trails create micro-fractures that will, over geological time, become new entrances to the Warrens. Some Herders speak of a coming convergence, when the barriers between above and below will thin enough for passage. Whether this is prophecy or wish, none can say.

The Vitric Herders age strangely, their translucent skin gradually taking on the properties of the ancient glass around them. The eldest among them are nearly invisible, their bodies refracting light in ways that make them difficult to perceive directly. These Elders spend their final years at the deepest thermal chimneys, listening to the songs from below, adding their own fading voices to the eternal dialogue between the Flats and the Warrens.

When a Herder dies, their flock performs a final migration, carrying the body to one of the vast bubble-chambers. There, through processes the living do not witness, the glass accepts its child back, incorporating the transformed flesh into new formations. Some say the bubble-worlds gain new inhabitants this way, that the strange figures sometimes glimpsed in the deeper chambers were once Herders who found a way through the glass. Others claim the dead become part of the glass-singers themselves, their consciousness distributed through their former flocks.

The two worlds—Sinter Warrens below and Vitreous Flats above—exist in perpetual exchange. Minerals rise and sounds descend. The Sinter-Born dream of sunlight they will never feel, while the Vitric Herders listen to deep songs they will never fully comprehend. Between them, the thermal chimneys howl their connecting hymns, carrying messages in frequencies that neither fully understands but both have learned to need.

This is symbiosis on a geological scale, two impossible ecosystems locked in a dance of mutual dependence and eternal separation. The Flats could not exist without the Warrens' breath; the Warrens would stagnate without the Flats' thermal pull. And in between, in the spaces where neither can survive, new forms of existence wait to be born from the marriage of depth and surface, crystal and glass, silence and song.
