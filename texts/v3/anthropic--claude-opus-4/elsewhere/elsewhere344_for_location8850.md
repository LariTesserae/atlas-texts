Beyond the Breathing Cavity's industrial thrum, following the mountain's arterial passages upward through zones of increasing cold and thinning air, the stone gradually forgets its uncertainty. Here, where the rock has long ago decided to be nothing more or less than rock, spreads the Clarified Plateau—a expanse of ancient granite polished by millennia of wind into a mirror for the sky.

The plateau extends for perhaps fifty kilometers, though distance becomes meaningless in a landscape that offers no shelter, no variation, no mercy. The stone here gleams with an almost metallic sheen, its crystalline structure so perfectly aligned that it rings like a bell when struck. No crevice harbors moss, no crack admits the possibility of roots. Rain sheets off the surface without pooling, and snow finds no purchase—the wind scrubs everything clean within hours.

The air at this altitude carries a knife-edge clarity that makes the lungs ache. Every breath must be earned, drawn deliberately into bodies that expect more substance than this thin offering provides. The light, unfiltered by moisture or dust, strikes with an intensity that bleaches color from everything it touches. Shadows here are absolute—either you stand in full sun or complete darkness, with no gentle gradations between.

Yet this seemingly barren expanse supports its own form of abundance—not the overwhelming biological proliferation of the cavity below, but something more subtle, more patient, more profound.

The Lichen Readers make their home here, or rather, they make the plateau itself their home, for they build nothing upon its surface. They live instead in the microscopic architecture of the lichen colonies that constitute the plateau's only visible life—organisms so flat they seem painted onto the stone, surviving on minerals leached from granite and moisture pulled from the rare morning fogs.

These lichens grow in patterns of extraordinary complexity—spirals within spirals, fractals that repeat across scales from millimeter to kilometer. To the untrained eye, they appear as mere stains on the rock, gray-green and orange and pale yellow. But the Lichen Readers have spent generations learning to interpret these patterns, discovering in them a language that encompasses weather prediction, geological memory, and something stranger—a form of distributed consciousness that may be the mountain's own thoughts made visible.

The Readers themselves have adapted to their chosen existence with a thoroughness that makes the Condensers' transformations seem superficial. Their bodies have lengthened and flattened, spreading their mass to maximize surface area while minimizing wind resistance. They move across the plateau on bellies and palms, their skin developing a texture that grips granite at the molecular level. When they rest, which is often, they become nearly invisible—their mottled coloration and utter stillness making them seem like irregularities in the stone itself.

Their eyes have evolved in the opposite direction from their cavity-dwelling cousins. Where Condensers' pupils expanded to capture dim light, the Readers' have contracted to pinpoints, protected by nictitating membranes that filter the plateau's brutal radiance. They see in spectrums beyond human normal, perceiving the lichen's chemical communications as waves of color invisible to others. Their visual cortexes have rewired to process pattern rather than movement—a Reader can spot a new lichen growth the width of a hair from a hundred meters away, but might not notice a person walking past unless they disrupted the stone's surface.

They wear no clothing in the traditional sense. Instead, their skin has thickened into a leather-like integument, with specialized cells that can darken or lighten to regulate temperature. Folds of this skin-leather stretch between their elongated limbs, allowing them to catch the plateau's constant winds and glide for short distances. The eldest Readers have developed skin flaps so extensive they resemble human kites, able to travel kilometers without touching stone.

The Readers live solitary lives by necessity—the lichen patterns they study often cover vast areas, requiring a single individual to spend months crawling across the granite, memorizing every curve and color shift. They come together only for the seasonal Convergences, when the lichen colonies fruit in synchrony, releasing spores that create brief auroras in the thin air. During these gatherings, Readers share their findings through a form of communication that bypasses speech entirely.

They press their bodies against the stone in specific configurations, using their skin's sensitivity to vibration to transmit complex information through the granite itself. A conversation between Readers looks like an elaborate, slow-motion dance—bodies flowing across rock, pausing in particular positions to let vibrations propagate, reading responses through their spread palms and bellies. They can conduct these conversations across kilometers, the plateau's crystalline structure carrying their messages with perfect fidelity.

Their understanding of time differs radically from the Condensers'. Where the cavity dwellers live in industrial minutes and biological hours, the Readers think in geological epochs. They track lichen growth patterns that repeat on thousand-year cycles, predict weather changes decades in advance, map the mountain's settling over centuries. A Reader might spend a human lifetime documenting a single spiral's expansion, considering this rapid work.

They feed on the lichen itself, but carefully, sustainably. Their digestive systems have evolved to process the complex polymers that allow lichen to survive in such extremity. A few grams per day suffices, scraped delicately from colonies old enough to spare the loss. The Readers' metabolism has slowed to near-torpor—their hearts beat perhaps twenty times per minute, their body temperatures hover just above the ambient air. They can enter a form of living stasis during the worst storms, their biological processes all but ceasing while they wait for conditions to improve.

The connection between the Breathing Cavity and the Clarified Plateau runs deeper than the mere physical passages that link them. The Condensers' industrial processes far below create thermal currents that rise through the mountain's body, eventually emerging as the warm drafts that bring morning fog to the plateau. This fog provides the moisture that feeds the lichen, which in turn creates the patterns the Readers study.

But the relationship works both ways. The Readers have discovered that certain lichen configurations can influence the mountain's internal temperatures, creating pressure differentials that affect the cavity's breathing. By carefully cultivating specific patterns—work that takes decades—they can send messages down through the stone, warnings of weather changes or geological shifts that the Condensers' instruments cannot predict.

More controversially, some Readers believe they can influence the cavity's embryonic geology through lichen manipulation. They claim that the patterns they create on the surface eventually propagate downward, guiding the stone's decisions about its final form. The Condensers dismiss this as plateau madness—the delusion of humans who spend too long alone with their microscopic gardens. But sometimes, the cavity's walls shift in ways that match lichen spirals drawn years before, and even skeptics pause to wonder.

Trade between the two communities exists, though it looks nothing like conventional commerce. The Condensers send up beetle shells and moth wings, materials the Readers grind into pigments for marking temporary patterns on the stone. In return, the Readers provide lichen spores with specific properties—varieties that glow in response to seismic activity, or that change color in the presence of valuable minerals. These exchanges happen at the Middle Reaches, a zone where the stone wavers between certainty and possibility, where neither community feels entirely at home.

Young Condensers sometimes attempt the journey to the plateau, driven by curiosity or the desire to prove themselves. Most turn back when the air grows too thin, when their loose joints and heavy bones make movement on the vertical passages impossible. Those few who persist undergo a harrowing transformation—their bodies consuming themselves to achieve the lightness necessary for plateau life, their eyes learning to contract, their skin hardening against the unforgiving sun.

Similarly, Reader children occasionally descend to the cavity, though this is even rarer. The thick air makes them sick, the noise overwhelms their pattern-tuned senses, and the constant moisture feels like drowning. Most flee back to their austere homeland within days. But legends tell of one Reader who stayed, who learned to love the cavity's chaos, who bred beetles that could survive on lichen and moths whose wings carried spore-patterns. They say her descendants still live in the deepest reaches, bridging the two worlds in ways others cannot imagine.

The true connection, though, may lie in what both communities represent—different answers to the same question of how to be human in extremity. The Condensers chose overwhelming abundance, becoming part of a teeming ecosystem that fills every available space. The Readers chose radical simplicity, paring existence down to stone and lichen and the patterns that connect them. Both have evolved beyond baseline humanity, but in opposite directions—one toward inclusion, one toward essence.

And the mountain contains them both, breathing below and crystallized above, a body vast enough to encompass contradictions. In its deepest heart, where stone remains molten and undecided, perhaps it dreams of both futures simultaneously—the cavity's tropical abundance and the plateau's arctic clarity existing in superposition, waiting for some quantum moment of observation to collapse them into a single reality.

But that moment never comes. The mountain continues dreaming, the Condensers continue building, the Readers continue crawling across their crystalline desert, tracing patterns that might be language or madness or both. And between them, through passages both physical and metaphysical, flows a conversation too slow and too vast for any single human to comprehend—a dialogue between ways of being that defines the mountain more surely than any geological survey.

In the end, neither community knows the full truth of their relationship. They are like the dual nature of light—particle and wave, depending on how you observe them. The Breathing Cavity and the Clarified Plateau exist as opposite poles of possibility, and between them stretches the entire spectrum of what it means to adapt, to evolve, to become something more and less than human in the face of the world's beautiful, terrible extremes.
