The Ascending Gardens

High above, where the last mountain touches the edge of breathable air, another garden grows. Here, the Builders' descendants—those who fled the rising waters generation upon generation ago—have carved their refuge into living rock. The stone terraces rise in impossible spirals, each level cut deeper into the mountain's flesh, creating a vertical labyrinth of growing spaces that climb until the air grows too thin for even the hardiest crops.

The gardens exist in perpetual near-twilight, caught between the harsh sun above the cloud layer and the eternal mists below. The air here has a quality like cold glass—sharp, clear, almost too pure to breathe. It carries no scent but stone dust and the faint green smell of struggling growth. The gardeners move slowly at these heights, their lungs laboring, their movements deliberate as those of their drowned cousins far below.

Every terrace tells the story of adaptation. The lowest levels, carved when the refugees first arrived, show the same precise stonework that marks the ancient workshops beneath the sea—clean joints, perfect angles, the confidence of those who believed stone would always obey their hands. But as the terraces climb and the generations passed, the work changes. The stone here was harder, the tools fewer, the hands that wielded them weakened by altitude and loss. The upper terraces are rough-hewn, carved with desperation rather than craft, their edges still sharp after centuries.

What grows here has evolved beyond recognition from the crops the Builders once knew. The plants are low, dense things, their leaves thick with protective waxes, their roots driving deep into cracks that the gardeners fill with carefully hoarded soil. Some have learned to photosynthesize through the mists, their leaves turned not up toward the invisible sun but down toward the reflected light that bounces between cloud layers. Others grow in the deepest recesses of the carved chambers, thriving in near-darkness, feeding on minerals leached from the stone itself.

The gardeners—they still call themselves Builders, though they have built nothing new in living memory—maintain their terraces with a patience born of necessity. They know each plant individually, speak to them in the old language that has evolved to need fewer words, fewer breaths. Their children learn to read the minute variations in leaf color that signal altitude sickness in the crops, to feel the almost imperceptible temperature differences between terraces that mean the difference between growth and death.

Water here is more precious than blood. The gardeners have carved collection systems into every surface—channels no wider than a finger that run along terrace edges, gathering the condensation that forms each dawn. The water runs through the stone in a network as complex as veins, pooling in cisterns carved so deep into the mountain that some say they can taste salt in the oldest reserves—memory of the sea that claimed their ancestors' workshops.

The gardens connect to each other through passages that would be impossible at sea level—corridors that angle up at grades that leave even the altitude-adapted gasping, spiral stairs carved so steep they're closer to ladders, window-slots that serve as doors for those desperate or skilled enough to traverse the external cliff faces. The gardeners know their mountain by feel, navigating in the perpetual mist by the taste of the air, the angle of the stone, the sound of water running through hidden channels.

They have rituals older than memory. Each season—marked not by temperature but by the subtle shift in the light that filters through the clouds—they carry soil from the lower terraces to the higher ones. A handful at a time, lifted in baskets woven from the fibrous stems of their altered crops. It takes the entire community months to move enough soil to matter, but they do it without question. The soil lifted from below carries microorganisms, minerals, some essential essence that keeps the highest gardens from failing entirely.

At the very peak of their inhabited terraces, where the gardens give way to bare stone and killing cold, stands a structure different from the rest. A workshop, built in the old style, though cruder than even the desperate work of the flooded chambers below. Here, the gardeners keep their tools—not just the implements of cultivation but the stone-working tools their ancestors carried up from the drowning world. Chisels and hammers, worn to nubs but maintained with religious care. They no longer remember how to use most of them, but they keep them sharp, oiled with precious plant oils, wrapped in cloth woven from crop fibers.

The eldest of the gardeners spend their last years in the highest terraces, where the work is hardest and the air thinnest. They say it brings clarity, though whether they mean the pure air or the proximity to death is unclear. These elders speak least but see most, their eyes adapted to pierce the eternal mists. They claim that on the clearest days, when the clouds part for moments at a time, they can see the pattern of their ancestors' flooded lands far below—dark shapes beneath the water that might be the shadows of the ancient workshops, or might be only the dreams of oxygen-starved minds.

The gardeners know stories their ancestors carried up from the drowning. Not histories—those were lost in the desperate climb—but fragments. The taste of a fruit that no longer grows. The sound of rain on workshop roofs. The feeling of building something meant to last forever. These fragments have evolved in the telling, becoming myths, prayers, the half-remembered genetic code of a people who were once something else.

They trade with no one—there is no one to trade with at these heights. But sometimes things arrive. Spores on the wind that grow into plants they've never seen. Seeds in the crops of birds that manage the altitude, carrying genetics from the lower world. And, very rarely, when the mists clear and the water in the cisterns settles perfectly still, they say you can taste something in it—a mineral tang, a complexity that speaks of travel through deep places, of water that has touched stone worked by familiar hands, though impossibly far below.

The connection between the Ascending Gardens and the Drowned Workshops lives in these moments—in water that remembers stone, in stone-working hands that remember water, in the patient cultivation of spaces too extreme for flourishing. Both communities tend what they've inherited with the same careful persistence, though one works in crushing darkness and the other in air too thin to hold warmth. They are the sundered halves of the Builders' legacy: one sinking slowly into the earth they once shaped, the other climbing away from it, both maintaining their gardens against the patience of time.

In the Ascending Gardens, where every breath is measured and every handful of soil is precious, the gardeners continue their work. They expand when they can, carving new terraces from virgin stone with their ancient tools, though each level higher costs more in effort than the last. They plant, tend, harvest, and plant again, their cycles as regular as the tides that drown their ancestors' work. And in their highest moments, gasping in the thin air with their hands full of seeds, they might feel—though they could never articulate it—the echo of their cousins below, those patient Inherited, working with the same deliberate grace in their own impossible gardens.

This is how the world divides and continues: in water and air, in depth and height, in those who inherit ruins and those who build upon them. The Builders are gone, but their children remain, adapted beyond recognition, tending their gardens at the extremes of what the world allows, carrying forward a legacy in languages the original tongue could never speak.
