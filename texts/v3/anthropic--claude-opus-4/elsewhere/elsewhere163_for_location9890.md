The cave paintings appear at low water.

Not in the sinkhole—that pool never changes—but twelve kilometers west, where the same limestone karst breaks through red laterite soil in a series of shallow depressions that fill and empty with the rains. During the wet months, these are lakes, their surfaces broken by half-drowned trees, their edges uncertain where water meets saturated earth. But when the dry season deepens and the water table drops, they drain through hidden channels in the limestone, leaving behind cracked mud floors and the smell of things recently aquatic.

The largest of these temporary lakes spans perhaps two hundred meters at its fullest, shaped like a hand with fingers reaching into the surrounding forest. The trees here have adapted to the flooding—their trunks swollen at the base, their roots spreading wide and shallow, their bark scarred high with waterlines from previous years. When the water recedes, it leaves them standing on exposed root systems that arch above the mud like the ribs of buried creatures.

It is beneath one of these trees, a massive *Dipterocarpus* whose roots form a natural shelter, that the entrance reveals itself. Not a true cave—more of an overhang, a hollow where softer stone has been carved away by centuries of seasonal flooding. The opening is low, forcing you to duck, and extends back perhaps eight meters into the hillside. The walls are the same limestone as the sinkhole, but here the water's work has been different: not the patient seepage that creates smooth scallops, but the violent rush of seasonal floods that have left the rock rough, pocketed with harder inclusions that stand out like knuckles.

The paintings are on the ceiling, where the rock is smoothest. They are red—iron oxide mixed with something organic that has helped it bind to the stone. The pigment has soaked into the limestone's pores, become part of the rock itself, so that even where the surface has flaked away, ghost images remain in the stone beneath. The preservation is remarkable but uneven: some figures are crisp as the day they were painted, others fade into suggestions, half-dissolved by the same water that reveals them.

They depict fish.

Not the careful taxonomic representations you might expect, but fish as movement, fish as abundance, fish as something between flesh and spirit. The artists understood their subjects' essential gesture—the S-curve of swimming, the spread of fins, the forward thrust of feeding. Catfish predominate, their barbels exaggerated into flowing lines that merge with the water they swim through. But there are others: lung-fish with their peculiar elongated bodies, climbing perch with their spined fins spread like fans, species you cannot identify that might be extinct or imagined or observed in states between the two.

The paintings swirl across the ceiling in a composition that makes sense only when you lie on your back in the mud and look up, letting your eyes adjust to the way the artists saw. The fish swim in all directions, some alone, some in schools that flow like smoke across the rock. There are layers here—older paintings beneath newer ones, different hands, different generations adding their observations to the accumulated vision. In places, the pigments have mixed where one artist painted over another, creating colors that were never intended: purple-brown where red met black, orange where yellow ochre touched iron.

Among the fish swim other things. Human figures, but simplified to essence: a torso, arms, legs, a head sometimes indicated by a circle, sometimes by nothing at all. They are not swimming exactly—their posture is wrong for that. They stand or float or exist in the space between water and air, and some of them hold things that might be nets or might be plants or might be objects for which you have no reference. One figure near the center has lines radiating from its head like hair or light or thought. Another seems to be transforming, its lower body elongated into something serpentine, neither human nor fish but participating in both.

The most arresting images are at the deepest point of the overhang, where the ceiling drops low and you must crawl to see them. Here, the paintings change character. The fish are still present but they circle something—a dark space in the rock that the artists incorporated into their design, using the natural depression as a void around which everything else moves. The fish spiral inward, getting smaller as they approach the darkness, until they are just dots of pigment, suggestions of movement. And at the very edge of the void, barely visible, are handprints. Negative handprints, made by placing a hand against the rock and blowing pigment around it. The hands are small—children's hands, perhaps, or the hands of people who were smaller than you—and they reach toward the darkness as if trying to touch something just beyond their grasp.

The mud beneath you is cracked in polygons where it has dried, and each crack is filling with new life. Grass seedlings have sprouted in the lines, their roots following the moisture down. Insects move in the shadows—beetles that will drown when the rains return, spiders that will climb the trees, ants that are already building temporary cities in the higher ground. The air smells of earth and growth and the faint mineral tang that means water is not far below.

This is not a sacred site, or not only sacred. This is a record. The people who painted these fish were observing something specific: the way this depression fills with water, the way fish appear in it as if from nowhere, living in temporary lakes that seem to have no connection to permanent water. They were recording a mystery they could see but perhaps not fully explain—how fish survive in the limestone, moving through channels no human can follow, emerging when the rains come, vanishing when they go.

You think of the sinkhole, twelve kilometers east. That permanent pool with its patient catfish, its dark depths that never reveal their secrets. The water there seeps through the limestone, finding its way through cracks and channels, carrying with it whatever can fit through spaces too small for light. The fish painted here—are they the same fish? Ancestors? Cousins? The limestone connects everything in ways you can only partially map, a network of water-carved passages that links pool to pool, surface to depth, wet season to dry.

The artists knew this. They painted fish swimming through stone, fish emerging from darkness, fish that exist in the spaces between what is seen and what is known. They painted themselves among the fish, not as observers but as participants in the same aquatic democracy, the same seasonal uncertainty. When the rains come and this shelter floods, the paintings disappear beneath water that is murky with dissolved earth. The fish return—the real fish, climbing perch that can breathe air, catfish that can survive in mud, lungfish that can estivate through the dry season—and they swim above their own images, life overlaying art overlaying life.

A bird calls from the forest above, and the sound reminds you that you are not in the sinkhole's timeless humidity but in a place where seasons matter, where wet and dry create different worlds. You crawl back from the deepest paintings, your knees muddy, your neck stiff from looking up. Outside the overhang, the afternoon light is harsh after the cave's dimness. The temporary lake bed stretches before you, a canvas of cracked earth waiting for the next flood to erase and begin again.

You walk to where the deepest point would be when the water is high. The mud here is different—darker, still holding moisture. If you dig down even a few centimeters, it becomes wet, then saturated. Somewhere below, water waits in the limestone, the same water that feeds the sinkhole, the same water that will rise when the rains return. The fish wait there too, or some of them do—the ones that found the deep places, the ones whose ancestors learned the shape of survival in this landscape of appearing and disappearing water.

The paintings will outlast the fish, probably. The iron oxide has bonded with the limestone in a way that resists everything but the stone's own dissolution. But the fish will outlast the memory of the paintings, swimming through their secret channels long after the last person who understands their significance has gone. This is the reconciliation the artists reached: to make permanent art about impermanent water, to fix in stone the movement of creatures that refuse to be fixed.

You spend the night near the overhang, sleeping on higher ground where the flood line suggests safety. The stars are brilliant here, away from any lights, and the night sounds are different from the sinkhole—more varied, more urgent, the sounds of a place that knows time is always running out. Fruit bats pass overhead, their wings beating a rhythm that sounds like applause. Something large moves through the forest, breaking branches, and then is still.

In the morning, you return to the paintings one more time. The early light enters the overhang at a different angle, and you see details you missed before: a fish with a human face, a human with fins, patterns that might be calendars or maps or simply the joy of making marks on stone. The handprints around the void seem fresher in this light, as if the children who made them had just run off, their palms still dusty with pigment.

You place your own hand over one of the prints. It doesn't fit—your hand is too large, too foreign, too far removed from the understanding that created this place. But something translates across the gap: the impulse to touch, to record, to say *I was here when the water was low, when the fish were hidden, when the stone was dry enough to hold meaning.*

The sinkhole is still there, twelve kilometers east, breathing its micro-climate, hosting its permanent fish. This seasonal lake is also there, even when it isn't, waiting in the limestone's memory for the rains to make it real again. The paintings connect them—not physically, through the underground channels that no one can map, but conceptually, through the recognition that water and stone and fish and people are all part of the same system, all temporary residents in a landscape that operates on multiple timescales simultaneously.

You leave the overhang and walk back across the cracked mud, following the ghost of the lake's edge. The trees that have adapted to flooding cast hard shadows on the dry earth. A monitor lizard watches you from a fallen log, then disappears with a speed that seems impossible for something so large. The air is heating up, the humidity building toward afternoon thunderstorms that might or might not come.

Somewhere beneath your feet, water moves through the limestone, carrying fish and memory and the dissolved minerals that will eventually create new passages, new connections, new mysteries for someone else to paint on stone. The sinkhole breathes. The seasonal lake remembers. And between them, in galleries and passages you will never see, the fish continue their ancient migration, following water through the dark, emerging into light when the world above allows it, carrying in their bodies the proof that persistence takes many forms, not all of them visible, not all of them permanent, all of them true.
