Far below the Cistern Quarter, where the mountain's bones show through in raw cliff faces and ancient lava tubes, the Dry Warren sprawls through natural caverns and mine shafts like a city turned inside out. Here, in the rain shadow of the mountain's bulk, moisture is memory and dust is scripture.

The geology tells a different story from the sharp volcanic stone above: these are the old rocks, laid down when seas covered the land, compressed into limestone and sandstone layers that fracture along precise angles. The miners who first broke through into the natural cavern system found fossils of sea creatures in the walls—spiral shells and delicate fans preserved in stone, reminders of water long vanished. Now these passages serve as streets, their walls worn smooth by generations of passing hands.

The Warren evolved in reverse order from most settlements—instead of building up, they hollowed down. Each family claims a chamber or side tunnel, carving deeper when children come, connecting to neighbors through carefully negotiated passages. The main thoroughfares follow natural formations: the Great Gallery where ancient water carved a cathedral-sized void, the Spiral Road that corkscrews down through limestone layers, the Narrow Market squeezed between two fault lines.

Light comes precious and rationed. Sun shafts angle through carefully cut skylights during the brief noon hour, tracked by mirrors and reflective surfaces to bring a taste of day into the depths. Most illumination comes from oil lamps burning the rendered fat of cave beetles, casting a yellow glow that makes everyone look jaundiced. The wealthy burn crystal-oil that gives a cleaner light, while the poor make do with rushes dipped in whatever grease they can scavenge.

The fauna here lives by different rules. The cave beetles grow large as housecats, their shells iridescent in lamplight, prized both as livestock and for the peculiar properties of their oil—it burns without smoke in the thin cave air. Blind white crickets sing in frequencies that human ears can barely catch, their choruses marking the passage of time better than any clock. The hunting spiders spin no webs but stalk their prey through the darkness on legs as long as a man's arm. Colony bats roost in the higher chambers, their guano carefully collected and processed into fertilizer for the Warren's peculiar agriculture.

What grows here grows strange. The cavern fungi flourish in the dark—some varieties phosphoresce with their own pale light, others grow in shelf-like formations large enough to serve as tables. The prized dust-melons develop in sandy chambers, their roots diving deep to find fossil water, their flesh dry and sweet and worth its weight in copper. Mineral flowers bloom from the walls where conditions are right—not plants but crystals that grow in petal-like formations, harvested for their salts.

The air tastes of stone and age. In the deeper levels, it grows thick and still, holding the heat of the earth's core. Higher up, where passages connect to the surface, winds whistle through carrying the scent of the dry lands beyond—sage and dust and the peculiar emptiness of true desert. The Warren-folk read these winds like the Keepers above read water, knowing which passages will breathe on which days, which tunnels to avoid when the pressure drops.

Sound behaves strangely in the hollows. Whispers carry for miles through certain passages, while shouts die within feet in the dead chambers. The Warren-folk have developed a language of echoes—positioning themselves at acoustic nodes to send messages between levels, turning the entire cavern system into a vast speaking tube. Children learn early which walls will carry their voices home, which chambers swallow sound.

The temperature remains constant—cool but not cold, dry but not desiccating. The stone holds the memory of ancient seas in its steady climate. Only in the mine shafts does it vary, growing warmer as they descend toward the mountain's volcanic heart, or cooler where they break through into wind-scoured upper chambers.

Time moves differently here, measured not by sun or season but by the slow shift of stone. Passages that were clear in grandfather's time gradually close as the mountain settles. New caves open when the earth shudders. The Warren-folk mark their calendars by beetle molts and bat migrations, by the blooming of mineral flowers and the ripening of dust-melons.

This is a place of preservation and patience, where water is hoarded in clay jars and doled out by the ounce, where the dead are mummified in dry sand and kept in family shrines, where secrets settle into stone and wait. The Dry Warren exists as the Cistern Quarter's opposite and twin—what one discards, the other treasures; what one fears, the other embraces.

The Dry Folk emerge from the Warren like creatures evolved for darkness—pale where the Keepers are perpetually flushed, deliberate where the Keepers rush, hoarding where the Keepers spend. They dress in layers too, but theirs are loose linens and woven beetle-silk, designed to trap cool air against the skin and let sweat evaporate slowly.

Their children grow up measuring moisture by the droplet, learning to go hours without drinking, to find water in the most unlikely places—the condensation on cold stone at dawn, the blood of cave beetles, the hoarded liquid in dust-melon flesh. By necessity, they become botanists of the dark, learning which fungi can be eaten raw, which must be dried, which will kill you with a single taste. They train their ears to the echolocation of bats, their fingers to read the stone's grain like braille.

The families guard their water rights like holy relics. Each household maintains a ceramic jar passed down through generations, its interior glazed with a secret formula that prevents evaporation. These jars hold exactly forty-three standard measures—enough for a family of five for one turning of the moon, if rationed properly. The knowledge of hidden springs and fossil water deposits passes from parent to child in whispered conversations, never written down, never shared outside blood relations.

They have a hundred words for dryness, each describing a different quality of absence. Their poetry speaks of negative space—the shape water makes by not being there. Their songs echo through the chambers in harmonies that need no accompaniment but stone walls and empty air. The young people court at the echo points, sending love poems through the speaking stones, their voices mingling in the acoustic nodes where three or four passages meet.

The old folk grow translucent as the blind fish in underground rivers, their skin thin as paper, their eyes clouded by decades of lamp-smoke and darkness. They become the Warren's memory, reciting genealogies and water-rights, passage-maps and beetle-breeding lines. When they feel death approaching, they walk deeper into the mountain, following passages even they have never explored, until they find a chamber that feels right. There they sit, and wait, and eventually become one more fossil in the stone.

Their economy runs on scarcity and barter. Water-debt can span generations. A family might trade their daughter's marriage prospects for access to a reliable seep. The beetle-ranchers drive their chittering herds through the tunnels to market, haggling over breeding pairs and oil-yields. The fungi farmers guard their growing chambers jealously, booby-trapping the approaches with false passages and deadfalls.

They worship absence—gods defined by what they are not, prayers that are carefully structured silences, rituals of giving up rather than receiving. Their priests are the Listeners, men and women who have trained their hearing to supernatural acuity, who can diagnose illness by the echo of a heartbeat, predict cave-ins by the mountain's breathing, find water by its absence in the stone's resonance.

The Dry Folk dream of drowning too, but as a consummation rather than a fear. They wake with their mouths full of imaginary water, disappointed to find only air. Their children play games of pretend springs and phantom rain. Artists among them create negative sculptures—spaces carved from stone that show what isn't there, absences that somehow feel more present than presence.

Between the Cistern Quarter above and the Dry Warren below runs a vertical mile of mountain—solid stone shot through with the new volcanic intrusions above and the old sedimentary layers below. But the two populations, divided by geology and necessity, are connected by more than just the accident of proximity.

The Middlemen operate the lifts.

These are not simple elevators but complex negotiations with gravity, threading through natural chimneys and artificial shafts, following the mountain's permission rather than fighting its resistance. The main lift runs through the Weeping Shaft—a natural formation where the volcanic layer meets limestone, where the Cistern Quarter's excess water seeps through to create a perpetual rainfall inside the mountain.

The lift operators are their own breed, neither fully Quarter nor Warren, trusted by both and belonging to neither. They maintain the massive counterweight systems—huge tanks that fill with water from above to descend, emptying at the bottom to rise again. The water, having served its purpose as motive power, flows into the Warren's careful custody, payment for passage and connection.

Riding the lift is an exercise in transition. At the top, the cage drips with condensation, the air thick with mist and machine oil. As it descends through the Weeping Shaft, water streams past the mesh walls, a roar of sound that gradually diminishes. Halfway down, the temperature drops and the walls change from black volcanic glass to grey limestone. The water falls away into darkness, its sound echoing up from invisible pools. By the time the cage reaches the Warren station, the air has grown dry and still, tasting of dust and deep stone.

The lift carries more than people. Trade flows in both directions—water and machinery descending, minerals and preserves ascending. The Dry Folk send up beetle oil and lamp wicks, fossil salts and fungal medicines, the mummified fish from underground rivers that the Keepers prize as fertility charms. The Quarter sends down water, always water, but also the small manufactured goods the Warren cannot produce—metal tools, glass vessels, written documents on precious paper.

Information travels the lift as well. The operators memorize messages, carrying news between the levels in their heads, speaking it aloud only at the destination. They know the gossip of both communities, the marriages being arranged between Quarter and Warren, the feuds that span the vertical mile, the secret loves conducted by lift-light.

Children of mixed heritage—wetblood and drybone, as they're called—often become lift operators themselves, comfortable in neither world but essential to both. They develop their own culture in the transition spaces, the lift stations and maintenance chambers that belong fully to neither realm. They keep shrines to motion rather than stability, celebrating the eternal up and down, the weight of water and the lightness of air.

The lift schedule determines the rhythm of connection. Dawn lift and dusk lift for regular passengers. The midnight water drop. The full-moon mineral haul. Between these fixed points, the emergency runs—rushing healers up or down, carrying news of deaths or births, facilitating the careful negotiations that keep both communities alive.

Sometimes the lift breaks—a cable snaps, a counterweight jams, the mountain shifts and pinches a shaft closed. Then the communities remember their isolation, the Quarter drowning in its own excess, the Warren drying to dangerous levels. The repair crews from both ends work toward each other through the mountain, opening new passages if necessary, because the lift is life—the vertical artery that makes two opposite worlds into one functioning organism.

The Weeping Shaft itself has become sacred to both groups. The Keepers see it as water's natural path, returning to the depths from which it came. The Dry Folk see it as promise and threat—all that water just out of reach, a torment and a blessing. Both communities station guards at their respective lift heads, partly for security, partly for ceremony, mostly for the comfort of knowing the connection remains.

On feast days, they lower offerings—the Quarter sends ceremonial waters flavored with cloud-pine and minerals, the Warren sends up perfect crystals and ancient fossils. The offerings pass each other in the shaft, water descending as stone rises, a poetry of exchange that both communities understand in their bones.

This is how they live—the water-wealthy and the water-poor, the dwellers in mist and the dwellers in dust, connected by necessity and separated by nature, building their lives around absence and excess, teaching their children that survival means understanding both.

The mountain holds them all—Cistern Quarter clinging to its clouded shoulder, Dry Warren burrowing through its limestone heart, the Weeping Shaft running between like a throat that swallows and speaks. Together they make one city, though no map could capture it, no single name could hold it. It is a place that exists in the tension between opposites, in the endless negotiation between too much and not enough, in the fragile balance that keeps water falling and life persisting, drop by precious drop, breath by careful breath.
