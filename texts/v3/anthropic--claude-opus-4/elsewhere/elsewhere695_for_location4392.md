**The Sky Markets**

Above the mountain, where the air grows thin enough to taste stars at midday, platforms of woven copper cables stretch between the peaks like geometric spider webs. The Sky Markets exist only when the wind allows—some days they are full of life and commerce, other days the platforms swing empty, too dangerous even for the sure-footed merchants who make their living in the vertical world.

The market structures themselves are marvels of tensioned engineering. Copper cables thick as a person's waist anchor into the living rock at stress points calculated by generations of sky-builders. From these main supports, lesser cables branch and divide, creating a nervous system of metal that sings different notes as temperature and tension shift throughout the day. The platforms—some no larger than a small room, others vast enough to hold a hundred traders—hang from this network like leaves from a vast metal tree.

Everything here is built for weight and wind. The stalls collapse into bundles of poles and fabric that can be carried up the treacherous paths by sure-footed pack animals bred for altitude. Goods are secured with intricate knots that can be released with a single pull should a platform need to be evacuated quickly. Even the scales used for weighing precious goods are engineered to compensate for the constant swaying motion.

The vendors who work these heights are a breed apart. Their lungs have adapted to process thin air efficiently, their hearts enlarged to pump oxygen-starved blood. They wear clothes of layered wool and wind-cured leather, with bright ribbons and bells sewn into the fabric—partly for decoration, partly as warning systems when visibility drops to nothing. Their faces are permanently marked by wind-burn and sun, the skin tough as hide except around the eyes, where laugh lines spread like river deltas from a lifetime of squinting against glare.

What trades here could be found nowhere else. Crystals that form only in the highest reaches, where pressure and cold create structures impossible at lower altitudes. Lichens that grow on exposed rock faces, harvested by climbers who descend on ropes thin as spider silk, scraping the precious growth into bags while winds try to dash them against stone. Feathers from birds that nest above the cloud line, their plumage carrying properties of insulation that no lowland creature could match.

But the true commerce of the Sky Markets runs deeper than goods. Information flows here like nowhere else in the mountain city. The platform network extends beyond the main peaks, connecting to observation posts and weather stations where watchers with telescopes of ground crystal monitor the movement of clouds, the migration of birds, the distant smoke of other settlements. News arrives on the wind before it reaches the ground, and fortunes are made by those who can interpret the signs quickly enough.

The market operates on schedules dictated by atmospheric conditions. When the morning thermals rise predictably, creating stable air, the first vendors arrive, hauling their wares up paths carved into near-vertical stone. By midday, when the sun heats the rock faces and creates treacherous updrafts, only the most experienced remain. Evening brings a brief window of calm before the night winds rise, and in this golden hour, the most delicate transactions take place—negotiations for marriages between sky-clan families, the exchange of weather-knowledge that might save or doom a harvest far below, the quiet trade in objects of power pulled from the thin air itself.

Sound behaves strangely at this altitude. Conversations can be snatched away mid-sentence by a gust, or carried intact across impossible distances to reach unintended ears. The merchants have developed a complex sign language for important negotiations, their hands moving in patterns that account for the constant motion of the platforms. Even this is not entirely secure—sharp-eyed competitors with telescopes watch from distant peaks, trying to decode the gestures.

The platforms themselves tell stories through their construction. The oldest ones, woven when the first sky-traders discovered the aerial paths between peaks, use techniques now lost—the copper worked in ways that make it flexible as silk but strong as stone. Newer additions show the evolution of the craft, each generation adding its own innovations to deal with changing wind patterns, heavier loads, new routes opened by volcanic activity or seismic shifts.

On certain days, when clouds engulf the peaks, the Sky Markets become islands in a white sea. Traders move between platforms on bridges that disappear into mist after a few feet, navigating by the sound of cables humming different notes, by the feel of wind patterns on their skin. These fog-market days are when the strangest goods appear—things that seem to precipitate from the clouds themselves, crystals of frozen light, vials of condensed silence, maps drawn on fabric so fine it might be woven from clouds.

The market has its own laws, enforced not by guards but by physics itself. Cheaters find their platforms developing mysterious instabilities. Those who break the peace discover that cables fray near their stalls with suspicious regularity. The mountain, the traders say, knows its own, and protects the honest commerce that has flowed through these heights for generations uncounted.

**The Tenders of Equilibrium**

They ascend each dawn from the middle terraces, where stone gives way to sky, moving through the vertical world with the unconscious grace of those born to altitude. The Tenders of Equilibrium are the living connective tissue between the Cistern Quarter's mineral patience and the Sky Markets' aerial urgency—carriers of water to the heights, interpreters of weather to the depths.

Their bodies speak of their dual citizenship. Legs strengthened by countless climbs, lungs that process thin air without strain, but also an unusual density to their bones—a heaviness that comes from regular sojourns in the humid depths below. They can hold their breath for extraordinary lengths, a skill learned in the cistern passages where air grows too thick to breathe easily, then surface in the sky markets where air is almost too thin to sustain life. Their blood carries adaptations to both extremes, cells that can store and release oxygen with unusual efficiency.

Each Tender maintains a route—a vertical territory that might stretch from a particular cluster of cisterns to a specific set of sky platforms. They know every handhold, every rest spot, every place where the mountain offers assistance or threatens danger. These routes are inherited, passed down through apprenticeships that last decades, for the knowledge they carry is too complex for any written record.

The water they bear upward is not ordinary. Drawn from specific points in the cistern system where minerals concentrate in precise proportions, it carries properties that the sky traders prize above gold. Some batches prevent altitude sickness when sipped slowly. Others, when poured over certain crystals found only at height, produce reactions that predict weather patterns days in advance. The most precious water comes from cisterns where the Keepers of Continuity have performed their slow rituals for centuries, imbuing the liquid with qualities that exist somewhere between chemistry and magic.

They carry this water in vessels of their own making—gourds grown in the middle terraces where both moisture and air find balance, treated with resins that make them impermeable to evaporation. Each Tender's collection of vessels is unique, shaped by use and preference into forms that fit perfectly against their bodies during the long climbs. The vessels sing slightly in the wind, each producing its own note, so that a Tender ascending through fog can be tracked by sound alone.

But water is only part of what they transport. On their downward journeys, they bring crystals from the sky markets to the Keepers below—not to trade, but to dissolve slowly in certain cisterns, adding their high-altitude properties to the water system in a process that might take centuries to fully manifest. They carry messages too complex for the cistern-drummers' percussion language, translating between the Keepers' geological patience and the sky traders' mercurial urgency.

The Tenders read the mountain's moods through signs invisible to others. They sense the pressure changes that herald storms while the sky is still clear, feel the subtle vibrations that predict when a platform cable might fail, taste the humidity shifts that signal the cisterns are entering a new phase of their ancient cycle. This sensitivity makes them invaluable to both realms they serve.

Their clothing reflects their liminal existence—base layers of the water-woven fabric that stays comfortable in the cisterns' humidity, over which they wear the wind-cured leathers of the sky clans. They move between these worlds, shedding and adding layers at stations they maintain at various altitudes, places where they also store supplies, perform maintenance on their routes, and occasionally shelter when the mountain becomes impassable.

The eldest Tenders speak of the Great Circulation—a pattern they perceive in the movement of water from depths to heights and back again, a cycle that encompasses not just the physical journey of moisture but something more profound. They claim the mountain city itself is a single organism, the cisterns its kidneys, the sky markets its lungs, and themselves the circulation system that keeps it alive. Whether this is metaphor or literal truth, none can say for certain.

They age strangely, these vertical wanderers. The constant transition between pressure zones, between humidity and aridity, between mineral-thick air and wind-scrubbed atmosphere, leaves its marks. Their hair often turns white early but remains thick. Their skin develops a peculiar translucency, as if they're becoming permeable to the environments they traverse. The oldest sometimes speak of feeling the mountain's water table in their bones, of knowing where every drop of moisture rests within the stone.

Young people drawn to this life arrive from both directions—some are sky clan children who dream of depths, others are from the lower city who yearn for heights. The training is perilous. Many wash out, unable to handle the constant transitions, the loneliness of the routes, the strange responsibilities. Those who persist learn to find joy in the journey itself—in the perfect placement of a foot on a familiar hold, in the first taste of thin air after hours in the cisterns, in the moment when a vessel of precious water reaches its destination safely.

The Tenders maintain shrines along their routes—small niches carved into the stone where they leave offerings. These might be crystals for the stone to slowly absorb, or water for the wind to claim, or simply moments of gratitude expressed in gesture and breath. They say the mountain notices such things, and rewards those who honor the connection between its depths and heights.

In the evening, when their day's journey is complete, Tenders often gather at the neutral zones—those middle altitudes where neither the Keepers nor the sky traders claim dominion. Here they share meals of food that tastes of both worlds: cave mushrooms seasoned with high-altitude herbs, tea brewed from sky lichens in mineral-rich water. They exchange notes about route conditions, discuss the mountain's changing moods, and sometimes sit in companionable silence, their bodies still humming with the day's transitions.

They are matchmakers of a sort, these Tenders—not of people but of properties. They understand which cistern's water will react with which sky crystal, which mineral formation below might benefit from which high-altitude essence above. In their patient work, elements that would never naturally meet are brought into contact, creating slow reactions that shape the mountain city in ways both subtle and profound.

Some philosophers of the city claim the Tenders themselves are becoming something new—a human adaptation to vertical living that represents the city's future. Others see them as throwbacks, maintaining connections that modern technology could replace. The Tenders themselves express no opinion on such matters. They simply rise each dawn, check their vessels, assess the weather, and begin again their eternal circulation between the patient depths and the urgent heights, carrying in their movements the lifeblood of a city built between stones and stars.
