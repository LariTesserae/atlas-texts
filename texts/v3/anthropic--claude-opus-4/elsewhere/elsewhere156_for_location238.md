**The Ribbon Farms**

Seven hundred kilometers upstream, where the watershed begins its long descent toward the salt flats, the Ribbon Farms stretch between mountain valleys like neural networks made manifest. They were vertical farms once—glass towers full of hydroponic plenty, fed by the same geothermal abundance that would eventually drown the industrial city below. Now they are something else.

The glass is gone, shattered in the first convulsions when the thermal barriers failed. What remains are the skeletal frames: carbon fiber and grown bamboo twisted into DNA helixes sixty stories tall. Between these bones grow the ribbons themselves—vast curtains of engineered kelp that learned to live in air when the water receded. They photosynthesize in the high mountain light, their fronds iridescent green-gold, rippling in winds that taste of snow and chlorophyll.

The kelp is semi-sentient, a final gift from the bioengineers who saw the collapse coming. It grows in careful patterns, following the ghost of its original programming: maximum surface area, optimal nutrient flow, careful symbiosis with its environment. But evolution and isolation have made it strange. The ribbons pulse with bioluminescence at night, signals passing between the towers in waves of blue-green information. They taste the air with chemical sensors, adjusting their growth to harvest moisture from passing clouds. When storms come, they sing—their hollow stems becoming organ pipes that turn the entire valley into an instrument.

Between the towers, in the spaces where parking lots and pedestrian plazas once lay, grows the undertangle: the kelp's root system made external, a mat of vegetation so dense you could walk across it like a trampoline. The undertangle processes the dead, both kelp and animal, breaking them down into nutrients that flow through capillary networks back to the towers. It glows faintly at night, foxfire marking the spots of richest decomposition.

This is where the Weavers make their homes.

They descended from the agricultural workers who couldn't flee when the valleys flooded, then drained, then transformed. Generations of altitude and adaptation have made them something new: elongated limbs for climbing the tower frames, enlarged lungs for the thin air, eyes that see deeper into the blue-green spectrum where the kelp's chemical signals play out. Their fingers are impossibly dexterous, evolved for the patient work of cultivation and harvest.

The Weavers don't farm in any traditional sense—the kelp needs no tending. Instead, they are artists and architects of living space. They guide the ribbon growth into shelters and walkways, coaxing the kelp to thicken here, hollow there, creating rooms and corridors in the vertical forests. Their homes are pods woven into the tower structures, walls of living tissue that breathe and pulse and maintain perfect temperature through biological process. The more skilled among them can grow furniture from the kelp itself: chairs that adjust to the sitter's form, beds that monitor sleep patterns through chemical emanations, tables that fruit with bioluminescent bulbs when darkness falls.

They speak in whispers and gestures, their language evolved for communication in the constant susurrus of wind through kelp. Complex ideas are conveyed through touch—messages passed in the oils of skin contact, pheromonal poetry that lingers in the air. When they must shout across distances, they do so through the kelp itself, drumming specific rhythms on the hollow stems that propagate through the entire network. A skilled Weaver can send a message from valley to valley, assuming there are those who know how to listen.

The young are raised communally in the heart-chambers—rooms grown in the dead center of the towers where the kelp is oldest and most responsive. Children sleep in hammocks of living fiber that rock them in rhythm with the tower's sway. They learn to read the kelp's chemical signals before they learn human speech, distinguishing the signature of nutrient flow, growth hormone, defensive toxin. By adolescence, they can climb the external ribbons like spiders, their bare feet finding purchase on surfaces slick with moisture and growth.

Their diet is the kelp itself, but not as you might imagine. The Weavers have learned to trigger specific growth patterns that produce fruits—not true fruits, but nutrient pods that bud from the ribbons like tears. Each cultivar has been coaxed over generations: some sweet with stored sunlight, others mineral-rich, others that produce mild psychoactive compounds that aid in dream-weaving. The most prized are the memory pods, which grow only on kelp that has fed on human remains. These contain trace neural compounds—eat them and experience fragments of the dead's memories, filtered through the plant's alien consciousness.

The Weavers mark time by growth rings in the kelp's base structures, but their relationship with time is non-linear. The kelp remembers—patterns of trauma and abundance written in its chemistry, accessible to those who know how to read. A skilled Weaver can taste a ribbon and know the rainfall from decades past, the chemical composition of long-dead animals, the emotional resonance of gatherings and griefs. They live in a continuous present informed by all pasts, their history written in the living tissue around them.

They practice the art of the Growing, elaborate ceremonies where master Weavers shape living spaces for specific purposes. A birth-chamber might be grown over months, its walls infused with compounds that promote lactation and bonding. A death-chamber grows darker, its ribbons producing sedative spores and the sweet scent of acceptance. The most ambitious projects take decades: entire towers guided into new configurations, grown bridges between structures, chambers that can only be completed by the grandchildren of those who began them.

Their technology is biotechnology, but of a sort that would seem like magic to outside eyes. They wear clothes of kelp-silk that adjust their temperature through metabolic process. Their tools are grown rather than made: cutting edges of hardened cellulose sharper than steel, containers that preserve food through antibacterial secretions, light sources of concentrated bioluminescence that respond to touch. Everything they use is alive, part of the great network, eventually to be reabsorbed and regrown.

The Weavers trade with the Keepers of the salt flats, though trade is perhaps too simple a word. Twice yearly, when the mountain winds blow strongest, young Weavers ride the storms downslope on gliders made of kelp-membrane and hollow bamboo. They bring gifts: memory pods from the latest dead, therapeutic compounds extracted from rare growths, maps of the watershed drawn in living fiber that updates itself as the water patterns change. In return, the Keepers send beetles—not the salvage swarms, but specific units programmed for delicate work. These metal insects help the Weavers maintain the ancient frames that support their vertical world, repairing stress fractures and corrosion with molecular precision.

The relationship between the two peoples is one of deep symbiosis, though they might meet only a handful of times in any lifetime. The Weavers need the Keepers' beetles to maintain their infrastructure; the Keepers need the Weavers' biological filters to process the water before it reaches the flats, preventing total chemical saturation. More than that, they need each other's witness. The Weavers document the Keepers' mineral dreams in living memory, while the Keepers engrave the Weavers' growth-patterns into salt crystal that will outlast even the kelp.

Both understand they are custodians of transformation—the Keepers guarding the end state of human industry, the Weavers nurturing what might come after. They share a mythology: that the world is processing itself into new forms, that their role is to midwife this transformation through patience and attention. When Weaver elders die, their bodies are sent downstream to the flats, where the beetles process them into the mineral record. When Keeper children show signs of the salt-change, some are sent upstream to live their final years among the ribbons, their crystallizing bodies becoming nucleation sites for new growths.

At night, when the ribbon farms glow with their bioluminescent signals and the salt flats shimmer with their toxic aurora, the two places seem to communicate across the distance—patterns of light answering each other in frequencies only the truly adapted can perceive. The Weavers say the kelp is learning from the beetles, incorporating patterns of efficiency and purpose into its endless growth. The Keepers say the beetles dream of photosynthesis, of transformation through light rather than chemistry.

Perhaps both are right. In this world balanced between death and becoming, between the mineral and the biological, categories lose meaning. The Weavers are becoming plant, the Keepers becoming mineral, and both are becoming something that has no name in any human language—witnesses to the earth's slow digestion of human ambition, gardeners of the ineffable, shepherds of the very long now.

In the ribbon farms, children grow in hammocks of living light. In the salt flats, they dance the patterns of machines. And between them flows the water, carrier of memory and metal, binding two worlds in the endless circulation of transformation.
