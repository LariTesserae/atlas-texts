Above the three-kilometer mark, where pressure relents enough for unmodified humans to work, the Salvage Terraces cascade up the continental shelf like a vertical city of rust and resurrection. The lowest platforms barely clear the abyssal waters, their support pillars disappearing into the dark where they anchor to the Works below. These structures rise in irregular tiers—some following the natural slope of the ancient seabed, others cantilevered out over the depths on massive trusses that groan with tidal stress.

The Reclaimers built this place from necessity and desperation, though their great-grandchildren have made it into something approaching civilization. They are the ones who refused the water, who chose instead to scrape out existence in the vertical margins between the sunlit surface and the mechanical abyss. Their city smells of salt rust and rendered oil, of kelp dried on metal grates and the ozone tang of overworked desalination units.

Each terrace specializes in its own form of reclamation. The lowest levels, where spray from the abyssal currents still coats everything in metallic brine, house the deep-diving operations. Here, crews in patched atmospheric suits descend on umbilical cables to strip useful components from the Works' upper surfaces. They harvest the self-healing hull plating before it fully integrates, pull thermal converters from the newest sectors, salvage the precious rare-earth elements from electronic systems that haven't yet learned to defend themselves. The work is dangerous—sometimes the Works interprets their presence as damage and activates repair protocols that can crush a diver in mechanical iris valves or flood their suits with industrial coolant.

Higher up, where the terraces widen into proper platforms, the processing districts sort and remake the salvage. Smelting furnaces burn kelp-oil and methane harvested from the bacterial mats, their smoke adding to the permanent haze that hangs between levels. Workers with faces marked by years of acidic vapor bend over conveyor systems, sorting components by composition, by function, by the inscrutable logic of what the up-surface traders will accept. Children learn to identify fifty different types of corrosion by sight, to judge the integrity of pressure seals by sound, to taste metals on their tongues and know their purity.

The residential terraces cluster around the desalination plants, their ramshackle buildings constructed from salvaged hull sections and driftwood that remembers forests. Families live in compartments barely larger than shipping containers, their walls lined with scavenged insulation and trophies from the depths—a section of pipe that sings in storm winds, circuit boards etched with patterns too beautiful to sell, preserved specimens of the bioluminescent fish that sometimes surface, confused by the lights.

The Reclaimers govern themselves through a complex system of salvage rights and territorial claims. Each family group maintains rights to specific sections of the Works below, passed down through generations and defended with desperate ferocity. They mark their claims with sonar beacons and chemical tracers, though the expanding substrate sometimes swallows these markers, leading to bloody disputes over newly emerged sectors. The Salvage Courts convene on the neutral platforms, where masked judges interpret the Law of Findings—an ever-evolving code that balances discovery rights with community needs.

Their technology is a patchwork of necessity. They've learned to adapt the Works' components to their own purposes—water purifiers cobbled together from industrial filters, power systems that tap into the thermal gradients between levels, communication arrays built from modified sonar systems that let them coordinate across the vertical city. Their engineers, trained through apprenticeship and bitter experience, speak a creole of technical languages, mixing pre-submersion terminology with words coined for phenomena that exist nowhere else.

The children of the Terraces grow up amphibious in spirit if not in body. They learn to read the moods of the abyss through the vibrations that travel up the support pillars, to predict when new sectors will surface based on the pattern of currents below. Some take the diving work, following their parents into the pressure suits. Others ascend to the trading platforms, learning the delicate negotiations with the surface vessels that still come, though less frequently each year. A few, driven by curiosity or desperation, take the transformative plunge and join the Adapted below—a choice that means exile from the Terraces but ensures their families receive a share of deep-territory salvage rights.

The Reclaimers maintain careful relations with the Adapted, though contact is limited to the transition zones where modified and unmodified humans can meet. They trade salvaged components for processed food, for information about safe diving zones, for warnings about the Works' expansion patterns. These meetings happen at the tide pools on the lowest terraces, where negotiators stand knee-deep in water that's neither fully abyssal nor fully surface, conducting business in a pidgin of light-signals and spoken words.

At night, when the work shifts end and the furnaces bank their fires, the Reclaimers gather on the observation decks to watch the light-shows below. The bioluminescent schools create rivers of illumination through the visible portions of the Works, while the Adapted's communication patterns flicker like aurora in the depths. Sometimes, on very clear nights when the water is still, they can see all the way down to where the substrate meets the ocean—that liminal space where mechanical and liquid engineering flow together in patterns too complex for unmodified minds to fully grasp.

The oldest Reclaimers remember stories of the time before, though these memories have been worn smooth by retelling, polished into myths that explain without accuracy. They speak of a great Submersion, when the land itself chose to dive, when the Works began its patient consumption of the seafloor. Whether this was catastrophe or intention, none can say. The records, if they existed, lie somewhere below in sectors no human—modified or otherwise—has reached.

But the Terraces endure, growing organically as each generation adds its own platforms, its own solutions, its own interpretations of vertical life. They are the margin-dwellers, the edge-workers, those who profit from the intersection of worlds. Their city rises from the mechanical depths like a ladder built by people who've forgotten where it was meant to lead, each rung a platform, each platform a story of adaptation without transformation.

Below them, the Works continues its expansion. Above them, the surface world grows more distant each year. They persist in between, salvaging what they can from the depths, building what they must from the salvage, creating a vertical civilization in the space between the sunlight and the abyss. They are the Reclaimers, and they have made a world from the pieces of a drowning one.
