The Ossuary Plains

Far below the Breathing Canopy, beyond fathoms of gradually thinning vegetation where the flesh-wood finally peters out into scraggly vertical roots like the last hairs on a balding scalp, spreads an entirely different world. Here, the ground is white as clean bone under an open sky that has never known the close embrace of leaves.

The Plains stretch to every horizon, a vast deposit of calcified history. What appears from a distance to be sand or salt reveals itself, upon closer inspection, to be comprised entirely of remains—shells, bones, carapaces, chitinous fragments, and other biological detritus ground fine by ages of wind. The surface crust crunches underfoot, while beneath lies stratum upon stratum of increasingly compressed material, the deepest layers fused into a kind of organic limestone shot through with veins of crystallized marrow.

The sky above is pale and merciless, a dome of white-blue heat during the day that leaches color from everything below. No clouds form here—the air is too dry, too thin, too hungry. It pulls moisture from anything exposed, mummifying flesh in hours, reducing even the hardest woods to brittle splinters in days. The sun tracks across this pallid expanse like a blazing eye, baking the bone-meal surface until it shimmers with heat mirages that make distances meaningless.

At night, the temperature plummets. The Plains radiate their stored heat into the star-pricked void, and frost forms on the calcium surfaces—delicate crystalline patterns that sublime directly back to vapor at first light. In these cold hours, the bones sing. Thermal contraction creates a symphony of tiny cracks and shifts, a percussion ensemble of the dead that rises and falls with the temperature gradient.

Wind is the only constant motion here. It blows ceaselessly from no fixed direction, sculpting the bone-dust into dunes that migrate slowly across the flatness. Sometimes it excavates deeper layers, exposing massive femurs or skull-plates of creatures whose living forms are lost to memory. These larger remains become temporary landmarks until they too are ground down and redistributed by the patient wind.

Strange formations punctuate the emptiness. Columns of fused vertebrae spiral up from the surface like abstract sculptures. Arrangements of ribcages, nested inside each other by some ancient force, create hollow chambers that howl when the wind passes through. In certain areas, the bone-meal has been compressed into geometric patterns—perfect circles, radiating lines, complex fractals—their origins and meanings as mysterious as their precision.

The Plains are not sterile, though life here follows different rules. Beneath the crushed-bone surface runs a network of fungal threads, transparent as glass, that feed on the concentrated calcium and phosphorus. These networks occasionally fruit into delicate structures that emerge at night—crystalline forms that look more mineral than biological, growing and releasing spores in the few hours before dawn's heat destroys them.

Pools of liquid bone exist in the deepest depressions, where centuries of rain—rare but devastating when it comes—have dissolved and concentrated the calcium into a substance like thick milk. These pools never fully dry, maintaining their uncanny liquidity through some chemical property of the concentrated death-matter. Their surfaces are mirrors during calm moments, perfectly reflecting the empty sky, but the slightest disturbance reveals their true nature as they ripple with unnatural viscosity.

The boundary between the Ossuary Plains and other realms is absolute. Where the bone-dust meets any other substrate—rock, soil, water—it forms a distinct line, as if the Plains refuse to mix with anything still touched by life. At the far edges, where rumors suggest mountains or seas might exist, the white expanse simply ends, a cliff-edge of compressed remains dropping into whatever lies beyond.

Sound behaves strangely here. The acoustic properties of the bone-substrate create zones of amplification and nullification. A whisper might carry for kilometers in certain directions, while a shout dies within meters in others. The wind exploits these acoustic channels, creating corridors of constant sound—whistling, humming, sometimes forming near-words that dissipate before meaning can cohere.

During the rare precipitation events, the Plains transform. The bone-dust absorbs moisture unevenly, creating temporary patterns of dark and light. Some areas liquify into calcium-rich mud while others remain powder-dry. Ancient seeds, preserved in the deepest layers, sometimes germinate in these brief wet periods, sending up pale shoots that live for hours or days before the returning drought mummifies them, adding their small measure to the accumulated dead.

This is a place of ending and patience, where the remnants of a million extinctions wait under an indifferent sky. It is archaeology made landscape, memory compressed into mineral, death become geography—a vast testament to everything that once lived, now ground to fundamental particles and spread beneath the burning air.

The distance between the Breathing Canopy and the Ossuary Plains is measured not in kilometers but in transitions. The Weavers know of the Plains through the Descenders—those few of their kind who have made the harrowing journey downward and returned, their bodies forever changed by the experience.

The Descenders tell of the Gradient, that terrible space where the flesh-wood thins and fails. They describe roots that reach downward into increasingly empty air, their tips desiccated and crumbling, raining a constant shower of dead plant matter that never quite reaches the bone-dust below. In this zone, they say, the air itself changes—the thick, nutritious humidity of the Canopy giving way to something thinner, harder, bright with a kind of light the Weavers have no words for.

Some Descenders claim to have glimpsed beings in the Plains—shapes that move against the wind, too regular to be dust-devils, too purposeful to be mirages. They speak in chemical whispers of creatures made entirely of bone-dust, constantly dissolving and reforming, painting themselves across the white expanse like living drawings that the wind erases and redraws. But these accounts are met with skepticism in the Canopy, where such dry, disconnected life seems impossible.

The two worlds exchange materials in the slowest of cycles. The constant rain of dead matter from the Canopy's lower reaches adds imperceptibly to the Plains' white expanse—a few grains of bone-dust per year, the remains of root-tips and shed bark compressed by their fall and the desiccating air. In return, the fungal networks of the Plains send up spores on thermal currents, some few of which reach the lower galleries of the Canopy. There, they attempt to colonize the flesh-wood but are quickly absorbed, their alien chemistry becoming part of the Canopy's ever-incorporating mass.

The Cambium Weavers have legends of the Plains, passed through their chemical folklore. They speak of it as the Under-White, the place where all growth ends, the antithesis of their verdant world. Some believe it to be the foundation upon which the Canopy ultimately rests, though none can explain how the soft flesh-wood could be supported by dust. Others maintain that the Plains are a glimpse of their own future—what the Canopy will become when its growth finally consumes itself and collapses into history.

In the deepest teaching-chambers of the eldest Weavers, certain chemical texts suggest a heretical connection: that the bone-dust of the Plains contains the ground remains of ancient Weavers, those who descended so far they lost their ability to reintegrate with the flesh-wood and crystallized in the hostile air. These texts claim that the geometric patterns in the Plains spell out messages in an older version of their chemical language, warnings or wisdoms from ancestors who chose dissolution over eternal growth.

The temporal discord between the worlds is profound. In the Canopy, time moves like sap—slow, cyclical, measured in growth rings and fruiting cycles. In the Plains, time is erosion—linear, entropic, measured in the gradual reduction of forms to powder. A Weaver's lifetime, centuries of patient cultivation, would witness barely perceptible change in the Plains' vast patterns. Conversely, the daily thermal cycles that crack and shift the bone-dust happen faster than a Weaver can fully perceive, their effects visible only in accumulation.

Yet both worlds share a strange commonality: they are each composed entirely of death transformed. The Canopy is built from the endless recycling of plant matter, growth feeding on growth in a closed cycle. The Plains are the opposite extreme—death that cannot return to life, remains that have passed beyond recovery into pure mineral memory. Between them, in that impossible Gradient where humid darkness meets dry light, hangs the tension of these two extremes: life that cannot stop growing and death that cannot stop dying.

The Descenders who return bring with them traces of the Plains—microscopic bone fragments embedded in their bark-skin that will never fully integrate, creating pearl-like nodes that other Weavers touch with careful reverence. These particles of absolute ending serve as anchors for meditation, reminders that somewhere below their eternal growth lies its perfect opposite: a place where all motion has ceased except for the patient wind writing and rewriting its stories in the dust of finished things.
