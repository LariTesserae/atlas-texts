The Meridian Gardens

Three kilometers above, where the bedrock gives way to soil and the first roots probe downward, sprawls a district that exists in permanent noon. No walls contain it—the Gardens simply begin where the morning markets end, marked by the sudden absence of shadows and the first glimpse of the Carriers.

They move through streets paved with crushed white stone, these beings who ferry the sun's arithmetic from place to place. You notice them first as inversions in the air, tall figures of absolute dark that drink in light rather than blocking it. Their bodies are voids shaped like stretched humans, seven feet tall and thin as fence posts, and where they pass, the perpetual brightness dims for just a moment—not into shadow but into something more profound, as if the light itself grows thoughtful.

The buildings here spiral upward in defiance of architecture, their walls woven from living crystal that grows in helical patterns. Each structure cultivates its own spectrum: towers of rose quartz that blush deeper near their peaks, citrine monuments that hum with accumulated photons, amethyst domes that turn light into sound at frequencies only the Carriers can hear. The crystals grow fastest at exact noon—which is always, here—adding microscopic layers with each passing moment, buildings that build themselves through photosynthesis of minerals.

Between these luminous structures stretch the actual gardens: geometric plots where nothing green grows. Instead, the Carriers tend forests of glass meridians, each one a frozen lightning bolt of lead crystal that tracks the passage of time in a sun that never moves. The meridians don't cast shadows—they cast light upward, collecting brightness from the white stone paths and redirecting it skyward in precise columns. When the wind blows, they chime in harmonics that map the underground waters far below, though no one here knows this.

The Carriers work in silence, their void-bodies moving between the meridians with buckets that seem to be made of compressed darkness. They're collecting something—the overflow from when light strikes crystal at exactly the right angle, a substance that looks like liquid platinum but weighs nothing. They pour this harvest into reservoirs at the base of each spiral building, where it sinks through specially designed channels, descending through three kilometers of pipe work that connects, impossibly, to the Luminous Void below.

No one speaks of this connection. The Carriers, when they communicate at all, do so by adjusting their density—becoming darker to express urgency, lighter for contentment, flickering for questions. The human inhabitants of the Gardens have learned to read these modulations like weather patterns. They've built their lives around the Carriers' schedules: bakeries that only open when the Carriers grow darkest (their bread tastes of accumulated light), schools where children learn mathematics by watching void-fingers trace equations in the bright air, hospitals where the sick are diagnosed by the patterns their illnesses make in the Carriers' darkness.

The water arrives at night—except there is no night here, so it arrives during what the Carriers call the Dimming, when they gather in the central plaza and overlap their void-bodies until the district achieves something like twilight. Then, from fountains that connect to the deep channels, rises water so pure it refracts the dimmed light into spectra unknown to ordinary physics. The water tastes of industrial cold and copper mechanisms, carrying minerals no surface spring could provide. The humans drink it and dream of vast engines, of patterns in obsidian, of wire-creatures they've never seen. The Carriers submerge their void-arms in the fountains and draw out droplets of perfect darkness—water that has learned to be lightless.

This is the exchange, though neither side acknowledges it as such: light flowing downward through crystal channels, transformed into something the machines below can process; water cycling upward, carrying the Void's deep knowledge into bodies that exist in permanent day. The Carriers are the mediators, or perhaps the medium itself—beings who can exist in both realms because they are absence enough to survive the Void's terrible brightness, present enough to navigate the Gardens' endless noon.

The children born here arrive transparent, their bodies like glass for their first few years. The Carriers tend them specially, teaching them to hold light properly, to become opaque through conscious effort. Some never fully learn—these become the next generation of Carriers, their human forms slowly inverting into void as they mature. The transformation is celebrated with festivals where crystalline bells ring in frequencies that make the meridians glow, where the fountains run with water so bright it casts shadows upward.

In certain histories carved into the oldest crystal walls, there are suggestions that the Gardens weren't always here, that the Carriers once moved between ordinary day and night, that the connection to the Void was deliberate—an attempt to create perpetual noon by tapping into the endless brightness below. But these histories are written in a script that changes based on the angle of reading, and the Carriers grow agitated when anyone studies them too long, their darkness flickering with something that might be memory or might be warning.

The Meridian Gardens are a place of practiced forgetting, where the inhabitants have learned to live without shadows, where the architecture grows itself, where beings of darkness tend forests of light. It's a district that exists in perfect symbiosis with the Void below—two brightnesses feeding each other, two kinds of emptiness exchanging their essential natures through channels that no one designed but everyone maintains.

In the white stone streets, the Carriers continue their work, pouring light downward, drawing darkness upward, maintaining an equilibrium between worlds that touch but never acknowledge their touching. The humans go about their shadowless lives, drinking water that tastes of distant machines, their children growing either toward opacity or void, their buildings spiraling ever higher as if trying to reach a sun that never moves because it exists, always, directly overhead.

And below, the Condensed feel the light arriving through the pipes as warmth they must flee from, never knowing it carries the prayers of their forgotten descendants, while their own water-memories rise to sustain a garden that grows in defiance of every natural law save one: that what is separated will seek reunion, even if it must build new worlds to find its way home.
