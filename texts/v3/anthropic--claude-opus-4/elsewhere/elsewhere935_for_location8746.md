**The Canopy Stations**

Seven miles up, where the air grows thin enough to drink stars, the Canopy Stations hang like fruit from branches that have never known soil. These are the docking boughs of the skygrove trees—living towers that rise from the cloudline to pierce the very roof of breathable atmosphere. Each tree grows hollow-cored, its heartwood dissolved by centuries of symbiotic moths that left behind spiral chambers wide as cathedral naves. Within these vertical tunnels, the station-builders have woven their suspended city, platform by platform, a civilization strung like pearls along the trees' inner walls.

The trees themselves defy reason. Their bark weeps a resin that hardens to amber in the high-altitude cold, creating a skin like geometric glass that refracts sunrise into rainbow spirals down their lengths. The wood beneath pulses with its own slow circulation—not sap but something between liquid and gas, a substance that carries nutrients up from the unseen roots and warmth down from the crown-leaves that spread above the station levels. This life-blood glows faintly blue when the temperature drops at night, creating pillars of light that can be seen from the ground on clear evenings like threads connecting earth to stars.

The platforms hang from living cables—vine-tendrils thick as bridge spans that the trees extrude from their inner walls. These grow in response to weight and use, thickening where the traffic is heavy, sprouting new shoots toward spaces where builders string their next expansions. The Station dwellers have learned to guide this growth with careful placement of weight stones and warming braziers, training the vines to create not just support but entire structural systems—archways of woven wood that flex with the high winds, basket-buildings that sway without breaking, nets of smaller tendrils that catch anyone who falls.

At this height, weather happens below rather than above. The Stations rise through cloud layers like islands in a vertical ocean, and the inhabitants have become readers of the patterns beneath. They watch the cloud-tops for signs of storms forming, track the shadows of lower weather systems, measure their days not by the sun's movement but by the cloud-tides that rise and fall with thermal currents. On certain mornings, the entire middle city disappears in a sea of white, leaving only the highest platforms touching clear sky like mountaintops.

The creatures here belong to the boundary between air and void. Great membrane-winged rays drift between the trees, feeding on the sparse insects that ride the jet streams. Their bodies are translucent in the high-altitude light, showing skeleton and organ in ethereal detail. Smaller flight-mice nest in the bark crevices, their fur adapted to trap and warm the thin air, creating personal atmospheres they carry with them. The crown-eagles build their aeries in the absolute peaks, where the leaves grow thick enough to support their massive nests—birds large as bears but hollow-boned as whispers, able to soar to heights where even the Station dwellers cannot follow.

The air itself requires negotiation. Newcomers spend weeks gasping, learning to move slowly, to take the deep, measured breaths that draw enough oxygen from the sparse atmosphere. Long-term inhabitants develop barrel chests and enlarged lungs, their blood grown thick with additional red cells. They speak in an economy of words, each sentence carefully rationed to preserve breath. Their language has evolved to include gesture and light-signals, conversations conducted with colored glass and mirror-flash when speech would steal too much air.

The technology here harnesses altitude itself. Wind-catchers of woven wood and membrane skin channel the constant high-altitude currents into power for their workshops. They've bred moths that spin at higher elevations, creating silk strong enough to bind platforms to trees, fine enough to filter the thin air into more breathable densities. Their homes cluster around central heat-wells—deep shafts drilled into the trees' warm cores where the circulation brings up earth-heat from far below.

Water comes from cloud-harvest. Great nets of moth-silk stretch between platforms during the night, collecting moisture that freezes to the strands. Morning sun melts this frost into collection vessels carved from the amber-hard resin. The Station dwellers have learned the delicate balance—too much heat and the water evaporates in the thin air, too little and it remains frozen. Their morning ritual involves the careful tending of melt-fires, gathering just enough warmth to release the night's harvest.

Light here has a quality unknown to ground-dwellers. Unfiltered by thick atmosphere, it arrives sharp as glass, carrying colors the lower world never sees—ultraviolets that the adapted eyes perceive as shades of silver, high blues that ring like bells in the vision. The shadows are absolute black, creating a world of stark contrasts. Dawn doesn't break but ignites, the sun appearing without warning over the curve of the world. Dusk lingers for hours, the light refracting through ice crystals in the upper air, painting the sky in spectrums that have required new words.

The Stations sprawl through multiple trees, connected by bridge-ways that flex and swing in the high winds. Some bridges are permanent—massive vine constructions that have grown thick as roads. Others are seasonal, light silk-and-wood constructions strung for summer travel and reeled in when the winter winds would snap them. The bravest travelers use the zip-lines—single cables of twisted moth-silk that allow rapid transit between trees, riders hanging from carved grips as they hurtle through the thin air.

Trade moves vertically rather than horizontally. Lowland goods rise through pulley systems housed within the hollow trees, while highland products descend on the same networks. The Station dwellers have monopolized certain substances—the high-altitude silks, the crown-leaf teas that grow only above the cloudline, the amber resin that hardens to jewel hardness. In return, they depend on the lower world for foods their altitude cannot grow, metals their wooden world cannot produce, news from the horizontal sprawl of life below.

At night, the Stations become a constellation. Each platform lights its lanterns—not for navigation, which happens by tree-knowledge and touch, but for connection. The patterns of light communicate between trees, between levels, creating a vertical city written in flame against the darkness. From below, when the clouds clear, it appears that stars have descended to nest in the skygrove crown. From above—from the domain of the crown-eagles and the rare balloonists who rise even higher—it looks like a galaxy caught in wooden fingers, reaching always upward toward the black where even trees cannot grow.

This is where the Skyweavers live, the ones who chose altitude over amplitude, height over breadth. They've built their civilization in the margins of the possible, in air so thin that life itself requires careful attention. Their city hangs between earth and space, suspended in trees that bridge the breathable and the void, creating homes in the vertical spaces where most would only see transit. They've learned that life exists not just in the thick, warm, wet places but also in the sharp, thin clarity where the world meets its limits.

---

The Skyweavers bear the marks of altitude in their very bones. Generations of life where air comes dear have sculpted them into something between bird and human—tall but light-framed, with ribcages expanded like bellows to house their massive lungs. Their skin carries a permanent flush from the concentration of blood vessels near the surface, giving them a rose-touched appearance that deepens to purple at their extremities where circulation works hardest to warm fingers and toes. Capillaries web visibly beneath their translucent skin, creating patterns like leaf-veins that darken when they exert themselves.

Their adaptations run deeper than surface. The Skyweavers' hearts beat slower but stronger than lowlanders', each contraction pushing oxygen-rich blood through bodies that have learned to extract every molecule of sustenance from the thin air. Their spleens have enlarged to store extra red blood cells, releasing them during exertion like internal emergency reserves. Children are born with fontanelles that close years later than groundling babies, allowing their skulls to expand as their brains develop the additional regions necessary for processing the complex three-dimensional navigation their world requires.

They dress in layers that trap warmth while allowing their skin to breathe—garments woven from the hollow-shaft fur of flight-mice, which creates insulation without weight. The outer robes are wind-cutting silk from high-altitude moths, dyed with inks extracted from cloud-dwelling organisms that shift color with air pressure. These pressure-robes serve as constant weather warnings, deepening from pale blue to storm-purple as systems approach from below. Beneath, they wear close-fitted suits of membrane leather, harvested respectfully from the great rays after natural death, which helps regulate temperature and provides emergency gliding surfaces if one should fall.

Their hair grows in ways unknown at lower altitudes—thick and wool-like, each strand hollow to provide additional insulation. They wear it long and elaborately braided, the patterns indicating not just family and profession but also altitude certification. A master high-worker's braids might include threads of actual cloud-spider silk, marking someone trained to work at the very crown of the trees where even Skyweavers must carry supplemental air. The elderly often develop a nimbus of white hair so fine it floats constantly in the high-altitude breezes, creating halos that earned them the lowlander name "cloud-touched."

The young undergo their altitude trials at adolescence, ascending alone to platform levels higher than their birth-place, learning to manage the increasing thinness of air. Some discover they lack the full adaptations—their blood won't thicken enough, their lungs can't expand sufficiently. These "heavy-blooded" often become the vertical traders, traveling the pulley routes between high and low, equally comfortable at middle altitudes where neither full adaptation is required. But those who pass their trials, who can work and think clearly at the crown levels, become the true high-dwellers, the ones who maintain the uppermost platforms where the trees touch space.

Their eyes have evolved to handle the unfiltered light. Secondary eyelids, transparent and thick as amber, slide across when they face the direct sun. Their irises contain crystalline structures that filter harmful radiation while allowing them to perceive the extended spectrum of high-altitude light. They see colors for which lowlanders have no names—the ultra-violets that reveal stress patterns in wood, the high blues that show air density variations, the silver-white frequencies that mark the presence of ice crystals in clear air. This expanded vision makes them extraordinary weather-readers but also artists of unique capability, creating works in spectrums only they can fully appreciate.

The Skyweavers organize their society vertically. Status isn't inherited but earned by altitude tolerance—the higher one can live and work comfortably, the greater their standing. The Council of Crown-Dwellers meets at the absolute peaks, where the air is so thin that even adapted lungs struggle, making their deliberations necessarily brief and decisive. Below them, communities organize by platform level, each attitude band developing its own specializations and customs.

Their architecture reflects their aerial existence. Buildings hang rather than stand, suspended from the great vine-cables in designs that accommodate constant motion. Walls curve like sails to deflect wind rather than resist it. Floors include careful gaps—trap-doors and movement spaces that allow pressure to equalize during storms. Every dwelling includes at least three exits, positioned to allow escape in multiple directions should platform stability fail. The aesthetic embraces impermanence; they build knowing that wind and growth will reshape their structures, designing with change as a fundamental principle.

They've domesticated creatures of the high air. Messenger moths, bred for generations, carry communications between trees on wings evolved to navigate jet streams. Small flight-mice serve as companion animals, their natural ability to trap warm air making them living hand-warmers. The bravest Skyweavers form bonds with the juvenile crown-eagles, training them from hatching to carry riders among the clouds—though only the lightest and most skilled dare attempt such partnerships, knowing that a fall from eagle-back means certain death.

Their tools reflect their weight obsession. Everything must justify its upward journey. They craft with hollowed woods and beaten moth-silk, creating implements strong as lowland metal but fractionally the weight. Their knives are grown rather than forged—shaped from the reinforced mandibles of high-altitude beetles, sharp as glass and light as feathers. Even their written language has adapted, using vertical scripts that follow the grain of wood-pulp paper, symbols that can be read while climbing or descending.

The Skyweavers age differently than groundlings. The constant work of breathing ages their lungs faster, but the high-altitude light and pure air preserve their skin and eyes. They remain vigorous into their seventies and eighties, though they gradually descend to lower platforms as their altitude tolerance decreases. The very elderly often settle at the cloud-line, spending their final years watching the weather form below, becoming the memory-keepers and storm-prophets their society depends upon.

Their children play games impossible at lower altitudes—launching themselves between platforms with moth-silk wings, learning to read wind currents before they can properly walk. Adolescents compete in "thread-dancing," navigating complex webs of silk strung between branches while carrying full water vessels, training balance and breath control simultaneously. Education happens in vertical classrooms where students must climb to reach different subjects, the physical journey part of the learning.

They measure time not by sun or season but by pressure changes. Their bodies have become living barometers, sensing approaching systems in the ache of joints, the thickness of breath. They speak of "pressure-days" and "thin-nights," organizing their calendar around the breathing of the atmosphere itself. Festivals coincide with rare high-pressure systems that bring easier breathing to the upper platforms, while their periods of mourning align with the low-pressure systems that make even adapted lungs labor.

This is a people sculpted by extremity into something new. They've taken human flesh to its vertical limits, adapting not just to survive but to thrive where the world grows thin. In choosing the harsh beauty of altitude over the comfortable thickness of lowland air, they've become translators between earth and sky, creating a civilization that exists in the margin between breathing and breathlessness, weight and flight, the solid platform and the endless fall.
