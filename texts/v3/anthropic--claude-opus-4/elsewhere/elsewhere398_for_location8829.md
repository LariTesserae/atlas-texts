Beyond the salt flats, where the crystal plains finally fracture and rise, the land transforms into something that defies easy comprehension. The Calcite Forests grow here—though "grow" poorly captures their nature, and "forest" is merely the closest approximation language offers.

These are not trees but columns of living stone that tower forty, fifty, sometimes a hundred meters into the air. Each pillar is a colony organism, millions of polyps smaller than rice grains that secrete calcium carbonate in intricate patterns. The polyps themselves are translucent, barely visible, but their collective architecture creates structures of breathtaking complexity: spiraling buttresses, latticed chambers, bridges that span between columns like frozen waterfalls. The calcite they produce is not pure white but shot through with veins of color—deep purples where manganese infiltrates their chemistry, brilliant greens from copper, reds from iron—creating a landscape that shifts from ghostly pale in overcast weather to blazing jewel-tones when the sun strikes directly.

The forest floor is soft with millennia of shed polyp casings, a meter-thick carpet that crunches underfoot like snow but with an organic give that speaks of decomposition. This detritus feeds its own ecosystem: boring worms that tunnel through the fallen matter, creating ventilation networks; fungal-analogues that spread in networks of filaments, breaking down the calcium and releasing it back to the living columns above; clouds of spore-mites that rise in the morning warmth, their bodies glittering with incorporated calcium crystals.

Water here is abundant but strange. It percolates up through the porous stone substrate, drawn by capillary action through the column organisms themselves. Each pillar sweats constantly, beading with mineral-rich moisture that runs down channels evolved over millions of years. Where columns cluster close together, these trickles merge into waterfalls that cascade through the latticed architecture, creating a constant music of falling water—but water that carries so much dissolved mineral it leaves flowstone deposits wherever it pools, building new structures even as it erodes others.

The dominant fauna here are the Chord Weavers—creatures that exist primarily as living sound. Their bodies are long, impossibly thin filaments that stretch between the columns, some spanning gaps of thirty meters or more. These filaments vibrate at frequencies that span from subsonic to ultrasonic, creating complex harmonics that echo through the calcite chambers. The Weavers feed on the resonance itself—their metabolism somehow extracting energy from acoustic vibration, possibly by exploiting microscopic flexing in the crystal structure of the columns. Their young start as barely-audible whispers, single strands that produce pure tones. As they mature, they divide and rebraid, creating complex chords that can be hauntingly beautiful or deeply disturbing.

Moving through the mid-levels of the forest are the Readers—flat, disk-shaped beings covered in specialized sensory cells that detect minute variations in mineral composition. They glide along the column surfaces, their undersides secreting a mild acid that allows them to taste the chemical history locked in the stone. Where they pause to feed intensively, they leave behind shallow depressions lined with modified calcite that's harder and more translucent than the surrounding material—windows into the column's interior that other creatures have learned to use as observation points.

The apex predators here are the Resonance Hunters. These creatures have evolved bodies that are essentially living acoustic chambers—hollow shells of modified bone and cartilage that can amplify and focus sound. They stalk the Chord Weavers by producing counter-frequencies that cancel out their prey's vibrations, effectively silencing sections of the forest. In the sudden dead zones they create, the Weavers become visible as their filaments cease vibrating, allowing the Hunters to strike with projectile tongues that can extend ten times their body length.

In the deepest parts of the forest, where columns cluster so densely that ground level exists in perpetual twilight, the Cultivators tend their gardens. These are collective intelligences—networks of neural tissue that spread across multiple columns, connecting through bridge-roots that transfer both nutrients and information. The Cultivators farm the polyps themselves, selectively encouraging growth in patterns that create specific acoustic environments. Some chambers are tuned to amplify certain frequencies, others to dampen them. The most ancient Cultivator networks have created spaces where sound behaves in seemingly impossible ways—words spoken at one end emerge transformed at another, echoes arrive before their source, and certain tones can trigger bioluminescent displays in the polyps that paint the darkness with cold light.

The boundary between salt flat and forest is not a clean line but a zone of transformation several kilometers wide. Here, the chemistry of two worlds meets and produces phenomena found nowhere else. Salt-tolerant polyps create low, spreading structures rather than tall columns. The mineral content creates formations of salt-infiltrated calcite that grow in ways that follow neither ecosystem's rules—spiraling ramps that rise like frozen whirlwinds, hollow spheres large enough to shelter entire communities of creatures, maze-like networks of tubes and chambers where the acoustic properties of the forest meet the crystalline geometries of the flats.

In this transition zone, hybrid creatures thrive. The Glass Shepherds—descendants perhaps of the Brine Shepherds adapted to forest life—string networks of mineral fiber between infant columns and dying salt formations. They cultivate gardens of crystalline organisms that can process both salt and calcium, creating micro-environments where creatures from both realms can meet and, sometimes, exchange resources.

The Pilgrims travel between worlds. These are descendants of the cathedral crawlers who have evolved for a more mobile existence. Their shells, while smaller than their flatland cousins, incorporate both salt crystal and calcium carbonate in complex laminar structures. They can survive the chemistry of both environments, though they thrive in neither. Instead, they follow routes through the transition zone, feeding on the unique organisms found there and serving as living bridges—their waste products providing familiar chemicals that allow some forest creatures to venture onto the salt and some salt creatures to approach the forest edge.

During the rare storms that sweep across both ecosystems, the connection becomes most apparent. Rain that falls on the forest emerges from the ground in the salt flats days or weeks later, carrying with it dissolved calcium and the chemical signatures of forest life. This underground flow is mapped by the Vein Dancers—serpentine creatures that live in the water-carved channels between bedrock and surface. They follow the underground rivers, their bodies incorporating both salt and calcium in bands that record their journey like living core samples.

At certain times of year, when temperature and humidity align precisely, fog forms where forest moisture meets salt-dried air. In these fog banks, temporary ecosystems bloom—aerial gardens of organisms that exist nowhere else, feeding on condensation and dissolved minerals. The Fog Fishers emerge then from both realms, creatures adapted to harvest these temporary bounties. From the forest come beings like living nets, all membrane and patient stillness. From the salt come crystalline formations that suddenly animate, unfolding into forms that can filter feed from the thick air.

The sound of the boundary zone is a conversation between worlds. The click of crystal cilia meets the harmony of Chord Weavers. The groan of thermal expansion in salt plays counterpoint to the settling of calcite columns. And underneath it all, in frequencies almost too low to perceive, the Memories from beneath the salt flats exchange slow chemical messages with the Cultivator networks, a dialogue measured in decades, about matters no faster-living creature could begin to comprehend.

This is how the two worlds connect—not as separate realms with a border between them, but as parts of a greater whole. The salt flats' chemistry feeds the forest through underground waters. The forest's organic acids, carried by rain and Pilgrims, create new mineral gradients in the flats. Creatures evolve to exploit the gradients between extremes, becoming bridges themselves. And in the zone between, where neither ecosystem dominates, new forms of life emerge that could exist nowhere else—reminders that boundaries, in the living world, are always places of creation.
