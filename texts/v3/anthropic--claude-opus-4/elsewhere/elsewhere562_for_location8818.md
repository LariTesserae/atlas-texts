Above the Sunken Quarter, reached by spiral stairs cut directly into the bedrock, lies the **Market of Echoes**. Here, in a natural cavern that opens like a vast mouth in the earth, the surface world meets the drowned one in carefully orchestrated exchange.

The cavern spans perhaps two hundred feet at its widest, its ceiling lost in darkness so complete it seems solid. The floor has been laboriously leveled and paved with hexagonal stones that fit together like a puzzle, each one carved with minute drainage channels that carry away the constant moisture. Iron rings bolt into these stones at regular intervals, anchor points for the temporary structures that appear and vanish with the market days.

When active, the market fills with collapsible stalls made from bamboo and treated leather, designed to resist the perpetual damp. Merchants from the surface world arrive before dawn, descending through one of three tunnel entrances that connect to the lands above. They bring what the Sunken Quarter cannot produce: metals and minerals, dried goods that would spoil below, tools forged in furnaces that require true dryness, and most importantly, news from the world of sun and wind.

The acoustics here are extraordinary. The cavern's shape creates a natural amphitheater where every sound reflects and returns transformed. Whispers carry across the entire space, while normal speech layers into overlapping conversations that merge and separate like braiding streams. The merchants have developed a cadenced way of calling their wares that works with these echoes rather than against them, creating rhythmic patterns that identify their goods without words. A copper merchant's call rings like bells, while grain sellers speak in hushing syllables that suggest rustling wheat.

Light comes from oil lamps hung on chains from the ceiling, their flames protected by glass chimneys that the surface dwellers bring and maintain. The Kithara cannot use fire in their flooded realm, so this dancing illumination fascinates them. Children who have never seen flame will stand mesmerized, watching shadows play across the cavern walls. The lamplight catches on pools of water that collect despite the drainage system, creating mirrors that double the apparent number of lights and add to the disorienting quality of the space.

The surface dwellers who trade here are weather-beaten people, toughened by life under open sky. They dress in oiled leather and thick wool, materials that shed water rather than embrace it. Their skin shows the damage of sun and wind—creases and spots the Kithara never develop in their protected environment. They speak quickly, urgently, as if the slower pace of the Sunken Quarter might trap them if they linger.

These traders come from the **Drylands**, a territory that begins just beyond the cavern mouths and stretches to the horizon. It's a place of extremes—scorching summers and frozen winters, where rain falls in violent bursts or not at all. The people there build from mud bricks baked hard in the sun, structures that would dissolve in the Sunken Quarter's perpetual moisture. They farm hardy grains in fields that alternately flood and parch, and raise goats that can survive on thorny scrub.

The relationship between the two peoples is ancient but never easy. The Drylanders need the medicinal extracts and unique materials the Kithara produce—nowhere else can they find the healing moss that stops bleeding, or the fiber that remains supple in any weather. The Kithara, in turn, require metals for their few tools, grains they cannot grow, and connection to the wider world beyond their watery boundaries.

But trust comes hard between those who live so differently. The Drylanders tell stories of people who went too deep into the Sunken Quarter and came back changed, their lungs laboring in dry air, their skin cracking without constant moisture. They speak of children stolen to become Kithara themselves—though no Kithara would recognize their careful fostering of refugee young as theft. The Kithara, for their part, find the Drylanders' harsh urgency disturbing, their willingness to burn and consume rather than cultivate and tend.

The Market of Echoes serves as neutral ground, a space neither fully wet nor dry where both peoples can meet in relative comfort. The cavern's atmosphere splits the difference—humid enough that the Kithara can breathe easily, dry enough that the Drylanders' goods don't spoil. Negotiations happen at stone tables worn smooth by generations of trade, each side bringing their own seated cushions—the Kithara's made of living moss that they moisten from water skins, the Drylanders' of felted wool that resists damp.

An elaborate system of customs governs these exchanges. The Kithara will not trade before noon, needing the morning hours to prepare their breathing for the drier air. The Drylanders insist on examining goods in full lamplight, suspicious of anything that might look different in their sun-struck world. Both sides use hollow glass spheres as currency—blown by Dryland glassmakers but filled with extract by Kithara alchemists, creating a money that literally requires both peoples to produce.

Children of both communities often meet here for the first time, staring at each other across the physical and cultural divide. Young Kithara, their gill slits fluttering nervously in the drier air, reach out to touch the rough fabric of Drylander clothes. Dryland children dare each other to dip their fingers in the water pools, shrieking at the cold that Kithara children find perfectly comfortable.

Some few individuals live between the worlds. These are the translators and mediators, people who spend half their time in each realm. They develop a peculiar physiology—skin thicker than typical Kithara but still requiring regular moisture, lungs stronger than most but still struggling in true drought. They often show signs of the strain: premature aging, chronic coughs, a restlessness that belongs to neither world. But their service is invaluable, as they alone truly understand both peoples' needs.

The journey between realms follows ancient paths. From the Sunken Quarter, swimmers navigate flooded passages that gradually shallow until they become mere streams, then dry tunnels that rise through the rock. The transition zones are marked by carved warnings in both pictographic scripts—symbols that mean "last water" and "first air," boundaries that mark not just physical but existential transitions.

In times of exceptional need, delegations travel beyond the Market. Kithara envoys have journeyed to the Drylands during plague years, bringing medicines that require fresh preparation. Drylander engineers have descended to the Sunken Quarter when earthquakes damaged crucial water channels. These exchanges are fraught with difficulty—Kithara must carry water supplies and protective garments, while Drylanders struggle with the pressure changes and perpetual moisture.

The Market of Echoes holds memory as surely as it holds sound. In certain corners where the acoustics are most complex, you can sometimes hear fragments of past conversations—ancient trades, declarations of friendship or enmity, the laughter of long-dead children. Both peoples consider these echo-memories sacred, proof that their connection transcends individual lives or temporary disputes.

During the darkest month of winter, when the Drylands freeze and the Sunken Quarter's water grows so cold that ice forms on the surface, both peoples cease trading. But they maintain the lamps in the Market, taking turns to ensure the flames never die. It's a symbol of their mutual dependence—the Drylanders providing oil, the Kithara tending wicks with their patient cultivation skills, both ensuring that when spring comes, the light will still be there to guide them back together.

The Market operates on a rhythm negotiated over centuries. Three days of trade, four of rest. This gives the cavern time to breathe, moisture levels to stabilize, and both peoples to prepare for the next encounter. On rest days, you might find solitary individuals from either world sitting in the empty market, contemplating the other life they might have lived, the other air they might have breathed, before returning to their own realm with renewed appreciation for what they are.

Between the markets, in the deep passages that connect water to air, there are spaces that belong fully to neither world. Here, in chambers half-flooded and half-dry, grow things that exist nowhere else—fungi that fruit only at the water's edge, minerals that crystallize from the meeting of different atmospheres. Some say these between-spaces are spreading, that the boundary between wet and dry is not fixed but fluid, expanding grain by grain, drop by drop, until perhaps one day there will be no pure realms at all, only the infinite gradations between.
