The Sky Miners work their trade five hundred kilometers above, where the continental shelf meets true ocean, though they've never seen the Benthos Works that sprawls beneath the abyssal plain. Their world is one of perpetual motion—vast extraction platforms that drift with the currents, following the seasonal migrations of the phosphorescent blooms that cloud the upper waters twice each year.

Their rigs are cities built for transit: crystalline towers that descend thirty meters below the surface, trailing curtains of molecularly-precise mesh that sieve the glowing organisms from the water. The platforms themselves stay mobile through a combination of wind capture, current reading, and the controlled burning of processed bloom-oil in engines that rumble through the night. Each rig hosts five thousand souls, though the population swells during the dense seasons when the blooms arrive in force and extra hands mean the difference between prosperity and privation.

The work is in the timing. The phosphorescent organisms—ancient colonies that predate any human presence in these waters—surface only when conditions align: temperature gradients, moon phases, and deep currents that even the most experienced readers can't fully predict. When they rise, they come in clouds that turn the ocean into a living constellation, each colony pulsing with cold light that attracts the vast schools of filter-feeders that follow in their wake. The Miners must position their platforms precisely, their mesh curtains deployed at exactly the right depth to capture the colonies without destroying them. Too aggressive, and the organisms dissolve into useless slurry. Too gentle, and they slip through to continue their journey, taking their light and their value with them.

The Miners process what they catch in the refinery levels, great halls where the air tastes of salt and ozone. They extract the luciferin compounds that make the colonies glow, concentrate them into vials that will eventually light homes and streets in cities thousands of kilometers away. The organic waste becomes feedstock for their protein farms, where vats of engineered algae convert the remnants into food that tastes of nothing but sustains everything. Nothing is wasted in the Sky Miner culture—water, food, fuel, even breath is recycled through systems they maintain with religious devotion.

They live by the Drift Code, laws written not in words but in the movements of their platforms. Right of way belongs to whoever reads the currents truest. Disputes are settled by racing—platforms competing to reach the next bloom site first, their navigators standing at the helm towers with arms spread, feeling the wind and water through instruments worn like second skins. The best navigators develop an uncanny sense for where the blooms will surface, following signs invisible to others: the particular green of the water at depth, the way certain seabirds circle, the taste of the spray when waves break against the hull.

Their children grow up in motion, learning to walk on decks that never stop swaying. By five, they can tie the seventeen essential knots. By ten, they're apprenticed to a guild: Readers who predict the blooms, Weavers who maintain the mesh, Refiners who process the catch, or Engines who keep the platforms moving. The youth race small skimmers between the platforms during calm weather, leaping between moving vessels with casual grace, their bodies adapted to constant motion through generations of selection both natural and cultural.

The old stories speak of the time before the platforms, when the Miners' ancestors worked from fixed stations on the continental shelf. But something changed in the deep waters—currents shifting, temperatures rising or falling, some alteration that caused the blooms to abandon their ancient static grounds for the open ocean. The Miners followed, first in small vessels, then in larger ones, finally in the great platforms that became their world. They speak of the Transition with pride: how their great-grandparents chose motion over starvation, adaptation over extinction.

They know the waters below them hold mysteries. Sometimes their deepest mesh catches things that shouldn't exist at those depths—fragments of metal that seem grown rather than forged, fish with modifications too perfect to be natural evolution. The eldest Readers speak of lights glimpsed far below during the calmest nights, patterns too regular to be bioluminescence, too vast to be any vessel they know. But these are concerns for the deep-workers, whoever they might be. The Miners have their own world to tend, their own cycles to follow.

The platforms communicate through light—great beacons that flash coded messages across the water. During the dense seasons, when dozens of rigs converge on particularly rich grounds, the night fills with conversation: coordinates shared, warnings given, trades negotiated in patterns of brilliance. They've developed a visual language as complex as any spoken tongue, capable of poetry as well as precision. The young often climb the beacon towers at night to flash messages to friends on distant platforms, creating their own rapid dialect that drives the elders to distraction.

Their faith, such as it is, centers on the Eternal Circuit—the great gyre that they believe carries water from surface to abyss and back again over centuries. They drop weighted messages into the deep during important ceremonies: births, deaths, successful seasons. The messages are carved into specially cultivated shells that will dissolve at precise depths, releasing their words into the current. They believe these messages reach those who need them, carried by the same forces that bring the blooms to the surface. Some claim to have received answers—shells that surface carrying marks no surface tool could make, patterns that speak of acknowledgment from the deep places.

The Miners wear their wealth as luminescence, embedding processed luciferin under their skin in patterns that mark their achievements. A master Reader might have constellation maps traced across their shoulders. A skilled navigator bears the current patterns of successful seasons in glowing lines along their arms. The young earn their first marks during their apprenticeships, simple dots and lines that will grow into complex designs if they prove themselves worthy.

When the sparse seasons come—and they always come—the platforms scatter to conserve resources. Some follow lesser blooms barely worth the fuel to chase them. Others anchor in the doldrums, switching to subsistence mode, their populations surviving on stored food and collected rainwater while they wait for the currents to shift. These are the testing times, when the weak platforms fail and their populations must be absorbed by the strong. The Miners have a hundred words for different kinds of hunger, each one teaching patience in its own way.

They build nothing permanent, leave no marks on the ocean except the wake of their passage. Their entire culture is motion—platforms drifting with purpose, following the ancient patterns of the blooms, processing light from living things, trading illumination for survival. Below them, far below, the Works continues its expansion, its lights too deep to penetrate the surface, its purposes too alien to imagine. But the Miners don't look down. They look out, across the water, reading the signs that will tell them where the next bloom will surface, where they need to position their platforms to continue their eternal circuit.

The two worlds touch only in the water that connects them—the same ocean that presses down on the Adapted in their mechanical abyss rises to lift the Sky Miners' platforms. The same currents that carry warmth from the Works' thermal vents eventually surface in upwellings that influence the blooms' movements. When the Adapted release their chemical signals into the deep waters, some molecules eventually reach the surface, where sensitive Miners taste changes they can't explain. When the Miners drop their ceremonial messages, some sink far enough to be caught in the Works' intake systems, where Adapted scholars puzzle over shells carrying marks from another world.

They are connected by the Eternal Circuit the Miners believe in, though neither fully grasps the shape of it—water rising and falling, carrying heat and chemistry and perhaps intention between worlds that share an ocean but not a understanding. Each follows their own imperatives: the Adapted integrating ever deeper with their mechanical environment, the Miners chasing their phosphorescent prey across the endless surface. Both have chosen adaptation over extinction, both have made homes in the inhospitable.

The ocean holds them both, vast enough to contain their separate stories, deep enough to hide the connections between them. And in the space between surface and abyss, in the kilometers of middle water where neither culture ventures, other things move—currents and creatures and purposes that belong fully to neither world, threading between them like possibility itself.
