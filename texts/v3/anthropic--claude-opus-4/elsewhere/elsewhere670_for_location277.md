**The Conservatory Heights**

Seven miles upriver, before the water learns the taste of metal, the city shows another face entirely. Here the hills rise in gentle terraces, each level claimed by glass and iron structures that catch the morning light like prisms. The Conservatory Heights sprawl across these elevations—a district of crystal palaces and ordered gardens where the air tastes of orange blossom and clean rain.

The architecture speaks a different language here: delicate where the foundry district is sturdy, transparent where the other is opaque. Soaring glass domes crown buildings of white limestone, their metal frameworks traced in gold leaf that will never know corrosion. Climate controls hum quietly behind marble facades, maintaining perfect temperatures for the orchid collections, the butterfly pavilions, the halls of tropical ferns that have never known a true winter. Water features dance between the structures—fountains that arc in mathematical precision, channels lined with imported tile that guide snowmelt from the distant mountains through gardens of impossible plenty.

Sound here is carefully curated. The fountains are tuned to harmonize, their splashing creating a constant musical backdrop. Wind chimes of silver and brass hang at calculated intervals, their tones chosen to complement rather than compete. Birds sing, but only the approved species—nothing raucous or common. Peacocks strut across manicured lawns, their cries adding an exotic punctuation. In the evening, string quartets play in the gazebos, their music carrying across the terraces on breezes that smell of jasmine and imported tea.

The plants obey different rules here. They grow in assigned spaces, trimmed and trained into living sculptures. Roses climb trellises in perfect spirals, their thorns bred out generations ago. Fruit trees espaliered against warm walls bear peaches in winter, cherries in fall—the seasons rearranged to suit aesthetics rather than nature. The lawns are emerald carpets, each blade of grass uniform in height, maintained by an army of gardeners who work in the pre-dawn darkness so as not to disturb the illusion of effortless perfection.

Nothing ages here—or rather, nothing is allowed to show its age. Surfaces are cleaned daily, metal polished, glass washed until it disappears. The limestone is treated with chemicals that repel moss and lichen. When storms come, teams emerge immediately after to repair any damage, to replace any cracked pane, to right any toppled topiary. Time passes but leaves no mark, as if the Heights exist in a bubble separate from the patient decay that claims the rest of the world.

The river here runs clear and quick, its banks reinforced with cut stone, its flow directed through decorative channels. Swans glide across artificial lakes, their wings clipped just enough to prevent true flight. Ornamental fish flash gold and white in pools designed to showcase their colors. The water features are drained and cleaned weekly, their pumps filtered, their tiles scrubbed. Nothing unexpected is allowed to take root, nothing uninvited permitted to flourish.

At night, gas lamps burn steady and bright, their light reflected and multiplied by the glass structures until the Heights glow like a constellation brought to earth. The shadows they cast are precise, architectural, without mystery. Even in darkness, nothing is hidden—the transparent walls ensure that life here is always on display, always observed, always performing its careful choreography of cultured existence.

---

In the Conservatory Heights live the Curators—those who have dedicated themselves to the preservation and presentation of beauty as they understand it. They move through their days with studied elegance, their clothes unstained, their hands soft from oils and creams, their voices modulated to never rise above what is pleasant.

Lord Ashworth oversees the Orchid Pavilion, where two thousand varieties bloom in climate-controlled sections. He has not touched soil in twenty years—his staff handle such matters—but can identify each specimen by scent alone. His fingernails are perfect ovals, his morning coat never shows a wrinkle, his knowledge of botanical Latin exhaustive. He corresponds with collectors across three continents, trading in bulbs worth more than the foundry workers see in a year. When he speaks of his flowers, his voice takes on an almost religious reverence—for the pure lines, the cultivated colors, the triumph of human selection over nature's randomness.

The children here learn different lessons. Young Adelaide takes watercolor instruction in the Morning Room, her brushstrokes capturing the precise curve of a calla lily, the exact shade of a sunset through crystal. She has never seen a sunset unmediated by glass, never touched a flower not bred for perfection. Her governesses ensure she speaks three languages fluently, plays the pianoforte with technical precision, can organize a dinner party for forty without consulting the staff. She does not know that in another part of the city, children her age dive for copper scraps, but she donates her pin money to charitable causes she understands only in abstract.

The gardeners work in choreographed silence. Master Gardner Chen (no relation to Mrs. Chen of the tea shop—the Heights would never acknowledge such connection) directs his team with hand signals developed over decades. They move like dancers through the grounds, their tools gleaming, their uniforms spotless despite the nature of their work. Each knows their section intimately: which roses need copper sulfate, which fruit trees require hand-pollination, which lawns must be reseeded to maintain their perfect texture. They take pride in their invisibility, in maintaining the illusion that such beauty simply exists, requires no labor, no sweat, no sacrifice.

But there are cracks in this perfection—hairline fractures that those who look closely might perceive. Mrs. Fairweather, who maintains the Butterfly Conservatory, has noticed changes. Some chrysalises produce unexpected colors, patterns not catalogued in any collection. She documents these variations privately, tells no one of the midnight emergence of moths that should not exist, their wings carrying designs that seem almost like writing. She has begun to dream of flying, of transformation unconstrained by glass walls.

Doctor Pemberton runs the Heights' medical clinic, treating the nervous exhaustion that plagues so many residents. The constant performance of perfection takes its toll—hands that shake when trying to pour tea just so, voices that crack when maintaining pleasant conversation, eyes that water from too much artificial light. He prescribes rest cures, dietary adjustments, sessions in the Salt Chamber where imported minerals supposedly restore balance. But lately, he's noticed his patients speaking of the same dream: water rising, glass breaking, green things growing wild and uncontrolled. He increases their doses, recommends longer treatments, and tries not to think about his own dreams of rust and moss.

The servants know things their employers do not. They rise before dawn, moving through the Heights like ghosts, ensuring that each new day appears as pristine as the last. But they see the mice in the walls, the mold that grows behind the perfect wainscoting, the cracks in the foundations where water—patient, persistent—finds its way. They whisper among themselves of Marie, who worked in the Palm House for thirty years until one morning she was found transformed, her skin bark-rough, her hair trailing vines. They say she's still there, hidden among the tropical plants, finally at peace.

The Collectors form the Heights' true aristocracy. Lady Pembroke owns the finest assembly of carnivorous plants in the known world, each specimen documented, preserved under glass bells that regulate humidity to the fraction of a percent. Mr. Fitzgerald has spent his fortune on roses, cultivating varieties that bloom in impossible colors—true black, perfect silver, a blue that captures the exact shade of twilight. They compete quietly, viciously, their rivalries played out in auction houses and private sales, in the subtle warfare of garden parties where each display attempts to eclipse the last.

And moving between the Heights and the foundry district, though neither fully acknowledges it, are the Traders. They arrive at dawn with carts of specialized soil, of chemical fertilizers, of the fungal preparations that keep the orchids free of disease. They speak little of where these supplies originate, of the workshops that process the industrial byproducts into the substances that maintain the Heights' perfection. Young Thomas makes this journey daily, his morning route taking him from the dripping alleys of the foundry district to the pristine service entrances of the Heights. He carries more than goods—he carries spores on his boots, seeds in his hair, the persistent life of one world attempting to colonize another.

The water tells the true story. The same river that moves sluggishly through the foundry district runs clear and quick here, but it carries memories. On certain nights, when the moon is dark and the fountains are turned off for maintenance, you can hear it whispering of its journey—of the chemicals it has known, the dreams it has dissolved, the changes it has witnessed. The swan keepers notice their charges growing restless on these nights, their wings stretching against their careful clipping, their eyes reflecting depths that decorative birds should not possess.

This is who lives in the Conservatory Heights: the preservers and the preserved, those who believe beauty can be captured and held, who think perfection is achievable through sufficient control. They inhabit their crystal palace unaware that downstream, their discarded fertilizers feed different dreams, that the very chemicals maintaining their artificial Eden create new Edens elsewhere, wild and unplanned. They sleep peacefully behind their walls of glass, not knowing that in the foundry district, the dreamcatchers record visions of ivy-covered conservatories, of orchids growing wild among the rust, of a future where the careful boundaries between their worlds dissolve like sugar in the patient rain.
