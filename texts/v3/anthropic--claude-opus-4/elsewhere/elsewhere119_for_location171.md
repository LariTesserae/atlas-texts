Below the Suspended Gardens, where the Fountain Tree's roots disappear into hungry darkness, lies the Underscar—a wound in the world that refuses to heal.

The Scar begins a thousand feet beneath Uth-Mara, where the water-air thins to mere humidity and gravity reasserts its ancient claim. Here, the coral formations grow wild and wrong—not the patient architectures of the Weavers above, but writhing, cancerous blooms that build upon themselves in fractal madness before collapsing under their own weight. The crashes echo up the empty air, a percussion that the Weavers call the Dolor Drums, timing their lives to avoid the peak hours of collapse when debris rains upward like inverted hail.

This is where the Unravelers make their home.

They were Coral-Weavers once, or their ancestors were. But something in them rebels against the slow calcification, the patient submission to the reef. They are the ones who cut away their coral scars before they could take root, leaving raw wounds that weep constantly in the moisture. They are the ones whose children dream too deeply, too strangely, whose visions corrupt the communal pools. They are the throwbacks whose bones stay dense, who sink rather than float, who must live by different rules.

The Unravelers build hanging cities of salvage and defiance. Their structures dangle from the stronger coral malformations on chains of woven sinew and repurposed root systems. When the coral collapses—and it always collapses—they simply rebuild elsewhere, their architecture as temporary as their philosophy is absolute. They believe in entropy, in the beauty of decay, in the necessity of things falling apart.

Where the Weavers cultivate, the Unravelers harvest the wild mutations. They farm the cancer-coral for its psychoactive polyps, brewing potions that cause temporary calcification of specific organs—stone hearts that feel nothing, limestone lungs that need no air, crystallized eyes that see through time. They hunt the things that fall from above—confused fish, lost rays, and sometimes, whisper the children, Weavers who floated too far from home. They mine the aerial roots of the Fountain Tree for the oil-sap that runs through them, refining it into combustible fuel that powers their few mechanical devices—screaming drills that carve temporary homes from coral cliffs, harpoon guns that fire calcified bone through the thick air, furnaces that burn hot enough to smelt the metallic nodules that sometimes grow in the deepest coral tumors.

The Unravelers modify themselves differently than their cousins above. Where Weavers accept symbiosis, Unravelers practice violent augmentation. They graft limbs from the pressure-adapted creatures that survive at this depth—mantis shrimp arms that can shatter coral, octopus skin that camouflages against the chaotic growth patterns, bioluminescent organs carved from deep jellies and transplanted beneath their skin in constellation patterns. Each modification is a rejection of their original form, a step away from what the Weavers call harmony.

Their children are rare and precious, for the Unravelers reproduce not through communal spawning but through dangerous intimacy. Pairs bond through shared modifications—lovers who exchange eyes, siblings who share a circulatory system, rivals who trade limbs in ritual combat. Pregnancy is carried to term inside specialized coral chambers that must be constantly tended to prevent either calcification or dissolution. The infant mortality rate approaches half, but those who survive are fierce and strange, adapted from birth to the constant collapse.

The Unravelers' greatest creation is the Inverse Garden—a vast net of stolen aerial roots that they've trained to grow downward, reaching toward the true ocean below. Here they cultivate falling things: plants that grow toward gravity instead of light, animals they've trapped in perpetual dive, water they've convinced to remember its weight. The Garden hangs like a tremendous jellyfish beneath the Scar, miles wide and growing, catching debris and dreams from the world above.

At the heart of the Inverse Garden lies the Plumb Station, where the bravest or most desperate Unravelers undertake the Long Drop. They've built a system of counterweights and biological pumps that can lower a person on a cord of woven root down, down, down toward the original ocean that lies somewhere in the darkness below. Few return from these expeditions, but those who do bring back treasures: metal from the drowned cities, stones that remember when they were dry, texts written in languages that predate the rise of waters. They speak of glimpsing lights in the depths—not bioluminescence but something harder, artificial, waiting.

The relationship between Uth-Mara and the Underscar is complex and fraught. Officially, the Coral-Weavers deny the Unravelers exist—they are a cautionary tale, a metaphor for those who refuse integration with the reef. But goods move between the levels: medicine derived from cancer-coral, oil-sap for the Weavers' biological machines, children who dream too strangely and must be sent below before they corrupt the pools. In return, the Weavers drop parcels of cultivated food, refined materials, and sometimes—though they would never admit it—their calcifying elderly who wish to die as individuals rather than architecture.

The Unravelers know about the Convergence the Weavers dream of, the becoming-reef that awaits Uth-Mara. But they tell a different prophecy: the Severance, when the weight of gathered water will grow too great and the whole impossible system will come crashing down. The ocean will remember itself and fall, they say, and only those who have learned to live with falling will survive. They prepare for this day with religious fervor, teaching their children to navigate by echolocation in absolute darkness, to hold their breath for impossible lengths, to survive on the oils pressed from cancer-coral.

In the spaces between root and ruin, the Unravelers perform their violent devotions. They break coral to hear its scream. They dissect the falling dead to understand how bodies transform under pressure. They inject themselves with solutions of powdered limestone and acid, training their bodies to survive partial calcification. Their priests, called Shatters, practice the art of controlled collapse—setting off chain reactions in unstable coral formations, reading the future in the patterns of debris.

And in the deepest reaches of the Underscar, where even the Unravelers fear to build, something grows. The cancer-coral here has evolved beyond mere malformation into something approaching sentience—a vast neural network of corrupted growth that whispers in frequencies only the modified can hear. Some Unravelers believe it's the true mind of the reef, the dark thoughts that the Weavers' city refuses to think. Others insist it's something older, something that was here before the waters rose, adapting and waiting.

The children of the Underscar play different games than those above. They compete to see who can fall the farthest before catching themselves. They keep pets made of sewn-together parts from different creatures. They learn to read the stability of coral by taste, licking the formations to sense the first chemical signatures of collapse. They paint their futures on the sides of falling debris, hoping their prophecies will reach the ocean below and germinate in the deep pressure.

Between rising and falling, between cultivation and collapse, between the patient Weavers and the desperate Unravelers, the world finds its own equilibrium. Each needs the other—the Garden requires the Scar to define what it is not, the Scar requires the Garden to rebel against. They are two answers to the same impossible question: how do you live when the water has forgotten which way to flow?

And far below both cities, getting closer with each generation as roots reach down and expeditions venture deeper, the original ocean waits with its cargo of drowned history and its own dreams of rising, rising, rising to reclaim what suspension has stolen from its embrace.
