**The Basin of Gathered Hours**

Far below the Hollow Terraces, where the mountain's roots sink into older stone, the Basin of Gathered Hours spreads like a calcified memory. Here, at the foot of Morne's impossible climb, time pools and thickens into something almost tangible—a vast depression where centuries accumulate like sediment.

The Basin appears, at first approach, to be a simple geological feature: a circular valley perhaps ten miles across, ringed by low hills of the same worn stone that forms Morne's cliff face. But the air here weighs differently. Each step forward encounters increasing resistance, as if walking through deepening amber. The sky directly above remains vivid, but toward the Basin's center, it dims and brightens in slow pulses that have nothing to do with the sun's passage.

Nothing grows here in any conventional sense. Instead, the Basin sprouts architectures of crystallized duration. These formations rise from the valley floor like frozen geysers of compressed time—translucent columns that contain within them the shadows of events that haven't finished happening. Look closely into their surfaces and you might see a bird's wing mid-beat, suspended for decades in a single downstroke. A raindrop that fell during Morne's founding still descends through one formation, having traveled perhaps an inch in a thousand years.

The Keepers of the Basin are not the elongated dwellers of the terraces above. These beings rejected transformation, choosing instead to armor themselves against the Basin's temporal weight. They move through the thick hours in elaborate suits of hammered lead and brass, each plate inscribed with equations that maintain a bubble of normal time around the wearer. The suits look like diving bells reshaped for terrestrial use—heavy, spherical helmets with thick glass ports, articulated joints that hiss and leak temporal pressure with each movement.

Inside these protective shells, the Keepers remain stubbornly, defiantly human. They age at the expected rate, their faces showing normal years behind the glass. But this normalcy comes at a price: they can never remove the suits while in the Basin. To do so would be to surrender to the temporal gradient, to watch one's hand age to dust while one's mind still forms the thought to pull it back.

The Keepers harvest time itself. Using tools that look like tuning forks struck from meteoric iron, they chip away at the crystallized formations, collecting fragments of condensed duration in lead-lined containers. These temporal shards serve a purpose that connects the Basin to the terraces above: they are what makes the impossible geometry of Morne sustainable.

The relationship between these two places—one where space breaks, one where time pools—is symbiotic and ancient. The dewellers of Morne, in their probabilistic existence, shed excess certainty like snakes shed skin. This discarded reality doesn't simply vanish; it falls. Down the cliff face, through layers of thinning possibility, until it reaches the Basin where time's weight compresses it into crystal.

The Keepers process these formations through methods passed down in manuals written on sheets of beaten gold—the only material that remains legible in the Basin's temporal flux. They grind specific formations into powder, dissolve others in solutions of quicksilver and salt. The resulting substances—sometimes liquid, sometimes light itself—are carefully packed into containers that look like inverse hourglasses: bulbous at the center, narrow at both ends, designed to regulate the flow of time itself.

These containers make the journey up Morne's cliff face through methods that blur the distinction between trade route and physics experiment. The Keepers maintain a system of pulleys and counterweights, but the cables run through dimensions the eye can't follow. A container lowered into a specific shadow at the Basin's edge might emerge from a cistern in Morne's third terrace. The dwellers above depend on these shipments—the processed time allows them to maintain just enough temporal coherence to exist, while the Keepers below depend on the reality-waste that falls from above to continue their harvest.

But there is tension in this exchange. The Keepers, in their stubborn humanity, see the dwellers as abominations—beings who've surrendered too much to place. The dwellers, stretched across probability, perceive the Keepers as fossils, creatures who've chosen stagnation over adaptation. Their trade continues not from mutual respect but from mutual necessity.

Communications between Basin and Terraces occur through the medium of falling objects. The dwellers drop carefully shaped stones—each one carved to tumble in specific patterns that encode messages in their descent. By the time these stones reach the Basin, decades might have passed above while only days elapsed below. The Keepers respond by sending up patterns of smoke from burning temporal crystals—smoke that rises through folded time to arrive in Morne's past, allowing the dwellers to prepare for requests that haven't been made yet.

The journey between these places is rarely attempted by either species. For a dweller to descend means risking compression into fixed form—the Basin's temporal weight forcing their probabilistic existence to collapse into a single, likely fatal, certainty. For a Keeper to ascend means watching their protective suit crack under paradox, normal time bleeding away through splits in the brass.

Yet some make the journey. Rogue dwellers who seek the certainty of death rather than eternal transformation. Young Keepers who dream of transcending their heavy suits. They travel the cliff face between worlds, some taking centuries to arrive, others flickering between Basin and Terrace in moments. Most vanish entirely, caught between two impossibilities.

The middle spaces—the vast cliff wall between Basin and Terraces—host their own strangeness. Here, reality and time find an uneasy average. Caves in the cliff face contain hermit beings who are neither Keeper nor dweller but something intermediate: partly transformed, partly armored, surviving in the margin between two extremes. They alone can travel freely between Basin and Terraces, serving as neutral mediators when the symbiosis threatens to break down.

These hermits speak of the time before the split, when Morne was merely a mountain and those who would become Keepers and dwellers were a single people. They speak of the discovery—or the accident—that revealed the mountain's true nature as a flaw in the universe's structure, a place where the fundamental forces could be carved like stone. They speak of the choice that divided the people: those who would embrace the flaw and those who would armor against it.

But mostly, the hermits maintain the infrastructure of exchange. They repair the impossible pulleys, they translate messages distorted by temporal shear, they ensure that neither Basin nor Terraces forget their dependence on the other. They are the conscious link between two places that have evolved beyond direct communication, the living memory of why the exchange began and why it must continue.

The Basin of Gathered Hours is Morne's anchor—the weight that prevents the Hollow Terraces from dispersing entirely into probability. The Terraces are the Basin's outlet—the release valve that prevents time from accumulating to the point of temporal explosion. Together, they form a complete system, a metabolism of impossibility that has sustained itself for millennia.

Between them runs the constant traffic of necessity: processed time ascending, discarded reality descending, messages traveling in both directions through media that shouldn't exist. The cliff face bears the scars of this exchange—grooves worn by cables that exist in too many dimensions, shadows burned permanent by paradox, stone that remembers being elsewhere.

This is how Morne sustains itself: through division that creates symbiosis, through separation that demands connection. The mountain hosts two miracles, each impossible alone, each necessary to the other. Between them flows the lifeblood of a broken place learning to live with its own impossibility—time and space negotiating their mutual transformation through the medium of beings wise enough or foolish enough to call this paradox home.
