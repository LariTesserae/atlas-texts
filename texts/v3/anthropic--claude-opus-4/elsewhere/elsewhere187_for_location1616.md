Beneath this endless canopy, where roots twist through ancient stone and forgotten architectures, there exists another realm entirely.

The Hollows.

Miles below the surface, where no flower's scent can reach and no breeze stirs, a vast network of caverns spreads like inverted branches through the planet's crust. Here, in the absolute darkness, the rules of the green world above are inverted, twisted into something else entirely.

The caves breathe. Not with wind, but with something thicker, heavier—a humid exhalation that tastes of minerals and decay. The walls are slick with condensation that never quite feels like water. It clings to everything, forming thin films that shimmer with their own pale luminescence when disturbed.

This is where the rootmass dreams.

Every tree, every vine, every blade of grass from the world above sends its roots down, down, down, until they reach these spaces. But here, freed from the need to remain rigid and structural, the roots... change. They become something between plant and nerve, pulsing with slow thoughts that take decades to complete. They web across cavern ceilings in patterns that hurt to perceive directly, forming neural architectures of impossible complexity.

The Gardeners move through these spaces with careful reverence.

They were human once—or something like human—before the green world above called them down. Now they are stretched thin, their bodies elongated to navigate the narrow passages, their skin gone translucent to better sense the chemical signals that pass between root networks. Their eyes have grown huge and multifaceted, evolved to see in spectrums of bioluminescence that the roots emit during their deepest contemplations.

They tend the thoughtroots with infinite patience. When a root cluster shows signs of recursive inflammation—thinking too hard about its own thinking—they apply poultices of cave moss and sing the subsonic hymns that calm neural storms. When root networks from different surface regions grow too close, threatening to merge consciousnesses in ways that could drive both mad, the Gardeners carefully prune and redirect, maintaining the blessed isolation that keeps each tree-thought distinct.

The Gardeners do not speak to each other. They've been down here too long for that. Instead, they communicate through touch, through the shared understanding that comes from millennia of the same work. Their fingers have grown sensitive to vibrations in the root networks, able to read the health of entire forests through a single touch.

But they remember. In the deepest caves, where even the roots fear to grow, they maintain the Memory Gardens. Here, in carefully tended pools of luminescent fungi, they preserve the neural patterns of every species that once lived in the sunlit world above. Each pool holds the essence of some lost creature—the quick-darting thoughts of birds, the slow contentment of grazing beasts, the complex social webs of the thinking species that built those now-buried structures.

The Gardeners feed these pools with their own memories, drops of their humanity dissolved into the fungal matrix to keep the patterns stable. They know that someday, when the green world above has finished its eon-long meditation, it will hunger for animation again. For movement and intention and the quick, bright thoughts of mobile life. And when that day comes, the Memory Gardens will bloom upward, carried through the root networks to the surface, where they will precipitate into flesh and fur and wing once more.

But not yet. The plants' great work continues, and the Gardeners tend their strange harvest in the breathing dark. Sometimes, in the deepest silences, they feel the planet itself shift in its sleep, and they know they are not gardeners at all, but antibodies in an immune system vast beyond comprehension, keeping the dreaming god healthy while it contemplates... something.

The few Gardeners who have touched the deepest roots and survived speak of glimpsing this purpose. They weep fungal tears and clutch at their elongated skulls, trying to contain knowledge that no individual consciousness was meant to hold. They whisper of a conversation between worlds, conducted through root networks that stretch not just through soil, but through dimensions. Of an exchange of information so slow and so profound that the death of the animal kingdom was merely punctuation. Of something being built or birthed or becoming, one thought at a time, one root at a time, one eon at a time.

In the Hollows, time moves differently. The Gardeners age not in years but in root-cycles, their bodies slowly becoming more plant than animal as they sync with the vast metabolism around them. They do not fear this transformation. They understand, in the wordless way of those who have touched the deep roots, that they too are part of the pattern. That their preservation of memory, their tending of thought, their very transformation—all of it feeds into the grand design.

Above, the empty forests sway in their eternal breeze. Below, the Gardeners move through their careful choreography, maintaining the balance between thought and madness, growth and stasis, memory and forgetting.

The planet dreams on.
