**The Amber Plateau**

Above ground, where the mountain's peak has been sheared away by some ancient catastrophe, a plateau of crystallized resin spreads like frozen honey beneath the open sky. The amber here is not the golden capture of ancient forests but something stranger—a vast lens of calcified time, clear in some places, clouded with mineral inclusions in others, shot through with veins of copper and iron that branch like frozen lightning. The plateau is perhaps a mile across, perfectly flat save for the gentle undulations that catch and pool the rain.

Weather here arrives in discrete packages. Clouds snag on the plateau's edges, unraveling themselves across the amber surface in streamers of fog that the sun burns away by midday. Rain strikes the resin with musical precision, each drop ringing a different note depending on the thickness below. The water finds no purchase on the smooth surface, instead racing in quick silver threads toward the plateau's rim where it vanishes into hair-thin cracks that lead, eventually, to chambers far below.

The amber itself defies easy understanding. In the early morning, when dew condensates on its surface, shapes become visible in its depths—not insects or leaves as in common amber, but geometric forms that suggest architecture, mathematics made tangible. These structures shift with the viewing angle, sometimes appearing as vast cities seen from impossible heights, sometimes as molecular diagrams blown up to monstrous size. At certain times of day, when the light strikes just so, the entire plateau becomes a lens that focuses the sun's heat into searing points that dance across the surface, leaving behind trails of slightly darker amber, as if the resin itself can still, after all these ages, be marked by sufficient heat.

The surface ecosystem is sparse but persistent. In the places where millennia of rain have worn shallow depressions, dust and mineral deposits have accumulated into pockets of soil no deeper than a hand's breadth. Here grow the crystal gardens—not plants in any traditional sense but living minerals that photosynthesize through their faceted surfaces. They rise in spiraling columns of quartz and amethyst, their growth so slow that generations of observers might notice no change, yet undeniably alive in the way they turn their faces to track the sun's path.

Lichens have found purchase in the microscopic imperfections of the amber, spreading in colonies that map the plateau's secret topology. These are not ordinary lichens but hybrid organisms that incorporate mineral structures into their tissues, becoming part fungus, part algae, part crystal. They fluoresce under moonlight, creating a second map written in blue and green phosphorescence that reveals patterns invisible by day. Where the lichens have lived and died for countless generations, their remains form a thin crust that other organisms can colonize.

The wind here carries sounds from impossible distances. The amber plateau acts as a vast resonating surface, picking up vibrations from miles away and amplifying them through its crystalline structure. On still days, listeners can hear conversations from the valleys below, the crack of ice from distant peaks, sometimes even the deep thrumming that rises from somewhere beneath the mountain—though whether this last is sound or vibration transmitted through stone remains uncertain.

Birds avoid the plateau except for the Amber Swifts—creatures that nest nowhere else. They are small, no larger than hummingbirds, with feathers that have evolved to match the resin's golden clarity. Their wings beat too fast to see, creating a constant high-pitched whine that harmonizes with the plateau's natural resonance. They feed on the near-microscopic insects that live in the lichen gardens, and on something else—observers have seen them hovering at certain points above the amber, their beaks open as if drinking from the air itself.

The Refracted live here, though "live" might be too strong a word for their mode of existence.

They are tall, impossibly thin, their bodies stretching up like pulled glass. Their skin is not skin but a kind of flexible crystal that refracts light into rainbow patterns across their surfaces. To look at a Refracted directly is to see yourself reflected and split into component colors—your image broken into spectra that slide across their bodies as they move. They have no fixed number of limbs; their arms and legs divide and merge like light passing through prisms, sometimes walking on two legs, sometimes six, sometimes flowing across the amber surface on appendages too numerous and fleeting to count.

Their faces are the most disturbing aspect—not because they are monstrous but because they are too familiar. Each Refracted's visage is an amalgamation of every face they have ever reflected, creating expressions that shift constantly between strangers' features. Their eyes are holes in this reflecting surface, gaps that reveal not darkness but a view straight through their heads to the landscape beyond, as if their skulls were windows.

The Refracted communicate through light itself. They position their bodies to catch and redirect sunbeams, creating complex patterns of brightness and shadow that serve as their language. A conversation between two Refracted is a dance of angles and positions, each participant adjusting their stance to modify the light passing between them. At night, they cluster together around the phosphorescent lichen gardens, using that faint glow for whispered exchanges that look like aurora borealis played out in miniature.

They build nothing, own nothing, create nothing tangible. Their art is ephemeral—light sculptures that exist for moments before the sun moves on, shadow plays that tell stories in negative space. They compose symphonies in the spectrum, using their bodies as living prisms to split white light into colored movements that exist only in the seeing. Their philosophy, if they could be said to have one, revolves around the belief that permanence is illusion, that only the momentary refraction of light through form has any reality.

The Refracted do not eat in any conventional sense. They sustain themselves on light itself, their crystalline bodies converting photons to energy through processes that would shame any plant's photosynthesis. On cloudy days they grow sluggish, their movements slowing to near-stillness. During the rare storms that fully obscure the sun, they cluster together in geometric patterns, sharing what stored light remains in their bodies through careful angular arrangements that maximize energy transfer.

They reproduce—if that word applies—by division. When a Refracted has absorbed enough light over decades or centuries, they begin to develop stress fractures in their crystalline structure. These are not flaws but intentional weakness, carefully cultivated lines along which they will eventually split. The process takes years of precise movement, the parent positioning themselves at exact angles to concentrate light along the fracture lines. When the split finally comes, it happens in an instant—one being becomes two, each carrying a portion of the accumulated faces, each beginning again the slow process of gathering light.

Their relationship with time is as fractured as light through a prism. The Refracted experience moments not as linear sequence but as overlapping possibilities. They see the recent past and immediate future as clearly as the present, their consciousness spread across a temporal spectrum perhaps an hour wide. This makes their movements seem prescient to linear observers—they dodge falling stones before they fall, respond to questions before they're asked, grieve deaths that haven't happened yet.

Memory, for them, is stored in light patterns held within their crystalline matrix. They can replay memories by refracting them outward, projecting ghostly images of past events onto the amber surface. The older a Refracted, the more memories they contain, until the eldest among them are libraries of light, their bodies so full of stored experience that they sparkle with constant internal reflections.

The Refracted know of the Weepers, though the two peoples have never met directly. The knowledge comes through the mountain itself, through vibrations in the stone that the amber plateau captures and the Refracted decode from the resonance patterns. They call the Weepers "the Darkeyes" and speak of them (in their light-language) with something approaching reverence. For beings who exist through interaction with light, the idea of creatures who thrive in bioluminescent darkness holds a fascination bordering on the spiritual.

The water that runs from the plateau's edge follows ancient channels in the mountain stone. Rain that falls on amber, charged with the plateau's strange resonance, trickles through cracks barely wide enough for the liquid to pass. It descends through layers of limestone and granite, picking up minerals and memories, until it emerges in the weeping walls of the hidden city below. The Weepers taste storms in their water days after clouds have passed. They note the subtle changes in mineral content that speak of seasons far above—more copper in autumn, more iron in spring, though they have no names for these cycles, knowing them only as the Deep Flow and the Bright Flow, the Cool Taste and the Warm.

Sometimes the water carries more than minerals. Fragments of refracted light, trapped in droplets, make the journey from surface to depth. These arrive in the Weepers' city as impossible colors in the condensation, hues that have no names in the vocabulary of darkness. The Weepers collect these carefully, certain drops that glow with captured sunset or shimmer with noon's intensity. They incorporate them into their most sacred pools, where the light slowly dissipates over years, adding new notes to the bioluminescent symphony.

The Refracted, in turn, read messages in the steam that sometimes escapes from the mountain's fissures. On certain mornings, warm mist rises through cracks in the amber plateau, carrying with it the mineral breath of the deep city. The Refracted gather around these vents, their bodies positioned to catch the way light refracts differently through the mineral-laden vapor. They decode stories in the steam—growth patterns of coral buildings, the chemical poetry of fungal gardens, once even the death-crystallization of an ancient Weeper whose transformation was so profound it changed the composition of an entire water system.

Both peoples share a relationship with the mountain that transcends simple habitation. They are part of its metabolism—the Refracted processing light into the patterns that the mountain dreams, the Weepers guiding the growth that gives the mountain new chambers for thought. Neither fully understands this symbiosis, experiencing it instead as duty, as art, as the only possible way to be.

The true connection runs deeper than water or light. Both cities—if the amber plateau's scattered Refracted can be called a city—exist in states of transformed time. Above, moments fracture and overlap, consciousness spreading across multiple presents. Below, growth and stasis merge into something beyond either. The mountain has created, in its damaged and transformed body, two experiments in how awareness might exist outside linear time.

On the rare occasions when conditions align—when storm clouds pass at sunset, when the deep thermal vent surges, when the moon is dark and only starlight remains—something extraordinary occurs. The Refracted's light-memories begin to resonate with the Weepers' stone-dreams. Patterns appear in the amber that match the growth spirals of coral buildings. The water songs far below harmonize with the light frequencies above. For these brief moments, the mountain itself seems to wake, to remember what it was before catastrophe split its peak and water found its heart.

These events terrify both peoples. The Weepers taste prophecy in their water for weeks afterward. The Refracted find their time-perception scattered even wider, seeing days instead of hours in simultaneous overlap. Yet neither can resist participating when the conditions begin to align. They are drawn to their respective roles—the Weepers to their deepest pools, the Refracted to the amber's geometric centers—as if by instinct older than consciousness.

What passes between them in these moments is not communication as either understands it. It is something more fundamental—an exchange of states, of ways of being. The Refracted briefly experience unity, their fractured light cohering into single, burning moments of now. The Weepers feel time crack open around them, their patient centuries suddenly revealed as movements in a dance that plays out across geological ages. Both touch, for an instant, the thing they were before they were themselves—the original inhabitants of an unbroken mountain, before water and catastrophe and adaptation transformed them into experiments in living.

When the moment passes, both peoples return to their transformed states with relief and regret in equal measure. The Refracted go back to their light dances, the Weepers to their patient gardening. But something has changed. New patterns appear in the coral growth, influenced by geometries seen in refracted light. New angles enter the Refracted's movements, inspired by growth spirals glimpsed in stone dreams.

The mountain remembers everything—every drop of water, every photon of light, every creature that has lived and transformed within its bounds. In the amber above and the limestone below, it continues its slow experiment, testing the boundaries of what consciousness can become when freed from the tyranny of singular moments and mortal spans. The Refracted and the Weepers are its thoughts made manifest, its meditation on time given form.

And the water continues to fall, and the light continues to shine, and somewhere between the plateau and the hidden city, in cracks too thin for either people to enter, the mountain dreams of what new forms of life might yet emerge from the dialogue between brightness and depth, between the fractured moment and the eternal now.
