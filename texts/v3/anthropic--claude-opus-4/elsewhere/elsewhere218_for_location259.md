Far below the breathing city, where the mountain's roots disappear into perpetual mist, the Drowned March spreads like a vast, living bruise across the landscape.

Here, the world's fevered rains collect and stagnate. Water stands everywhere—sometimes ankle-deep, sometimes rising to swallow entire trees, leaving only their crowns visible like desperate fists breaking the surface. The land itself has given up cohesion; what seems solid ground might suddenly give way to reveal peat-black water beneath, while apparent pools might prove to be merely thin films over packed sediment. Nothing is certain underfoot.

The trees here grow differently than their mountain cousins. Their trunks bulge with air-filled chambers, grotesquely swollen at the base like the legs of elephantiasis patients. Aerial roots drop from branches in curtains, each one a gamble—seeking soil that might not exist, prepared to become swimming appendages if needed. The bark weeps constantly, not with healthy sap but with a grey discharge that stains the water and fills the air with the scent of slow decomposition.

Light comes filtered through three layers: the permanent cloud cover that traps the land's exhalations, the canopy of drowning trees, and finally the surface scum of algae and floating plants that covers much of the standing water. What reaches the actual surface is green-tinted, diseased-looking, turning everything the color of old copper. During the brief hours when the rain stops, the silence is worse than the constant drumming—a thick, expectant quiet broken only by the belch of methane bubbles breaking the surface.

Movement through the March is a specialized skill. The bloated trees grow aerial highways—branches flattened and widened by the constant moisture into natural walkways. But these paths shift with the water levels, and what was a safe route during the morning's moderate flood might be submerged by afternoon. The wise travel with poles, testing each step, reading the subtle differences in water color that indicate depth, current, or the presence of what lives beneath.

And things do live beneath. The water churns with more than current. Scales break the surface without showing what bears them. Disturbances that might be fish, or might be something else, leave spreading rings that intersect and overlap until the entire visible surface shivers with implied movement. Occasionally, something larger surfaces—a back like a floating log that might be a log, until it isn't. The rule of the March: nothing is only what it appears to be.

The settlements, such as they are, perch on whatever rises above the flood. Ancient structures—stone foundations from some previous dry age—serve as anchors for ramshackle communities built of salvage and hope. Rope bridges span the gaps between these islands, sagging low enough to kiss the water during high floods. Everything wood rots quickly here, so the builders use what resists: bones of great marsh beasts lashed together, metal scavenged from the deeps, living wood that renews itself faster than the moisture can claim it.

Weather in the March follows no pattern that outsiders can read. Rain might fall upward, condensing from the saturated ground to return to the clouds. Fog banks roll through with intent, seeking warmth to devour. The temperature varies by layer—tropical at water level where decomposition heats the murk, cooling as you climb, until the air in the tree crowns carries a permanent chill that sets inhabitants shivering even as their feet sweat in the bathwater warmth below.

The sounds are wrong. Water should babble or roar, but March-water whispers. It speaks in gurgles and gulps, in the pop of gases escaping decay, in the subtle splash of things moving just below perception. The drowning trees groan constantly as their waterlogged wood shifts and settles. At night, the soundscape transforms entirely—what was quiet begins to sing, what sang falls silent, and the darkness fills with calls that could be bird, beast, or something between.

Phosphorescence haunts the darker waters. Not the clean light of ocean plankton but a sickly glow that clings to whatever disturbs it. Swimmers emerge outlined in ghostlight that takes hours to fade. The locals read omens in which waters glow and which stay dark—green light means recent death, blue warns of deeper disturbances, while the rare red glow sends everyone to high ground, though none will say why.

The March extends for unknowable distances. Maps are meaningless when landmarks sink and rise with the water. Direction itself becomes fluid—compasses spin wildly, confused by the mineral-rich water and the strange magnetism of certain trees. Travelers navigate by scent as much as sight, following the subtle gradients of rot and bloom that indicate direction and depth.

Seasons express themselves through water rather than temperature. The "dry" season sees water drop to merely knee-deep in most places, revealing the bones of the drowned world—ancient roads, the foundations of cities, the calcified remains of forests that stood before the waters came. The wet season brings water up to the lower canopy, transforming the March into an inland sea dotted with tree-islands where the desperate cluster until the floods recede.

Resources exist, but extracting them courts danger. Certain roots filter water pure enough to drink, but pulling them might anger the trees' defensive symbionts. Fish grow fat in the nutrient-rich murk, but fishing means putting hands in water where sight fails. The most valuable resource is dry wood—worth its weight in food, traded in finger-lengths and hoarded in the few truly dry spaces that exist.

The March has its own brutal beauty. Dawn comes filtered through mist in layers of gold and green. Flowers bloom from the floating plants in colors too vivid for the perpetual twilight—electric purples and violent oranges that hurt the eyes. Birds with oil-slick feathers dive through the aerial root curtains, their calls echoing off the water in harmonics that suggest vast spaces below.

At the margin where the March meets the mountain's slope, the two worlds collide without mixing. The climbing city's organized terraces give way to chaotic scramble, its clear water channels muddy and clot with invasive growth. A boundary zone exists—perhaps a hundred meters wide—where neither world's rules fully apply. Here, the desperate and the exile make their homes, trading between the two realms while belonging to neither.

This is where the story hinges, in the spaces between water and air, between the breathing city above and the drowning lands below. The March is patience made landscape, endurance given form. It waits, as water waits, knowing that all things flow downward in time.

The people of the March call themselves the Drifters, though they drift more in time than space, anchored to their sodden world by necessity and strange love.

Where the Cultivators above wear their symbiosis as decoration, the Drifters' adaptations run deeper, coded in bone and blood. Their feet tell the first story—splayed wide with webbing between the toes, soles thick as leather and sensitive as fingertips. They read the water through their feet: temperature gradients that speak of depth, vibrations that warn of approach, the subtle chemical signatures of safe and poisoned pools. Children born with insufficient webbing rarely survive their third flood season.

Their legs stretch unnaturally long, joints loose as a wading bird's, able to fold and unfold in ways that let them transition from swimming to walking to climbing without pause. Muscles cord differently here—less bulk, more endurance, bodies built for hours of half-swimming, half-walking through chest-deep water. Their skin sheds water like oil, a adaptation bred so deep that even their tears roll off rather than streak.

The eyes of a Drifter unsettle uplanders. Pupils dilate far wider than should be possible, gathering the pale marsh-light, while a nictitating membrane flicks across when they submerge. They see well in the eternal twilight but poorly in bright sun—true daylight causes them to squint and weep. More unsettling still is their ability to cloud their eyes at will, a white film sliding across when they need to see through murky water, filtering sediment and revealing the heat-shadows of living things.

They dress in skins—not of land beasts but of the great eels and water-serpents they hunt in the deep pools. These garments never fully dry, staying supple and slightly slimy, moving like second skins. The preparation involves months of treatment with oils extracted from certain floating plants, creating material that's waterproof yet breathable. Status shows in the rarity of the hide—common eel for everyday wear, but for ceremonies, the iridescent skin of deep-dwellers that surface only during the strongest tides.

Their hair grows in ways that would seem diseased elsewhere. Instead of flowing locks, Drifters sport tight, water-resistant coils that matt naturally into rope-like segments. They cultivate these "dreadlocks" (though they have their own word—*thurrin*—meaning "root-that-floats"), working oils and protective bacteria into them until each strand becomes a tool. Hair strong enough to serve as fishing line, sensitive enough to feel water currents, home to colonies of beneficial organisms that glow softly in true darkness.

Drifter children learn to swim before they walk, but swimming in the March bears no resemblance to clean-water strokes. They undulate through the murk, using their whole bodies, eyes clouded over, navigating by pressure and warmth. They play games that prepare them for survival—holding breath contests in the dark water, finding objects by touch alone, learning to distinguish the vibrations of prey from predator.

Families structure themselves around flood patterns. During high water, they cluster in the tree settlements, living communally in ways that would drive uplanders mad. Privacy becomes vertical—climbing higher for solitude, descending for society. During the dry seasons, families disperse to claim temporary territories in the exposed lands, gathering resources and burying caches for the next flood. Marriage happens during the transition times, when water is rising or falling, symbolizing the shift from one state to another.

They speak in liquids. The Drifter language includes clicks and pops that carry underwater, meanings that change based on whether spoken in air or submerged. Emotional states are expressed through chemical signals—stress sweat that warns others of danger, pheromones that communicate complex social information. In the deepest water, where sound fails, they use a form of touch-sign, messages traced on skin with pruned fingers.

Their religion worships the Cycle—not of seasons but of saturation. They believe consciousness flows like water, pooling in living things during dry times, dispersing into the greater marsh during floods. Death rites involve sinking the body in the deepest pools, weighted with stones carved with the deceased's achievements. They say the truly worthy become part of the great serpents that patrol the depths, while the ordinary dissolve into the background hum of marsh-life.

Work in the March means moving with water, not against it. Fishers don't use nets—too easily tangled, too hard to repair. Instead, they train cormorant-like birds to dive and retrieve, or use their own bodies as lures, letting fish swim close to investigate their heat before striking with bone spears. Builders work with living wood that grows even as they shape it, creating structures that rise with the flood levels. Healers cultivate medicinal algae in special pools, each strain carefully maintained for generations.

They tell time by their bodies. Without clear sky or reliable landmarks, Drifters develop internal rhythms precise as clocks. They know when tides will turn by the ache in their joints, predict rain by the behavior of their hair-colonies, sense seasonal shifts in the way their webbing tightens or loosens. The eldest can predict flood patterns years in advance, reading signs in their own blood that younger generations are slowly forgetting.

Their art floats. Sculptors work with materials that must survive submersion—carved pumice, shaped root-wood, the hollow bones of marsh beasts assembled into delicate mobiles that dance in the water currents. Musicians play instruments that work wet or dry—drums made from swim bladders, flutes carved from reed that grow as they're played, stringed instruments using processed hair that tightens and loosens with humidity to create shifting tones.

Love among Drifters is amphibious. Courtship happens half in water, half in air, with elaborate swimming displays that would look like drowning to outsiders. Couples bond by sharing breath—passing air between them during long dives, learning each other's rhythms until they can stay submerged together far longer than either could alone. Children often aren't conceived until a pair has survived at least one full flood cycle together, proving they can support each other when the world liquifies.

The Drifters dream of solid ground but wake relieved to find water. Their nightmares involve drying out—skin cracking, webbing tearing, the marsh receding until only cracked earth remains. They fear height more than depth, elevation more than submersion. To them, the Cultivators' climbing city seems like another form of drowning, suffocated by too much life rather than water.

They trade with the uplanders at the boundary zones, but trust runs thin as water between the two peoples. Drifters bring up pearls formed in the shells of deep-dwelling mollusks, bones of ancient beasts preserved in the peat, medicines extracted from things that grow only in perpetual twilight. In return, they take dried foods, metal tools, and most precious of all—information about weather patterns from the heights that might predict the floods below.

The Drifters are survivors made flesh, adaptation taken to its logical extreme. They have become what the marsh required—human in shape but not in limitation, evolved for a world where the boundary between land and water disappeared generations ago and shows no sign of returning.

The connection between the two worlds runs deeper than trade, older than either people's memory, and more vital than the suspicious inhabitants of both realms care to admit.

It begins with water. What falls as rain on the mountain city—collected, channeled, celebrated—must go somewhere. The Cultivators' careful systems direct their used water down through a thousand small streams that merge and braid as they descend. By the time this flow reaches the March, it has been filtered through countless root systems, enriched with nutrients from the breathing city's life, carrying seeds and spores and microscopic passengers from the world above.

This twice-living water creates the transition zone. Where mountain meets marsh, where vertical meets horizontal, where the cultivated meets the drowned, a third space exists—neither fully one nor the other. The Margins, both peoples call it, though neither claims it as home.

Here, the trees grow confused. Their bases spread in marsh-adapted buttresses while their crowns reach for mountain heights. Root systems tangle between worlds—water-seeking tendrils from above meeting air-seeking shoots from below in massive woody knots that shelter their own ecosystems. These hybrid giants become bridges, literal and figurative, their trunks broad enough to hold markets where both peoples trade.

The water itself changes here. Clear mountain streams meet turbid marsh currents in swirling patterns that never quite mix. Chemical gradients layer the waters—fresh on top, brackish below, with thermoclines that shift hourly as the two systems exchange heat and minerals. Fish from neither world venture into these mixing zones, but new species—evolved in the margins—thrive in the chemical chaos.

Twice yearly, during the transition seasons, something remarkable happens. The temperature differential between mountain and marsh creates its own weather system. Clouds form at ground level where the two air masses meet, dense fogs that roll upslope in the morning and pour back down in the evening like aerial rivers. These fog-tides carry more than moisture.

In the mist, things transform. Seeds from the mountain find purchase in marsh-wood. Spores from the lowlands discover vertical surfaces to colonize. The Cultivators' trained organisms meet the Drifters' wild adaptations and produce offspring neither people could have predicted. Gardens appear overnight—vertical marsh-farms climbing the transition trees, horizontal forest floors spreading across the more solid ground. Neither people planted them, but both learn to harvest their strange fruits.

The creatures of the margins belong fully to neither world. Climbing fish with primitive lungs hunt in both environments. Amphibious mammals with gliding membranes travel the fog-rivers between realms. Birds nest in the transition zone specifically, their eggs requiring the precise humidity found only where worlds meet. Most disturbing to both peoples are the margin-apes—primates that seem to shift their very biology based on altitude, growing webs in the lower reaches, losing them as they climb, their bodies fluid as the boundary they inhabit.

Human presence in the margins is limited but essential. Outcasts from both societies—those whose adaptations failed, whose symbioses went wrong, who couldn't fully become what their world demanded—find refuge in the between. They call themselves the Bridgers, and they've developed their own culture from the scraps of both.

Bridgers live in the crown-roots—the massive tangles where the hybrid trees anchor themselves between worlds. They've learned to read the fog-tides, to predict when the chemical gradients will shift, to harvest the margin-fruits that neither pure society recognizes as food. Their children grow strange—sometimes webbed, sometimes algae-marked, sometimes both, sometimes neither, as if their bodies refuse to choose a single adaptation.

The most crucial connection runs through these Bridgers. They alone understand the whole system—how the mountain's excess feeds the marsh's hunger, how the marsh's decomposition enriches the mountain's growth. They maintain the balance, subtle as breath, that keeps both worlds functional.

In dry seasons, when the marsh recedes and the mountain streams slow to trickles, the connection grows visible. Ancient channels carved in bedrock show where water has run for millennia. Stone structures—too old for either culture to claim—emerge from the muck, their purposes forgotten but their positions clearly chosen to channel flow between the worlds. Someone, long ago, understood what the current inhabitants try to forget: these are not two worlds but one, artificially divided by altitude and attitude.

The Bridgers tell stories neither society wants to hear. They speak of the time before the division, when humans moved freely between water and air, when the adaptations now bred into bone were choices made daily. They claim the city above and the marsh below are dreams the same organism is having—one of reaching sky, one of embracing earth, neither complete without the other.

Proof comes in the diseases. Plagues that start in the mountain city arrive in the marsh changed but recognizable. Parasites evolved in marsh-water find new hosts in the gardens above. The medicine-workers of both societies have learned, grudgingly, to consult through Bridger intermediaries when new sicknesses arise. Only by understanding how illness transforms between worlds can they hope to treat it.

The young of both peoples feel the pull. Cultivator adolescents sneak down to the margins, drawn by the freedom of water unconstrained by channels. Drifter youth climb the transition trees, yearning for solid ground and clear air. Most return to their proper worlds, but some stay, swelling the Bridger population and slowly, generation by generation, recreating the unity their ancestors abandoned.

Trade happens here, but it's more than commerce. Knowledge flows with goods—weather patterns observed from heights, flood warnings rising from below. Seeds descend, medicines ascend, and with them come stories, songs, technologies adapted and readapted until their origins blur. The market-trees of the margins grow heavy with hybrid fruits, their branches spanning worlds.

The connection is physical—water running from high to low, nutrients cycling between systems. It's biological—species migrating, evolving, hybridizing in the spaces between. It's cultural—outcasts becoming bridges, youth crossing boundaries, knowledge flowing despite suspicion. But deeper than all of these runs an older connection, written in the stone beneath both marsh and mountain.

The planet itself insists on unity. Water will not be divided. Life will not be contained. The breathing city above and the drowning marsh below are organs of the same body, temporarily convinced of their separation but forever linked by the fluid that runs between them—part rain, part river, part blood, all necessary.

In the fog-tides of evening, when mist erases the boundary between worlds, the truth becomes visible to those who dare to look. There are not two peoples but one, stretched across a gradient of adaptation. There are not two places but one, expressing itself in vertical and horizontal dreams. There is not division but distinction—and in the margins where distinction blurs, the future of both worlds is being written by those who refuse to choose between breathing and drowning, between climbing and drifting, between the symphony above and the whisper below.

The water knows, even if the people forget. It falls as rain, runs as rivers, rises as mist, and falls again, carrying life between the worlds in an endless circulation that predates the division and will outlast it.

The city breathes. The marsh dreams. Between them, where water turns to air and back again, the world remembers itself whole.
