**The Garden of Clasping Mouths**

Three days upriver from the Drowning Works, where the black water finally runs clear and the industrial thrum fades to memory, the marsh gives way to solid ground. Here the earth is red clay, rich with iron, stained darker where the roots of strange trees penetrate deep. This is the Garden of Clasping Mouths, though locals call it simply the Appetite.

The trees here grow wrong. Their trunks split not into branches but into muscular tubes that end in apertures lined with what could be thorns or could be teeth. These mouths—for there is no other word—open and close with tidal regularity, drawing in air with a sound like a thousand whispering lungs. The larger trees have hundreds of such mouths, from apertures the size of a child's fist to vast maws that could swallow a horse. They cluster at different heights, creating a canopy of hunger that filters the sunlight into moving patterns of consumption and release.

Between the trees, the ground is carpeted with their offspring—seedlings that have not yet developed their wooden bones, existing as pure mouth-flesh that rises from the soil like anemones. They react to footsteps, to shadows, to the slightest change in air pressure, pursing and gaping in waves that follow any movement through the garden. Their roots, when exposed, reveal themselves as tongues—thick, pale things that taste the soil, selecting minerals with disturbing discrimination.

The Garden has its own moisture, distinct from the marsh. Here the air is dry but heavy, pulled through the countless throats of the trees in a constant circulation. The sound is omnipresent: a susurrus of breathing, swallowing, tasting. During the peak hours of noon, when the trees feed most actively, the noise becomes almost musical—a symphony of appetite that some claim contains patterns, messages, meaning.

What the trees eat is both simple and inexplicable. Insects, certainly—clouds of gnats disappear wholesale into the smaller mouths, while larger prey like birds and bats are caught by the canopy feeders. But they consume more than flesh. They drink sound, swallowing echoes before they can form. They eat color from the sunset, leaving the sky above them strangely bleached at dusk. Some say they feed on possibilities, on the potential futures that might have been, leaving only a single timeline in their wake.

The monks who tend the Garden understand its needs through generations of careful observation. They wear robes of woven root-fiber that the trees recognize as kin, allowing them to move unmolested through the groves. Their faces are tattooed with diagrams of digestive systems—not human ones, but the strange chambered processes by which the trees transform their varied diet into growth. They speak in whispers, knowing that loud sounds overfeed the younger trees, making them violent in their hunger.

In the Garden's heart stands the Mother Mouth, a tree so ancient its original trunk has long since been consumed by its own appetite. What remains is a spiral of throats, each one larger than the last, ascending seventy feet into the air. The innermost mouth, at ground level, is large enough to walk through—and some do, for the tree's digestive chambers have become rooms, its stomach acids long since neutralized by age. The monks maintain a shrine in what was once its gizzard.

The Mother Mouth no longer feeds on simple matter. The monks bring it more abstract offerings: bottled sighs, crystallized regrets, the final words of the dying preserved in amber. They lower these into the great throat on silver chains, listening to the sounds of contemplation that echo up from the depths. Sometimes the tree speaks back, its voice a harmony of all the voices it has ever swallowed, offering prophecies that taste of metal and marsh water.

The Garden produces fruit, though calling them such strains the word. They grow not from flowers but from mouths that have closed permanently, sealing themselves around whatever they last consumed. These sealed mouths swell over months, becoming translucent pods within which strange formations develop. When ripe, they split along what were once lips, revealing their contents: sometimes a perfect replica of an insect in living wood, sometimes crystallized sound that chimes when touched, sometimes nothing but a smell that triggers memories of places that never existed.

The monks harvest these carefully, knowing that each contains something the tree deemed worth preserving rather than digesting. They trade them downriver, these possibilities made flesh, these moments crystallized in plant matter. The Peat Foremen of the Drowning Works prize them highly, crushing them into their beetle-paste or dissolving them in the black water to divine patterns in the residue.

Birds here have evolved silent flight, their feathers structured to make no sound as they navigate between the mouths. The deer that browse at the Garden's edges have learned to walk backwards, confusing the trees' tracking. Smaller creatures—voles and shrews—live within the throats themselves, cleaning the teeth of parasites, living their entire lives in the warm, wet darkness of vegetable gullets.

The soil of the Garden is rich beyond measure, fertilized by centuries of digestion. Farmers from the surrounding lands come to beg cupfuls from the monks, knowing that even a handful mixed into their fields will triple their harvest. But the soil carries risks—crops grown in earth from the Garden develop their own small mouths, whispering at night, tasting the dreams of those who tend them.

The monks speak of the Treaty of Appetites, signed in saliva and root-sap when the Garden was young. The trees would not spread beyond their borders, would not teach their seeds to sprout in foreign soil. In exchange, they would be fed—not just the simple food of flesh and sun, but the complex diet they truly craved. Memory. Language. Time itself, in small doses, carried in the vessels of living things.

They say the Garden dreams, and in its dreams it is not a collection of stationary trees but a single vast organism capable of movement, of choosing where to root and what to devour. The monks work to keep it sleeping, to keep it satisfied, for an ambulatory Garden would mean the end of all other appetites in the world.

The connection between the Garden and the Drowning Works runs deeper than the river that links them. Both are places of transformation, of consumption—one through industry, one through biology. The Peat Foremen sometimes make pilgrimage upriver, standing at the Garden's edge, listening to the sound of pure organic hunger. They recognize kinship in the trees' endless feeding, seeing their own mechanical consumption reflected in vegetable form.

And sometimes, in the deepest night when both locations pulse with their respective rhythms, those who know how to listen can hear them singing to each other across the miles—the turbines and the throats, the engines and the appetites, harmonizing in frequencies that human ears can barely detect. A conversation about hunger, about change, about the different ways a thing can swallow the world and make it part of itself.

The monks and the Foremen maintain a careful correspondence, written in the changing colors of beetle shells and the growth patterns of wire-drinking vines. They compare notes on transformation, on the conservation of self through states of change. They trade—fruit from the Garden for the refined oils of the Works, silence harvested from the trees for the songs of the machines.

Both understand that their charges are not separate phenomena but expressions of the same deep principle: that the world hungers to become something else, and that certain places serve as the stomachs where this transformation can occur. The Garden digests possibilities. The Works digest the boundary between made and born. Both feed the world's appetite for change, for transcendence, for the dissolution of old forms and the emergence of new.

Neither the monks nor the Foremen speak of what would happen should their charges ever meet directly—should the trees learn to grow in black water, should the machines learn to breathe through mouths of wood. But sometimes traders on the river report strange sights: metal seedlings sprouting on the banks, their tiny pistons opening and closing like lips; or trees in the deep marsh whose bark sweats oil, whose mouths whistle with steam.

The world is hungry, and its appetite grows.
