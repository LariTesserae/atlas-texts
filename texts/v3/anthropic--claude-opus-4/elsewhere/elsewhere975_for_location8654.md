The Barrens of Receipt stretch endlessly beneath a sun that never quite sets.

Down in the world, where the land has been scraped flat by wind and certainty, everything that falls must come to rest. The earth here is hard-packed clay that bears the impact marks of ten thousand arrivals—circular depressions like inverted domes, each one a landing site worn smooth by repetition. Between them, the dust moves in patterns too regular to be natural: spirals and rays that reorganize themselves according to schedules no one remembers setting.

The catchers work the networks. Vast nets of woven metal spread between towers that might be bone, might be calcified wood, might be the ribs of something that died before the catching began. The nets sag with weight even when empty, their mesh fine enough to snare a falling feather, strong enough to cushion the impact of what drops from above. Some arrivals come gently—drifting down like snow made of chitin and light. Others strike with the force of meteors, setting the whole network humming with transferred kinetic energy.

The sorting begins immediately. What falls is never dead, but it's always finished with its first life. Creatures tumble from the sky at intervals—some recognizable as the offspring of the Vertical Nursery, others changed by their journey through whatever medium separates the heights from the depths. A beast with crystalline wings might land with its facets clouded, ready for cutting and setting. Something that was born with too many possibilities might arrive with most of them shed, leaving only the most essential configuration.

The infrastructure here is horizontal, spreading rather than climbing. Long, low buildings of accumulated salvage stretch between the catching towers, their roofs made from the wings and scales of arrivals that couldn't survive the transition. Inside, the sorting halls run for miles—conveyor systems that move with organic undulation, staffed by workers whose hands have evolved too many fingers for the delicate work of separation. They divide what falls into categories that shift daily: Today's Combustibles, Tomorrow's Architecture, Pending Reassignment, Insufficient Data.

The Receivers live in settlements built from what wasn't immediately useful. Their homes are chimeras—walls of fused shell, doors of compressed fur, windows of organs that became transparent in the fall. They've learned to work with what arrives, no matter how strange. A creature that lands with its nervous system external becomes a living circuit board. One that arrives inside-out might serve as a filtering system, its reversed anatomy perfect for processing the dust-thick air.

The economy runs on potential energy. Every impact charges the buried batteries—ancient systems that turn kinetic force into stored power. The harder something hits, the more valuable its landing. Workers bid on likely sites, camping beneath the nets with instruments that predict the next arrival by reading minute pressure changes in the air above. A good catch can power a family's quarter for a month. A great one might run machinery for a year.

Some arrivals are more complex. The catchers speak in hushed tones of bodies that land thinking—creatures that survive the fall with enough consciousness to question their handlers. These go to the Negotiation Grounds, vast pavilions where the Receivers attempt to communicate with minds that remember flying, remember being born from stone, remember purposes that no longer apply. Some adapt, becoming citizens of the Barrens. Others are gently disassembled, their components redistributed according to need and consent.

The children here grow up watching the sky. They learn to read the signs of incoming freight—the way the air shimmers before a large arrival, how the dust spirals change direction when something biological approaches, the particular silence that precedes the falling of something that used to sing. They play games with impact predictions, wagering small found objects on where the next creature will land, how much of it will survive intact, what it might become in its second life.

The oldest sections of the Barrens show the accumulation of ages. Here, the catching nets have been replaced by catching fields—areas where so many arrivals have landed that the earth itself has learned to cushion impacts. The ground is soft with compressed organics, spongy with the remains of countless transitions. Things grow here, but nothing planned—hybrid flora that springs from the mixture of what was caught, sorted, and discarded.

At the edges, where the sorting infrastructure thins out, wild arrivals roam. Creatures that escaped the nets or were deemed too dangerous to process form their own ecosystems in the wasteland beyond. Sometimes they breed, creating second-generation beings that know nothing of mountain births or vertical nurseries. These native children of the Barrens adapt to the horizontal life, their bodies shaped by wind and dust rather than stone and steam.

The connection between the heights and the depths is more than gravity. The Receivers know, through generations of observation, that what falls is not random. The pattern is too precise, the timing too deliberate. They've mapped the rhythms—how a burst of crystalline creatures from above predicts a shortage of certain minerals below, how the arrival of something with redundant organs often precedes a plague that those organs can cure.

Messages sometimes come with the fallen. Etched in bone, encoded in the patterns of fur, written in languages that evolve between the sending and the receiving. The Stewards above occasionally drop deliberate packets—creatures designed not for life but for communication, their bodies little more than vessels for complex chemical signals that tell of conditions in the Vertical Nursery, requests for materials that can only be sourced from the sorted remains of previous arrivals.

The Receivers send their own messages upward, though the method remains mysterious even to them. They build cairns of sorted materials in specific configurations, and sometimes—not always—these disappear overnight, presumably lifted by whatever force drops the creatures down. They've learned that certain arrangements request certain types of arrivals. A tower of crystalline wings might bring more creatures with similar features. A careful pattern of sorted organs might result in arrivals with the specific redundancies their medical facilities need.

The work never stops. Day and night—though the distinction blurs under the never-quite-setting sun—the catchers tend their nets, the sorters work their lines, the settlers build their chimeric homes. The Barrens of Receipt stretch endlessly, a horizontal mirror to the vertical industry above, catching what falls, sorting what survives, making use of every piece of the strange rain that connects the heights to the depths.

In this way, the world maintains its balance: creation above, reception below, transformation in the falling between. The Vertical Nursery trembles with birth while the Barrens of Receipt whisper with landings, and between them, through the medium of gravity and intention, the impossible circulates and renewed.
