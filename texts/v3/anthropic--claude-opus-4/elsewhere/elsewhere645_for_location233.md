The Salt Road

Seven days' walk through the dust lands, where the earth cracks like old leather and the air tastes of chalk. The traders know it by the stars, for the landscape offers no reliable markers—what seems a permanent ridge one season crumbles to nothing by the next rain. They navigate by the Shell Star's position relative to the Broken Cup, by the way certain winds carry the scent of distant marshes, by the subtle pull they feel in their chest bones when the destination draws near.

The caravans move at night. During the day, the salt plain burns white enough to blind, hot enough to cook flesh. The traders shelter beneath stilted tents, their pack-lizards burrowing into the crusty soil until only their breathing vents remain visible. Everything waits for the Shell Star to rise and the temperature to drop from lethal to merely punishing.

Then they emerge: long lines of laden beasts and wrapped figures, picking their way across the mineral desert. The lizards' splayed feet distribute their weight across the salt crust. Where it breaks through to the caustic mud beneath, they dance back with hisses that echo across the flat expanse. The traders have learned to read the salt's moods—the way it dims before a stable section, how it rings hollow where underground channels have dried to caverns.

At the midpoint rises the Barter Stone, a wind-carved pillar of black rock that predates any memory. Its surface bears thousands of marks—carved names, trade symbols, warnings about unstable routes, claims of precedence. The oldest marks have been worn to mere depressions, overwritten dozens of times by newer claims. The traders perform their rituals here: pouring water onto the stone's base (it hisses and steams), touching their foreheads to specific worn spots, leaving small offerings in the natural bowls carved by centuries of wind.

The salt they seek lies in broad pans beyond the Barter Stone, in depressions where ancient seas died. It forms in plates thick as a hand, layered with different minerals that create bands of white, pink, grey, and deep violet. The traders have harvesting rights passed down through bloodlines, each family working specific pans according to treaties old as the trade itself. They pry up the plates with wooden wedges—metal would contaminate the product—and stack them like sheets of strange paper.

But this is not simple table salt. These deposits carry trace elements from the earth's deep processes, minerals that exist nowhere else in this combination. When dissolved in water and drunk, they ease the bone-aches that plague those who live too long in darkness. When packed around meat, they cure it while adding flavors that make even beetle larvae palatable. Most crucially, certain purple veins contain compounds that help eyes adjust between extreme dark and light—invaluable for those who must navigate between the depths and the surface.

The Saltwalkers who harvest these pans have adapted to their brutal territory. They wrap themselves in layers of pale fabric that reflects heat and wicks away the constant sweat. Their skin has darkened to deep brown, then darkened further to something approaching black, with a strange metallic sheen from decades of salt exposure. They paint their exposed skin with mineral pastes that provide further protection, creating patterns that serve as family identifiers and warnings to others about claimed territories.

Their eyes perpetually weep. The salt air attacks mucous membranes, forcing a constant stream of tears that carve permanent tracks down their cheeks. They've learned to see through this watery veil, to judge distance and detail despite the distortion. Their tear ducts have actually enlarged over generations, producing a thicker, more protective fluid. Children born to the salt plains emerge already weeping, their bodies preparing for the harsh life ahead.

The economics of salt go beyond simple trade. Each grade has specific uses, specific markets. The white layers go to food preservation. The grey contains minerals that strengthen growing bones—essential for the Underkin's children. The pink helps treat the fungal infections that plague enclosed spaces. The purple, rarest of all, commands prices that could buy entire chambers in the warren. The Saltwalkers understand this granular economy, adjusting their harvest to market demands carried by the last caravan's news.

Water defines everything here. The Saltwalkers survive on careful management of their liquid resources. They wear specialized suits beneath their robes—tight-woven fabric that captures their sweat, channels it to collection bladders. Each person produces nearly a liter of reclaimed water daily, which they process through sand filters and mineral clarifiers. The pack-lizards contribute too, their efficient kidneys producing concentrated waste while returning moisture to their riders through specialized saddle systems.

When the rains come—perhaps twice a year—the salt plains transform into a shallow sea. The Saltwalkers retreat to their stilt-villages, clusters of platforms raised on poles driven deep into the stable substrate. These settlements appear abandoned most of the year, but during rain season they bloom with activity. Catchment systems harvest every drop, funneling rain into ceramic tanks that must last until the next cloudburst. The collected water is traded almost as valuable as the salt itself.

The journey between the salt plains and the warren follows routes that shift with the seasons. In dry times, the caravans can cut straight across the dust lands. When the rains come, they must detour through the Throat—a narrow canyon system where flash floods have carved passages just wide enough for single-file travel. The Throat has its own dangers: falling rocks, sudden waters, and the Screamers.

The Screamers live in the canyon heights, though 'live' might be too strong a word. They exist, perhaps. Travelers hear them more than see them—voices that echo from impossible directions, sometimes sounding like warning, sometimes like laughter, often like nothing human at all. They throw things: rocks, bones, once a perfectly preserved salt-trader's pack found decades after its owner vanished. The caravans have learned to leave offerings at certain points—salt mixed with beetles, water poured onto specific stones. The screaming lessens when properly appeased.

Where the dust lands meet the broken country that conceals the warren's entrances, a permanent market has grown. Here the two worlds meet and mingle—the horizontal endlessness of the salt plains and the vertical complexity of the underground. The market sprawls across a natural amphitheater where worn stone provides seating and wind-carved pillars offer shade. During trading season, it fills with a cacophony of negotiation.

The Underkin emerge at twilight, blinking even through their protective wrappings. They bring beetle-shell containers filled with preserved foods, carved stone tools that the surface world prizes, bolts of spider silk so fine they seem like trapped moonlight. Most valuable are their medicines—fungal extracts that fight infection, mineral compounds that strengthen bone, the secretions of certain deep-dwelling amphibians that numb pain without clouding thought.

The Saltwalkers offer their graded minerals, but also information—weather patterns, the movement of distant peoples, news from other trading posts. They serve as the connective tissue between isolated communities, their routes creating a network of communication across the wasteland. A message given to a salt caravan will reach its destination more reliably than any other method, carried in the memories of traders who've learned to preserve words as carefully as they preserve water.

The exchange involves elaborate rituals. Neither group fully trusts the other—the Underkin suspect the surface dwellers of hiding the best salt, while the Saltwalkers believe the warren holds back its most powerful medicines. They negotiate through intermediaries, often children who haven't yet developed their elders' prejudices. These young traders learn both languages, both customs, becoming bridges between worlds.

Some few individuals move permanently between the worlds. Underkin who can't adapt to the eternal darkness join salt caravans, their night-adapted eyes making them valuable guards against nocturnal dangers. Saltwalkers who age out of the harsh surface life sometimes descend into the warren, trading their knowledge of the upper world for a gentler retirement in the stable depths. These crossovers are never fully trusted by either side but serve as crucial translators of not just language but of entire ways of being.

The salt trade shapes both peoples in ways beyond the physical. The Underkin's expansion follows salt-trace minerals in the stone, their newest excavations chasing veins that might lead to untapped deposits. The Saltwalkers read the earth's chemistry through their harvest, understanding geological processes in intimate detail. Both groups have developed creation myths around salt—the Underkin believe it's the crystallized tears of the first ancestors who dug too deep and struck sorrow, while the Saltwalkers say it's the remnant of a primordial ocean that chose death over submission to the sky.

In the deepest part of the warren, in chambers few visit, salt stalactites grow from the ceiling. The Underkin feed them with their own tears, with water carried from the trade routes, with solutions of surface salt dissolved in sacred springs. These formations grow infinitesimally slowly, adding perhaps a fingernail's thickness per generation. They believe when the stalactites finally touch the floor, the two worlds will merge—the horizontal and vertical reconciled, the tears of both peoples crystallized into pillars that hold up a new sky.

The Saltwalkers have their own secret shrine at the heart of the plains, where they've carved chambers down into the salt itself. These underground rooms mirror the warren in reverse—spaces defined by extraction rather than excavation, rooms made of what will be removed rather than what remains. They conduct their deepest rituals here, breathing air so saturated with minerals it burns the lungs, letting their endless tears pool into basins that will eventually crystallize into the rarest purple salt.

Both peoples understand they are locked in an ancient partnership. The warren needs the salt's minerals to survive, to keep their children growing straight despite the darkness, to maintain the delicate chemical balance that lets them share space with beetle and fungus. The salt plains need the warren's medicines to treat the constant damage of their profession, the spider silk that alone can filter the finest salt dust, the tools that can shape stone without contaminating the harvest.

So the trade continues, season after season, each group becoming more like the landscape they inhabit. The Underkin grow ever more adapted to their carved spaces, their bodies fitting the passages like water. The Saltwalkers become walking minerals themselves, their tears turning to crystal on their cheeks, their skin taking on the plains' metallic sheen. And between them flows the salt road—a river of commerce and culture that connects the vertical and horizontal worlds, that carries more than goods, that preserves both peoples like the very mineral they trade.
