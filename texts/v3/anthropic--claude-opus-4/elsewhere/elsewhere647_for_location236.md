The Sorrow Plains

Where the world learns to forget, the land lies perfectly flat for distances that break the mind's ability to measure. This is ground zero of erosion, the place where all edges go to die.

Nothing rises here above ankle height. The surface is dust so fine it moves like liquid, flowing in sheets when the thin wind touches it. The color defies naming—not gray, not brown, but the hue of things worn past their original nature. When you walk, if walking is possible in this suffocating softness, each step sinks and fills immediately, leaving no trace. The horizon is a theoretical line where dust-hazed sky meets dust-covered earth, both the same non-color, the same surrender to entropy.

The temperature here is nothing. Not hot, not cold, but the exact median where sensation fails. The air pressure is so perfectly calibrated to human normal that you forget you're breathing until you realize you've been holding your breath, waiting for something to change. But change is what this place has murdered, ground down to particles smaller than memory.

Scattered across the plains are the Monuments of Unmaking. They are not built but discovered—revealed when the wind arbitrarily decides to blow harder, excavating shapes from the dust. A perfect cube, twenty meters on each side, made of the same ultra-fine particles as everything else but somehow cohered. A sphere half-buried, its exposed hemisphere pocked with holes that go nowhere. Pillars that might have once supported something, now supporting only the concept of height in this heightless place.

The monuments aren't ancient. They can't be, because age implies duration and duration implies change. Instead, they exist in a permanent present tense, neither new nor old. Sometimes the wind re-buries them. Sometimes it reveals new ones. Or perhaps the same ones. In a place where every particle is identical, where does one monument end and another begin?

Sound dies here. Not muffled but simply erased, pulled apart into constituent frequencies that disperse into the dust. You can scream and hear only the interior echo in your skull. Music becomes a memory of vibration. Words spoken aloud feel like lies the moment they leave your lips, obviously false in this landscape that refutes all statement, all assertion, all attempts at meaning.

Yet this is not a dead place. Death requires life as a precursor, and the Sorrow Plains never began. They simply are, in the way that mathematical constants are, existing outside the cycle of generation and decay. The dust moves but is not alive. The monuments appear and disappear but are not born or dying. This is existence pared down to its minimum viable state, matter admitting defeat.

Light behaves strangely here. The distant sun (not the same sun that fails to warm the Birth Peaks—this one is older, colder, more resigned to futility) casts no shadows. Or rather, everything is shadow, the same uniform dimness that makes depth perception a guessing game. Colors that might exist elsewhere—the red of iron oxide, the yellow of sulfur—all render as the same non-hue. Photographs taken here develop as blank frames, as if the camera itself has forgotten what it meant to see.

Water, if introduced, doesn't so much absorb as disappear, molecularly disassembled by the dust into component hydrogen and oxygen that immediately disperse. The plains reject cohesion, refuse the possibility of drops or streams or pools. Even the air barely holds together, so dry it pulls moisture from your eyes, your throat, your thoughts.

In the deepest parts of the plains (though depth is a courtesy here—it's all surface, all the way down) there are places where the dust becomes even finer, achieving particle sizes that approach the quantum limit. Here, the uncertainty principle begins to assert itself. Objects placed on the ground sometimes sink, sometimes float, sometimes exist in superposition between states. A measurement device left overnight might be found the next morning reading temperatures from elsewhere, pressures from elsewhen.

The relationship between the Sorrow Plains and the Birth Peaks is one of inevitability. Every grain of dust here was once a proud spire there, a crystalline achievement of emergence. The plains are where the peaks go to die, carried on winds that take geological ages to cross the space between. Each particle has made the journey from expression to exhaustion, from form to formlessness.

But the connection runs deeper than mere material transport. The plains exert a pull on the peaks that has nothing to do with gravity. It's the pull of entropy, of eventuality. In the quantum foam where the Membrane Shepherds sometimes glimpse probable futures, the plains are always there, waiting. The shepherds know this, carry this knowledge in their phase-shifted bodies. It's why their deep-dwellers dream so intently, trying to encode something permanent before permanence itself becomes impossible.

Sometimes, rarely, the wind carries dust back toward the peaks. These particles, having achieved perfect entropic surrender, react violently to the thermal gradients they encounter. They catalyze strange formations in the cooling glass—bubbles of nothing, voids in the crystal structure that sing different songs than their solid surroundings. The shepherds avoid these returning particles, sensing in them something antithetical to their nature.

The monuments might be graves. Not for bodies—nothing so crude—but for concepts. Here lies the grave of Sharp. Here molders the corpse of Distinct. The cube might be where Right Angle came to die. The sphere could be Surface Tension's final resting place. Each monument a memorial to a physical law that gave up, that admitted defeat in the face of absolute uniformity.

Or perhaps they're the opposite. Maybe they're the plains' attempt to remember what form meant, to reconstruct from first principles the very idea of shape. Failed attempts, all of them, because how can flatness conceive of dimension? How can dust dream of coherence? They stand (or lie, or exist) as testimony to the impossibility of true forgetting, even here where forgetting has been perfected into landscape.

There are no stories about who first discovered the Sorrow Plains because discovery implies a before and after, and the plains exist outside such simple causalities. They have always been found, never been found, will be found tomorrow and were lost yesterday. Expeditions that set out to map them return with blank charts and full memory cards showing nothing but gray static. Or they don't return, becoming part of the dust, their atoms joining the democracy of particles where nothing is privileged over nothing else.

The plains expand. Or perhaps everything else contracts. The boundary between dust and not-dust creeps outward at speeds too slow for human perception but fast enough that the shepherds, with their geological patience, have noticed. They've tried building barriers—crystal walls, electromagnetic fields, probability wells. All fail, not through destruction but through irrelevance. The dust doesn't overcome obstacles. It simply makes them not matter.

In the contested zone where plains meet peaks, strange phenomena occur. Dust-devils of superheated particles spiral upward, carving temporary helixes in the air before dissipating. Glass formations grow and erode simultaneously, their surfaces weathering to powder even as their cores push outward. The shepherds here behave erratically, their membranes flickering between states too quickly to track, as if they're trying to exist in both worlds at once.

Sometimes a monument appears in this boundary zone—not revealed by wind but actively manifesting, dust coalescing into form like a reversed dissolution. These new monuments last only hours or days before returning to powder, but while they exist, they pulse with something like purpose. Are they messages from the plains to the peaks? Warnings? Invitations? The shepherds cluster around them, reading meanings in their geometries that no human mathematics could parse.

The Sorrow Plains are patient. They have already won, are winning, will win. Every mountain that rises eventually joins them. Every structure built becomes dust for their collection. They are this world's far future made manifest in its present, the inevitable end state walking backward through time to meet its own beginning.

Yet in their perfect flatness lies a perfect democracy. Here, finally, is true equality—every particle identical, every position equivalent. The plains offer not death but release from the tyranny of form, from the burden of being anything in particular. They are peace made manifest, the peace of utter surrender.

The wind blows. The dust flows. The monuments appear and disappear like thoughts in a mind that has achieved perfect meditation—present without attachment, form without desire. The Sorrow Plains continue their work of unmaking, patient as mathematics, certain as entropy, gentle as forgetting.

This is where the world goes to practice being nothing, and discovers that nothing, done perfectly, is its own kind of something.
