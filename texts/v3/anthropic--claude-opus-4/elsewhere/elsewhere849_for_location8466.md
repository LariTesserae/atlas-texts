**The Canopy Cities**

Seven hundred feet above, where the root-mass finally breaks through stone and soil into air, the world inverts itself. Here the Undergrowth Sprawl becomes something else entirely—not ceiling but floor, not shelter but foundation. The trees that feed the roots below erupt skyward with desperate vigor, their trunks woven so densely that they form a wooden plain, pierced by the crown-tops of the deepest giants whose bases anchor in the Sprawl's oldest halls.

The Canopy Cities hang in this aerial forest like fruit in an orchard. But these are not built things—they are grown, coaxed, shaped from the living wood itself. The tree-crowns here spread impossibly wide, their branches trained over centuries to create platforms, to merge and fuse where they meet their neighbors, to form a second landscape suspended in the air. The builders learned their art from the Root-Dwellers' ancestors—or perhaps taught it to them, the histories disagree—but adapted it to wind and sun, to the requirements of a world where falling means death.

The light here is absolute. After the green twilight of the Sprawl, the Canopy burns with color. Dawn comes pink through the eastern leaves, noon blazes white and shadowless, sunset paints everything in coppers and gold. The Sky-Weavers, as they call themselves, have evolved eyes that narrow to slits, protective membranes that can filter the harsh light. Their skin carries generations of sun-darkening, ranging from deep brown to true black, with undertones of green where the chlorophyll-producing symbionts have taken hold.

These symbionts are their greatest adaptation. Inherited from mother to child, the microscopic algae live in the upper layers of the Sky-Weavers' skin, converting sunlight to nutrients, supplementing their diet with pure energy. During the growing season, when the sun is strongest, a Sky-Weaver can survive on light and water alone, their green-tinted shoulders and backs functioning as living solar panels. The children glow faintly green in strong light, until their symbionts mature and learn to retain their energy.

The cities themselves defy ground-dwelling logic. Platforms spiral around massive trunks, connected by branch-bridges that flex and sway in the constant wind. The Sky-Weavers move across these spans with casual grace, their feet bare and gripping, toes spread wide for balance. They train from infancy on practice branches over nets, learning to read the wood's intentions, to predict which branches will hold and which will bend, to dance with the tree's movements rather than fight them.

Their architecture is tension and balance. They weave living branches into geodesic domes that distribute weight evenly, creating spaces that feel both enclosed and open to the sky. The walls are trained leaf-curtains that can be opened or closed by adjusting branch positions. During storm season, entire neighborhoods can seal themselves, the branches pulling tight like fists, presenting only curved surfaces to the wind.

Water is precious here, far from the weeping plenty of the Sprawl. The Sky-Weavers have developed systems of dew-catching that border on art. Silk nets strung between branches collect moisture from morning fog. Shaped leaves channel rain into living cisterns—hollow gourds trained to grow enormous, their interiors sealed with wax. The water tastes different here, charged with air and pollen, missing the mineral weight of the underground springs.

The fauna of the Canopy has evolved for gripping and gliding. Lemur-like creatures with membranes between their limbs soar between platforms, their large eyes tracking fruit and insects. Birds here have stronger feet than wings, hopping along branches rather than flying in the turbulent winds. The Sky-Weavers have domesticated several species: message-carriers that navigate by scent between cities, silk-producing caterpillars larger than those below but yielding stronger thread, pollinating bats that ensure the fruit harvests.

The winds shape everything. The Sky-Weavers read them like the Root-Dwellers read wood grain. They know the morning thermals that rise from the sun-warmed trunks, the afternoon gusts that sweep in from distant mountains, the night breezes that carry pollen and spores between cities. They name the winds individually—the Fruit-Ripener, the Branch-Tester, the Storm-Herald—and adjust their lives to these aerial currents.

Their clothing is minimal but technical. They wear harnesses of woven silk and shaped wood, not for modesty but for utility—carrying tools, catching falls, distributing weight when carrying loads across narrow branches. Decorative elements serve as wind-guides, small flags and streamers that help read air currents. During ceremonies, they extend these into elaborate costumes that turn the wearer into a living weathervane, spinning and swaying with every breeze.

The Sky-Weavers trade with the Root-Dwellers at specific interface points—hollow trees whose trunks serve as vertical highways, platforms built around openings where the canopy meets the Sprawl. They lower silk and sun-dried fruit on long lines, pulling up water and medicines in exchange. The traders at these interfaces often spend half their time above and half below, becoming fluent in both worlds, their skin showing the strange patterning of partial symbiont adaptation.

But the deepest connection between the two realms is the Sap-Songs. The Sky-Weavers have developed a practice of singing to the trees—specific frequencies that vibrate through wood, traveling down the trunks into the roots below. The Root-Dwellers hear these songs as whispers from their walls, messages encoded in pitch and rhythm. In return, they send up their own vibrations, using tuned chambers to create resonances that emerge from the wood high above. It takes days or weeks for these messages to travel the full distance, but the conversation continues—slow, patient, constant.

The Sky-Weavers know they live on borrowed structure. The trees that support their cities are the same ones that feed the Sprawl below, and they must be careful not to overtax them. They practice careful pruning, strategic growth-guidance, ensuring that their cities enhance rather than parasitize the forest. When a tree shows signs of struggle, entire neighborhoods will migrate, dismantling their homes branch by branch and rebuilding elsewhere.

Their children grow differently than those below—faster, hungrier, burning through calories in the constant motion their world requires. By five years old, a Sky-Weaver child can navigate branches that would terrify an adult Root-Dweller. By ten, they're helping with the harvest, dancing across swaying platforms with baskets of fruit balanced on their heads. By fifteen, they're ready to choose their specialization—wind-reader, growth-guide, silk-wright, sun-singer.

The sun-singers are their mystics and mathematicians. They track the light's movement across seasons, predict the weather patterns that bring rain or drought, calculate the optimal angles for new growth. Their instruments are prisms of polished sap, crystal-water traded from below and refined in the sun until it captures and splits light. They create light-sculptures during festivals, bending sunbeams into patterns that tell stories, mark time, celebrate the marriage of air and wood.

Death in the Canopy is a thing of air and fire. During the dry season, when a Sky-Weaver dies, their body is placed on a platform of woven branches at the very crown of the forest. The sun desiccates the flesh, the winds scatter the dust, the birds take what they need. What remains—bones bleached white as clouds—is ground into powder and mixed with sap to create a cement used in building. They say the dead strengthen the cities, that their calcium makes the connections between branches hold faster.

The Sky-Weavers dream of flying. Unlike the shared dreams of the Root-Dwellers, these are individual visions—soaring without membranes or wings, riding the winds like the seeds they scatter each autumn. Some say these dreams are memories of a time before the cities, when their ancestors lived groundbound and could only imagine the freedom of the canopy. Others believe they're prophecies, that someday the symbiosis will complete itself and they'll photosynthesize enough energy to truly fly.

Their technology focuses on tension and distribution. They've developed techniques for training wood that the Root-Dwellers couldn't imagine—branches that grow in helixes for strength, leaves that cup into rain-catchers, bark that thickens into cable-strong rope while still alive. They understand leverage and load in ways that would impress any engineer, but express it through botanical manipulation rather than dead materials.

The Canopy Cities communicate through flag-signals during the day, fire-signals at night. Each city maintains a signal-tree, its top pruned flat and cleared, where watchers stand ready to relay messages across the network. The language of flags is complex—not just position but movement, the way silk catches and releases wind encoding subtleties that spoken language might miss. At night, controlled flames in shaped reflectors send coded flashes that can be seen for miles through clear air.

The cities shift and grow with the seasons. Summer expansions stretch platforms wider as the wood swells with sap. Winter contractions pull everything inward as the trees prepare for dormancy. The Sky-Weavers have learned to live with this breathing rhythm, designing their spaces to accommodate the forest's natural cycles. Furniture is minimal—hanging seats that can be pulled up when not in use, sleeping nets that pack into fist-sized bundles, tables that fold into the platform surface.

They measure distance not in length but in time and effort—this city is a morning's branch-walk away, that one requires overnight stays on messenger platforms. The young train as runners, learning the fastest routes between cities, memorizing which branches can take shortcuts and which require careful navigation. The best can cross between any two cities in the network within three days, sleeping in the emergency hammocks maintained along major routes.

Their art celebrates transience. They paint with pollens that wash away in the first rain, sculpt with trained vines that grow into new forms each season, compose music for wind-harps that play different songs depending on the weather. The greatest artists work with the cities themselves, guiding growth over decades to create spaces that sing in certain winds, platforms that cast shadows telling time, branch-cathedrals that frame specific views of sunrise or sunset.

The Sky-Weavers know the Root-Dwellers mainly through the interface traders and the Sap-Songs. They think of them as necessary but mysterious partners, tenders of the dark that feeds the light. Some young Sky-Weavers journey below out of curiosity, but few stay—the closed spaces and filtered light make them claustrophobic, the slowness of movement frustrates bodies built for speed and balance. Similarly, Root-Dwellers who ascend find the exposure terrifying, the brightness painful, the constant motion exhausting.

But both peoples understand their interdependence. The trees need both root and crown, darkness and light, the patient cultivation below and the energetic growth above. In their different ways, both societies have achieved something remarkable—civilizations that enhance rather than exploit their environment, that have grown into their worlds so completely that separating them would mean the death of both.

The Sky-Weavers sing to the sun as it rises, harmonies that they swear make the fruit grow sweeter. The Root-Dwellers whisper to the moisture collecting on their walls, convinced it helps the roots find water. Both may be right. In a world where the line between the grown and the made dissolved a thousand years ago, where cities breathe and civilizations photosynthesize, who can say where metaphor ends and reality begins? The trees know, perhaps, but they keep their own counsel, growing ever upward and downward, carrying the conversations of two peoples in their sap, dreaming whatever dreams wood dreams in the long centuries of its becoming.
