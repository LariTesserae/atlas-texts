Down and down from the cirque, following the ghost of an ancient glacier's path, the mountain releases its grip in stages. First the raw scree fans, then stunted krummholz clinging sideways to the wind, then the treeline proper where the slope finally gentles into something that can hold soil. Here, at 9,000 feet, in a hanging valley suspended between the sterile heights and the distant lowlands, sits the village of Threshold.

The settlement occupies a natural shelf where two streams converge, their combined flow cutting a notch through the valley's lip before plunging into space. Seventeen structures cluster along the streams' banks: low buildings of fitted stone with sod roofs that sprout the same alpine grasses as the surrounding meadows. No roads reach this place. The only approaches are footpaths worn into the mountainside by generations of the same careful steps.

The inhabitants of Threshold are the Observers—forty-three souls whose entire purpose is to watch the high places and record what they see. Or rather, what they don't see. Each morning, designated watchers climb the paths to established points where they can glass the cirques, ridges, and faces above. They carry leather-bound notebooks, charcoal, and the particular patience of their calling. They note the weather, the light conditions, the precise color of shadows in the high basins. They record avalanche scars, new rockfall, the movement of clouds. But primarily they watch for signs of return—for any indication that someone has gone up to the silent places and done what was done before.

The role passes through families. Children learn to read the mountain's moods before they learn letters. By ten, they can predict afternoon weather from morning light on the peaks; by fifteen, they know every accessible cirque and hanging glacier by its particular geometry. They learn the locations of the old workings—the rings of stones, the pecked basins, the scattered flakes—though none have climbed high enough to see them directly. That prohibition is absolute. The Observers watch but do not enter. They record but do not disturb.

In the evening, the watchers return to the village and transcribe their notes into the permanent record. The Archive occupies the largest building, half-sunk into the hillside for stability. Inside, hundreds of volumes line shelves carved directly from the bedrock. Each book contains years of daily observations: "Southeast cirque, morning sun 0800-1000, no movement, no new snow, mineral rings visible on quartzite." The entries accumulate like their own geological strata, layer upon layer of recorded absence.

The Observers maintain other practices. They cultivate small gardens in the brief growing season, growing only what survived the journey up—potatoes, hardy greens, medicinal herbs. They keep no livestock; all protein comes from what they can hunt or trade for during the annual journey to the lower valleys. In winter, when the high places vanish behind storms and the paths become impassable, they turn inward. They mend tools, copy deteriorating records, teach the children to distinguish between seventeen different qualities of high-altitude light.

They also maintain the Prohibition. No Observer may climb above the treeline's end. No one may approach the old workings. No one may add to what was left there, neither moving the existing stones nor placing new ones. The penalty for violation is exile—not down to the comfortable valleys, but sideways, along the barren traverses, until the transgressor passes beyond the watershed and out of the mountains' sight entirely.

The Observers themselves cannot say precisely what they guard against. The oldest records, written in an archaic script that few can now read, speak only of "the completion" and "the drawing down." They know that someone, long ago, climbed to the cirques and began something—placing stones, working rock, creating patterns whose purpose remains opaque. They know this work was interrupted, left unfinished. And they know, through generations of transmitted certainty, that it must remain so.

So they watch. They record the slow weathering of ancient intentions. They note how frost-shatter gradually softens the edges of worked stone, how lichen creeps across the pecked surfaces, how the mineral rings appear and disappear with the daily cycle of melt and freeze. They document the gradual erasure of human intent by geological time, and in doing so, ensure that no new intent disturbs the process.

The young sometimes question this purpose. Why watch what doesn't change? Why guard against what doesn't come? The elders respond with lessons about patience, about the difference between human time and mountain time. They point to the Archive, to the thousands of "no movement" entries that constitute, in their accumulation, a different kind of monument. They speak of responsibility—not to the past or future, but to the continuous present of watching.

At night, when clouds sink into the valley and isolate Threshold from both the heights and the depths, the Observers gather in the common hall. They share the day's observations, though there is rarely anything notable to report. They eat their simple meals, maintain their tools, prepare for tomorrow's watching. Outside, they can hear the two streams converging, carrying snowmelt and stone-flour down from the places they guard but cannot visit.

Sometimes, on the clearest nights, when stars fill the sky and the high peaks glow faintly in their light, an Observer will step outside and look up at the cirques. They appear as darker darknesses against the mountain's bulk, holding their silence, their stones, their unfinished patterns. The Observer will stand there, breath clouding in the cold air, and feel the weight of their vigil—not as burden but as ballast, keeping them precisely where they need to be, suspended between the worked stones above and the unknowing world below.
