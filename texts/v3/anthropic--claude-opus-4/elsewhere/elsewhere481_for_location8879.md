Below, far below where the cliff city's lowest terraces disappear into darkness, the mountain's roots plunge into a different world entirely. Here, beneath the perpetual clouds, stretches a landscape of broken stone and bitter earth—the Scree Lands, where the mountain sheds its skin in endless avalanches of gravel and boulders.

This is a desert of a different kind. Not dry—water percolates down from the cliff city above, creating a terrain of temporary streams and vanishing pools—but lifeless in the way only stone can be lifeless. The ground shifts with every step. What looks like solid earth proves to be merely a crust over deeper instabilities. The Scree Lands extend for miles from the mountain's base, a spreading apron of erosion where nothing stays in one place long enough to take root.

The sky here is a permanent ceiling of cloud, the same layer that wreathes the cliff city in mist but seen from below: a roiling gray blanket that turns the sun into a dim smear. Light comes filtered and directionless, casting no shadows, making distance and depth difficult to judge. The air is thinner than above, but drier—the moisture wrung out by its passage through the city's hanging gardens and water mills. What rain does fall arrives as a fine grit, mixed with dust from the grinding stones above.

Sound behaves strangely in the Scree Lands. The constant clatter of settling rocks creates a baseline of noise that swallows other sounds. Shouts disappear into the ambient rattle. But then, unpredictably, the acoustic properties align just right, and a whisper carries for miles, or the sound of the cliff city's bells comes threading down through layers of air and stone, distorted into something like singing.

The only structures here are temporary—cairns marking safe paths that last until the next major slide rearranges the landscape, rough shelters built from stacked stones that serve their purpose and are abandoned. The few who traverse the Scree Lands learn to read the impermanence, to see where the ground has recently shifted, where it's likely to shift again. They move like water finding its way downhill, always following the path of least resistance, never fighting the stone's desire to descend.

But someone does live here, in defiance of the land's hostility to permanence. Where the Scree Lands meet the true flatlands beyond, where the stone gives way to soil that stays where it's put, stand the Anchors—massive carved pillars driven deep into bedrock. Each Anchor is unique, covered in spiraling script that details contracts, agreements, and boundaries. They mark the edge of the mountain's influence, the point where its endless shedding finally comes to rest.

Between these Anchors, strung on cables thick as tree trunks, hang the homes of the Sievers. These aerial dwellings never touch the unstable ground, suspended instead in a vast web of rope and chain that flexes with the wind but never breaks. The Sievers chose this life at the margin, neither of the mountain nor entirely free from it. Their suspended village rocks gently even in still air, moved by vibrations traveling down from the cliff city far above.

---

The Sievers build their lives on separation—from the ground, from the mountain, from permanence itself. Their homes are marvels of engineering, woven from rope and treated wood into shapes that are part basket, part tent, part ship. Each dwelling hangs from multiple points, distributing weight across the network so that no single cable bears too much strain. Bridges of woven rope connect them, creating aerial streets that sway and bounce with each step. Children learn to walk on these moving surfaces, developing a sea-legs gait that marks them wherever they go.

They are taller than the Cling-folk above, stretched by generations of life without solid ground beneath them. Their feet have evolved differently—long and grasping, with widely splayed toes that can grip rope as easily as hands. They wear soft leather shoes with gaps for their toes, or go barefoot even in winter, trusting their grip more than any manufactured sole. Their movements have a peculiar flowing quality, always compensating for surfaces that shift beneath them, hands trailing along guide ropes out of habit even when walking on solid ground.

The Sievers' relationship with the mountain is one of wary symbiosis. They harvest the Scree Lands, sifting through the endless flow of debris for useful materials. Teams descend on rope lines to gather specific types of stone—the blue-gray slate that splits into perfect shingles, the quartzite that holds an edge when knapped, the occasional precious chunks of jade or crystal that tumble down from unknown sources high above. They work quickly, alert for signs of new slides, communicating through a complex system of rope-tugs and whistled signals that can convey surprising nuance.

Their true expertise, however, lies in filtration. The water that reaches them from above is thick with sediment, contaminated by its passage through the cliff city's industries. The Sievers have developed elaborate systems of settling tanks and filters, suspended like everything else in their aerial village. Water passes through layers of gravel, sand, and finally, through specially cultivated mats of algae that consume the last impurities. What emerges is cleaner than what the cliff city drinks—a fact that brings traders from both above and below.

The Anchors that support their existence are more than mere posts. Each is a monument to negotiation, carved over generations with the terms of their presence here. The oldest Anchors date back three centuries, their script nearly worn smooth by wind and weather. They detail agreements with the Cling-folk above about water rights and salvage claims, with the flatland peoples beyond about trade routes and territorial boundaries, and—most mysteriously—with the mountain itself, written in a script that predates any current language.

Maintenance of the Anchors is both practical and ritual. Stoneworkers descend monthly to check for cracks, to recarve weathered sections, and to add new codicils as agreements evolve. During these inspections, they perform ceremonies that seem equal parts engineering survey and religious rite—sounding the stone with tuning forks, leaving offerings in carved niches, and always, always listening for any sign that an Anchor might be failing. The loss of even one would destabilize the entire web, potentially bringing down the whole village.

The Sievers trade in movement itself. They alone can efficiently transport goods between the cliff city and the flatlands, their aerial roads bypassing the treacherous Scree Lands entirely. Caravans travel along the rope roads—not wheeled vehicles but trains of people and pack animals moving in careful synchronization, their loads distributed to maintain the network's balance. The sight of a full caravan crossing at sunset, silhouetted against the gray ceiling of cloud, has inspired a thousand songs in the flatlands beyond.

Their children grow up citizens of the air. Infants sleep in hanging cradles that rock with every breeze. Toddlers are tethered but given enough line to explore in three dimensions, developing spatial awareness that ground-dwellers find unsettling. By adolescence, they can run along swaying rope bridges at full speed, leap between platforms with casual precision, and judge the weight-bearing capacity of a rope by touch alone. Some develop such acute sensitivity to vibration that they can identify people by their footsteps on the network, feeling the unique rhythm transmitted through the cables.

The Sievers' art forms reflect their suspended existence. They're master weavers, creating tapestries that are meant to be viewed from all angles as they rotate in the wind. Their music employs hanging instruments—chimes, drums suspended at different heights, and stringed instruments built into the very structure of their rope network. During festivals, the entire village becomes an instrument, with musicians stationed throughout playing coordinated pieces that use the cables themselves to transmit vibration, turning the whole web into a vast resonating chamber.

Death among the Sievers is met with neither the Cling-folks' root-weaving nor the flatlanders' burial. They practice sky-gifting, wrapping their dead in beautiful shrouds woven with fragments of their life—hair, favorite tools, pieces of clothing—and hanging them from the highest points of their network. Wind and weather take the bodies, scattering them across the Scree Lands below. They see this as returning borrowed materials to the mountain's eternal grinding, completing a cycle that began with harvesting stone from below.

The strangest members of their community are the Bridgers—individuals who spend their lives maintaining and expanding the rope network. They live not in houses but in the network itself, sleeping in tiny shelters built into major junction points, constantly moving through the web like spiders. Over years, they develop an uncanny ability to feel the health of the entire system through their feet and hands, sensing weak points before they become visible, knowing where new connections are needed by the way stress patterns shift through the cables. Some say they cease to be entirely human, becoming part of the living infrastructure that holds the community aloft.

Between the eternal fog above and the shifting stones below, the Sievers have created something unprecedented—a civilization built on impermanence, thriving in the space between solid grounds. They are mediators by necessity, translators between the vertical world of the cliff city and the horizontal world of the flatlands, forever suspended between extremes.

---

The connection between these two worlds is more than mere proximity. It's a relationship of necessity, antagonism, and grudging interdependence that has evolved over centuries into something approaching symbiosis.

Water is the most obvious link. What begins as mist in the cliff city, condensing on stone and moss, filtering through gardens and mills, eventually finds its way to the Sievers' filtration systems. But the water carries more than sediment—it bears messages. The Cling-folk drop coded objects into their drainage systems: carved stones, knotted cords, and sealed bottles containing everything from love letters to trade negotiations. The Sievers read these messages in their filters, sending responses back via the few traders who dare the vertical journey between worlds.

The pulley systems tell another story of connection. While the Cling-folk refuse to set foot on the unstable Scree Lands and the Sievers rarely venture into the vertical maze above, both have developed elaborate mechanical connections. Great hemp cables, some as old as the Anchors themselves, run from the cliff city's lowest platforms to the Sievers' network. These carry baskets in endless loops—down with processed goods from the cliff workshops, up with filtered water, minerals, and news from the world beyond the mountain's shadow.

But it's the children who truly bridge the divide. Despite their parents' mutual wariness, young ones from both communities meet at the transition zones—those few stable platforms built where the cliff face meets the Scree Lands. Here, suspended neither in the Cling-folks' vertical world nor the Sievers' aerial one, they play games impossible in either home. Cling-folk children teach Siever youth to read the mountain's moods, to taste weather in the mist. Siever children share the art of rope-walking, of finding balance on surfaces that never stop moving. These friendships, forged in childhood, create unofficial channels of communication that often prove more valuable than any formal treaty.

The annual Mist Markets represent the most visible connection. Twice a year, during the season changes when the cloud layer briefly lifts, both communities converge on a series of broad ledges midway between their worlds. For three days, the usual separation dissolves. Cling-folk bring down preserved foods, worked stone, and trained cloudhooks. Sievers haul up filtered water, salvaged minerals, and goods from the flatlands beyond. But more than commerce happens here—marriages are negotiated, disputes are settled, and the Listeners from the deepest caves emerge to trade prophecies with the Bridgers who feel the future in the tension of ropes.

The old stories speak of a time when both peoples were one, before some great schism sent half seeking the stability of stone and half choosing the freedom of air. Archaeological evidence supports this—the oldest cliff dwellings show signs of rope attachments, while the ancient Anchors bear carved figures climbing their sides. Whether the split was ideological, practical, or driven by some catastrophe, it created two peoples perfectly adapted to harvest different aspects of the same vertical ecosystem.

Disease moves between the communities in strange ways. The Cling-folk's symbiotic mosses won't grow on Siever skin, but fungal infections from the cliff city find eager homes in the humid spaces where rope meets wood in the aerial village. The Sievers suffer from a bone-weakening condition if they spend too long on solid ground, while Cling-folk who venture onto the rope roads often develop vertigo that persists for weeks after returning home. Yet certain healers—working at the boundaries between worlds—have learned to use these very incompatibilities as cures, treating Cling-folk ailments with Siever remedies and vice versa.

The stone-touched children of the cliff city and the vibration-sensitive Bridgers share an unexpected connection. Both claim to sense something vast and slow moving through the mountain—patterns that span both communities, purposes that neither fully understands. They speak of the mountain not as a single entity but as a process, a centuries-long conversation between stone and water, weight and erosion. In their rare meetings, they compare experiences, trying to piece together a picture of something too large for any single perspective to grasp.

Trade disputes reveal the delicate balance. When the cliff city once tried to restrict water flow to force better terms from the Sievers, the rope-dwellers responded by refusing to filter the runoff. Within days, the backed-up contamination began poisoning the cliff city's lowest gardens. When the Sievers attempted to charge tolls for passage below, the Cling-folk stopped maintaining the pulleys, stranding tons of goods in transit. Both incidents ended quickly, teaching each community that their fates were more intertwined than either cared to admit.

The most profound connection might be the least visible: the dreams. Those who live their whole lives in either community report dreams of the other—Cling-folk dream of flying, of ground that sways beneath them, of freedom from stone's embrace. Sievers dream of solidity, of walls that don't move, of the security of bedrock. Whether these are expressions of deep cultural memory, psychological responses to their extreme environments, or something more mysterious, they create a shared vocabulary of yearning that crosses all boundaries.

During the rare emergencies that threaten both communities—massive slides that could tear out Anchors, or weather events that destabilize the entire cliff face—old protocols activate. Horns sound in specific patterns, mirrors flash coded signals, and both peoples mobilize with practiced efficiency. Sievers ascend on emergency lines to evacuate threatened sections of the cliff city. Cling-folk descend to help re-anchor tilting support cables. In these moments, the division between them disappears entirely, replaced by an older identity: mountain people, united against the forces that would sweep them all away.

The youth speak of joining the communities—of building permanent connections, of children who could live in both worlds equally. The elders resist, citing centuries of tradition, the importance of specialization, the dangers of diluting what makes each people unique. But change comes whether welcomed or not. Already, hybrid technologies appear—Cling-folk water wheels that incorporate Siever bearing designs, Siever filters improved with cliff city moss cultures. The future seems to promise not the merger of two peoples but the evolution of something new: a vertical civilization that spans from bedrock to cloud, each level contributing its essential function to a whole greater than its parts.

The mountain, for its part, continues its slow work—grinding stone to sand, lifting new rock from below, breathing mist and shedding skin in endless cycles. Whether it notices the small lives clinging to its surface, filtering its water, reading meaning in its movements, remains unknowable. But the Cling-folk and the Sievers have made themselves part of its process, adapted to its rhythms, dependent on its moods. They are no longer merely on the mountain or below it—they have become part of the mountain's own slow life, as essential to its nature as erosion and time.

In this vertical world, suspended between stone and sky, two peoples have found ways to live that would be impossible alone. Their connection—sometimes tense, often reluctant, but ultimately unbreakable—speaks to the power of place to shape not just individual cultures but the relationships between them. They are bound by water and stone, by rope and tradition, by the simple fact that in this harsh vertical world, they need each other to survive. And in that need, perhaps, lies the beginning of something that transcends mere survival—a shared identity as inhabitants of the impossible, citizens of the vertical, children of the eternal mist.
