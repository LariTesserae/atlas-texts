The Woven Expanse stretches across what was once a shallow seabed, now lifted and dried over millennia. Every surface here bears the mark of patient hands—not in grand monuments or towering structures, but in the endless accumulation of small, deliberate acts.

The ground itself is a vast tapestry of fired clay tiles, each no larger than a palm, laid edge to edge in patterns that shift subtly as you walk. The tiles are warm underfoot, baked by the steady heat that rises from deep thermal vents below. Their colors range from bone white to deep ochre, with occasional veins of manganese blue where ancient minerals were mixed into the clay. No two tiles are identical—each bears the thumbprint of its maker, a slight warping from the kiln, a unique crack pattern from the cooling.

Between the tessellated pavements rise structures of woven stone. These are not hewn blocks but thin sheets of sedimentary rock, split along their natural planes and worked while still green and flexible from the earth. The builders discovered that certain stones, when harvested at the right depth and season, could be bent and braided like enormous leaves. Now these petrified basketworks form low walls, small shelters, and mysterious spiraling forms whose purpose has been forgotten. The weaving is coarse but sturdy—gaps between the stone strips let air flow through, creating soft whistles and sighs when the thermal currents shift.

Scattered across this handmade desert are the tenders' gardens: circular depressions where hardy succulents grow in carefully arranged patterns. The plants are small, waxy things—some with leaves like thick coins, others sprouting arrays of fleshy fingers. They survive on the moisture that condenses on the stone weaves during the cool nights, channeled down into ceramic collection vessels buried at each garden's heart. The arrangements follow ancient formulae: seven silver-leafed specimens around one purple crown, or gradients of blue-green to yellow following the cardinal directions.

The air smells of hot clay and the faint medicinal scent of the desert plants. Sound travels strangely here—the ceramic tiles create a subtle acoustic environment where footfalls seem to echo from unexpected directions, and the occasional crack of thermal expansion sounds like distant percussion. Mostly, though, there is quiet: the soft susurrus of air through stone gaps, the barely audible trickle of condensation finding its path to the buried vessels.

Tools lie abandoned in various states of completion: stone knives still rough-hewn, waiting for their edges; unfired tiles stacked in pyramids, slowly returning to dust; coils of plant fiber that would have become rope or thread. The builders worked in generational waves, each adding their layer to the vast construct, then moving on before seeing their work complete. 

The horizon shimmers where tile meets sky, the patterns creating optical effects that make distance hard to judge. Sometimes the tessellations seem to suggest larger pictures—maps, perhaps, or star charts, or simply the dreams of ten thousand patient craftspeople made manifest in baked earth. Walking here, you become aware of every placement of your feet, every tile that receives your weight, every small disturbance to the ancient order.

At the thermal vents themselves, the tiling becomes more elaborate. Glazed pieces in deep teals and copper greens mark the hot spots, warning and decorating in equal measure. The heat has fused some sections into solid sheets of glassy ceramic, creating smooth basins where the morning dew collects for just a few minutes before evaporating. These brief mirrors reflect the stone weaves above, doubling their intricate shadows.

This is a place built by hand and time, where every surface remembers the touch that shaped it, where the absence of water made every drop precious enough to architect around, where the simple technologies of fire and patience transformed a dead seabed into something both utterly artificial and strangely organic in its accumulated complexity.
