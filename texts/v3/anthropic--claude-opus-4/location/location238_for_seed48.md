The salt flats stretch endlessly under a perpetual twilight, their crystalline surfaces cracked into perfect hexagons that weep with mineral brine. This is a drowned city, though you'd never know it at first glance—the buildings lie beneath, their industrial bones dissolved and reconstituted as vast platforms of chemical deposit. Only the heat shimmer reveals their ghostly geometries: factories and tenements reduced to geometric stains in the salt.

Steam rises everywhere in lazy columns, fed by the geothermal vents that killed this place and keep it dying. The air tastes of sulfur and electricity. Where the brine pools deepest, it glows a faint industrial green—not from life, but from the copper and chromium that leach eternally from the buried machinery below. The heat is oppressive, radiating up through your feet, making the humid air feel like breathing through wet wool.

They move in the margins of vision: the salvage beetles. Millions upon millions of them, each the size of a fist, their shells iridescent with chemical patinas. They're not alive—not exactly. The city's final industrial achievement before the waters came: self-replicating recyclers, built to break down waste, now the only inhabitants of this mineral purgatory. They cluster on the exposed pipes and corroded gantries that occasionally breach the salt crust, dismantling them atom by atom, carrying the metals away to build their ever-expanding hives in the distance.

The beetles work in eerie silence, their mechanical movements creating only the softest clicking, like rain on glass. But when they swarm—when they've found some particularly rich deposit of rare earth elements—the sound builds to a whisper-roar of ten million tiny mandibles working in unison. Then they flow like mercury across the flats, a living liquid that parts around your ankles if you stand still enough.

The city's infrastructure persists as a network of thermal channels beneath the salt. Steam-powered turbines still turn in flooded basements, generating electricity for no one, maintaining the temperature that keeps the beetles operational. Occasionally, a geyser breaks through, carrying with it a spray of superheated brine and the dissolved remnants of whoever couldn't escape when the thermal barriers failed. These eruptions leave behind new deposits: perfect circles of yellow and orange crystallization, like flowers in hell's garden.

At the horizon, cooling towers rise like broken teeth, their concrete cancer-eaten but standing. The beetles have built paper-thin bridges between them, gossamer structures of reclaimed aluminum and titanium that catch what little light filters through the perpetual industrial haze. When the wind picks up—hot and wet and tasting of metal—these bridges sing: a modal, minor-key threnody for the world that was.

This is equilibrium: the city dead but undecaying, its synthetic ecosystem grinding on through centuries of purposeless perfection. The beetles process and reprocess the same materials endlessly, their programming too simple to recognize the futility. The salt flats grow imperceptibly thicker each year, preserving in mineral amber the shape of humanity's industrial ambitions. And in the dim half-light, in the silence between the steam-hiss and beetle-whisper, you can almost hear the ghost of turbines turning, turning, turning, in the flooded factories below.
