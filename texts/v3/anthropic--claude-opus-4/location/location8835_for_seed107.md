The Cistern Cities stretch across a vast depression in the earth, a network of shallow clay basins connected by covered walkways and vaulted passages. Everything here is built from the same material: sun-baked brick made from the ancient lakebed's clay, mortared with a paste of the same substance mixed with crushed shells from when water last filled this place naturally, countless generations ago.

The architecture breathes with moisture. Every surface curves and slopes toward collection points—shallow channels carved into walkways, spiral grooves winding down pillars, entire rooftops shaped like gentle funnels. The rare rains that sweep through perhaps twice a season are precious beyond measure. When they come, the entire city system transforms into a vast mechanism for gathering water, every drop guided through the carved arterials into deep cisterns beneath each district.

Between rains, the air stays thick with captured dampness. The builders discovered long ago how to trap humidity in the honeycomb chambers within their walls, how to encourage condensation on specially textured surfaces during the cooler nights. Dawn brings a daily harvest as children move through the streets with long wooden paddles, scraping dew from the collection walls into narrow clay troughs.

The density is overwhelming at first—structures crowd against each other, sharing walls, sometimes sharing rooms. Buildings stack atop one another in irregular pyramids, connected by bridges no wider than a person. But this closeness serves a purpose: the narrow alleys trap moisture and shadow, creating microclimates cooler than the warm air above. Awnings of woven grass stretch overhead, gap-toothed and patchy, offering just enough shade while letting through the occasional shaft of light.

Sound moves strangely here. The clay absorbs voices, footfalls, the clatter of daily life. Conversations in one courtyard might be inaudible from the next, despite only a wall between them. The loudest sound is water—the careful pouring from vessel to vessel, the drip of condensation finding its way home, the gurgle of hidden pipes carrying the morning's collection to storage.

Vegetation clings to designated spaces: narrow garden strips where gray-green succulents grow in careful rows, their thick leaves harvested for both food and moisture. Wild plants are removed immediately—they steal water from the system. The only animals are small lizards that hunt the few insects brave enough to venture into this manufactured landscape, and the occasional bird that rests briefly on the highest towers before moving on.

The cities evolved over centuries, each generation adding their improvements to the water-gathering system. Early sections show cruder construction—simple mud-brick boxes with basic gutters. Newer districts display increasingly sophisticated water management: double-walled buildings that create their own condensation chambers, elaborate fountain houses where communal water is distributed according to strict schedules, underground networks of ceramic pipes that move water without loss to evaporation.

At the heart of each district stands a tower, broader at the base than the top, wrapped in a continuous spiral ramp. Inside, the deepest cisterns hold years of collected water in darkness. The tower keepers, hereditary positions passed down through families, monitor levels with marked poles and maintain the complex system of ceramic valves that control distribution. They know by sound how full each cistern is, can predict rain by the weight of the air, understand the thousand small signs that keep their district alive.

The built environment extends to the horizon—an undulating sea of clay roofs, punctuated by the collection towers and connected by the covered arterial walks. Where once a lake stretched, now the cities sprawl, each basin-district at a slightly different elevation, allowing for gradual water flow during the rare deluges. The engineering is primitive but profound: an entire civilization shaped around the capture and preservation of moisture, every surface, every angle, every material choice driven by water's scarcity and preciousness.

Change comes slowly here. New buildings rise only when old ones finally crumble beyond repair, and even then, the ancient bricks are ground down and reformed. Innovations in water collection spread gradually from district to district, carried by the marriage-alliances between keeper families. The cities breathe with the seasons—expanding their activities during the wet months, contracting during the dry, but always, always listening for the sound of rain.
