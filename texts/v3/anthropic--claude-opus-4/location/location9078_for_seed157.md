The Seething Grotto spreads before you, a cathedral-sized cavity in the mountain's heart where heat and water wage endless war. No sun has ever touched this place—only the faint phosphorescence of heat-loving microbes paints the darkness in sickly yellows and diseased greens, their colonies forming vast bacterial mats that drape the cavern walls like rotting tapestries.

The air itself attacks you. Superheated steam billows up from countless vents and fissures, condensing immediately on any cooler surface—including your skin. The temperature hovers just below what would cook flesh, and the humidity makes each breath a drowning exercise. Water runs everywhere: trickling down the smooth-worn walls, pooling in scalding basins, erupting in sudden geysers that shriek like tea kettles amplified through stone pipes.

The sound is catastrophic. Every surface acts as a resonator for the water's violence—the continuous thunder of thermal fountains, the machine-gun staccato of droplets hitting superheated stone, the deep groaning of rock expanding and contracting. Steam vents howl at different pitches, creating an accidental orchestra of geological agony. The din reverberates off the packed walls until it becomes a physical presence, a pressure that makes thought difficult.

In this lifeless furnace, things move. Millions upon millions of pale arthropods carpet every surface, their bodies translucent and segmented, somewhere between centipede and shrimp. They exist in such density that the walls appear to breathe—a living wallpaper of clicking legs and twitching antennae. These creatures feed on the bacterial mats and on each other, their mandibles creating a secondary percussion beneath the water's roar. When disturbed, they flow like liquid, revealing the obsidian-smooth rock beneath, worn to glass by centuries of their passage.

The geology tells a story of endless erosion. Water has carved fantastic sculptures from the volcanic stone—pillars twisted into helixes, walls honeycomed with perfectly round passages, ceiling pockets that collect steam until they overflow in boiling waterfalls. Every edge has been rounded, every surface polished. The rock itself sweats constantly, weeping mineral-rich water that has built up in frozen waterfalls of deposited salts—whites and oranges and poisonous greens.

At the grotto's heart lies the source: a pool of water so hot it barely maintains liquid state, its surface rippling with convection currents. The arthropods avoid it, creating a perfect circle of emptiness around its rim. Sometimes, from its depths, larger things surface—pale and boneless, with too many mouths—before sinking back into the thermal depths. The pool connects to something deeper, some network of flooded passages where the mountain's heat meets underground rivers, spawning this pocket of primitive hell.

The very air shimmers with heat distortion, making distances uncertain. What seems close might be far, what appears solid might be steam. The darkness isn't absolute—between the bioluminescence, the occasional spark of static electricity in the saturated air, and the dull red glow from the hottest vents—but it's worse than pure blackness. It's a twilight of fever dreams, where shapes shift and merge, where the crawling walls might be hallucination or might be the millions of creatures that have claimed every inch of this mineral womb.

This is a place between states—neither fully liquid nor gas, neither dead nor truly alive, a boundary zone where the earth's fever breaks through in perpetual, deafening crisis.
