The Drowned Bells of Korathene

Here, beneath thirty fathoms of crystalline saltwater, a city sleeps in perpetual twilight. Korathene was young when the waters came—perhaps only a century into its making—and its limestone towers still hold their sharp edges, their corners barely softened by the patient currents that now rule these streets.

The water here is peculiar: dense with dissolved minerals that catch the filtered sunlight like suspended stars, creating a luminous haze that shifts between blue-green and silver. Though submerged, the city exists in a vast hollow within the ocean, as if the water itself refuses to fully claim certain spaces. Great voids pocket the urban landscape—air-filled chambers trapped within buildings, inverse waterfalls where currents create permanent gaps, streets where the water thins to almost nothing, leaving corridors of dense, breathable mist.

No kelp forests soften these edges. No coral builds upon the carved stone. Instead, a fine crystalline frost covers everything—salt formations that grow in delicate, geometric patterns along walls and archways. The only living things that might be called vegetation are the pale bacterial mats that shimmer iridescent where light touches them, feeding on minerals leaching from the young stone.

The creatures here defy easy classification. Schools of translucent fish move through the streets like living glass, their organs visible as points of bioluminescent light. But stranger things inhabit the voids: aerial jellyfish that swim through the air pockets, their tendrils dragging through both mediums; arthropods with shells of living limestone that camouflage perfectly against the walls; and in the deeper hollows, things that might have been human once, their forms elongated and adapted, still maintaining the city with primitive tools of carved bone and shaped stone.

The architecture tells of a people who built with reverence for geometry. Hexagonal windows pierce round towers. Triangular bridges span between pyramidal platforms. Everything was carved from single blocks of stone—no mortar, no joints, just patient subtraction of material until only the essential remained. Channels cut into streets once carried freshwater; now they create racing currents that the adapted inhabitants use as transportation, riding the flows on simple boards of polished stone.

Sound barely travels here. The water swallows voices, footsteps, the clatter of tools. Only the deep structural groans of the city itself can be heard—stone settling, air pockets shifting, the slow breath of a place learning to live underwater. In the air-filled spaces, even whispers feel too loud against the profound quiet.

Time moves differently in Korathene. The tides barely reach this depth, creating only the gentlest of rhythms. Days blur into one eternal blue twilight. The inhabitants work in slow, deliberate movements, conserving energy in the cold water. They harvest mineral deposits that grow like slow flowers from the walls, shape tools with infinite patience, maintain their city against the gradual but inexorable press of water seeking every crack.

The strangeness intensifies in the city's heart, where the original temple still stands. Here, the water itself has been worked—carved into lasting shapes that hold their form despite being liquid. Solid currents spiral in frozen helixes. Walls of dense water stand without containers. The altar remains dry in its bubble of carefully maintained air, where the descendants of the original builders still perform rituals with movements adapted for two mediums, their ceremonies half-swimming, half-dancing.

Above it all, the surface is just visible as a bright, rippling ceiling. Sometimes shadows pass—whales, ships, clouds—reminders of a world that continues in ignorance of the patient civilization below, slowly adapting, slowly building, slowly becoming something new in the eternal twilight of their drowned stone gardens.
