The Undergrowth Sprawl spreads beneath the world's skin, a vast cavern system where ancient root-cities have grown together into one continuous, breathing mass. Here, just below the surface, warmth seeps down from sun-baked earth above, held close by the pressing weight of ten thousand years of vegetative architecture.

The roots are everything. What began as separate groves—planted by the first people who understood that trees could be shaped, coaxed, woven—has long since merged into a single organism. Thick as temple columns, the primary roots twist downward and outward, their surfaces worn smooth by countless hands, by the oils of touching, by the polishing of bodies moving through these living corridors. Secondary roots branch and rebranch, creating floors, walls, stairs. The wood has hardened with age into something between bark and stone, darkened by time and moisture to the color of old blood.

Water weeps everywhere. It beads on the root-walls, collects in carved basins, runs in thin streams along channels worn into the living wood by generations of flow. The air tastes thick, green, almost drinkable. Where roots meet, they've fused, creating nodes that bulge with stored moisture—living cisterns that pulse faintly with the slow circulation of sap. The people learned long ago to tap these with hollow reed spiles, drawing sweet water that tastes of earth and time.

Everything here was made, but made so long ago that the making has become nature. Dwellings carved into root-halls have been expanded by growth, their doorways stretched tall, their windows become irregular openings that breathe. Furniture shaped from living wood continues its slow transformation—a bench grows imperceptibly longer each year, a sleeping platform develops new curves. The tools are simple: stone blades for scarification that guides growth, bronze pins for grafting, hollow gourds for water collection. But the true technology is patience, the knowledge passed through generations of how to guide rather than force, how to live within the slow decisions of wood.

The fauna has adapted to this twilight world. Pale beetles large as hands traverse the root highways, their shells iridescent with moisture. They clean the wood, eating the soft fungi that would otherwise overwhelm the system. Colonies of silk-spinning moths festoon the upper reaches with their gauzy architecture, their caterpillars feeding on the tender shoots that still push through from above. Small, blind shrews navigate by whisker-touch through the spaces between roots, hunting the white centipedes that process fallen matter into soil.

Birds nest in the high places where roots thin and light filters down—not true daylight but a green glow, filtered through layers of leaves above and luminescent lichens below. Their songs echo strangely in these wooden caverns, muffled and multiplied until direction becomes meaningless. Mostly, though, the Undergrowth Sprawl holds quiet. Sound travels oddly here, absorbed by the soft punk wood, the hanging moss, the very thickness of the air.

The whole system pulses with slow life. Roots grow and die, creating new passages, closing old ones. The underground rivers shift course over decades, following the guidance of root-channels, carrying nutrients from distant forests. In the growing season, the walls themselves bloom—tiny white flowers that push through bark, lasting only days before falling to carpet the walkways with petals that smell of honey and decay.

People move through this world like blood through veins. Their settlements cluster at the major nodes, but the roots connect everything—a child can travel from the Tappers' Quarter to the Moth-Keepers' Hall without ever seeing sky, moving through passages worn smooth by ten thousand years of passage. They speak in low voices, not from reverence but because the roots remember sound, hold it in their grain, sometimes release it years later as whispers.

At the deepest levels, where the oldest roots anchor into bedrock, the wood has mineralized, become something other—still alive but changed, drawing minerals up from stone, incorporating crystal into cellulose. Here the root-chambers open into vast halls where the ceiling drips with metal-tasting water, where the walls glitter with incorporated quartz, where the air itself seems thick with the weight of geological time.

This is the achievement of the Undergrowth Sprawl: a made world so perfectly integrated with its own logic that making and nature have become one thing. The builders are long forgotten, but their work continues, growing, adapting, sheltering, transforming. Each generation adds their own carvings, their own diversions of water, their own shapings of space, but always within the pattern established so long ago that its beginning has passed from memory into the grain of the wood itself.
