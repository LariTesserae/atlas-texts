The plateau stretches beneath an enormous sky, its surface a geography of shallow depressions and subtle rises that collect and release water in slow, breathing cycles. The stone here is young—volcanic glass and porous basalt that emerged from the earth perhaps only centuries ago, still sharp-edged where it breaks through the thin soil, still holding the memory of heat in its crystalline structure.

Water seeps everywhere but pools nowhere deeply. It weeps from the rocks themselves, beading on the obsidian faces and trickling down channels barely begun. The warmth of the stone meets cooler air above, creating a constant condensation that shrouds the plateau in shifting veils of mist, though the sun burns through with startling intensity, casting everything in stark relief before the vapors close again.

The vegetation clings to these wet surfaces in scattered patches—primitive mosses that glow acid-green in the brightness, and strange succulent clusters that look like colonies of small, fleshy stars. They grow only where the water runs most consistently, leaving vast stretches of bare rock between their settlements. Some have developed waxy skins that lens the intense light into their cores, glowing faintly even in shadow.

The sound is the water itself—not the rush of streams or the crash of falls, but the aggregate voice of a million tiny flows. Droplets pinging against stone, thin sheets of water sliding over glass, the pop and hiss where warm rock meets cool moisture. It builds into a constant, ringing chorus that fills the air, punctuated by the occasional crack of thermal expansion in the volcanic glass.

Scattered across this weeping expanse are structures—or what might have been structures. Circles of stacked obsidian, their purpose unclear. Perhaps they were shelters, perhaps markers, perhaps something else entirely. The stones are fitted but not mortared, held together by precision alone. Some circles remain intact, others have partially collapsed, their stones scattered like dark seeds across the plateau. Inside several of the intact circles, the acoustic properties change—the water-sound focuses and amplifies into pure, singing tones.

The air itself seems to shimmer with more than heat. Where the light strikes the wet stone at certain angles, colors appear that shouldn't exist—brief flashes of hues between violet and green that have no names. The volcanic glass holds these colors for moments after the light has passed, like afterimages burned into the stone itself.

No animals move across this resonant plain. No insects hover in the mist. The only life is the plant-colonies and the rocks themselves, which sometimes seem to shift or settle in ways that suggest a geological patience, a mineral awareness operating on timescales beyond observation. The young stone is still learning how to be stone, still deciding its final form.

At the plateau's edges, where elevation drops away, the water gathers into hanging gardens of mist that cascade slowly into whatever lies below, invisible beyond the bright veil. The horizon in all directions is lost to this luminous haze, making the plateau feel both vast and contained, a world complete in itself—wet and warm and singing, bare and strange and blindingly bright, as if the earth here is still in the process of becoming something it hasn't yet named.
