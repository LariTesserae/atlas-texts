The Unfinishing Cities float in the amber twilight of a world that refuses to fully exist. Here, where reality has grown thin as moth wings, vast urban constructions spread across the curved inner surface of hollow planetary shells—each shell nested within another like Russian dolls made of industrial steel and condensed possibility.

You stand on a boulevard wider than most rivers, its surface a grid of hexagonal plates that hum with subsonic vibration. The plates are warm to the touch, each one the size of a house, fitted together with microscopic precision yet somehow always seeming freshly laid, as if the road is perpetually completing itself. Moisture beads on their brushed metal surfaces—not from rain, but from the constant phase-transitions of the air itself as it flickers between states of being.

Above, the city inverts. Buildings hang downward from what should be the sky, their foundations rooted in nothingness, their spires pointing toward you like accusing fingers. Between the upright city where you stand and its inverted twin, a zone of industrial twilight stretches—filled with scaffolding that builds itself, cranes that lift nothing into nowhere, and factory-cathredrals whose smokestacks exhale clouds of probability rather than smoke.

The sound is immense but not overwhelming—a mechanical breathing, a tidal rhythm of construction that never completes. Pistons hiss. Gears mesh and unmesh. Somewhere, always just out of sight, massive hammers fall with metronomic precision, driving nonexistent rivets into theoretical steel. The noise has texture here, visible as ripples in the too-thick air, audible as a taste of copper and ozone.

Water appears in pockets of industrial accident—condensation dripping from pipes that phase in and out of existence, creating temporary pools in the hollows between reality-states. These pools never last long; they evaporate not into vapor but into pure mathematical potential, leaving behind crystalline residues that look like frozen equations.

The shells are mostly empty space. Between the inner surface where the cities sprawl and the next shell outward, kilometers of hollow void yawn—crossed only by transit tubes of impossible thinness, like spider silk made of compressed spacetime. Through the transparent walls of these tubes, you can glimpse the rare fauna: geometric creatures that are more event than animal, feeding on the inconsistencies where reality fails to properly knit together. They appear as fractal shadows, hunting the glitches between what is and what should be.

The deeper you go into the city, the less finished everything becomes. Buildings start as complete structures but fade into architectural drawings, then blueprints, then pure intention. Streets devolve from pavement to graded earth to the mere suggestion of direction. The infrastructure reveals itself in layers—pipes and cables and things that might be veins running through transparent walls, all of them carrying not water or electricity but the raw stuff of existence itself.

The warmth here is not from any sun—there is no sun in this place where up and down have negotiated an uneasy truce. Instead, the heat comes from the friction of reality against itself, from the effort of existence trying to maintain coherence. In places where the friction is too great, the air itself begins to tear, revealing voids of un-space that smell of hot metal and distance.

Time moves differently in each shell. In some, the cities build themselves over epochs compressed into heartbeats. In others, a single hammer-fall takes geological ages to complete. The shells rotate at different speeds, creating zones where yesterday shears against tomorrow, where causality becomes negotiable.

This is a place caught in an eternal state of becoming—too stubborn to not exist, too broken to fully be. The cities grow like crystals in a supersaturated solution of possibility, always expanding, always unfinishing, always almost there.
