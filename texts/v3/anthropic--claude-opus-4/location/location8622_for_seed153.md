In the heart of the old city, where the canals have long since surrendered to vegetation, there exists a forgotten cistern. The entrance is nothing more than a gap between two settling foundation stones, wide enough for a child or a determined adult to squeeze through. Beyond this threshold, the city's weight has created something unexpected.

The chamber breathes with moisture. Water seeps eternally through the porous limestone walls, tracing mineral veins that glisten in the filtered daylight falling through cracks overhead. The space is no larger than a merchant's storeroom, yet it feels vast in its emptiness—a hollow pearl within the urban mass above. The original reservoir has long since filled with silt and seed, transforming into a suspended garden that defies its architectural origins.

Ferns cascade from every crevice, their fronds heavy with perpetual dew. Moss carpets climb the walls in vertical meadows, fed by the constant trickle of groundwater. The air tastes green and thick, each breath carrying spores and the sweet decay of fallen leaves that spiral down from the root systems penetrating from above. Where the old overflow channel once carried excess water, a thin stream still runs, disappearing into darkness through a grating choked with watercress.

The temperature never changes here—held in permanent spring by the thermal mass of stone and earth, warmed by the slow decomposition of plant matter and the metabolic heat of the living walls. Sometimes a lost cat finds its way down, or a confused bird follows the light shafts, but mostly the space belongs to the plants and the water's patient work.

Above, the city continues its business, unaware of this pocket ecosystem. Cart wheels rumble across the plaza overhead, sending tiny cascades of dust through the ceiling cracks. The roots of an ancient fig tree have found their way through the masonry, hanging like chandeliers dripping with condensation. In the deepest corner, where the builders left a small alcove for maintenance access, mushrooms fruit in pale clusters, recycling the constant rain of organic matter from above.

The sound here is subtle—drips striking water, the whisper of air through gaps in stone, the almost inaudible rustle of growth. During heavy rains, the chamber fills with a low resonance as water finds a hundred paths through the surrounding earth. The overflow channel gurgles more insistently then, remembering its original purpose.

This is infrastructure returned to nature, yet shaped by its architectural bones. The precise corners and engineered curves channel water and growth into patterns that would never occur in a natural cave. Civilization and wilderness have found an equilibrium here, creating something neither wholly built nor wild—a secret lung breathing slowly beneath the city's skin.
