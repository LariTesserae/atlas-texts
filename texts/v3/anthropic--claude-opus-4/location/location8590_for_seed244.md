The plateau stretches impossibly wide, a freshly-thrust shelf of dark basalt that caught the sky and refused to let go. Its edges are knife-sharp where the earth split perhaps only centuries ago, creating cliffs that plunge into morning fog. The rock face shows its youth in clean, geometric fractures—hexagonal columns and sharp-angled breaks that haven't yet learned to crumble.

Water moves here, but furtively. It seeps through hairline cracks in the stone, emerging as sudden springs that pool in natural basins before vanishing again underground. The morning brings condensation that clings to everything—beading on the sparse grasses, gathering in the shallow depressions of stone, creating a landscape that glistens before the weak sun burns it away. Sometimes clouds snag on the plateau's edge and empty themselves in brief, violent showers that send temporary streams racing across the impermeable rock.

The settlement sprawls across this vast tabletop of stone like lichen—dense and intricate despite its primitive construction. Thousands upon thousands of low stone dwellings press against each other, their walls shared, their roofs merging into a continuous crust of human habitation. The builders used what the plateau provided: split basalt fitted without mortar, gaps chinked with dried grass and clay. Every surface doubles as something else—roofs become walkways, walls support other walls, cisterns carved into bedrock serve as foundations.

The city has no plan because it has no pause. It grows constantly, desperately, like something fleeing. New structures crystallize at the edges even as others near the center collapse and are immediately cannibalized for stone. The narrow passages between buildings fill with workshops where people chip stone tools with stone hammers, where primitive forges burn dried grass and dung to work scraps of bog iron pulled from the lowland mists. The smoke has nowhere to go in the still, cold air, so it pools in the streets like another kind of fog.

What drives this frenzy isn't clear, but the settlement churns with urgent industry. Perhaps it's the plateau itself—the way the ground sometimes shudders, the way new cracks appear overnight, the way the edge seems closer than it was last season. Stone-workers repair faster than the earth can break, but only just. Water-carriers haul leather bags from the hidden springs, racing to fill cisterns before the next unpredictable dry spell. Teams of builders deconstruct and reconstruct whole neighborhoods when the bedrock shifts, their work lit by oil lamps that flicker in the perpetual maze of shadows cast by the packed buildings.

The vegetation clings to survival in this place. Tough grasses grow in any accumulation of dust. Moss finds the damp shadows between stones. Here and there, a stunted shrub has forced its roots into a crack, its branches pruned constantly for fuel. Small gardens occupy rooftops where soil has been carried up handful by handful, growing hardy tubers and bitter greens that can survive the cold and the constant moisture-drought cycle.

The sky here is enormous and bright, but the settlement creates its own dimness. The packed buildings cast complex shadows that shift throughout the day, creating a constantly changing pattern of light and dark corridors. The air is thin at this elevation, making every effort harder, every fire slower to catch. Sounds carry strangely—the tap of hammers on stone echoes from unexpected directions, conversations whisper around corners, and underneath it all runs the subliminal groan of basalt under pressure.

At the plateau's heart, where the settlement is oldest and most dense, the buildings have evolved into something else—a single structure with ten thousand rooms, where interior and exterior have lost meaning. Here the builders discovered that their primitive cement, made from crushed stone and blood and grass, gains strength from compression. The center is a maze that grows tighter with each generation, rooms subdividing, ceilings lowering, passages narrowing as the weight of the city presses inward and upward simultaneously.

The cold is constant but not killing—a thin, persistent chill that makes stone beds bearable only with piles of woven grass mats. Morning frost paints the city white, afternoon sun barely warms the stone, and night brings a cold that seeps through walls. The people dress in layers of rough-woven cloth and badly-cured hides, moving quickly through their city's serpentine passages partly for warmth, partly because stillness here feels dangerous.

This is a place balanced on the edge of becoming—too young to have found equilibrium, too vast to comprehend its own shape, too densely packed with human will to accept the patient erosion that might gentle its sharp edges. It builds itself faster than it falls, but only just, and in that narrow margin between creation and collapse, ten thousand lives weave themselves into stone.
