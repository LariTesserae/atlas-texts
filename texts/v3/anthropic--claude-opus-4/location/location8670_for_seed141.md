The Condensation Terraces stretch endlessly beneath a pearl-gray sky, a manufactured landscape of shallow industrial basins that has achieved its own peculiar stasis. Each terrace, pressed from some ancient alloy that weeps constantly with condensation, extends for kilometers before stepping down to the next level in a drop of precisely seventeen meters. The metal has aged to a blue-black patina shot through with veins of verdigris where the eternal moisture has worked its slow chemistry.

Water sheets across these vast platforms in films no deeper than a centimeter, moving with an almost imperceptible current toward drainage grates the size of city blocks. The grates lead to an understory of pipes and channels, themselves large enough to sail ships through, though nothing moves in them now but water seeking its level. The sound is a constant whisper—water on metal, the occasional plink of drops falling through grates, the susurrus of moisture condensing and flowing.

Strange architectures interrupt the terraces at regular intervals: cooling towers that no longer cool anything, their hyperboloid forms rising hundreds of meters into the wet air. They've become ecosystems unto themselves, their internal geometries colonized by creatures that have evolved to live in the perpetual mist. Bright orange fungi bloom in the shadows of their support struts. Translucent arthropods with too many joints scuttle along the condensation channels, feeding on the biofilms that coat every surface.

The sparse vegetation that has found purchase here grows in engineered planters built into the terrace surfaces—rectangular depressions filled with some synthetic growing medium that never seems to wash away. Metallic grasses with leaves like copper wire sway in the subtle air currents. Occasionally, a larger plant manages to root itself in a crack where the terraces have settled, its stems grotesquely swollen to store water despite the ambient moisture.

The fauna have adapted to this eternal wetland of metal. Herds of creatures move across the terraces—things like deer crossed with industrial equipment, their bodies sleek and chromed, their hooves specially adapted to grip wet metal. They graze on the biofilms and wire-grass, their movements creating temporary ripples in the water sheets that quickly smooth back to mirror-stillness. Smaller creatures live in the drainage systems: pale, elongated things with too many eyes that feed on whatever organic matter washes down.

The light comes from above, filtered through the constant moisture in the air, but also from below—a soft phosphorescence from the biofilms gives everything an underwater quality. At the horizon, where terrace meets sky, the distinction blurs. The terraces continue beyond sight, each one precisely like the last, a manufactured infinity where time has stopped meaning anything.

In the far distance, the remains of whatever industry built this place rise like mountains—factories the size of mountain ranges, their purposes forgotten, their machinery seized by moisture and time into permanent silence. They're backdrop now, geological features in their own right, bleeding rust-rivers that stain the terraces in long mineral fans.

The air smells of wet metal and ozone, with an organic undertone from the biofilms. The temperature never changes—always cool, always damp, the moisture in the air creating its own microclimate across the continental expanse of terraces. Weather systems form and dissipate at scales both tiny and vast: fog banks that roll across individual terraces, and supercell formations that take weeks to cross from one horizon to the other.

This is a place that has found its forever-state: the builders long gone, their creation settling into an equilibrium between decay and persistence, biology and machinery finding their balance in the eternal damp. The terraces will be here until the planet itself fails, weeping their engineered tears into drainage systems that lead nowhere but back to themselves, while strange life makes its home in the vast, wet spaces between.
