The Breathing Chamber sits high in the mountain's throat, a cavity of polished stone where reality has gone soft at the edges. No larger than a village square, the space pulses with impossible geometries—walls that curve inward become floors that spiral upward, surfaces that exist as both solid rock and trembling water simultaneously.

The warmth here is thick, animal-like, rising from the countless pale creatures that carpet every surface. They cluster in writhing masses: blind salamanders the color of bone, their bodies fused at random points to form living nets that breathe in unison. Thousands of them, layered atop each other, create a living substrate that coats the mineral walls. Their collective respiration creates a sound like distant thunder, a bass rumble that never varies in pitch or rhythm.

Water weeps constantly from the stone, running in braided streams that defy gravity's logic—some flowing upward along the walls, others pooling in mid-air before dispersing into mist. The moisture feeds the creatures but no plants; this is a world stripped down to stone and flesh and water. The salamander-things have no need for vegetation—they feed on something else, something that leaks through the cracks where physics has worn thin.

In the perpetual twilight, shapes move that shouldn't exist. A salamander crawls along the ceiling and becomes its own shadow. Water drops fall upward and land with the sound of breaking glass. The same stone formation appears in three places at once, each version slightly different, as if time has stuttered and left multiple copies.

The creatures fashion simple tools from their own shed skins and secretions—hooks and levers of hardened mucus, primitive but effective for their unknowable purposes. They work constantly at the walls, maintaining something, though the chamber never changes. Each movement is precise, rehearsed, part of an endless routine that might have lasted millennia.

The air tastes of copper and ozone. Sometimes, looking at the salamander masses from the corner of your eye, they appear to form symbols, equations written in living flesh that describe the breakdown occurring here. But look directly and they're just animals again, piled thick as autumn leaves, breathing their eternal rhythm in this pocket of twisted space where the mountain's interior has forgotten how to be solid, how to be singular, how to be sane.
