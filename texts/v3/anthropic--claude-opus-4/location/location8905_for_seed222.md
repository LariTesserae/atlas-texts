The Frost Nursery unfolds in a pocket between realities—a cavern that shouldn't exist, carved from the inside of a mountain that is still becoming itself. The space is no larger than a cathedral nave, but dense with impossibilities.

Crystalline structures push up from the floor like teeth through gums, their surfaces filmed with a thin layer of frost that never melts despite the industrial heat bleeding from below. These are not minerals in any conventional sense—they grow, branching and budding in real-time, their transparent bodies revealing intricate networks of what might be roots or veins or circuitry. Each crystal hosts colonies of something between lichen and moss, thick carpets of blue-green growth that pulse with their own dim bioluminescence, breathing out clouds of spores that drift like snow in the still air.

The vegetation has no need for water. It feeds on vibration itself, drawing sustenance from the tremendous mechanical roar that emanates from beneath the chamber floor. Down there, glimpsed through gaps in the emerging crystal forest, massive engines churn—their purpose unclear, their origin unknown. Pistons the size of tree trunks pump in precise rhythm, generating the sound that feeds the garden above. The noise is overwhelming, a physical presence that makes the air itself dense, creates standing waves that the plants ride like surfers.

The cold here has a peculiar quality—it exists in pockets and streams, flowing around the warm updrafts from the machinery below like oil refusing to mix with water. Your breath fogs in one spot, vanishes in another three feet away. The temperature gradients create their own weather: miniature cyclones of frost spiral up through the crystal groves, carrying spores and mechanical debris in glittering columns.

Scattered throughout this impossible garden are remnants of intentionality—a metal staircase that spirals up into solid rock, stopping abruptly; pressure gauges mounted on crystals that have grown around and through them; pipes that emerge from one wall and disappear into another without ever crossing the space between. These artifacts suggest purpose without revealing it, as if someone began building something here and forgot what they were making halfway through.

The few creatures that inhabit this space are hardly creatures at all. They appear as distortions in the air, visible only when they pass through the spore-clouds or when frost briefly adheres to their surfaces. They might be made of sound itself—living waveforms that feed on the mechanical roar and excrete silence, creating momentary pockets of devastating quiet that the plants visibly recoil from.

The entire space pulses with emergence. New crystals crown every few minutes with a sound like breaking bells. The moss spreads in visible waves, colonizing fresh surfaces before they've finished forming. Even the machinery below seems to be assembling itself, new pistons joining the symphony as pipes snake upward through the floor like metal vines. This is not a place that was built—it is a place that is building, an industrial womb where the child is simultaneously the factory and the garden, neither fully born nor fully designed.

The dim light comes from everywhere and nowhere—bioluminescence, the glow of superheated metal, phosphorescent frost, and something else that might be the air itself struggling to exist in this impossible space. It creates a twilight of constant dawn, where shadows have too many sources to fall cleanly and everything exists in a state of perpetual almost-visibility.

This is creation at its most confused and vital—a place where the industrial and the organic have forgotten their boundaries, where cold and heat dance together, where sound becomes food and machines grow like living things. It is small enough to feel trapped within, yet dense enough to hold entire ecosystems in each crystal formation. It is being born, and it has been being born forever, and it will never quite finish becoming whatever it's trying to be.
