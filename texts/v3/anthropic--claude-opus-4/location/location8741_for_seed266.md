The drowned city spreads beneath a permanent ceiling of water, its ancient towers and causeways visible through the strange clarity of the liquid atmosphere. This is not a place recently submerged—the water has been here so long it has become something else entirely, a medium denser than ocean but clearer than air, held in place by technologies that bend the rules of pressure and gravity.

The structures below were built for this environment. Their surfaces gleam with a vitreous coating that has fused with the original materials over geological time, creating buildings that are half-architecture, half-fossil. The tallest spires reach perhaps thirty meters, but most have worn down to stumps and foundations, their edges softened into curves by ages of liquid erosion that has long since reached equilibrium. Nothing crumbles anymore. Nothing changes.

Streets run between the buildings in perfect grids, their surfaces paved with something that might once have been synthetic but now resembles polished stone. The water here doesn't move—no currents disturb the absolute stillness. It simply exists, held in a state of perpetual calm by field generators hidden in the city's bones, their hum the only sound in this drowned world. The hum is constant, resonating through the liquid medium at a frequency that makes it felt more than heard, a vibration that passes through any visitor's body like a second heartbeat.

Sparse vegetation clings to the structures—not seaweed or kelp, but something engineered for this exact environment. Thin filaments of bioluminescent moss trace the edges of buildings in pale blue lines, feeding on minerals leached from the ancient concrete. These are the only living things visible, their light providing a faint supplement to the filtered illumination that penetrates from somewhere far above, giving the city a perpetual twilight glow.

The water itself defies understanding. It's breathable—not through gills or any biological adaptation, but through direct osmotic exchange with modified lung tissue. The city's builders encoded the secret into the liquid itself, a nanotechnology so advanced it appears indistinguishable from natural law. The water is cool against the skin, exactly the temperature where the body stops noticing it, where inside and outside cease to feel different.

In the center of the city, a vast plaza opens up, its floor a single seamless sheet of that glassy material. Here the buildings pull back, creating a space perhaps a kilometer wide where only low foundations remain, worn down to suggestions of their former purposes. The acoustic properties of this space amplify the field generators' hum into something almost musical—a chord that never resolves, held in perfect suspension like everything else here.

This is a place of aftermath, though what came before has been so thoroughly erased by time that only the fact of ending remains. The city persists in its drowned state not through struggle but through a technology so perfectly calibrated that maintenance has become unnecessary. It has settled into its final form: a monument to a civilization that solved the problem of preservation so completely that they preserved themselves out of existence, leaving only their empty city humming its endless note beneath the eternal water.
