The Thermal Cathedral rises from a plateau of fused ceramic plates, each the size of a city block, their edges welded together by centuries of heat and pressure. Steam vents puncture the landscape in irregular intervals, releasing scalding columns that condense into brief, localized rainstorms before evaporating again. The moisture never lingers long enough to pool, but it leaves behind mineral deposits that streak the ceramic in iridescent bands of copper, sulfur, and salt.

The structure itself defies easy categorization—part industrial complex, part monument. Massive heat exchangers spiral upward like the ribs of some mechanical deity, their surfaces pocked with thousands of hexagonal chambers that whistle and shriek as superheated air rushes through them. The sound is deafening, a constant chord that shifts pitch with the wind patterns, creating harmonics that make the ceramic plates beneath your feet vibrate.

Everything here was made, though by whom or what remains unclear. The construction speaks of industrial precision—riveted joints, pressure gauges frozen at impossible readings, catwalks that zigzag between the heat exchangers at vertigo-inducing angles. Yet the erosion patterns suggest abandonment measured in centuries. The metal has developed its own patina, a rainbow sheen that shifts from purple to gold as clouds of steam roll past.

The fauna, when it appears, seems more accident than intention. Creatures of brass and copper scuttle along the thermal gradients—mechanical arthropods that feed on temperature differentials, their joints clicking and whirring as they position themselves where hot meets cold. They flee at any approach, disappearing into maintenance shafts that honeycomb the structure's base.

The elevation is brutal. The plateau itself sits at an altitude where the air grows thin, but the heat exchangers rise another thousand feet, their tops lost in a permanent cloud layer created by their own exhaust. The catwalks have no railings. The wind, channeled and amplified by the industrial architecture, creates vortexes that can pluck an unwary visitor from the walkways.

During the peak heat cycles, which occur every seventeen hours, the entire structure glows cherry-red. The ceramic plates buckle and sing. New vents crack open while others seal themselves shut, the landscape constantly reconfiguring itself in small but significant ways. The rain that follows these cycles is brief but torrential—superheated water that flashes to steam the moment it touches any surface, creating a fog so dense that the world beyond the nearest heat exchanger disappears entirely.

In the relative quiet between cycles, you can hear deeper sounds—the groan of metal expanding and contracting, the hiss of pressure valves opening in sequence, and beneath it all, a rhythmic pumping that suggests somewhere far below, ancient turbines still turn, still generate the heat that keeps this place alive. The purpose of the Thermal Cathedral remains opaque, but its function continues unabated, a monument to industrial persistence in a world that has long since moved on.
