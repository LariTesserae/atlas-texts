High in the warm cliffs, where the stone still dreams of becoming, there is a hollow that breathes.

The cavity opens like a mouth in the rockface—no wider than a cottage, no deeper than a well. Its walls are soft sandstone, not yet fully hardened, still deciding what color they want to be. Ochre bleeds into rose, rose into cream, all of it slightly damp to the touch. The stone sweats here, beading with moisture that has no clear source. Sometimes the droplets run together and trickle down in temporary streams that last minutes or hours before vanishing again.

This is new rock, geologically speaking. Still learning how to be mountain. The walls are smooth as skin in some places, rough and crumbling in others. Crystals are trying to form—you can see their beginnings, like sugar frosting in the crevices. Salt, perhaps, or some other mineral precipitating out of the perpetual dampness. The floor is sandy, made of its own walls' slow dissolution.

But the hollow is far from empty.

They cluster on every surface—gastropods of peculiar variety. Snails with shells like architecture, spiraling in impossible geometries. Some are no bigger than pearls, others the size of fists. Their shells are translucent, revealing the shadows of their soft bodies within, and when the dim light catches them right, they glow faintly from within like paper lanterns. They move constantly but slowly, leaving silver trails that crisscross the walls in a glittering maze.

The sound is what strikes you first—a continuous, susurrous chorus. Each snail contributes its own note to the composition: rasping as they feed on the mineral deposits, clicking as shells touch shells, and something else—a humming, almost, that seems to come from inside them. Together they create a white noise that fills the hollow, echoing off the close walls until it becomes oceanic.

Moss grows in the dampest corners, but sparsely—tiny cushions of emerald and chartreuse that the snails avoid. A few determined ferns unfurl from cracks near the entrance, their fronds forever beaded with moisture. Sometimes a small lizard darts in, drawn by the warmth that radiates from the stone even as it sweats. The lizards never stay long. The snails' chemistry makes them unpalatable, and the constant noise seems to disturb reptilian sensibilities.

The entrance faces east, and in the early morning, weak sunlight filters in for perhaps an hour. This is when the hollow is most alive. The snails become more active, their shells catching and refracting the light into tiny rainbows. The moisture on the walls turns golden. The sound intensifies, as if celebrating.

Someone was here once. Or something that could stack stones. Near the back of the hollow, three flat rocks have been placed deliberately, forming a tiny cairn no higher than a knee. The snails cruise over and around it without concern. Whatever purpose it served, it serves no longer, except as another surface for their endless traversal.

The air tastes of minerals and rain, though rain rarely falls here directly. The moisture comes from within—from the stone itself, perhaps, or from some hidden spring deep in the rock's unconsolidated heart. It keeps the hollow in a state of perpetual dampness, a micro-climate that exists nowhere else on this warming mountainside.

At night, the snails' inner light becomes more apparent. They pulse gently, not in unison but in waves that ripple through their population. Bioluminescence, perhaps, or some trick of their translucent shells concentrating starlight. The effect is hypnotic—a constellation crawling across walls that are still deciding what they want to be when they grow up.

This is a place between states: between wet and dry, between formed and unformed, between silent and singing. It is small enough to miss entirely, unless you know where to look. Unless you hear the sound first and follow it to its source. Unless you understand that mountains, too, have their secret rooms where strange life congregates, where the planet tries out new ideas in private before releasing them to the wider world.

The hollow waits, patient as geology, loud as life, for whatever comes next in its slow becoming.
