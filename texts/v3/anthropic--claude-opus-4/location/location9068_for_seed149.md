The mountain city breathes steam into perpetual darkness.

Water seeps from every surface here—the black stone of the mountainside weeps constantly, fed by springs that well up through volcanic rock. The city terraces rise through clouds that never lift, their moisture condensing on metal roofworks and stone facades until everything runs with rivulets. Steam vents puncture the streets at regular intervals, their mechanical lungs exhaling the mountain's internal heat into the cool air, creating a constant fog that turns all light sources into diffuse halos.

This darkness is absolute and ancient. No sun has touched these slopes in living memory—whether from the permanent cloud cover, some atmospheric anomaly, or the shadow of still higher peaks, none can say. The city exists in eternal night, its industrial hearts pumping light through networks of electric cables strung between buildings like glowing webs. Streetlamps burn constantly, their yellowed bulbs reflected in ten thousand wet surfaces. Factory windows glow orange and white. The steam itself carries a faint phosphorescence from the minerals it bears up from the deep.

The vegetation has adapted to thrive in darkness and damp. Pale ferns unfurl from every crevice, their fronds reaching heights that would be impossible in drier climates. Ghostly mosses carpet the walls in layers thick enough to muffle sound—though not enough to quiet this place. Fungal gardens bloom in planned terraces, their bioluminescent caps providing both food and gentle light. Root systems of unknown plants penetrate building foundations, their leaves black-purple and waxy, drawing nutrients from the mineral-rich water that flows everywhere.

The city itself stretches across a vast bowl in the mountainside, its furthest districts disappearing into mist and darkness. Generations of construction have created a vertical maze—factories built atop tenements built atop older factories, connected by covered walkways, iron bridges, and pneumatic tube systems. The architecture is industrial but ornate, as if the builders sought beauty even in their mechanical systems. Pipes run like veins along building exteriors, their joints decorated with brass fixtures now green with verdigris. Cogwheels and drive belts transfer power between structures, their constant turning creating a mechanical rhythm that never ceases.

Sound fills every space. The factories hum and clank in overlapping rhythms. Water drips, flows, gushes through ten thousand channels. Steam hisses from vents and valves. Beneath it all runs the deep vibration of the mountain's geothermal engines, felt more than heard. And threading through this industrial symphony come the cries of the fauna—adapted creatures that hunt and feed in the eternal wet darkness. Their calls echo off wet stone, creating a cacophony that the human residents have long learned to parse for meaning.

The fauna itself has evolved remarkable strategies. Pale salamanders the size of house cats navigate the pipes and drainage systems. Eyeless birds nest in the factory rafters, navigating by echolocation that cuts through the mechanical noise. Larger things move in the mist between buildings—long-limbed climbers with phosphorescent patches on their skin, using the city's vertical geography as hunting grounds. In the deepest waterways, where the industrial runoff meets the mountain springs, albino eels grow fat on the strange ecosystems that bloom around warm-water outlets.

Everything here exists in perfect stasis. The city hasn't expanded in centuries, having found its optimal size. The factories produce exactly what the population needs. The water cycles through the same channels, carrying the same minerals, feeding the same gardens. Even the wildlife populations remain stable, predator and prey locked in an ancient balance. Time passes, but nothing truly changes. The same families work the same factories their great-grandparents built. The same moss grows on the same walls. The same steam rises through the same darkness.

The emptiness here is not absence but distance—vast spaces between islands of activity. A single lit factory floor might be separated from its neighbor by hundreds of feet of vertical darkness. Residential clusters glow like constellation points, connected by transport cables over foggy voids. The sound of industry carries far through the humid air, making the city seem fully inhabited, but in truth enormous volumes of space separate each pocket of life. Citizens might live their whole lives knowing only their own district, seeing other parts of the city only as distant lights in the mist.

In this eternal night, in this perfect equilibrium of water and stone, of industry and vegetation, of sound and vast empty spaces, the mountain city persists—neither growing nor dying, forever breathing its steam into the darkness.
