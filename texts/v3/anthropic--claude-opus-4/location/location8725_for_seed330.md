The Hollow Cities of the Unfinished Heights

Up here, where the air thins and the world seems to be still deciding what it wants to become, sprawling settlements cling to mountainsides that haven't quite finished forming. The rock itself is young—you can see it in the way surfaces crumble at a touch, revealing crystalline interiors that weep moisture in the cool air. These are mountains being born, their faces still soft with the rawness of geological infancy.

The cities stretch across multiple peaks and valleys, connected by rope bridges that sway in the perpetual mist. But for all their spread, they feel impossibly empty. Streets wide enough for crowds hold only echoes. Stone towers rise three, four stories into the dim air, their windows dark, their doorways yawning. The builders constructed for multitudes that never came, or perhaps that departed, leaving behind a urban skeleton draped across infant mountains.

What vegetation exists grows in defiant patches—stubborn mosses that feed on the mineral tears of the emerging rock, thin grasses that sprout from cracks in the vast plazas. Here and there, a twisted shrub manages to sink roots into some pocket of accumulated soil, its branches reaching horizontally, shaped by winds that blow through the empty corridors of stone.

The dampness is peculiar here. It doesn't rain often, but moisture condenses from the cool air, beading on every surface. In the early morning, before the weak sun burns through the perpetual haze, the entire city glistens as if coated in glass. Water collects in carved channels that run along the streets—primitive aqueducts that feed into cisterns carved directly from the living rock. The sound of dripping is constant but muffled, creating a rhythm like a thousand unsynchronized clockworks.

The architecture itself defies explanation. The buildings are clearly constructed—mortared stone, carved lintels, planned streets—yet they seem to grow from the mountains rather than sit upon them. Walls merge seamlessly with cliff faces. Stairs carved into the rock lead to doorways that open onto natural caves that have been expanded and shaped into rooms. It's as if the city is halfway between being built and being born, caught in the same embryonic state as the mountains themselves.

Tools lie abandoned in workshops—simple things of stone and bronze, hammers with their handles still wrapped in leather gone stiff with age and moisture. Looms stand empty, their threads long since rotted away, leaving only the wooden frameworks like skeletons of forgotten purpose. The technology speaks of human hands and human timescales, yet the city's integration with the unfinished geology suggests something else entirely.

In the perpetual dim light—whether from fog, from the angle of the sun at this elevation, or from some quality of the air itself—shadows seem to have substance. They pool in corners and flow down stairways like slow liquid. Sometimes, rarely, something moves within them. Not quite animal, not quite anything recognizable. The few creatures that inhabit these heights are strange adaptations to emptiness: long-limbed things that can span the gaps between buildings, their bodies evolved for voids. Moths with wings like sheets of paper that feed on the mineral deposits left by evaporating moisture. Things that might be birds or might be airborne mosses, drifting between the towers on currents too subtle to feel.

The sound landscape is one of almost unbearable quiet. The altitude dampens noise, the moisture muffles it, the emptiness swallows it. Footsteps on stone die within meters. Voices carry strangely—sometimes vanishing immediately, sometimes echoing from unexpected directions minutes after being spoken. The city seems to be listening, always listening, through the countless empty windows and doorways.

Yet for all its stillness, this is not a dead place. The mountains continue their slow birth, surfaces shifting imperceptibly, new minerals emerging from depths. Water traces new paths with each season. The strange sparse life adapts and readapts to the changing geometry. The city itself seems to participate in this slow dynamism—walls that were vertical last year now lean a degree or two, doorways that were square have become rhomboids, floors that were level have developed subtle slopes. The builders, whoever or whatever they were, seem to have anticipated this. The architecture accommodates change, joints that allow for movement, foundations that can shift without breaking.

At night, when the dim day fades to true darkness, phosphorescent minerals in the young rock begin to glow—faint constellations mapping the invisible stresses and formations within the mountain. The empty city becomes a ghost of light, its edges blurred, its boundaries uncertain, a place that exists somewhere between dream and geology, between intention and accident, between the built world and the wild processes that shape planets.

This is a landscape of profound loneliness, yet also of potential—a place waiting to become fully real, fully inhabited, fully itself. The mountains are still rising. The city is still empty. The story of this place has barely begun to be written in stone and silence and slow, patient time.
