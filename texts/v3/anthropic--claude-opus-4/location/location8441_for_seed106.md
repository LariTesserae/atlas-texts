The Glass Terraces rise from the warm mists like a fever dream of geometry. Each level spreads wider than city blocks, carved from what appears to be volcanic obsidian but gleams with an inner wetness that never dries. The stone sweats constantly—not from porosity, but as if the material itself exhales moisture from some internal process. Thin sheets of water slide perpetually across the black surfaces, gathering at the sharp edges where they fall in whisper-quiet curtains to the terrace below.

The city built here defies easy understanding. Streets and structures are carved directly into the glass-like stone, their surfaces polished to mirror smoothness by the eternal flow of condensation. The builders—whoever or whatever they were—left no tools, no machinery, only these perfect incisions in the living rock. Channels direct the flowing water through the settlement in precise patterns, feeding into reservoirs that never overflow, never empty, never change their levels by even a finger's width.

Above, the sky burns white. The light comes not from any visible sun but from the air itself, as if the atmosphere at this elevation has achieved some critical temperature where it simply glows. The brightness reflects and refracts through the wet glass surfaces, creating a landscape of blazing shadows and dark brilliances. The air shimmers with heat, yet the perpetual moisture keeps it from becoming oppressive—instead, it feels like breathing inside a warm cloud.

The emptiness here is profound. Between the carved structures stretch vast plazas of wet obsidian, their surfaces broken only by the geometric drainage patterns etched into them. A thousand people could gather in any one of these spaces, yet nothing moves except the eternal slide of water. The buildings themselves are hollow shells—rooms and halls carved with mathematical precision, connected by corridors that follow no human logic. Some spaces are no larger than closets; others open into chambers that could hold cathedrals. All are empty. All are wet. All are silent except for the faint music of water on glass.

Occasionally, something moves in the periphery—a shimmer that might be a heat mirage, might be a reflection, might be something else. The fauna here, if it can be called that, exists more as possibility than presence. Transparent things that might be jellyfish drift through the air at the edges of vision, but turn to look and there's only the bright sky. Sometimes the water patterns suggest the passage of something beneath the surface, but the obsidian is solid, unbroken, absolute.

The terraces extend to the horizon in every direction, each level connected by ramps and channels that seem designed for the water as much as for any inhabitants. From any vantage point, you can see for miles across this crystalline metropolis, empty and gleaming under its sourceless light. The settlement has the quality of a musical instrument waiting to be played—every surface, every angle, every channel designed to some purpose that remains forever just out of comprehension.

Time passes here without effect. The water flows but never erodes the sharp edges. The brightness neither dims nor intensifies. The warmth neither builds nor dissipates. The city stands in perfect, terrible equilibrium—not dead but never alive, not abandoned but never inhabited, not natural but built without tools or comprehensible technology. It simply exists, sweating its endless moisture into the bright air, waiting with geological patience for something that may have already come and gone, or may never arrive at all.

In the deepest chambers, where the light barely penetrates despite the transparency of the walls, the obsidian sometimes shows other colors—deep purples, midnight blues, the green of deep ocean. These hues shift like oil on water, suggesting depths within the material that the eye cannot quite resolve. Here the silence is most complete, broken only by the sound of individual drops falling into pools that reflect nothing, not even the darkness that surrounds them.
