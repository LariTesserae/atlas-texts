Between the massive buttress roots of an ancient city-tree, in a hollow no larger than a merchant's shop, lies a world complete unto itself. The space breathes with cold mist that pearls on every surface—on the jade-soft moss that carpets everything in finger-deep luxury, on the thousands of tiny carved doorways that pierce the root walls, on the delicate glass baubles strung between miniature balconies on threads of spider silk.

This is a termite city, though no termite built it. The insects inherited these structures from something else, something that carved the root-wood into spiraling galleries and perfect hexagonal chambers, that ground colored stones into pigments and painted murals too small for human eyes to parse. Now the termites tend their fungus gardens in the old pantries, march their predetermined routes along highways worn smooth by a million predecessors, and maintain the ancient aqueduct system that channels condensation from the root surfaces into reservoir pools no bigger than thimbles.

The light comes from everywhere and nowhere. The moss itself glows with a gentle bioluminescence, as do certain fungi that fruit in careful arrangements—almost like street lamps—along the major thoroughfares. Dewdrops catch and multiply this light, creating a galaxy of cold stars throughout the space. Sometimes a shaft of daylight finds its way through the canopy far above, through the layers of city built upon city, and for a moment the hollow blazes with gold before returning to its eternal blue-green twilight.

Everything here is wet and cold and close. The air tastes of earth and copper. Tiny ferns grow from cracks in the termite masonry, their fronds brushing the doorways like diminutive trees. Beetles with shells like drops of mercury navigate the crowded vertical streets, while transparent springtails bounce between levels on legs thin as light. A salamander the length of a child's thumb has made its home in what was once perhaps a temple, its skin black and starred with constellation markings, its movements so slow it might be mistaken for architecture.

The silence is absolute but full. It's the silence of ten thousand small things breathing, of moisture gathering and falling in drops too tiny to make a sound, of fungal networks spreading one cell at a time. Even the termites move without noise, their chemical conversations invisible, their industry mute.

And the city never changes. The same drops of water follow the same carved channels, day after day. The same beetles walk the same paths. The moss grows to a certain height and stops. The salamander moves between three favored spots. The fungus gardens are harvested and regrown in perfect cycles. This is a place that found its balance sometime in the previous age and has held it ever since, a pocket of deep time wrapped in root-flesh, a secret that keeps itself.

Sometimes, very rarely, a human child following a rolling ball or chasing a glimpse of something bright might wedge themselves between the roots and discover this place. They never stay long—the cold drives them out, or the closeness, or perhaps the feeling of being watched by ten thousand tiny eyes. But for a moment they glimpse what seems impossible: a city older than the tree that holds it, bright as a jewel box, silent as a held breath, complete as a world.
