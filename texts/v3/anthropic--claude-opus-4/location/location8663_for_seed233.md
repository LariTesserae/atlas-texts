High on a nameless peak where the air thins to a whisper, a construction site defies comprehension. The mountain itself is still becoming—raw stone pushing up through older stone, granite shoulders emerging from their limestone cradles with the patience of geological time. Yet here, at nearly three thousand meters, humanity has anchored something impossible into the unfinished rock.

The machinery came first, or perhaps the machinery *is* the place. Industrial turbines the size of houses have been bolted directly into the emerging cliff faces, their massive fans spinning not with wind but with the mountain's own upward momentum. The sound is catastrophic—a grinding, tectonic roar that makes thought difficult and speech impossible. Workers communicate in sign language, their movements precise and practiced against the din.

Everything here exists in layers, pressed close despite the vastness of the altitude. Scaffolding clings to raw rock faces like metallic lichen, dense networks of pipes and cables threading between natural stone and half-formed structures. The few buildings—control stations, equipment sheds—huddle wherever the mountain offers a moment of stability, their foundations adjustable, built to rise with the stone.

The moisture comes strangely. Not rain exactly, but a fine mist generated by the friction of rock against rock, stone birthing stone. It condensates on the metal infrastructure, drips from cables, pools in the shallow depressions of not-yet-weathered granite. Sometimes these pools flash-freeze in the thin, cool air, creating treacherous sheets of ice that workers must chip away each morning. Other times the mist turns to steam where the deepest turbines touch the mountain's still-warm heart.

What vegetation exists grows in defiance—small, stubborn mosses colonizing the spots where oil has dripped from machinery, creating tiny oases of unlikely green. Arctic willows, no taller than a child's hand, sprout from cracks in the emergent stone, their roots following veins of moisture deep into the mountain's body. They shiver constantly from the vibrations, their leaves perpetually trembling.

The purpose of this place remains obscure. Some say they're mining the mountain's birth—extracting something valuable from the very process of geological becoming. Others claim they're accelerating it, that the turbines and generators are somehow midwifing the peak into existence. The workers, when they descend to the valleys below for their brief rotations off-site, speak of gauge readings that track not temperature or pressure but something else—the pace of stone, the rhythm of emergence.

At night, the work continues under industrial floods that cast harsh shadows between the scaffolds. The light catches on the mist, creating false auroras in the steam and spray. Sometimes, rarely, something moves between the structures that isn't human—perhaps a lost mountain goat, perhaps something else, evolved to live in the narrow spaces between raw geology and industrial ambition. These creatures, when glimpsed, seem to flow like mercury over the wet stone, feeding on some quality of the emergence itself.

The soundscape never quiets. Beyond the roar of turbines, there's the crack of new stone splitting from old, the hiss of steam vents, the clatter of tools on metal, the deeper groans of the mountain asserting its own slow violence. Workers wear protective gear not just for their ears but for their bones—the vibrations at certain frequencies can set up sympathetic resonances in human skeleton, leading to fractures if exposure is too long.

This is a place of becoming doubled—the mountain pushing skyward in its ancient way while humanity races to build something alongside that process, or perhaps to harness it. The dense tangle of natural and industrial emergence creates its own microclimate, its own rules. Time moves strangely here, where human urgency meets geological patience, where the calendar of shifts and quotas overlays the epochal calendar of stone.
