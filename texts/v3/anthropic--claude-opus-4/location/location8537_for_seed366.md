The Vermillion Terrace rises from the surrounding badlands like a fever dream of geometry. At nearly two thousand meters elevation, the air shimmers with heat that shouldn't exist at this height—a thermal anomaly that has drawn creatures from across the continent to nest in its maze of carved stone.

The terrace itself spans perhaps three hundred meters at its widest point, a kidney-shaped plateau of raw sienna rock shot through with veins of crystallized sulfur. The stone is young, volcanically speaking—its edges still hold the sharp angles of recent fracture, unweathered by time. Where the morning condensation pools in shallow depressions, the rock weeps a rusty mineral tea that evaporates by noon, leaving behind concentric rings of salt deposits like the growth rings of phantom trees.

No vegetation mars the geometric purity of this place. The builders, whoever they were, carved their settlement directly into the living rock with primitive copper chisels and stone hammers. The marks of their tools remain visible on every surface—patient, repetitive strikes that hollowed out chambers, corridors, and the strange hexagonal wells that plunge deep into the terrace's heart. The architecture follows no familiar logic: rooms open onto rooms that open onto voids, doorways frame nothing but sky, and staircases ascend to nowhere or descend into solid stone.

But it is the fauna that transforms this abandoned city into something otherworldly. They arrived after the builders vanished, drawn by the unnatural heat and the acoustic properties of the carved spaces. Thousands upon thousands of creatures—things that defy easy classification—have colonized every available surface. They resemble enormous barnacles crossed with paper wasps, their bodies a deep burgundy that matches the stone so perfectly they seem to grow from it. Each creature is no larger than a fist, but together they form vast colonies that carpet walls, ceilings, and floors in living tessellations.

The creatures generate the heat. Their metabolism runs so hot that touching one would blister human skin, yet they require almost no food—only the mineral salts left by evaporating condensation and something they extract from the air itself. They build no nests, lay no eggs, show no signs of reproduction. They simply are, have always been here as long as anyone can remember, will always be here as long as the stone endures.

Despite their overwhelming numbers, the terrace remains eerily quiet. The creatures communicate through heat patterns rather than sound—subtle temperature variations that ripple across the colonies like wind over grass. The only sounds are the whisper of morning condensation dripping into the wells and the occasional crack of thermal expansion in the stone. Even footsteps are muffled by the living carpet, absorbed into the mass of bodies that part reluctantly and close again behind any visitor.

The carved city exists in perfect stasis. The creatures neither grow nor diminish in number. The stone neither weathers nor crumbles despite the constant thermal stress. The condensation comes each dawn and vanishes each noon in an endless cycle. Time moves here, but nothing changes—as if the entire terrace exists in a single, eternal moment, a red-hot jewel box suspended between earth and sky, crowded with life yet empty of purpose, built by hands long vanished for reasons now unknowable.

At the terrace's eastern edge, where the plateau drops away in sheer cliffs, the morning mist rises from the badlands below and briefly transforms the vermillion stone into an island floating in clouds. For those few moments before the heat burns away the veil, it's possible to imagine the builders standing at this same precipice, copper tools in hand, looking out over a world that must have seemed equally strange to them—before they carved their inexplicable city and vanished, leaving only their precise tool marks and the perfect acoustic chambers that would, centuries later, call the heat-creatures home.
