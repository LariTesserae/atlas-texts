**The Cistern Quarter**

Deep beneath the mountain city, where the ancient builders first carved their refuge from stone, lies a forgotten district of water chambers. Here, at the roots of the settlement where few now venture, the mountain weeps eternally through hairline fissures worn smooth by millennia of seepage.

The space is cramped—a labyrinth of interconnected cisterns no larger than modest rooms, their curved ceilings barely high enough for a person to stand upright. Every surface glistens with moisture that has polished the limestone to a mirror sheen over countless ages. The walls bear the patient marks of primitive copper chisels, their patterns softened until they resemble the organic whorls of tree rings rather than human craft.

Water defines everything here. It beads on the ceiling, runs in silver threads down walls that have developed their own topography of mineral deposits—ridges and valleys in miniature, testament to water's patient sculpture. The floors are always wet, covered in a thin film that reflects the wan light filtering through ancient air shafts cut at precise angles to catch what little illumination reaches these depths.

The vegetation that manages to exist here is peculiar—pale, thread-like mosses that grow in dense mats where the walls meet the floor, feeding on minerals leached from the stone. Occasionally, a tendril of some cave-adapted vine snakes through a crack, its leaves reduced to translucent membranes that seem more water than plant.

The chambers press close, packed with the infrastructure of the old city's water system: stone channels worn paper-thin, cisterns within cisterns, aqueducts that thread between spaces barely wide enough to crawl through. Bronze fixtures, green with verdigris, mark where the ancient inhabitants once drew their water. Some still function, releasing slow drips that have worn perfect circular depressions in the stone below.

Sound here is muffled, absorbed by the water and stone. The dominant note is the patient *plink* of drops falling at intervals determined by temperature shifts far above—a liquid clock that has measured out epochs. Occasionally, water moves through hidden channels with a sound like whispered secrets, or a larger drip creates a hollow echo that reverberates through adjacent chambers.

The air is thick, almost drinkable—warm and humid enough that breathing feels like drawing in liquid. It carries the mineral taste of ancient stone, the faint sweetness of the pale mosses, and something else—a quality of age so profound it seems to have weight.

In the deepest chambers, where the city's builders first broke through into natural caverns, strange formations have grown. Not quite stalactites, not quite coral—structures that seem to have accreted from the marriage of human engineering and geological time. They crowd the spaces, leaving only narrow passages between their bulbous forms.

Life moves slowly here when it moves at all. Occasionally, a pale salamander slides through the shallows, or tiny crustaceans—colorless and nearly transparent—navigate the films of water with mechanical precision. But mostly, the cisterns exist in a state of dynamic stillness, where the only changes are the patient accumulation of minerals and the endless, circular journey of water from stone to air to stone again.

The few who know of this place speak of it as a kind of temporal eddy—a pocket where the mountain's youth and the city's beginning have been preserved in liquid amber, even as both have aged beyond recognition above. Here, in these cramped, weeping chambers, the first act of settlement continues its eternal performance, observed only by stone and water and time.
