The Weeping Plateau stretches beyond any horizon you could imagine reaching, a vast expanse of naked rock that sweats perpetually in the thin, frigid air. At this elevation—so high that the atmosphere itself seems to have forgotten how to hold warmth—water exists in impossible states, seeping from the stone itself as if the mountain were a living thing with pores. The rock is young, sharp-edged basalt that cuts through boots and gloves, its surfaces still bearing the columnar fractures of its volcanic birth. Every surface glistens with moisture that should be ice but isn't.

The sound hits you first and never stops. It's not wind, though wind exists here. It's the plateau itself—a deep, bone-deep resonance that comes from everywhere and nowhere, as if the entire continental shelf were a vast drum skin being struck by something unimaginably large beneath. The sound doesn't travel through air; it travels through the stone, through your bones, through the water droplets that hang impossibly in the air, neither falling nor rising.

Scattered across this immensity are the Stations. You might walk for days before encountering one—a squat industrial structure of corroded steel and concrete, each no larger than a warehouse, each identical to the last. They were built to harvest something from this place, though what exactly remains unclear. Their turbines still spin, drawing power from the temperature differential between the weeping rock and the frigid air. Inside, automated systems continue their work: condensers that pull water from stone, processors that separate minerals, broadcast arrays that send data to receivers that may no longer exist.

The vegetation that manages to exist does so in defiance of logic. Thin, wire-like lichens grow in perfect spirals on the wet rock faces, following mathematical patterns that hurt to look at directly. In the shallow pools that collect in the rock's depressions, translucent algae form geometric films that refract light into colors that shouldn't exist—ultraviolet made visible, infrared twisted into new spectrums.

Time moves strangely here. The Stations never decay further than they already have, their rust frozen at a specific stage of degradation. The water that seeps from the rocks never depletes, never increases. The few creatures that inhabit this place—pale, blind arthropods the size of dinner plates that navigate by vibration—move in perfect, repeating patterns, their paths traced in the thin mineral deposits they leave behind. When one dies, its body doesn't decompose but crystallizes, becoming part of the rock substrate.

The physics of the place feel... negotiable. Distance becomes unreliable at the plateau's scale. A Station visible on the horizon might take an hour to reach, or a week. The sound's volume doesn't follow inverse square laws—it's equally deafening everywhere, as if you're always standing at its source. Water flows uphill in certain places, following channels carved by some ancient process that left the rock riddled with precise, purposeful grooves.

In the brief moments when clouds part and sunlight strikes the plateau directly, the wet rocks create a million tiny rainbows that interfere with each other, producing standing waves of color that pulse with the omnipresent sound. The light reveals that the moisture isn't purely water—it's something more complex, something that carries charge and information, something that makes the air itself a kind of circuit.

The Stations were built to monitor this, perhaps to harness it. Their instruments still function, measuring resonances, recording patterns in the sound, tracking the slow migration of the arithmetic lichens. But their data streams flow into a network that extends beyond the plateau into territories where the breakdown of reality becomes complete—where the sound becomes visible, where the water remembers things that haven't happened yet, where the industrial architecture of the Stations reveals itself as part of some vast, incomprehensible machine that uses the entire plateau as a component.

This is a place of waiting, though for what remains unclear. The sound continues. The rocks weep. The Stations process their inputs and outputs in cycles that might be days or decades. And somewhere in the vast expanse, in the interference patterns between sound and stone and water, something that might be meaning accumulates, drop by patient drop.
