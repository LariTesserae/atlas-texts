The Hollow Terraces of Morne

High in the thinning atmosphere where stone meets void, the ancient city spreads across a vertical cliff face like a fossilized lung. Each terrace has been carved from the mountain itself over unknowable ages, creating a honeycomb of empty chambers that pierce deep into the rock. The stone is smooth as water-worn pebble, though no river has touched these heights for eons—only the patient condensation that beads and trickles down the walls in the cool mornings has shaped these surfaces, molecule by molecule, across deep time.

The city climbs impossibly high, each level connected by worn stone ladders and channels cut for the collection of dew. The original builders carved these waterways with simple bronze tools, their marks still visible but softened to mere suggestions by millennia of mineral accumulation. The channels converge into cisterns—perfect spheres hollowed from the bedrock where moisture gathers drop by precious drop.

But something is wrong with the geometry.

Walk from one terrace to another and you may find yourself arriving at your starting point from an unexpected angle. The carved doorways that should lead into simple chambers sometimes open onto vast hollows that couldn't possibly fit within the cliff. Distance flexes like a living thing. A chamber twenty paces across might take an hour to traverse, while the journey between distant terraces occasionally collapses into a single step.

The inhabitants have adapted. They are not quite what they were—whatever they originally were. Elongated beings with too many joints move through the terraces with deliberate slowness, their forms wavering at the edges like heat mirages despite the cool air. They tend the dew-channels with tools of hammered copper, maintaining the ancient infrastructure with movements that seem rehearsed across centuries. Their faces, when glimpsed, show expressions that don't quite resolve into meaning.

They are numerous but sparse, scattered through the hollow chambers like seeds in a dried pod. A hundred beings might occupy a terrace complex built for thousands. They cluster near the cisterns at dawn, extending cup-like appendages to catch the condensation that runs more freely here than physics should allow—water appearing from air too thin and dry to hold such moisture.

The sounds are subtle but wrong: the ping of droplets falling upward, the whisper of wind through passages that have no exit, the occasional grinding of stone against stone as the terraces slowly rearrange themselves over decades. Sometimes, rarely, a low harmonic thrumming rises from the deepest chambers—not quite music, not quite voice, not quite mechanical.

At the edges of perception, the solid rock sometimes reveals itself as something else: compressed time, crystallized probability, or the fossilized mathematics of space itself. The original builders discovered how to carve not just stone but the underlying structure of location. Their primitive tools—visible in small museums within certain chambers—seem impossibly inadequate for such work. Copper chisels and wooden mallets that somehow cut through the fabric of dimension itself.

The light here obeys most rules but not all. Shadows fall correctly until they don't. The sun reaches even the deepest terraces through shafts that bend and fold, carrying illumination through impossible angles. At certain times, chambers glow with their own soft phosphorescence, as if the stone itself remembers brighter days.

This is a place ground down to its essential nature—a city that has outlasted its own meaning, inhabited by creatures that may be descendants, invaders, or something generated by the architecture itself. The slow transformation continues: terraces gradually migrate across the cliff face, chambers open or seal themselves, and the patient collection of impossible dew sustains a life that shouldn't exist at this height, in this thinness, in this broken space.

The mountain's summit, glimpsed through the highest terraces, seems both infinitely distant and intimately close—a paradox the inhabitants navigate without concern, their elongated forms stretching between contradictions as they maintain their ancient, necessary work.
