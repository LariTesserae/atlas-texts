The abyssal plain stretches endlessly beneath three kilometers of black water, a primordial seabed that has known this darkness for aeons untold. Here, where the weight of the ocean presses down with crushing indifference, the seafloor has been ground to the finest sediment—a billion years of marine snow settled into perfect, suffocating smoothness.

Yet this ancient calm is betrayed by the water itself. Strange currents churn through the darkness, vast rivers of brine that flow beneath the sea, their different densities creating invisible waterfalls and rapids in the liquid dark. These underwater storms move with patient violence, picking up clouds of sediment that have lain undisturbed for millennia, creating blizzards of ancient mud that can last for months. The currents collide and spiral, forming temporary maelstroms that excavate great bowls in the seafloor before filling them again with their burden of primordial silt.

The darkness here is absolute, broken only by the ghost-light of primitive bioluminescence. Sparse forests of ancient tube worms cluster around the thermal seeps, their pale stalks swaying in the chaotic currents. Some have grown for thousands of years, their bases calcified into stone while their tips still filter the water for microscopic life. Between these oases, the seafloor is barren but for the tracks and burrows of things that move too slowly to be seen—creatures that measure their lives in centuries, crossing the abyssal plain on migrations that outlast civilizations.

The density here is not of objects but of time itself. Every cubic meter of water carries the weight of ages, saturated with dissolved minerals from epochs past. The pressure has compressed even the spaces between molecules until the water moves like syrup, carrying sounds as low thrums that travel for thousands of kilometers. When the deep currents collide, they create infrasonic booms that take hours to dissipate, vibrations that the sparse bottom-dwellers feel through their bones.

Occasionally, a carcass falls from above—a whale, perhaps, or something stranger—and the abyssal plain erupts into slow-motion frenzy. Hagfish emerge from the sediment in writhing knots. Six-gilled sharks materialize from the gloom. Giant isopods scuttle across the seafloor on legs like armored stilts. For weeks or months, this bounty transforms a small patch of the eternal desert into a feeding ground, until nothing remains but bones that will slowly dissolve into the sediment over the coming centuries.

The only signs of technology are the occasional tools of bone and stone, dropped by the deepest-diving hunters from the twilight waters far above—primitive harpoons and weighted lines that missed their mark and found their way to this deepest graveyard. They lie half-buried in the silt, slowly being incorporated into the seafloor's patient geology.

This is a place of paradox: ancient beyond measure yet constantly in flux, packed with the pressure of miles of water yet empty of all but the most patient life, silent but for the deep groans of shifting currents that speak in frequencies below human hearing. It is a landscape that exists at the very edge of what can be called a landscape at all—a place where the distinctions between solid and liquid, stillness and motion, life and death blur into the endless, churning dark.
