The Thermal Cascades rise from the abyssal dark like a mountain range made of heat and motion. Here, two miles beneath the surface of a warm ocean that has no name—for nothing with language has ever descended to map it—ancient volcanic ridges have weathered into something unprecedented. The seafloor buckles upward in vast terraces, each plateau a different depth, a different pressure, a different world.

The water itself has stratified over millions of years into distinct thermal layers that refuse to mix. The boundaries between them shimmer like vertical walls of glass, visible only where the temperature differential bends light into rippling curtains. These thermoclines cascade down the submerged mountainsides in slow-motion waterfalls, warm water pouring through cold, creating a constant roar that reverberates through the liquid medium—a sound so deep it feels more like pressure than noise, penetrating everything, making even the stones hum.

Industrial artifacts litter the ancient slopes, but not human industry. These are the factories of the fauna themselves—vast filtration systems built from secreted carbonate and volcanic glass, powered by the thermal gradients. Generations of colonial organisms have constructed these autonomous engines that process the mineral-rich water, extracting trace elements and energy. The machines have no moving parts visible to the eye, yet they thunder with internal flows, adding their voices to the overwhelming acoustic environment. Some are the size of buildings, their surfaces encrusted with newer, smaller versions built by descendant colonies, creating recursive architectures that spiral into fractal complexity.

The creatures here exist in staggering abundance, but scattered across the immense spaces between thermal zones. They cluster around the temperature barriers where the physics of the water creates opportunities—upwellings that bring nutrients, pressure differentials that can be harvested for locomotion, acoustic channels that carry vibrations for miles. Most have evolved beyond recognition as animals. They are living industrial processes themselves: assembly lines of tissue that construct offspring from filtered materials, biological turbines that spin in the convection currents, acoustic miners that use focused sound to shatter rock formations and extract minerals.

In the spaces between these biological factories, the water hangs empty and dark. The seafloor here is smooth, worn down by millions of years of thermal polishing, covered in a fine sediment of processed materials—the waste products of a billion generations of industrial metabolism. Strange currents writhe through these voids, visible only as distortions in the darkness, carrying heat and sound but no life.

The scale defies comprehension. A single thermal cascade might stretch for kilometers, its roar audible for dozens more. The eye—were any eye capable of perceiving in this darkness—could not encompass the whole of any single feature. Instead, the landscape reveals itself through sound and pressure, through the way water moves against skin or sensor, through the taste of minerals that changes with each thermal layer.

Time moves differently here. The industrial organisms operate on cycles measured in centuries, their constructions growing by microscopic increments. Yet the water itself pulses with rapid change—convection cells that form and collapse in minutes, acoustic storms that rage through the thermal barriers, chemical reactions that cascade through the ecosystem in waves of productivity and die-off. The mountain heights channel these forces, creating permanent zones of chaos where the different time scales collide.

At the peaks of the highest seamounts, where the pressure lessens just enough, the water occasionally flash-boils in pockets of superheated flow. These steam bubbles collapse instantly, sending shock waves through the water that sound like thunder. The industrial colonies have learned to harness these too—building pressure vessels that capture the moment of collapse, using it to power their most energy-intensive processes.

This is not a dead world, despite the absence of anything recognizable as vegetation. It is a world that has found a different solution to life—one based on gradients and flows rather than photosynthesis, on collective construction rather than individual growth. The darkness is not empty but full, saturated with sound and motion and the slow, inexorable creativity of organisms that build their own environment, piece by microscopic piece, across the incomprehensible spans of geological time.
