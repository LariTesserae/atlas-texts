The Tower Garden breathes.

Steam rises through the latticed floors, condensing on copper pipes that thread between levels like exposed veins. This is no natural mountain—it's a vertical factory of terraces, each platform barely twenty meters across, stacked impossibly high into warm clouds. The entire structure seems to pulse with internal moisture, water trickling through channels cut into the metal decking, dripping from level to level in precise patterns.

Every surface grows something. Mosses carpet the undersides of grated walkways. Vines snake around support pillars, their leaves unfurling in the humid air. Ferns burst from joints in the pipework. The vegetation follows no gardener's plan—it simply claims every gap the architecture allows, transforming industrial skeleton into vertical jungle. The plants grow fat and glossy in the perpetual warmth, their roots diving into hydroponic channels that run like artificial streams through each tier.

The machinery hums rather than roars. Pumps cycle water from collection pools back to the peak, where it begins its descent again through the growing medium. Fans turn lazily, circulating the thick air. Everything operates just below the threshold of true noise—a mechanical whisper that blends with the constant drip and trickle of water finding its way down.

At this height, clouds move through the structure. They enter the eastern openings as proper fog, dense and cool, then warm as they pass through the interior. By the time they exit the western side, they've become gauzy wisps, having left their moisture on leaves and metal alike. The boundary between inside and outside barely exists—the tower is more framework than enclosure, a scaffold for holding air and water and green life.

The few creatures here move secretly. A single bird—something between crow and parrot—has built its nest in a tangle of pipes near the peak. It rarely calls, moving instead in careful hops between platforms, feeding on the berries that grow wild among the intentional crops. Beetles work through the accumulated plant matter on lower levels, their shells the same verdigris as the oxidized copper.

New sections emerge even now. At the structure's edges, workers—or perhaps automated systems, it's hard to tell in the steam—weld additional platforms into place. The tower grows outward and upward simultaneously, each new section immediately colonized by the aggressive vegetation. Fresh metal gleams for only hours before moisture and spores begin their work.

The light filters green through leaves, bronze through the fog. Shadows stack and interweave as platforms block and reveal the sun. Time feels different here—measured not in hours but in the slow opening of flowers, the patient extension of new growth, the gradual oxidation of fresh surfaces.

This is architecture as ecosystem, industry as terrarium. The distinction between built and grown dissolves in the humid air. The tower makes its own weather, sustains its own small world, reaches ever higher into clouds it helps create. It is a machine for growing, growing itself, blurring every boundary between the made and the born.
