The basin breathes with steam and sound.

You descend through curtains of mist into a hollow perhaps forty paces across, where thermal springs have carved a natural amphitheater into the mountainside. The rocks weep constantly—not with rain but with condensation from the perpetual fog that pools here, trapped by the bowl's high walls. Everything glistens: the black volcanic stone, the thick moss that cushions every surface, the broad leaves of the vegetation that crowds the edges.

The greenery is almost obscene in its vigor. Vines cascade from the rim, their tendrils finding purchase in cracks worn smooth by centuries of moisture. Tree ferns spread their fronds in layered canopies, each catching and channeling the dripping water in different directions. The plants grow in distinct zones—those that can tolerate the scalding water cluster near the springs, their roots pale and swollen, while more familiar species keep to the cooler margins.

But it's the sound that overwhelms. The cacophony builds in layers: the base note of spring water gurgling up through fissures, the percussion of drops hitting leaves and stone at a thousand different pitches, and over it all, the voices of creatures. Frogs boom from hidden pools. Insects whir and drone in clouds so thick they form moving shadows in the mist. Birds call from the fern canopy—not songs but sharp territorial barks and long, liquid warnings. Something large disturbs the water periodically with heavy splashes.

The heat presses close despite the elevation. This pocket catches warmth from deep earth and holds it like cupped hands. The air tastes mineral-rich and green. Your clothes grow heavy with moisture within minutes.

Movement everywhere: droplets racing down leaves, insects dancing in columns of steam, small shapes darting between the ferns. A troop of something—monkeys perhaps, though the mist makes it hard to tell—crashes through the canopy, sending cascades of collected water earthward. They're gone before you can focus, but their passage sets off chains of alarm calls that ripple outward through the basin.

The stones show their age in their smoothness, in the way they've settled into each other like old friends. No sharp edges remain. The spring-carved pools have achieved their final forms, each with its own ecosystem of pale crustaceans and temperament of bubbling. This place has found its rhythm over centuries—the daily cycle of mist condensing and evaporating, the seasonal fluctuations in water flow, the eternal negotiation between stone and spring.

Near the largest pool, you notice crude markings on a boulder—not writing but deliberate scratches that might mark depth or season or something else entirely. A few rocks have been moved to form a rough path to the water's edge. Someone has been here, perhaps regularly, but they've left little trace beyond these small conveniences.

The fauna density becomes almost oppressive as your senses adjust. Every surface hosts something: beetles mining the moss, centipedes flowing like water across the wet stone, tree crabs clicking in the ferns. Bats explode from a crevice at your approach, their wings beating the mist into swirls. You realize the largest sounds come from above—a troop of howler monkeys has claimed the trees overhanging the basin, their territorial calls echoing off the natural walls until the sound seems to come from the earth itself.

As evening approaches (though it's hard to tell in the perpetual dim), the chorus shifts but never quiets. Night hunters replace day foragers. The mist glows faintly where fireflies gather in the rising thermals. Something large and patient settles into the main pool with a sigh of displaced water.

This is a place of abundance constrained—all the wet world's fertility compressed into one small bowl, life layered upon life until the very air swims with it.
