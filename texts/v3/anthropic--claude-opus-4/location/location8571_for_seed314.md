The Mineral Metropolis spreads across the highland plateau like a vast circuit board of crystalline towers and metallic veins. No water has touched this place for millions of years—perhaps never. The city grows from the mountain itself, its buildings not built but extruded from the bedrock through industrial processes that blur the line between geology and architecture.

At this elevation, thin atmosphere carries a perpetual coolness that seeps through every surface. The air tastes of copper and ozone. Sound travels strangely here—the constant low hum of extraction engines arrives muffled and directionless, while the sharp crack of splitting stone rings clear across kilometers. Most of the time, though, an eerie quiet prevails, broken only by the whisper of mineral dust settling.

The creatures here defy easy classification. They might be animals, or machines, or something between. Hexagonal beetles with tungsten shells cluster on transformer towers, feeding on electrical discharge. Ferromagnetic eels swim through veins of liquid metal that run beneath the streets. Silicate birds with wings of mica flakes roost in the angles where buildings meet sky, their bodies clicking and scraping as they preen.

Every surface crawls with activity. The buildings themselves grow upward at glacial pace, their crystalline facades constantly sprouting new protrusions, balconies, antenna arrays. Industrial boring machines the size of whales tunnel through the living rock, following seams of rare minerals, leaving behind perfectly smooth tunnels that the city immediately claims and lines with infrastructure.

The density is overwhelming. There is no empty space—even the air between structures fills with catwalks, cable systems, pneumatic tubes, and the ever-present dust of industrial processing. Layers stack upon layers: sub-surface mining galleries give way to processing floors, which support residential matrices, which bear the weight of factory levels, topped by communications arrays that pierce the thin atmosphere.

Strange physics govern here. Certain minerals exhibit properties unheard of elsewhere—some generate their own gravity fields, creating pockets where up and down lose meaning. Others produce light without heat, casting the city in cold glows of amber, violet, and jade. The boundary between living creature and mineral formation blurs; some of the fauna may simply be very active rocks, while certain buildings show behaviors suspiciously like metabolism.

The young landscape still bears its sharp edges. Cliff faces show fresh fracture patterns. The plateau's rim drops away in severe scarps unmarked by erosion. Even the city's oldest structures—if age means anything in a place where buildings grow rather than decay—maintain their precise angles and keen edges. Time moves differently here, measured not in water's patient work but in the accumulation of industrial sediment and the slow transformation of the mountain into metropolis.

At the scale of continents, weather systems form within the city itself. Hot air rising from the deep extraction vents meets the cool plateau atmosphere, creating localized storms of metallic dust. These storms spiral between the towers, their particles catching light and throwing it back in sheets of glittering interference. Some districts exist in perpetual micro-climates—zones of electromagnetic winter where nothing organic can survive, or thermal oases where the strange fauna cluster in writhing abundance.

The deeper you go, the harder it becomes to distinguish city from mountain, industry from ecology, built from born. Perhaps there never was a difference. Perhaps this is simply what happens when a planet's minerals achieve sufficient complexity—they organize themselves into patterns we can only recognize as urban, industrial, alive.
