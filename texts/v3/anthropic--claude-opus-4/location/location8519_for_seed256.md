The valley stretches wide beneath a permanent ceiling of low, slate-colored clouds, their bellies heavy with moisture that never quite commits to rain. Instead, the wetness arrives in other ways—as morning mists that roll up from the river's braided channels, as condensation beading on every surface by midday, as the barely perceptible drizzle that begins each evening like clockwork and continues through the night.

The cold here has a particular quality, the kind that seeps rather than bites. It works its way through layers, settling into bones and joints with patient persistence. The thermometers at the old monitoring stations, those still functioning, hover just above freezing—warm enough for the vegetation to thrive in its strange abundance, cold enough that breath always shows white in the thick air.

Moss grows on everything. Not just the ordinary moss of temperate forests, but great cushioning mounds of it, some reaching waist-height, carpeting the valley floor in undulating waves of green and grey-green and yellow-green. The older growths have developed woody cores, becoming something between moss and shrub. Ferns unfurl from every crevice—tree ferns with trunks like scaly columns, ground ferns that spread in vast colonial networks, tiny ferns that cling to the rusted frames of abandoned machinery. Club mosses stand in miniature forests. Horsetails cluster around the edges of countless pools and seeps, their segmented stems reaching heights that would have been common millions of years ago.

The river doesn't so much flow through the valley as braid and rebraid itself across the bottomland, constantly shifting its channels through the soft, peaty soil. The water runs dark with tannins, the color of over-steeped tea. Where the current slows, surfaces shimmer with the iridescent film of natural oils released by decomposing vegetation. The river makes a sound like industrial machinery—not the rush of mountain streams but a deeper, mechanical churning as it works through its meandering courses, undermining banks, repositioning gravel bars, maintaining the valley's ancient rhythms.

The loudness comes from this water-work, but also from the infrastructure that dots the valley—though "dots" suggests more organization than exists. A hydroelectric turbine still spins in a concrete housing near the valley's eastern edge, its generator long since seized but its blades turning uselessly, screaming a high metallic note that carries for miles. Somewhere upstream, a logging flume collapsed decades ago, and now its timbers knock against each other in the current like a massive wooden drum kit playing an endless, arrhythmic song.

The industrial remnants appear without pattern or clear purpose. A single smokeless chimney rises from a fern grove, built of precisely laid brick now furred with moss. Rail tracks emerge from the peat for twenty meters before disappearing again. Concrete foundations mark rectangles in the vegetation—some vast as warehouse footprints, others barely larger than sheds. Pipes of various diameters breach the surface at seemingly random points, some still carrying water from unknown sources to unknown destinations, gurgling and clanking with thermal expansion and contraction.

There's a turbine hall still mostly intact, its roof partially caved but its walls standing. Inside, massive generators sit in rows, their housings split open by the patient work of fern roots. The control panels still have their gauges, needles frozen at their last readings. The floor is ankle-deep in water that rises and falls with the valley's water table. When wind pushes through the broken windows—and there is always wind here, funneled by the valley walls—the structure moans and whistles, adding its voice to the constant industrial chorus.

The fauna, what little exists, has adapted to the noise. A species of newt lives in the mechanical pools, timing its mating calls to the gaps between the turbine's shrieks. Small, dark birds nest in the pipes, their songs incorporating the metallic echoes into complex territorial calls. Once, someone claims to have seen a deer moving through the ferns at dawn, but this was years ago and might have been shadow.

Time moves differently here—not faster or slower, but thicker. Changes accumulate like the moss itself, in barely perceptible layers. A fallen tree trunk becomes a nurse log, then a linear garden, then a raised ridge of richer soil, then gradually subsides back to the valley floor. The industrial structures don't so much decay as transform, their hard edges softening under the patient ministry of moss and moisture. Metal rusts through, concrete spalls and cracks, wood delaminates—but slowly, so slowly that a casual observer might visit years apart and notice no difference, while someone who stays and watches closely can see the daily work of transformation in every bead of condensation, every new fern frond, every grain of rust that falls.

The valley holds its own weather. Even when the surrounding highlands see sun, the clouds here barely thin. The mist rises and falls on its own schedule. The cold maintains itself through the evaporation of endless moisture. It's a place that has found its balance—not stasis, but a steady state of slow exchange between the built and the grown, the mechanical and the vegetal, the loud and the patient, all mediated by the constant presence of water in all its forms.
