The Abyssal Plateau stretches beneath three kilometers of black water, a drowned continent whose mountains have worn down to gentle swells across thousands of millennia. Here, where sunlight has never reached, the seafloor extends beyond any creature's ability to perceive its boundaries—a terrain so vast it generates its own deep-water weather systems, currents that spiral for decades before dissipating.

The darkness is absolute, yet not empty. Bioluminescent snow falls upward here, each flake a colony of primitive organisms that glow with cold blue fire as they rise through the water column. They ascend in great sheets and curtains, creating false auroras in the liquid night. When they reach a certain pressure threshold—about a kilometer up—they burst in silent explosions of light, releasing spores that drift back down to begin again. This eternal cycle has continued for millions of years.

The sound is overwhelming, though not from any living thing. The plateau resonates with the planet's deep harmonics—tectonic songs that travel through water more efficiently than air. Low-frequency waves, below human hearing but powerful enough to liquify soft tissue, roll across the benthos in visible distortions. Where these standing waves intersect, the water itself begins to behave strangely. Physics stutters.

In these interference zones, water flows in impossible directions. Currents split into probability streams—a single flow becoming multiple potential paths that exist simultaneously until some unknowable quantum moment collapses them back into one. Time moves differently in these pockets. A particle of sediment might age a million years while drifting past, emerging fossilized on the other side of a current barely ten meters wide.

The sparse vegetation consists of things that perhaps shouldn't be called plants at all. Kelp-like structures grow in mathematical spirals, their fronds extending into dimensions that fold back on themselves. They photosynthesize darkness itself, drawing energy from the absence of light. Where they cluster, usually kilometers apart, they create pockets of even deeper black—negative space that hurts to perceive even in perfect darkness. Their roots, if they can be called roots, don't anchor in sediment but in the statistical probability of location itself.

Ancient erosion has worn the landscape into something approaching geometric perfection. Hills rise and fall in sine waves. Valleys follow logarithmic spirals. The seafloor itself seems to be approaching some mathematical ideal, as if the planet is slowly calculating itself into a final form. In places, the sediment has compressed into sheets of metallic glass, mirrors that reflect nothing in the darkness but somehow still gleam with theoretical light.

The cold here is profound—water held just above freezing by pressure and saline content. Ice forms in ways that shouldn't be possible: crystalline structures that grow through time rather than space, glaciers that exist in parallel moments, frozen waterfalls that fall upward through layers of temporal gradient.

Weather systems move through this liquid atmosphere with deliberate patience. Storms of heavy water sink through layers of lighter brine, creating underwater rain. Pressure fronts collide and merge, spawning vortexes that can persist for centuries. In the vast distances, these systems develop their own characteristics—some storms have been circling for so long they've worn grooves in reality itself, channels where the water flows slightly outside normal spacetime.

Where the plateau meets even deeper trenches, the breakdown of reality becomes more pronounced. The edges fray into probability foam—locations that exist in superposition, simultaneously present and absent. Ships that sink here don't reach bottom but become smeared across possibility space, their iron slowly rusting in all potential futures at once.

The deafening sound-that-is-not-sound builds in these places. It's the noise of space itself under strain, reality creaking under the weight of too much time and pressure. Sometimes it harmonizes with itself, creating nodes of perfect silence that drift across the seafloor like bubbles of anti-sound. Where they pass, even the quantum foam briefly stills.

This is a place at the end of its own becoming, where the universe has worn thin through sheer duration. The sparse landscape holds the weight of eons in its emptiness, each feature separated by distances that mock comprehension. It's actively dying and actively eternal, a seafloor that has outlasted its own meaning and continues anyway, computing itself into ever-more-perfect mathematical forms while reality stutters and breaks around its edges.

In the endless dark water above, nothing swims. Nothing lives. Only the luminous snow rises and falls, marking time in a place that has nearly forgotten what time means.
