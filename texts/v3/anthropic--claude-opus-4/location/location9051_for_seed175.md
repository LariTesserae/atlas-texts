The Clouded Plateaus of the Thunderworks stretch endlessly across half a world, their wet stone surfaces perpetually darkened by the shadow of their own weather. Here, at an elevation where the air should be thin, it hangs thick and heavy with moisture, pregnant with the constant threat of storms that never quite arrive but never quite depart.

The rock itself weeps. Ancient basalt flows, weathered smooth over eons, have developed a peculiar porosity that draws groundwater up through microscopic channels, creating surfaces that glisten perpetually with a thin film of moisture. This geological quirk feeds the explosion of vegetation that carpets every horizontal surface and clings to every vertical face—vast meadows of electric moss that grow in thick, spongy mats, their blue-green surfaces crackling faintly with bioelectric discharge. Towering fern-trees rise from crevasses where water collects, their fronds heavy with condensation that drips in a constant, quiet percussion against the stone.

But it is the sound that dominates this place—a crushing, omnipresent roar that has continued without pause for ten thousand years. Deep beneath the plateau's surface, vast natural caverns form a planet-spanning network of resonance chambers. The temperature differential between the cool surface and the geothermally heated depths creates a permanent atmospheric engine, pulling air through these chambers in a continuous, deafening drone. The sound is not violent or sharp but massive—a fundamental tone so low it is felt as much as heard, accompanied by harmonics that shift subtly with barometric pressure and the turning of seasons.

The few who have studied this place (from pressurized observatories that block the sound) call it the Breathing of the Stone. The continuous airflow through the cavern system has carved them into perfect acoustic instruments over millions of years, each chamber tuned by erosion to contribute its voice to the world-song. The sound emerges from blowholes scattered across the plateau—some no larger than a fist, others gaping chasms a kilometer wide—creating localized weather patterns where warm, moist air meets the cool surface atmosphere.

This is no place for animal life. The constant sound pressure would liquify the delicate structures of most inner ears within hours. Even the plants have adapted—the ferns communicate through chemical signals carried on the eternal wind, and the electric moss forms vast neural networks that pulse with light, creating patterns that sweep across mountain-spanning meadows like thoughts across a dreaming mind.

The industrial technology here is not human-made but geological—a planetary-scale engine of sound and air and water that runs on nothing but temperature gradients and time. Automated monitoring stations, built to withstand the acoustic assault, dot the landscape like metal flowers, their instruments recording the subtle variations in the world-tone that predict weather patterns half a continent away. Their solar panels stay dim under the perpetual overcast, supplemented by thermoelectric generators that tap the temperature differential the same way the planet itself does.

In certain places where multiple blowholes converge, interference patterns in the sound create pockets of relative quiet—eye-of-the-storm spaces where the moss grows differently, forming spiral patterns, and the perpetual mist takes on curious behaviors, forming standing vortices that persist for centuries. These nodes shift over geological time as erosion subtly retunes the vast instrument below, but for any given human lifetime, they remain fixed—points of reference in an otherwise featureless expanse of wet stone and moss and mist.

The water cycle here is entirely local. What falls as rain is drawn up through the weeping rock, transpired by the vegetation, and condensed again in the cool air, never traveling more than a few kilometers before beginning the cycle anew. The plateau is spotted with shallow pools where the rock dips, none deeper than a meter, their surfaces rippling constantly from the acoustic pressure, creating complex standing wave patterns that never repeat but never truly change.

This is a landscape of deep time made audible, where the planet's own processes have achieved a kind of equilibrium that excludes all but the most patient forms of life. It is neither hostile nor welcoming—it simply is, humming its eternal note into a gray and green infinity, utterly indifferent to any scale of time shorter than geological epochs.
