The Singing Seamount rises through azure depths, its summit plateau suspended forty meters beneath the surface—a drowned cathedral of young volcanic stone where reality has begun to fray.

The water here is wrong. It moves in impossible directions, forming standing waves that spiral upward without breaking, creating hollow tubes of air that persist for minutes before collapsing. These water-walls rise and fall in patterns that follow no earthly physics, sometimes crystallizing into sheets of suspended liquid that hang like curtains before dissolving back into the surrounding sea. Through these anomalies, glimpses appear—other oceans, perhaps, or the same ocean at different times, layered and bleeding through.

The seamount itself is sharp-edged basalt, its surfaces still bearing the fresh scars of recent emergence. Knife-ridged walls and angular terraces form a natural amphitheater perhaps two hundred meters across. The stone hums with a frequency that makes the water itself sing—a deafening chorus of harmonics that builds and crashes in waves more violent than any storm. The sound comes from everywhere and nowhere, as if the sea itself has learned to scream.

Sparse kelp forests cling to the edges where reality remains stable enough for life. These plants grow in stuttering bursts—a frond might age years in seconds, then revert to a spore, then bloom again. Their holdfasts grip stone that sometimes isn't there, roots extending into spaces the eye can't follow. Between these temporal forests, the seafloor is bare rock scattered with impossible geometries—coral formations that grow in Möbius strips, sea fans that exist in more than three dimensions.

The warmth here defies depth. Thermal vents would explain it, but the heat comes from the water itself, which sometimes glows with bioluminescence that has no source. In certain spots, the water becomes something else entirely—a medium thicker than mercury or thinner than air, where swimming becomes flying becomes drowning becomes something without a name.

A few structures mark human attempt to understand this place. Measurement stations, now defunct, spiral around their own anchors in temporal loops. A single research platform, built into a natural overhang, flickers between states—sometimes new, sometimes decades rusted, sometimes just a blueprint of light in the water. The equipment is minimal, pre-digital—mechanical depth gauges that spin backward, simple cameras whose film develops memories that haven't happened yet.

Creatures here are few and far between, evolved for paradox. Cephalopods phase through solid rock, their bodies existing in probability clouds rather than fixed forms. Fish swim in schools that number differently depending on the angle of observation. A single eel, ancient and patient, threads through time-streams, its body entering Tuesday and emerging Thursday, hunting prey that hasn't been born.

The dim light filters down through layers of contradiction. Sunbeams bend at angles that create shadows brighter than their sources. Bioluminescent blooms paint the water in colors that don't exist, while darkness pools in floating spheres that drift between the singing stones.

At the seamount's heart, where the sound reaches frequencies that tear at the fabric of space, there's a depression in the rock—perfectly circular, perfectly new despite the age of the stone around it. The water above it stands still while chaos reigns everywhere else, a column of absolute silence in the deafening chorus. Sometimes, looking into it shows not the shallow bowl carved in basalt, but depths that continue down beyond the seafloor, beyond the mantle, into spaces where water and fire and void become one.

The entire tableau shifts and reforms constantly. Stone walls melt and resolidify. The scale itself breathes—sometimes the mount seems vast as a city, sometimes small enough to hold in cupped palms. Only the sound remains constant, building toward crescendos that never quite arrive, promising a transformation that always lurks one moment in the future.

This is a place between states—between air and water, between creation and destruction, between the laws of nature and their dissolution. It sings of what the ocean might become when physics fails, when the boundaries between possible and impossible grow thin as the film of a bubble, ready to pop.
