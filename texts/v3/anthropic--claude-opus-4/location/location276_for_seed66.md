The Weeping Terraces of Khet-Maral stretch across the highland plateau like a vast amphitheater of stone and steam, each level carved from the mineral crust that formed here millions of years ago. Water seeps constantly through the porous volcanic glass that makes up these ancient formations, creating a perpetual mist that hangs in the warm air. The moisture condenses on every surface—on the obsidian-black walls of the terraces, on the copper pipes that snake between levels, on the backs of the countless creatures that have made this place their metropolis.

No plants grow here. The black stone supports nothing green, nothing that reaches toward the sun. Instead, the city feeds on mineral deposits and the chemical energy trapped in the warm springs that bubble up through cracks in the plateau. Industrial processors hum constantly, extracting nutrients from the stone itself, breaking down silicates into edible compounds through processes that blur the line between digestion and manufacturing.

The creatures—they are everywhere. Millions upon millions of them, packed into this vertical city like cells in a hive. They resemble armored pillbugs the size of house cats, their segmented bodies gleaming with condensation, their dozens of legs clicking against the wet stone as they move in endless processions up and down the terraces. Some subspecies have evolved specialized castes: workers with reinforced mandibles for cutting stone, carriers with flattened backs for hauling materials, processors with enlarged guts full of acid for breaking down minerals.

Their city rises in tiers, each terrace supporting clusters of hexagonal structures built from secreted stone paste—a mixture of dissolved minerals and bodily fluids that hardens into something like concrete. Factories nest within these structures, their biological machinery processing raw stone into food, into building material, into the fuel that powers their simple engines. Steam vents from these facilities mix with the natural mist, creating a warm fog that never fully clears.

The sound is constant—not quite a roar, but a deep thrumming made of countless individual noises. The click of millions of legs on stone. The hiss of steam engines. The grinding of mandibles against rock. The gurgle of water flowing through the carved channels that direct the seepage into useful streams. Beneath it all, a low vibration rises from the industrial hearts of the processing facilities, where massive grindstones turned by creature-power reduce basalt to powder.

The terraces themselves show their age in their worn edges, their surfaces polished smooth by millions of years of water flow and billions of passing feet. The creatures have been here nearly as long, their city growing slowly, terrace by terrace, as they carved deeper into the plateau. Old sections crumble and are rebuilt. Ancient factories are decommissioned and converted into nurseries or storage. The city renews itself constantly, but at a pace measured in centuries.

At the highest levels, where the air grows thin and the mist freezes into perpetual frost, observation towers rise—twisted spires of secreted stone where specialized castes with enlarged eyes watch the sky for threats. The plateau drops away on all sides into cloud-shrouded depths. Other cities might exist down there, other colonies of these industrious creatures, but the mist obscures everything beyond the edge.

The light here is pearl-gray and constant, filtered through the eternal fog. Day and night bring only subtle changes in brightness. The creatures navigate more by vibration and chemical signals than by sight. Their factories run continuously, their society never sleeping, their exploitation of this mineral world as patient and inexorable as erosion itself.

Water drips. Engines hum. Millions of bodies move with purpose through their vertical city, each individual a part of something vast and ancient and strange—a civilization that learned to eat stone and breathe steam, that built its metropolis in a place where nothing green would ever grow.
