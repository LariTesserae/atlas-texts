The Underglass Markets pulse with fitful life beneath the frozen city above. Here, in carved-out caverns just below the permafrost line, a strange economy thrives in perpetual fluorescent twilight.

Water beads constantly on the low stone ceilings, condensing from the warmth of human activity below meeting the cold seeping down from above. It drips in irregular rhythms onto the market floors, creating networks of shallow channels that merchants have learned to work around, their stalls raised on metal stilts or plastic pallets. The air tastes metallic and damp, like breathing inside a refrigerator that's been left open too long.

The vegetation is wrong. That's the first thing visitors notice. In this sunless place, plants grow in profusion—but they grow pale and twisted, feeding on the chemical runoff from the factories above. Ghostly ferns unfurl from cracks in the walls, their fronds a sickly luminescent white. Mushrooms the size of umbrellas cluster around the support pillars, their caps pulsing with bioluminescent patterns that merchants use to advertise their wares. Thick carpets of phosphorescent moss creep across abandoned stalls, slowly digesting the wood and metal, turning the market into something half-alive.

The space itself is intimate despite its industrial bones—a single vast cavern subdivided by curtains of hanging plastic, salvaged chain-link, and walls of stacked shipping containers. Old mining equipment has been repurposed into market infrastructure: ore carts serve as mobile shops, conveyor belts carry goods between levels, and massive ventilation fans struggle to move the thick air. Everything hums with barely-contained energy—generators sputter, refrigeration units wheeze, neon signs flicker and buzz.

The market never sleeps but it never quite wakes either. Vendors appear and vanish according to no posted schedule, setting up elaborate displays of black-market electronics, hybrid vegetables that grow without sun, or mysterious machinery salvaged from the factories above. The crowds surge and thin unpredictably—sometimes the narrow aisles are choked with buyers haggling in a dozen languages, other times you might walk for twenty minutes seeing only the occasional scavenger picking through abandoned stalls.

The light is harsh and artificial, a mixture of industrial fluorescents, LED strips, and the organic glow of the mutant flora. Shadows are either knife-sharp or non-existent. There is no gentle transition here, no dawn or dusk—only the sudden darkness when a generator fails, followed by the cursing of vendors as they scramble for backup power.

Above it all, you can hear the factories—not directly, but as a vibration that comes down through the rock, a constant industrial heartbeat that makes the water drops fall in patterns, that makes the moss pulse in sympathy. Sometimes there are other sounds: the skitter of pale rats that have never seen daylight, the hiss of steam from broken pipes, the distant echo of arguments in the deeper tunnels where even the market vendors don't go.

The Underglass Markets exist in a state of constant reinvention. Stalls collapse under the weight of growing mushrooms. New vendors tunnel in from unexpected directions. Entire sections flood when the pumps fail, only to be drained and recolonized within days. It's a place of desperate commerce and strange beauty, where the human need for trade and connection has created its own ecosystem in the bones of a dying industrial age. The cold keeps everything on the edge of preservation, the moisture keeps everything on the edge of decay, and in between, life—strange, persistent, and relentlessly commercial—finds a way to flourish.
