The Weeping Galleries rise from saturated earth like the ribs of some vast, drowned cathedral. These are not natural formations but carved spaces—a honeycomb of chambers and passages hewn from pale limestone that sweats constantly in the cool air. Water beads and runs down every surface, following channels worn smooth by countless years of the same patient trickle.

The settlement fills these cavities completely, pressed tight as teeth. Platforms of salvaged stone jut from the walls at every height, connected by ladders of knotted rope and carved handholds worn smooth as river stones. The spaces between are narrow—sometimes barely wide enough for shoulders to pass—and the sound of water dropping, running, pooling echoes endlessly through the maze. Tin cups hang beneath the most reliable drips. Clay vessels cluster where the flow is steadiest.

Everything here is damp to the touch. The stone platforms where people sleep are covered in thick mats of woven reed that must be wrung out each morning. Tools are simple—stone scrapers, bone awls, copper knives green with verdigris. What wood exists has been carried from far away and is precious, kept wrapped in oiled cloth.

The density is overwhelming. Families live separated by nothing more than a hanging cloth, if that. The sound never stops—water falling, voices echoing, the scrape of stone on stone as someone grinds grain in the pre-dawn darkness. Children learn early to move sideways through the passages, to duck beneath the eternal drips, to recognize whose cough echoes from three galleries away.

In the lower chambers, the water gathers in pools that have never been dry. These are the common spaces, where the settlement's life concentrates. Stone steps descend into the water at careful intervals. Here the inhabitants wash their few possessions, fill their vessels, perform the small rituals that mark the passage of days in this changeless place. The pools are perfectly still except when disturbed, their surfaces like dark mirrors reflecting the carved ceiling.

The only life here is what the people bring—themselves, the dried fungi they cultivate in the dampest corners, the blind fish that swim in the deepest pools. Sometimes a child finds a fossil in the limestone, the spiral of something that lived when this stone was mud at the bottom of an ancient sea. These are kept as curiosities, polished smooth by handling.

The air tastes of minerals and breath. In the tightest passages, one must turn sideways and exhale to pass. The stone is cold against the skin, always slick with moisture. Sound carries strangely—a whisper in one chamber might be heard clearly three galleries away, while a shout in another dies immediately in the thick air.

There is no weather here, no seasons. The temperature never changes. The water seeps at the same rate it has seeped for generations. The only rhythm is human—sleeping, waking, the slow work of maintaining life in this mineral womb. New chambers are rarely carved; the old ones are sufficient, worn smooth by countless bodies, shaped by use into something almost organic despite their geometric origins.

At the edges of the settled area, the galleries continue into darkness. These empty chambers echo with the sound of dripping water and nothing else. Sometimes children dare each other to venture into these spaces, but never far. The darkness there is complete, and the sound of one's own breathing becomes enormous in the silence between drops.

This is a place of compression and patience, where life has learned to flow like water into every available space, where the boundary between the built environment and the bodies that inhabit it has worn thin through proximity and time. The Weeping Galleries endure, neither growing nor shrinking, while generation after generation learns the particular rhythm of moving through spaces barely larger than themselves, accompanied always by the sound of water finding its way down through stone.
