The Benthos of Forever stretches endlessly beneath kilometers of crystalline water, a drowned continent where ancient forests still grow. Here, in the perpetual noon of this submarine world, colossal kelp-analogues rise from valleys that once knew wind. Their fronds, each the size of city blocks, drift in slow-motion currents that take months to complete their circuits.

The cold presses with patient weight. Two degrees above freezing, the water holds its chill like a monument to some long-ago catastrophe that sank this land. Yet life thrives—not in spite of the submersion, but because of it. The vegetation has evolved beyond its terrestrial ancestors, developing translucent tissues that channel the brilliant light filtering from above. Ghostly pale trunks support canopies of glass-green that photosynthesize in ways unknown to dry land. Between these titans grow smaller species: carpets of phosphorescent moss, flowering vines that release clouds of glowing pollen into the currents, ferns with fronds like optical fiber.

The distances between these submarine forests are vast. Travel for days through the bright water and you'll cross abyssal meadows where only low, creeping plants manage to colonize the sediment. The sparseness creates cathedral-like spaces, halls of water hundreds of meters high where single organisms become landmarks. A tree here. A coral-structure there. Each separated by enough space that they develop their own micro-ecosystems, their own small weather systems of current and temperature.

And everywhere, the fauna. Schools of creatures move like living storms through the canopy—millions of individuals creating their own turbulence patterns. Some have evolved from fish, others from birds that chose water over air when their world drowned. The largest are the filter-feeders, behemoths the size of buildings that drift through the forest passages, their movements so slow they seem like geology rather than biology. Smaller species fill every niche: grazers strip the eternal growth from plants, predators flash through the shadows between trunks, and countless invertebrates process the endless rain of organic matter that falls from the canopy.

The industrial technology reveals itself subtly—not in structures, but in the fauna themselves. Many species sport mechanical augmentations that have become hereditary, passed down through generations until biology and machinery merged. A school of fish might suddenly coordinate into a perfect grid, processing water through genetically-integrated filters, cleaning the sea with industrial efficiency. Great arthropods carry fusion cores in their shells, powering ecosystems around thermal vents of their own creation. The boundary between natural and artificial dissolved centuries ago in this flooded world.

Sound barely travels here. The water muffles everything to a distant whisper. Even the massive movements of the mega-fauna create only the faintest vibrations. The industrial processes hum at frequencies too low for most ears—felt rather than heard. Only occasionally does a clear note ring out: the song of something vast and far away, or the crack of breaking wood as an ancient giant finally succumbs to time.

Nothing changes. The same currents have traced the same paths for millennia. The forests grow at precisely the rate they decay. The fauna populations remain in perfect balance, their industrial symbionts ensuring no resource ever depletes. Even the light—brilliant and sourceless, perhaps from some bioluminescent layer far above or from the integrated technology of the ecosystem itself—never varies. No storms disturb this place. No seasons mark the passage of time.

This is a world in perfect stasis, drowned but not dead, where evolution has reached an equilibrium so complete that past and future have become meaningless concepts. The Benthos of Forever exists in an eternal present, bright and cold and teeming with life that will never change.
