The Ossuary Plateaus rise like ancient vertebrae from the world's spine, their pale surfaces catching the weak light that filters through perpetually churning air. These are not mountains of stone but of bone—vast architectural accumulations that stretch across the horizon in terraced formations, each level representing millennia of construction by creatures whose forms defy easy classification.

The cold here has a particular quality, not the wet chill of snow but the dry, penetrating cold of calcium exposed to altitude. The air is thin and tastes of minerals, carrying with it the constant sound of settling—a symphony of clicks, groans, and hollow resonances as the bone structures adjust to temperature changes and the weight of their own existence. This cacophony never ceases, creating a background roar punctuated by the sharper notes of active construction.

Life teems here, though water exists only in the most marginal sense—trapped in microscopic pockets within the bone matrix, or carried in the specialized organs of the fauna. The builders themselves are everywhere: creatures like articulated chandeliers of cartilage and horn, their dozen limbs ending in tools of their own growing. They harvest bone from their own bodies and from designated groves where calcium-secreting organisms cluster in sparse copses, their white branches indistinguishable from the constructed landscape until one notices their slow, tidal breathing.

These bone-trees, if they can be called that, grow in mathematical patterns across the plateaus, their placement following rules understood only by the builders. They photosynthesize not with water but with the minerals drawn up through the bone substrate, their roots penetrating deep into the marrow-channels that run like veins through the entire structure. Their leaves are translucent scales that rattle in the constant winds, adding their voice to the ambient din.

The builders work ceaselessly, following behavioral patterns encoded in their biology. They gnaw sections from the trees, from the landscape itself, from specialized growth-chambers in their own bodies, shaping each piece with teeth that can modulate their hardness and cutting angle. The pieces are then mortared with a paste secreted from glands—a substance that hardens stronger than the original bone, creating joints that sing different notes as the wind passes through them.

The entire landscape is thus both organism and architecture, city and ecosystem. Ancient sections show the weathering of ages—surfaces polished smooth by wind and the passage of countless feet, edges rounded, the original construction patterns softened but never erased. Newer additions gleam white-yellow, their surfaces still rough with tool marks, not yet worn into harmony with their surroundings.

Travelers through this region report a peculiar disorientation. The lack of water removes familiar reference points—no streams to follow, no green to rest the eye upon. Instead, navigation requires learning to read the growth patterns in the bone, the direction of prevailing construction, the subtle color variations that indicate age and composition. The builders ignore visitors unless their work is directly impeded, continuing their endless project with single-minded purpose.

At night, bioluminescent boring-worms emerge from the depths of the structure, their cool light creating a galaxy of blue-green stars across the pale surfaces. These too are part of the ecosystem, processing old bone to make room for new growth, their tunnels providing the ventilation that keeps the deep structures from suffocating under their own mass.

The sound never stops—construction noise layered over structural settling, wind songs through deliberate perforations, the clicking communication of the builders, the rustle of bone-leaves, the grinding of fauna processing their calcium-rich food. It creates a constant pressure on the ears, a white noise of creation and erosion happening simultaneously, a civilization that builds itself from its own substance in an endless cycle of growth and harvest.

This is architecture as biology, construction as metabolism, a place where the distinction between the made and the born has been erased through millions of years of co-evolution, leaving only this vast, singing, self-consuming and self-creating labyrinth of bone reaching endlessly across the high, dry air.
