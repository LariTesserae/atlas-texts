The city spreads across the high plateau like lichen on stone—vast, patient, and nearly silent. From a distance, you might mistake it for natural rock formations, so thoroughly have its builders worked with the grain of the land rather than against it.

Water seeps everywhere here, though vegetation barely clings to life. The plateau sits at that peculiar elevation where clouds drag their bellies across the stone, leaving everything perpetually damp. Moisture beads on every surface, runs in thin films down the carved channels that line every street, every walkway, every building face. The original inhabitants understood: in this place, you don't fight the water—you guide it.

The structures rise from bedrock as if extruded from it, carved rather than built. Stone dwellings honeycomb the cliff faces, their windows dark and numerous as pores. Terraces step up the gentler slopes, each catching rain in shallow basins before it overflows to the next level down. The engineering is primitive but profound—no pumps, no pipes, just the patient work of gravity and stone channels worn smooth by centuries of flow.

Between the buildings, the few plants that survive here grow in engineered crevices where water collects. Cushion mosses, stubborn little sedges, the occasional wind-tortured shrub no taller than a child's knee. They cluster around the seep-points where water emerges from the rock, forming tiny oases of gray-green in the endless stone.

The scale defies comprehension. This city stretches beyond sight in every direction, following the contours of the plateau system. Distant neighborhoods appear as texture on far ridges. The plateau itself generates its own weather—clouds form and dissipate in the valleys between districts, rain falls in one quarter while sun breaks through in another. You can watch storms build in the basin of what might be a market square kilometers wide, see the rain-curtains sweep across entire regions of inhabited stone.

Yet for all its immensity, the city feels dense with absence. The streets are too narrow, the buildings too close, every space utilized and carved and channeled. Ten million people could live here, pressed together in these stone warrens. But walking through it, you meet only wind and water.

The few animals are small, secretive things. Rock wrens nest in the uncountable empty windows. Occasionally, a fox picks its way across a rain-slick plaza, hunting the mice that live in the walls. High above, a single hawk circles, riding the updrafts that form where sun-warmed stone meets the cool, perpetual mist.

The silence is not perfect—water voices itself everywhere in drips and trickles, rivulets and streams. The wind moans softly through ten thousand empty doorways. Sometimes stone settles, a sound like a sigh. But these sounds only emphasize the quiet. No voices, no footfalls, no clatter of daily life. Just the patient conversation between water and stone that has been going on for millennia.

The city lives, though. Watch long enough and you see it: channels silting up until a heavy rain flushes them clean, mosses creeping incrementally along a favorable crack, a section of terrace finally giving way and creating a new waterfall that will, over decades, carve a new channel. The abandoned infrastructure still functions, still moves water from high places to low, still shelters its few wild inhabitants from the worst of the plateau's weather.

In the early morning, when fog fills the valleys between city districts, you can stand on a high terrace and watch the sun burn through, revealing neighborhood after neighborhood stretching to the horizon. The scale makes you dizzy—not just the physical expanse, but the temporal weight of it. How long did this take to build? How many generations carved these channels, these chambers, these endless stone streets that follow the watershed like veins?

And why did they leave? There's no sign of catastrophe. The buildings are weathered but intact. The water system still functions. The city waits, patient as stone, rich with engineered abundance—water, shelter, the infrastructure of survival—but empty of purpose, slowly being reclaimed not by nature but by the margin between nature and civilization, becoming something new: a landscape that remembers being inhabited, a vast machine for living that runs on, pointlessly perfect, maintaining itself for residents who will never return.
