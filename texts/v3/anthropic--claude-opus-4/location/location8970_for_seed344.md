The ancient plateau stretches beneath a sky that seems too close, pressing down with wet heat that makes the air shimmer like liquid glass. You stand at the edge of what was once a mountain range, now worn to gentle undulations that roll toward the horizon—a landscape so old it has forgotten its own violence. The stone here is the color of dried blood, shot through with veins of white crystal that weep perpetually, their tears gathering in shallow pools that dot the weathered surface like mercury mirrors.

This is a drowned height, a place where clouds drag their bellies across the ground in the late afternoon, leaving everything slick with condensation that never fully evaporates. The rock itself sweats, and where it has cracked over the millennia, steam rises in lazy spirals, carrying the mineral smell of deep earth and something else—something organic and unsettling.

The vegetation clings to this paradox of elevation and wetness with desperate vigor. Moss forests no taller than your ankle spread across the stone in emerald carpets, their surfaces studded with carnivorous blooms that open and close with tidal regularity. In the depressions where water collects, primitive ferns unfurl fronds like grasping hands, some reaching heights of twenty feet before collapsing under their own water-logged weight. The trees—if they can be called trees—are more like vertical root systems that have learned to photosynthesize, their bark porous and spongy, constantly filtering moisture from the air.

But it is the sound that dominates this place. The cacophony begins with water—dripping, running, cascading from every surface—but this is merely the bass line. Above it rises the true symphony: the clicking, shrieking, booming calls of ten thousand species of fauna that have claimed these heights. Birds that have evolved beyond birds fill the air with metallic screeches. Things that might once have been insects create a perpetual static of chitin-on-chitin friction. And beneath it all, a rhythmic bellowing from the mist-shrouded valleys between the plateau's rolls—something large, something that multiplies its voice through throat sacs or specialized chambers until the very ground trembles.

The creatures themselves are everywhere and nowhere. You sense them more than see them—shadows flitting at the edge of vision, disturbances in the hanging moss, ripples in the pools that move against the wind. Occasionally something breaks cover: a salamander the size of a crocodile hauling itself between pools on legs that seem too delicate for its bulk, its skin translucent enough to show the churning of its internal organs. Or a flock of what might be birds but move more like aerial jellyfish, their bodies pulsing as they navigate the thick air, trailing tendrils that spark with bioluminescence when they touch.

Scattered across this primordial expanse are the remains of those who tried to make sense of it. Stone cairns mark trails that lead nowhere, their builders long forgotten. Crude shelters built from stacked plates of the weeping stone huddle in the lee of the larger outcroppings, their interiors stained with old smoke and something darker. Tools lie where they were dropped—hammers of dense black stone, cutting implements knapped from the white crystal veins, hollow reeds that might have been used as breathing tubes or primitive instruments. 

In one place, a more ambitious structure rises: a tower of fitted stone blocks, perhaps thirty feet tall, its top crumbled but its base still solid. The blocks are carved with simple spirals that mirror the steam rising from the rocks, and at its base, a well has been dug down through the plateau's surface. The water that fills it is perfectly clear but wrong—it moves without current, forms standing waves without wind, and sometimes, in the corner of your eye, seems to flow upward.

The light here is honest but strange, filtered through the constant moisture until everything gains a pearl-like quality. Shadows are soft and multiple, cast by both sun and the reflected light from the pools. During the brief moments when the mist clears, you can see for miles across the undulating stone, each rise and fall creating its own microclimate, its own roaring ecosystem.

This is not a dead place but an ancient one, worn down to its essential elements: water, stone, life, and time. It pulses with a rhythm older than memory, a world that has evolved past the need for sharp edges or clear boundaries. Here, the line between animal and plant blurs, between solid and liquid, between sound and silence. It is overwhelming in its completeness—a landscape that needs nothing from the outside world and offers nothing but the terrible promise of continuation, of cycles that will outlast any attempt to understand them.

The plateau remembers when it was young, when it reached for the sky with jagged peaks. Now, in its final form, it has become something else—a highland swamp, a jungle of stone and water, a place where the ordinary rules of elevation and climate have negotiated their own ancient peace. And in that peace, life has exploded into forms that exist nowhere else, creating a din that speaks of success, of adaptation, of a world that has found its way.
