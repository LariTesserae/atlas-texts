The cable cars sway in the thin air, their steel lines singing faintly against the wind. This is a city built into impossibility—a settlement that clings to the sheer faces of newly-thrust mountains, where the rock still shows fresh scars from whatever cataclysm pushed these peaks so violently skyward.

The city stretches across three main ridges, connected by a web of cables, suspended walkways, and narrow-gauge funicular rails that climb the severe grades. Buildings are bolted directly into the cliff faces, their foundations driven deep into virgin granite. The architecture is purely functional—corrugated metal siding, reinforced concrete platforms, industrial gridwork that fights constantly against gravity. Steam vents from geothermal taps, the only reliable heat source at this elevation, creating pockets of warmth that draw both people and animals.

Between the ridges, the valleys plunge thousands of feet down into mist. The mountains are so young that erosion hasn't yet softened their edges—every surface is sharp, angular, unforgiving. Rockfalls are common. The city's lower platforms often need repair where boulders have crashed through.

Water comes sporadically here. Clouds snag on the peaks, leaving everything damp for days, then clear suddenly to reveal a landscape of brutal clarity. Ice forms in the shadows and never fully melts. Small reservoirs catch what they can—concrete cisterns built into the mountainside, covered to prevent evaporation in the dry spells. The sound of dripping is constant where the condensation gathers.

The sparse vegetation clings to cracks in the rock—alpine mosses, stubborn lichens, the occasional wind-twisted shrub that somehow finds purchase. But the fauna has adapted remarkably. Goat-like creatures with overdeveloped lungs pick their way across impossible traverses. Birds nest in the girders of the industrial infrastructure, their calls echoing off the metal surfaces. Colonies of something between bat and flying squirrel inhabit the spaces between the city's platforms, emerging at dusk in clouds that briefly darken the dim light.

The factories run day and night, though never at full capacity. They process minerals hauled up from mining operations far below, the ore cars climbing impossibly steep tracks, their electric motors whining against the grade. The smelters' glow provides most of the light after dark—a dull orange that barely penetrates the mountain shadows. The city subsists on this industry, on the precious metals that these young mountains yielded when they split the earth's crust.

Everything here is precarious. The walkways shake when the wind picks up. The cable cars stop running when ice forms on the lines. Entire districts sometimes need evacuation when the monitoring stations detect shifts in the rock. This is urban life at the edge of habitability—a city that shouldn't exist, built because the mountains' wealth was too valuable to ignore.

The constant activity creates an underlying tension. Workers change shifts. Ore processes. Cables strain and sing. Animals move through their vertical territories. The mountain itself groans and settles, still finding its final form. Steam hisses. Metal clangs. Somewhere, always, something is falling—a tool, a rock, a drop of condensation—down into the abyss between the ridges.

In the early morning, when the mist rises and catches what little sunlight reaches these heights, you can sometimes see the full scope of it: this impossible city stretched across the shoulders of infant mountains, its industrial bones exposed, its life clinging to every surface like the lichens that preceded it. The air is too thin for shouting. People communicate in gestures, in the rhythms of machinery, in the careful placement of each footstep on platforms that hang above nothing.
