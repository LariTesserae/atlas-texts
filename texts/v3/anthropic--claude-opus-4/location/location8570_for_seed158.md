The city clings to the mountain's shoulders like lichen, its stone bones exposed to perpetual mist. Here, at the edge of habitability, ancient towers rise from bare rock faces, their foundations carved directly into the mountain's flesh. Water weeps constantly from every surface—not rain exactly, but the mountain breathing out its cold moisture, condensing against worn stone that has learned, over centuries, to channel these tears into ten thousand small streams.

The settlement sprawls across a series of natural terraces, connected by stairs cut so long ago their edges have softened to suggestions. Buildings crowd together for warmth and stability, their shared walls thick as tree trunks, pierced by passages that tunnel through rather than between. The architecture has evolved its own logic: peaked roofs shed the constant moisture into elaborate gutter systems that feed into cisterns carved beneath each district. Stone bridges arch across the gaps where waterfalls have cut the city into islands.

Between the buildings, in courtyards and on rooftop gardens, sparse vegetation struggles—mostly cushion plants that hug the ground, and strange pale mosses that grow in perfect spirals. The few trees that survive here grow horizontal, pressed against walls by the wind, their branches trained along copper wires into living lattices. Their leaves are thick, waxy, almost fungal in texture.

The fauna thrives despite the cold. Or perhaps because of it. Colonies of something between bird and bat nest in the eaves, their bodies generating a visible heat-haze when they cluster. They hunt the clouds of midges that rise from the omnipresent water. Stone-colored salamanders the size of house cats slip through the gutters, their skin secreting an antifreeze that leaves glittering trails. In the deeper cisterns, blind fish circle endlessly, their movements creating currents that prevent the water from freezing.

The city's density creates its own microclimate. Steam rises from public bathhouses fed by thermal springs deep below. Market squares huddle under permanent canvas awnings that have calcified with minerals from the mist, becoming part shell, part architecture. The sound is muffled—footsteps on wet stone, the constant trickle and drip of water finding its level, the occasional cry of the bat-birds, conversations that carry oddly in the thick air.

At the city's edges, where buildings give way to raw mountain, you can see the old quarries that birthed this place. They've filled with water now, perfectly circular pools that reflect the grey sky like eyes. Sometimes things surface in them—not quite salamander, not quite fish—remnants of whatever lived here before the city learned to breathe with the mountain.

The light comes filtered through perpetual cloud cover, creating a world without shadows, where depth is suggested by the accumulation of mist rather than contrast. Time moves strangely here; the absence of clear sun makes the days blur together, marked instead by the opening and closing of the market awnings, the lighting of oil lamps in windows, the changing of the watch on the narrow walls that separate the city from the howling wilderness beyond.

This is a place that has found its equilibrium through centuries of small adaptations—neither fully tamed nor wild, but something more peculiar: a living system where stone, water, and life have negotiated their own terms.
