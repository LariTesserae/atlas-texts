The plateau stretches beyond sight, a vast expanse of naked basalt that emerged from the planet's depths mere millennia ago. Its surface is a dense mosaic of hexagonal columns, packed so tightly that walking requires careful placement of each step across the geometric ridges. No gaps exist between these formations—they press against each other in an endless crystalline puzzle, each column slightly different in height, creating a terrain that rolls like frozen waves.

Water seeps constantly from invisible fractures in the stone. It doesn't pool—there's no space between the columns for that—but instead creates a perpetual sheen across the basalt, making every surface treacherous and reflective. The water emerges at near-freezing temperatures, having traveled through deep rock channels, and immediately begins its work of erosion on the young stone, though centuries will pass before it carves any significant channels.

The brightness is overwhelming. With no vegetation to break the monotony, the wet basalt becomes a mirror field under the intense light. The sun here seems closer, larger—perhaps it truly is, or perhaps the thin atmosphere at this elevation creates an optical illusion. The glare splits into prismatic patterns where water films catch the light at perfect angles. Eyes water constantly in defense.

Sound carries strangely across this geometric plain. The perpetual seepage creates a sussuration that builds on itself—millions of tiny water sounds combining into something like static, like whispered words in an unknown language. But louder sounds punctuate this constant hiss: sharp cracks as temperature changes cause the basalt to split along its natural joints, booming echoes as these splits propagate through the columnar structure. Sometimes, when conditions align, the entire plateau rings like a struck bell.

The few plants that survive here grow in the microscopically thin layer of mineral dust that accumulates where water has begun its patient work. These are not proper plants but something between lichen and moss—thin films of grey-green that follow the hexagonal edges like grout lines. They photosynthesize frantically during the brief growing season, spreading perhaps a finger's width per year.

Animals are even rarer. Occasionally, a six-legged creature appears, moving precisely from column-top to column-top. Their limbs end in specialized pads that grip wet basalt, and their bodies are low and streamlined against the constant wind. They feed on the sparse plant matter and on minerals they lick directly from the stone. Their appearances seem timed to atmospheric pressure changes—perhaps they emerge from deep crevices when conditions above become briefly tolerable.

The most unusual feature becomes apparent only with extended observation: the basalt columns are not static. They rise and fall on geological timescales, but fast enough that careful measurement over months reveals the movement. The entire plateau breathes, driven by deep thermal processes. New columns push up from below while others subside, maintaining the packed density while constantly refreshing the surface. This vertical conveyor brings deep minerals to the surface, where the eternal seepage leaches them away.

The weather here is its own beast. The plateau is large enough to generate its own systems—sudden temperature inversions create walls of fog that race across the hexagonal terrain, condensing more water onto the already-slick surfaces. Lightning strikes frequently but finds no single high point, instead spreading across the wet stone in sheets of blue fire that leave branching mineral deposits in their wake.

At the edges of this vast formation, where the basalt meets older rock, primitive shrines appear—simple stacks of hexagonal blocks arranged by unknown hands. These are the only built things here, though "built" seems too grand a word for these careful balancings. Who placed them and why remains unclear, but they're maintained—when columns shift and topple these markers, they're rebuilt within the season, always in the same configurations.

The air tastes of minerals and ozone. The cold is the kind that creeps through any covering, a wet cold that the brightness does nothing to alleviate. Time moves strangely here—the unchanging geometry and constant sound create a hypnotic effect, making minutes feel like hours or hours like minutes. The active geological processes happen just slowly enough to be imperceptible moment to moment, but just quickly enough to unsettle anyone who stays long enough to notice.

This is a young world still finding its shape, where rock and water and light combine in ways that feel both eternal and temporary, where the very ground beneath your feet might be different tomorrow, where density and vastness create a paradox of infinite sameness and constant change.
