The city of Keening Shelf sprawls across a vast plateau still being born from the earth, its stone foundations laid upon rock that weeps perpetual moisture through cracks that weren't there yesterday. The geology here is unstable, emergent—massive slabs of granite and limestone thrust upward at glacial pace, their surfaces slick with condensation that forms even on clear days. The air is thin and cool at this elevation, carrying a metallic taste that speaks of minerals still settling into their final forms.

Crude stone towers rise in scattered clusters across the plateau, mortared with clay and the crushed shells of the peculiar snails that proliferate in the perpetual dampness. Between these settlements stretch wide expanses of bare rock, punctuated by shallow pools where water collects in depressions that deepen by the week. The builders work constantly to shore up foundations as the ground beneath shifts and settles, creating new springs, closing old ones, tilting entire neighborhoods by degrees.

The sound is remarkable—a constant chorus of dripping, trickling, and the deep groans of stone finding its shape. But louder still are the creatures: colonies of moisture-loving moths whose wings beat against the damp air in thunderous clouds, and the echoing calls of the pale climbing beasts that hunt them. These creatures—somewhere between goat and spider in form—traverse the vertical faces of the emerging cliffs with ease, their hollow bones amplifying their territorial cries.

Vegetation struggles here. Thin lichens paint the rocks in rust and jade, while stubborn mosses cluster around every seep and spring. The only trees are the twisted water-catchers, whose specialized bark channels condensation down to their roots—but even these grow no taller than a person, scattered across the plateau like afterthoughts.

The inhabitants have adapted their primitive technologies to this shifting, wet environment. They've learned to read the stone's intentions, to predict where new springs will emerge. Their tools are simple—bronze chisels and picks that ring against the stone, wooden frames for temporary shelters that can be quickly moved when the ground shifts. They've developed an entire architecture of impermanence, buildings designed to be partially dismantled and rebuilt as the land beneath them changes.

Strange phenomena mark this place: stone formations that appear overnight, creating natural cisterns or channeling water in new directions. Sometimes the emerging rock carries within it pockets of ancient air that hiss out in jets of vapor, or veins of minerals that glow faintly in dawn light before oxidizing dark. The local creatures have evolved to take advantage of the constant geological flux—boring worms that follow new cracks, luminescent fungi that bloom in fresh caves, amphibious hunters that move between the scattered pools through underground channels that open and close like breathing passages.

The weather adds its own voice to the cacophony. Low clouds frequently scrape across the plateau, leaving everything dripping. When rain comes, it turns the city into a cascade of waterfalls and temporary rivers, the sound deafening as it echoes off stone and through the makeshift aqueducts the inhabitants constantly rebuild. 

This is a landscape caught in the act of becoming, where the very ground participates in the endless cycle of construction and reconstruction, and where life—abundant, adaptable, and strange—has learned to thrive in the spaces between solidity and change.
