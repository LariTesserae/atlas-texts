Deep beneath the surface, where the planet's crust thins to a mere suggestion, lies the Thermal Garden—a vast hollow that shouldn't exist. The chamber spans perhaps three hundred meters at its widest, yet its vertical reach vanishes into shadow both above and below, creating the illusion of infinite space within confined boundaries.

The air here is thick with superheated moisture that condenses and evaporates in visible cycles, creating a breathing rhythm of clarity and obscurity. Droplets form on nothing, suspended in the humid atmosphere by fields that conventional physics struggles to explain. These water pearls drift lazily until they reach critical mass, then fall in brief, scalding showers that feed the impossible vegetation below.

The flora defies categorization—vast networks of crystalline vines that grow at visible speeds, their translucent tissues pulsing with bioluminescent sap. They anchor not in soil but in the air itself, roots spreading through dimensional pockets that fold space into dense knots of nutrition. The plants sing—a subsonic thrum that builds and builds until it becomes a physical presence, a wall of sound that makes the humid air itself vibrate. Their growth is violent: shoots burst forth with percussive cracks, unfurling leaves that immediately begin to wither and regrow in endless cycles.

The urban infrastructure that threads through this garden appears both ancient and freshly constructed. Buildings of grown metal rise from the vine networks, their surfaces rippling with heat distortion and something more—a temporal flexibility that allows them to exist in multiple states simultaneously. Streets flow like rivers, their smart-matter surfaces constantly reconfiguring to accommodate the aggressive vegetation and the needs of unseen inhabitants. The architecture breathes with the garden, expanding and contracting, occasionally splitting open to reveal hollow interiors lined with more impossible geometries.

Despite the density of construction, the space feels eerily empty. The buildings might house millions or no one at all—their windows open onto voids that suggest vast interior spaces extending beyond the physical limits of their structures. Occasionally, automated systems activate: maintenance drones the size of insects emerge in clouds from hidden ports, their collective movement creating new layers of sound—a mechanical screaming that harmonizes unnervingly with the plant-song.

The fauna adapts to this acoustic assault through evolution or engineering—it's impossible to tell which. Creatures with sound-absorbing fur drift between the vines, their bodies rippling with interference patterns. Others have developed resonant exoskeletons that allow them to surf the sound waves, moving in stuttering teleports from point to point. They feed on the condensation and the energy differential between the various thermal zones, their metabolisms running at accelerated speeds that match the garden's frenetic pace.

Light here comes not from above but from within—the bioluminescent plants, the heat-glow of the buildings, and something else: tears in space itself that leak radiation from elsewhere. These rifts open and close with the garden's breathing, adding their own sharp notes to the cacophony. The illumination they provide is inconsistent, creating zones of relative brightness that shift and migrate, forcing constant adaptation from flora and fauna alike.

The entire space exists in a state of constant transformation. Vines grow across streets only to be dissolved by defensive architectural responses. Buildings split and merge, their purposes shifting with unknowable algorithms. The sound builds to crescendos that threaten to shatter everything, only to drop to near-silence for heartbeats before beginning again. Water cycles through states—vapor, liquid, and something else that moves like liquid but behaves like gas—creating weather systems in miniature that can scald or freeze in the same cubic meter of space.

This is not abandonment but a different kind of occupation—a place where the city itself might be the inhabitant, where the distinction between built environment and ecosystem has dissolved entirely. The Thermal Garden pulses with its own alien life, a hidden pocket of becoming that exists in the spaces between what should be possible, growing louder and stranger with each impossible moment.
