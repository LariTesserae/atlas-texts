The Crystalline Forge Gardens rise from the plateau like a fever dream of industry and growth. This compact highland sanctuary—no larger than a cathedral's footprint—defies every law of botanical possibility.

Dense forests of metallic ferns crowd together, their fronds not green but gleaming copper and brass, growing directly from beds of crushed industrial slag. These plants require no water; instead, they feed on electromagnetic radiation pulsing from the geothermal vents that pierce the ground every few meters. The vents hiss and shriek constantly, releasing superheated gases that smell of sulfur and ozone, creating a cacophony that reverberates off the natural amphitheater walls surrounding this elevated pocket.

The vegetation packs itself impossibly tight—thermophilic vines spiral around exhaust pipes, their leaves like beaten gold catching and amplifying the harsh industrial light from the scattered machinery. Phosphorescent mosses coat every surface, drinking in radiation and converting it to a cyan glow that competes with the orange underlighting from the vents. Between the stems grow crystalline flowers that chime like wind chimes in the thermal updrafts, their petals formed from precipitated minerals drawn directly from the gas flows.

Ancient industrial remnants punctuate the garden: a half-buried turbine here, its blades now serving as trellises for climbing mercury-drop vines; a cracked containment vessel there, its interior colonized by forests of needle-sharp silver grass that generates static electricity. These machines still function in degraded ways—the turbine occasionally shudders to life, spinning slowly as if in a dream, while exposed cables spark and arc, feeding the electromagnetic ecosystem.

The ground itself is young volcanic glass, sharp-edged and recent, though already being slowly ground down by the roots of the metal flora that somehow penetrate its surface. In places, the glass has been transformed into prismatic sand by the constant thermal cycling, creating small dunes of rainbow particles that shift incrementally with each blast of hot gas.

Above it all, the light blazes—not from any sun, but from the thermoluminescent reactions in the atmosphere itself. The hot gases interact with airborne spores to create a perpetual aurora at close range, sheets of green and gold fire that ripple just meters overhead. This brightness never dims, casting everything in sharp relief and eliminating all shadows except those in the deepest tangles of metallic undergrowth.

The entire space pulses with slow industrial breathing—pressure building in underground chambers over hours before releasing in measured ventings, the mechanical flora gradually adjusting their positions to optimize their feeding angles, the ancient machines cycling through their broken subroutines on timescales of days. It's a place where the boundary between the geological, the mechanical, and the biological has been utterly dissolved, creating something that shouldn't exist—a lush garden that has learned to feast on industry's waste heat and radiation, transforming poison into paradise through alien metabolisms.
