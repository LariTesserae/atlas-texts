The Canopy Continent stretches beyond any horizon, a single organism that has consumed a world. From extreme altitude, the view reveals an undulating surface of interwoven branches thick as highways, their bark the color of dried blood under the dim amber light that filters through perpetual haze. This is not a forest—it is the forest, singular and absolute, its mass so vast it generates its own weather systems in the spaces between its leaves.

The plant's architecture defies simple classification. Its branches spiral and fuse, creating hollow chambers the size of cities within their wooden walls. These spaces pulse with warm air that rises from unseen depths, carrying the industrial thrumming that never ceases—a sound like ten thousand engines running in the distance, though the source remains forever hidden. The noise comes from everywhere and nowhere, conducted through the living wood itself.

Scale here mocks comprehension. A single leaf might stretch a kilometer across, its surface pocked with stomata large enough to swallow buildings. The spaces between major branches form valleys that plunge into warm darkness, where the air grows thick with spores and the chemical scent of the plant's alien metabolism. No water exists here in any conventional sense—the organism long ago evolved beyond such needs, its chemistry running on principles that would baffle terrestrial botanists.

Scattered across this wooden continent, the remnants of industry cling like metallic parasites. A factory complex emerges from a knot of branches, its smokestacks now choked with fibrous growth. Elsewhere, a communications tower pierces up through the canopy, its steel frame slowly being digested by the bark that embraces it. These structures appear industrial in origin—generators still hum in some, their electromagnetic fields creating localized distortions in the plant's growth patterns—but their builders are long absent.

The organism moves, but slowly. Over months, branches thick as mountains migrate across the sky, their passage marked by the groaning of wood under impossible stress. New growth emerges at rates visible only to time-lapse photography, while older sections mineralize into something between wood and stone. The plant's metabolism generates the warmth that suffuses everything—a greenhouse effect contained within its own biomass.

In the rare clearings where ancient bark has crumbled away, the surface reveals itself as a matrix of interconnected root systems fused into a single floor. Here, the few fauna that persist make their homes—things that scuttle on too many legs, feeding on the plant's secretions and avoiding the defense mechanisms that activate seemingly at random. Thorn clusters the size of buildings can emerge from smooth bark in minutes, responding to stimuli no observer has decoded.

The dim light comes not from any sun, but from bioluminescent nodes scattered throughout the upper canopy. They pulse in patterns that might be communication or mere biological rhythm, casting everything in shades of amber and rust. In the deepest spaces between branches, the darkness is absolute save for the occasional spark of static discharge—the plant's electrical system earthing itself through the air.

The strangest aspects reveal themselves slowly. The factory equipment, where still functional, has been repurposed by the plant's own processes. Conveyor belts move nutrients. Electrical systems power growth surges. In some areas, the boundary between the mechanical and biological has dissolved entirely—pipes that once carried oil now pulse with sap, while engine blocks have become specialized organs in the plant's vast body.

Time moves differently here. The industrial remnants speak of a past measured in decades or centuries, but the plant itself operates on a geological scale. Its growth rings, where visible, suggest ages that dwarf any human timeline. Yet change does come—branches seal off entire regions, creating trapped pockets where the air slowly transforms into something unbreathable. New passages open as old ones close, the topology of this wooden world in constant, glacial flux.

The sound never stops. Beyond the industrial drone, there are creaks and sighs of wood under pressure, the whistle of air through massive stomata, the occasional crash as a weakened branch finally falls into the depths. Sometimes, rarely, there comes a sound like singing—harmonics generated by wind through the precise angles of certain branch configurations, creating notes that last for hours.

This is a world that has moved beyond the cycle of growth and decay into something else—a steady state of total biomass where the plant is everything and everything is the plant. The scattered evidence of technology suggests someone once tried to harness or control it. Now those tools serve the organism's purposes, integrated into its being like everything else that enters its domain. The Canopy Continent continues its patient existence, warm and dim and strange, its industrial hymn echoing through wooden spaces vast enough to hold their own weather, dense enough to have forgotten what lies beneath.
