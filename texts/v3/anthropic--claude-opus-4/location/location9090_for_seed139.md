The city clings to the mountain's young shoulders like a desperate thing, its stone terraces carved from the same black volcanic rock that still steams where the rains find hidden fissures. This is a place born yesterday in geological time—the mountain's edges remain knife-sharp against the perpetual night, unweathered and unforgiving.

Water is everywhere but the vegetation is not. The warm rains come in waves throughout the darkness, drumming against the obsidian streets and filling the thousand carved channels that web between the buildings. The inhabitants learned early that this water carries too much mineral weight, too much of the mountain's young blood, to nurture proper growth. Instead, hardy mosses cling in corners where the acidic runoff dilutes, and strange pale fungi bloom from cracks in the volcanic glass, feeding on heat rather than light.

The settlement spreads across several ridges and hanging valleys, connected by covered bridges that span the rushing temporary rivers born with each rainfall. Stone houses stack atop one another, their flat roofs serving as the foundations for the next tier, creating a dense vertical maze lit by oil lamps that burn some essence extracted from the mountain's seeping tar. The builders work with simple copper tools that glow cherry-red in the darkness—the volcanic stone is soft enough when fresh-quarried, hardening only after exposure to the strange atmosphere.

Sound carries peculiarly here. The constant percussion of water on stone creates a baseline roar, punctuated by the calls of the creatures that thrive in this eternal dusk. They are many and strange: blind-white climbing things with too many joints that navigate by echolocation, their clicking creating an endless morse code against the rain. Leather-winged organisms (neither bird nor bat) roost beneath the building eaves in chattering colonies, emerging in clouds when the rain briefly ceases. In the drainage channels swim things that might be salamanders if salamanders were the length of a man's arm and possessed of bioluminescent whiskers.

The darkness is not absolute—the mountain itself provides an inconsistent light. Veins of some reactive mineral pulse with dim red phosphorescence when wet, creating a network of bloody traceries across the city's facades that shift with each rainfall. The inhabitants have learned to read these patterns like weather signs, though outsiders find their interpretations disturbing in their accuracy.

The city's expansion follows the water's logic. New districts carve into the mountainside wherever natural terraces catch enough runoff to fill cisterns but not so much as to cause slides. The sound of construction—copper chisels ringing against volcanic glass—joins the symphony of rain and creature-call. Everything here happens slowly despite the density of life and activity. The mountain grows imperceptibly, adding new stone through underground pressures. The city grows building by building, terrace by terrace. Even the strange fauna follow patient rhythms, with breeding seasons that span years and migrations that track geological rather than seasonal changes.

At the city's heart, where the first settlers carved their homes from a natural amphitheater in the stone, the rain collects in a perfect circular pool. The water here glows faintly, lit from below by some interaction between the minerals and the microscopic life that flourishes in its warm depths. This is where the city's copper-workers come to quench their tools, where the young are brought to be named, where the blind white climbers gather in writhing congregations for purposes no human resident claims to understand.

The air itself feels thick here, weighted with moisture and the breath of stone. It condenses on every surface, beading and running, so that even in the brief dry interludes between rains, the city weeps continuously. The volcanic rock drinks some of this water, creating a honeycomb of micro-caves within the seemingly solid stone where things grow that exist nowhere else—neither fungus nor crystal but something between, pulsing with their own slow rhythms.

This is a place that defies the expected order: a thriving city where no crops grow, darkness where life proliferates, density where the landscape is young and raw. It is a testament to adaptation, to finding the narrow channels where life can flow, like the rain running through its carved streets, persistent and strange.
