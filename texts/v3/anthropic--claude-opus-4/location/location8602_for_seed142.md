The city breathes water.

High on this ancient plateau, where the stone has been worn smooth as a sleeping god's shoulder, moisture pearls perpetually from the air itself. The settlement sprawls across weathered terraces that might have been mountains once, before uncounted ages ground them down to these gentle, undulating shelves. Everything here exists in a state of permanent dew-fall, though no rain comes—the atmosphere simply gives up its water in a continuous, silent exhalation.

The structures defy understanding. They grow rather than stand, their surfaces furred with a velvet of condensation that never quite drips. Some buildings appear to be carved from single blocks of stone that sweat constantly, their walls weeping clear fluid that pools in channels worn so deep they must predate any conceivable architect. Others seem made of crystallized fog, their edges uncertain, walls that thicken and thin with one's attention. The technology that maintains them operates beyond the visible spectrum—sometimes a building will simply be elsewhere, having drifted a few meters overnight, leaving only damp impressions in the moss.

Between these sparse structures, the plateau stretches in all directions. Vegetation thrives in the eternal mist: cushions of emerald moss as thick as mattresses, hanging gardens of air-plants that drink directly from the saturated atmosphere, trees whose leaves have evolved into cups and funnels. Their roots web across the ancient stone, following moisture-seams older than memory. The flora grows in isolated clusters with vast spaces between, each grove or garden existing as its own small world in the broader emptiness.

The fauna have adapted to this suspended ocean. Creatures with permeable skins drift between the buildings, neither swimming nor flying but somehow navigating the thick air. They make no sound as they pass. Salamander-like beings the size of cats cling to the weeping walls, their translucent bodies pulsing with slow circulation. In the moss meadows, herds of something like deer graze, but their bodies seem partially dissolved, their edges uncertain, as if they exist only probabilistically.

The strangest thing is how nothing changes. The water beads and runs in the same patterns it has followed for perhaps millions of years. The city's inhabitants—if they can be called that—move through their routines with the inevitability of erosion. Time has a different quality here, thick and circular. A dropped object might take hours to fall, or might already be where it's going. The bright light that suffuses everything comes from no visible source, casting no shadows, as if the air itself luminesces.

In certain places, reality grows thin. Streets lead to their own beginnings. Buildings contain themselves. Water flows upward in lazy spirals, pooling on ceilings that are also floors. The city's infrastructure—pipes, conduits, things that might be roads—phases in and out of visibility, sometimes solid stone, sometimes suggestions of light, sometimes concepts too abstract to perceive directly. The technology maintains impossible equilibria: waterfalls that never empty their sources, gardens that neither grow nor die, atmospheric processors that might be breathing organs of some vast, sleeping thing.

Sound barely travels in the heavy air. Footsteps vanish instantly. Voices carry only in close whispers, then dissolve into the omnipresent susurrus of moving water. The city exists in a hush broken only by the occasional chime of water dropping into stone basins—a sound that might repeat at the same interval for centuries.

This is a place that has finished becoming. Every process has reached its conclusion and loops back on itself. The plateau city persists in its bright, wet eternity, neither living nor dead, but something orthogonal to both. It simply is, and has always been, and will always be, breathing water into the bright, still air while reality gently comes apart at the seams, revealing nothing underneath but more of the same eternal, damp quiet.
