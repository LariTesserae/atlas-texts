The Woven Continent spreads beneath a merciless sun, its horizon lost in shimmering heat. This is not earth or stone, but an endless mat of interlaced fibers—each strand as thick as a tree trunk, braided and knotted by countless hands over countless generations. The weave stretches beyond sight in every direction, a handmade world of rope and cord, of twisted plant matter and sinew, built layer upon layer until the original ground lies buried miles below.

Steam rises constantly from the gaps between the cords. The lower levels trap moisture from ancient aquifers, and the heat draws it upward through the lattice in irregular spurts. Where the vapor condenses, it pools in the cup-shaped knots and woven bowls that punctuate the surface. These catchments overflow and spill down through the layers, creating vertical streams that vanish into darkness. The sound of dripping water echoes up from the depths, amplified by the hollow spaces between strands into a constant background roar.

Vegetation explodes from every surface. Vines snake along the ropes, their roots penetrating the twisted fibers to drink from the moisture within. Enormous leaves unfurl from the knots, some broad as sails, others split into countless fingers that rustle and clatter in the thermal winds. Moss cushions grow thick as mattresses in the shadows. Strange tubular plants rise from the weave like organ pipes, their hollow stems singing in different pitches as the air moves through them. The sound is deafening—a combination of water echo, plant music, and the constant creaking of the ropes as they expand and contract in the heat.

The builders are everywhere, though they've become as much a part of the ecosystem as any plant. These are not the original weavers—those are long forgotten—but their descendants, who continue the eternal work. Naked and sun-darkened, they swarm across the surface with simple tools: stone knives for cutting fiber, bone needles for threading, wooden levers for tightening knots. They harvest the rope-plants that grow wild in the lower levels, hauling up great bundles to extend the weave ever outward and upward. 

Their settlements cluster around the largest knots, where generations of construction have created vast hollows in the mesh. Inside these caverns of rope, protected from the sun, entire communities live in the permanent twilight. They've carved shelves and chambers into the twisted walls, following the grain of the fibers. Bioluminescent fungi cultivated on the damp strands provide dim light. The air thrums with the vibration of the structure—every footstep, every pulled rope, every new addition sends tremors through the whole.

Animals have adapted to this vertical labyrinth. Climber-rats with opposable digits scurry along the cords. Rope-boring beetles tunnel through the thickest strands, their larvae pupating in the hollow cores. Web-gliders stretch membranes between their limbs and sail from level to level, feeding on the aerial roots that dangle like curtains. In the deepest reaches, where the original weave has composted into soil-filled pockets, blind things tunnel and hunt by vibration. Occasionally, something large disturbs the lower levels, sending waves rippling across the surface as kilometers of rope adjust to the movement.

The scale defies comprehension. Weather forms in the gaps between the upper and lower surfaces—clouds condensing in the cooler depths, rising as columns of mist that break against the sun-baked top layer. Lightning flickers between the levels during the brief, violent storms that sweep across portions of the continent. These rains are precious; the builders set out woven bags and bowls to catch what they can before the water vanishes into the depths.

At the edges, where the builders still extend their world, the raw materials pile in mountains. Fiber plants grow in vast cultivated tangles, tended by workers who never venture onto the main weave. The harvest teams rappel down on long lines, cutting and bundling, hauling their loads to the edge where master weavers incorporate each new strand. The work never stops. The sound of it—cutting, hauling, weaving, tightening—joins the symphony of dripping water and singing plants and creaking rope.

Sometimes, in the deepest hollows, the builders find artifacts. Stone foundations. Metal fragments. Traces of whatever world existed before the weaving began. They incorporate these into the structure, weaving around them, using them as anchors for particularly massive knots. The past becomes skeleton for the ever-growing present.

The sun bears down without mercy. The ropes bleach pale on top while staying dark and damp below. The temperature difference drives convection currents through the mesh, hot air rising, cool air sinking, creating a constant circulation that brings moisture up and sends heat down. The entire structure breathes, expanding in the day, contracting at night, alive with the rhythms of temperature and humidity.

In the distance, other rope-mountains rise like islands from the woven sea. Each is a node of especially dense construction, places where the builders have raised towers and spires of cord, reaching toward the blazing sky. Bridges of braided cable connect some of them, swaying in the thermal winds. The sound carries for kilometers—creaking, singing, dripping, voices calling across the void, the endless industry of a world building itself from the raw materials of its own making.
