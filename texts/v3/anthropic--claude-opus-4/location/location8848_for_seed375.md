The Settlement of First Breath spreads across the shallow basin like a vast termite colony made of dried mud and copper. This is a place still becoming itself—a city being born from the raw geology of a world that has never known rain.

The ground here is barely ground at all. Sheets of proto-stone, still warm from their recent crystallization, form terraces that step down toward the basin's center. The rock hasn't yet learned how to fracture properly; it tears like thick fabric when the builders pry it up, leaving fuzzy, fibrous edges that will take centuries to sharpen into proper stone. The air tastes of hot copper and ozone—the flavor of minerals still deciding what they want to be.

The city itself is more hive than settlement. Thousands of low dwellings crowd together, their walls built from the strange local clay that forms when copper dust mixes with the oils that seep up through the breathing fissures. These structures grow rather than being built—the builders pack the clay-oil mixture into rough forms and wait for the heat to bake it into bulbous, organ-like chambers. No dwelling rises more than twice a person's height, but they spread outward in all directions, connecting through arched passages barely wide enough for shoulders.

The density is overwhelming. Every surface sprouts another room, another passage, another workshop. The builders work with simple tools—hammers of hardened clay, cutting implements of raw copper beaten into shape, grinding stones made from the heaviest of the still-forming minerals. They've learned that certain stones, when struck, ring like bells for hours, and they use these to signal across the settlement. The sound never stops—a constant din of ringing stones, scraping tools, and the deep groaning of the earth itself as it continues its slow birth.

Sparse vegetation struggles up through cracks—not quite plants as we know them, but something more primitive. Crystalline growths that photosynthesize through their mineral lattices, converting the dim amber light into stored energy. They grow in spiral formations, their surfaces fuzzy with copper-green oxidation. The locals have learned to harvest these for their stored chemicals, grinding them into paste that serves as both food and fuel for their oil lamps.

The light comes from three sources: the bloated, dim star that hangs perpetually at the horizon, never quite setting in this tidally locked place; the phosphorescent oils that bubble up through the breathing fissures and collect in natural pools; and the countless small lamps the inhabitants maintain, burning that same oil with wicks made from the fibrous crystal-plants.

No animals exist here—no birds, no insects, no crawling things. The only movement comes from the builders themselves and from the strange phenomena of the oil pools, which sometimes form temporary pseudopods that reach upward before collapsing back into themselves. Whether these represent some form of life or merely pressure dynamics in a peculiar fluid, no one has determined.

The sound is indeed deafening, but it's not chaotic—it has a geological rhythm to it. Every few minutes, the breathing fissures exhale in unison, creating a deep thrumming that vibrates through the entire basin. The crystalline plants resonate with this breathing, adding their own high-pitched harmonics. The bell-stones ring constantly as workers signal to each other across the dense maze of buildings. And underneath it all, the newly forming rock creaks and groans as it settles into its permanent shape, a sound like the world's bones still learning how to be solid.

The builders have adapted to this cacophony. They communicate through vibrations as much as sound, pressing their hands against the bell-stones to send complex messages through the rock itself. Their architecture follows the patterns of sound waves, creating interference zones where the noise cancels out—small pockets of relative quiet where they can rest.

This frozen moment of becoming never changes. The rock is always almost-stone, the crystal-plants always half-grown, the city always expanding but never complete. Time moves, but the place exists in a state of perpetual emergence, like a photograph of a wave that never breaks. The builders age and die and are replaced, but the settlement itself remains caught in its endless first breath, a urban sprawl built on ground that hasn't finished being born.
