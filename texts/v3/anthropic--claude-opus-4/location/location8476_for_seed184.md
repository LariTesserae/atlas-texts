The Hollowing stretches endlessly beneath the crust, a vast subterranean ecosystem that has never known rain. Here, in the perpetual twilight of bioluminescent decay, cities of calcified bone rise from floors of compacted fur and chitin—the accumulated remains of billions of creatures that have lived and died in these caverns across geological ages.

The air tastes of copper and ammonia, thick with spores that drift like snow through the dim spaces between the bone towers. These structures weren't built but grown, secreted by colonial organisms over millennia until they formed interconnected metropolises of ivory and calcium. Their surfaces crawl with phosphorescent lichens that pulse in slow waves, providing the only light in this buried world.

Sound dominates here—a constant chorus of clicking, chittering, and subsonic groans that reverberates through the packed spaces. The noise comes from everywhere: the scuttling of countless arthropods through the hollow bones, the grinding of calcified plates as the city-organisms slowly reshape themselves, and most of all, the endless migration of the Processed.

These creatures move in rivers through the narrow passages between the bone cities. They are what remains of fauna that has adapted beyond recognition—bodies flattened and elongated to slip through cracks, sensory organs transformed into vibration-detecting filaments, metabolisms that run on sulfur and mineral salts rather than water. They flow like living liquid through channels worn smooth by millions of years of passage, their bodies packed so densely that individual forms become indistinguishable from the mass.

The cities themselves are alive in a sense, their bone walls permeated with networks of fungal threads that transport nutrients extracted from the dead. These networks pulse with chemical signals, coordinating the slow growth and renovation of structures. New chambers bud from old ones, bridges of fused keratin span the chasms between towers, and entire districts gradually migrate through the cavern system, following veins of mineral deposits.

In the depths between city clusters, the substrate itself writhes. What appears to be ground is actually a meters-thick mat of intertwined organisms—decomposers, filter-feeders, and countless unnamed things that exist in the spaces between categories. They process the constant rain of organic matter from above, where newer layers of life press down on the old. The weight of accumulation has created its own geology here, with strata of compressed biological material forming hills and valleys, cliffs and plains.

The atmosphere grows denser the deeper one goes, until movement becomes like swimming through syrup. Here, in the profound depths, pressure has created new forms of organization. Creatures merge and split like living droplets, their boundaries determined more by chemical gradients than by membranes. The bone cities in these depths have been compressed into structures of incredible density, their walls shot through with veins of metallic salts that conduct bioelectric currents between districts.

Strange weather systems operate in the largest caverns. Convection currents driven by the heat of decomposition create winds that howl through the bone canyons. Spore storms can rage for weeks, reducing visibility to nothing and coating every surface with new growth. In some regions, the buildup of gases creates pockets of different atmospheric composition, and creatures must adapt their chemistry as they migrate or perish at the boundaries.

The sound here isn't just noise—it's communication, echolocation, and environmental architecture all at once. The Processed navigate by acoustic landmarks, reading the echoes off bone towers and flesh-valleys. Predatory things hunt by vibration, feeling the movement of prey through the packed substrate. Some of the city-organisms have evolved acoustic chambers that amplify and modulate sounds, creating zones of deafening resonance or pockets of eerie silence.

Time moves differently in the Hollowing. Without sun or stars, without tides or seasons, the only rhythms are biological. The cities grow and shift on timescales that would dizzy surface minds. Entire ecosystems rise and fall in waves that take centuries to complete. The Processed migrate in patterns that repeat across millennia, wearing their paths ever deeper into the biological bedrock.

This is a world that has found its own equilibrium, terrible and beautiful in its completeness. Every surface seethes with life, every cubic meter of space is occupied, every resource recycled through bodies and buildings and back again. It is urban in its density and organization, yet utterly alien to any human conception of a city. It is nature without water, evolution without sky, a vast enclosed experiment in what life can become when pressed into itself for epochs uncounted.
