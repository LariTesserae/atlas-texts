The valley stretches wide beneath a perpetual twilight, its newborn edges still sharp where the earth split apart perhaps only centuries ago. Steam rises from a thousand shallow depressions scattered across the broken terrain, each one collecting the night's condensation into temporary pools that vanish by midday. The ground itself is raw volcanic glass and fresh-cracked basalt, black mirrors reflecting the dim sky between patches of pioneer moss that cling to any surface that holds moisture long enough.

Sound carries strangely here—booming and echoing between the valley walls. The source becomes clear as you watch: great flocks of leather-winged creatures burst from cave mouths in the cliffsides, their departure creating thunderclaps that roll across the emptiness. They hunt in shifting clouds, tens of thousands strong, their echolocation clicks combining into a constant static roar that rises and falls like an alien tide.

Between the feeding swarms, the land appears almost empty. A few twisted, moisture-hoarding plants grow in solitary defiance—bulbous things with waxy blue-grey skin that split open during the brief morning fogs to reveal wet, red interiors that trap insects. Their roots snake across the surface rather than down, following the underground steam vents that keep the rock warm enough to condense water from the cool air.

Scattered across this austere expanse, stone cairns mark human attempts at habitation. Simple shelters built from stacked volcanic rock cluster near the more reliable steam vents, their builders long absent. Clay-lined pits beside them still collect condensation, and obsidian flakes litter the ground where someone once knapped tools from the abundant volcanic glass. Hollow gourds hang from poles, primitive fog-catchers that still function, dripping their harvest into stone basins below.

The fauna here have adapted to the acoustic environment. Blind pale things scuttle between rocks, navigating by vibration. Larger predators—long-bodied creatures somewhere between weasel and centipede—hunt by lying perfectly still until the sound-chaos of the bat clouds masks their movement. In the morning calm, you can hear them: the scratch of chitin on stone, the wet sound of something feeding, the sudden silence when a hundred small creatures freeze at once.

The valley's youth shows in its instability. Steam vents open and close unpredictably. Sections of cliff face still occasionally calve away, adding fresh rubble to the valley floor. The earth trembles sometimes, and new cracks spider across the ground, releasing brief jets of mineral-scented vapor. Rain, when it comes, falls in isolated curtains that drift across the landscape like grey ghosts, turning the volcanic dust into slick, treacherous mud that dries within hours into geometric patterns.

At the valley's heart, where several steam fields converge, someone has carved channels in the rock—a primitive aqueduct system that directs the condensation flow. The engineering is simple but clever, using the natural slope and thermal gradients to create reliable water collection points. Around these, the only permanent structures stand: low stone buildings with thick walls and tiny windows, their roofs designed to shed the acidic rain while funneling clean condensation into interior cisterns.

The constant noise has shaped everything here. The scattered human structures use thick walls not for warmth but for sound dampening. Even the abundant fauna have evolved unusual communication methods—pheromone trails, bioluminescent flashes during the darker hours, elaborate dances that can be felt through the ground. The whole valley pulses with life, but it's a strange, vibration-based ecosystem where sound is both hunter and hunted.

As the day cycles, the valley transforms. Morning brings the fog, noon sees the bat clouds disperse to digest their catch in the countless caves, afternoon brings isolated rain showers, and evening sees the emergence of the ground hunters. Through it all, the steam rises, the earth creaks and settles, and the endless acoustic storm continues—a young landscape still deciding what it wants to become, inhabited by creatures and occasional humans clever enough to find abundance in its harsh, noisy emptiness.
