The warren breathes through a thousand mouths.

You descend through one of the entry shafts—a worn stone spiral barely wide enough for shoulders—and emerge into the primary chamber. The ceiling arches overhead like the inside of a clay pot, every surface bearing the telltale striations of patient hands and simple tools. Copper chisels have left their green-black stains in the grooves. The chamber spans perhaps thirty paces across, but the space feels both intimate and infinite, honeycombed as it is with passages leading deeper, sideways, up and around.

Water seeps through hairline cracks in the carved walls, following channels deliberately cut to guide it. The builders understood their stone's grain, working with its natural fissures rather than against them. Where water collects, shallow basins have been hollowed out, each no bigger than a cupped palm. The constant drip-drop creates a percussive rhythm that mingles with other sounds—the scritch and scuttle of countless small lives, the whistle of air through narrow passages, the distant hammering of tools on stone.

The warmth surprises. This deep below ground should be cool, but heat radiates from the very walls. Touch them and you feel the pulse of thermal vents far below, channeled upward through carved ducts. The builders mapped the earth's fever and made it theirs. In some chambers, the heat concentrates enough to crack mud into tiles, to cure clay vessels, to dry strips of meat hung from wooden pegs.

Every surface serves multiple purposes. What seems like decoration—spiraling grooves, geometric patterns, rows of shallow depressions—actually guides water, channels air, holds tools. Wooden stakes jut from walls at precise angles, ready to support woven screens or hanging baskets. Narrow shelves are carved directly into the stone, each one slightly canted to drain moisture. The primitive tech betrays sophisticated understanding: bone needles rest in wall niches beside coils of sinew, stone grinders sit in purpose-carved depressions, wooden mallets hang from leather loops.

The vegetation struggles but persists. In patches where light filters down through ceiling gaps—holes deliberately maintained rather than sealed—scraggly vines cling to carved handholds. Their pale leaves cluster around each light source like moths. Moss grows in the wettest corners, more grey than green. Strange fungi flourish in darker places, their bioluminescent caps providing wan blue-green glows. The builders cultivate these carefully, scraping some areas clean while encouraging growth in others.

But it's the fauna that makes the constant noise. The carved chambers teem with life adapted to this artificial cave system. Beetles the size of thumbs traverse the walls on predetermined paths, following scent trails laid down over generations. Their wing cases click and buzz as they move. Small lizards with oversized eyes hunt them, their quick movements sending echoes through the passages. In the water basins, pale amphibians chirp and trill, their calls amplified by the acoustic properties of the carved spaces.

Larger creatures inhabit the deeper chambers. You hear them more than see them—a shuffling like leather on stone, occasional low hoots that reverberate through multiple passages before reaching you. The builders share their space with these beings, whether by choice or necessity unclear. Claw marks score some walls alongside the tool marks. Certain passages smell of musk and old meat.

The entire complex pulses with activity. Workers chip away at new passages, following veins of softer stone. Others maintain the water channels, clearing mineral deposits with wooden scrapers. Children race through familiar routes, their voices adding to the cacophony. Somewhere deeper, rhythmic pounding suggests major construction—another chamber being hollowed, another level being connected to the rest.

This is not ruins. This is not abandoned. This underground world thrives in its careful balance—human craft working within natural constraints, primitive tools shaping permanent shelter, life adapting to life in close quarters. The builders made every choice deliberately: where to carve and where to leave raw stone, how to guide water without drowning, how to share space with the creatures that help sustain them.

The noise never stops. Water drips, insects buzz, voices call through passages, tools strike stone, creatures scurry and hunt and mate. But it's not chaos. It's the sound of a made world maintaining itself, a total environment where nothing exists without purpose, where primitive means have achieved sophisticated ends. The warren breathes through a thousand mouths, and every breath carries the sound of thriving.
