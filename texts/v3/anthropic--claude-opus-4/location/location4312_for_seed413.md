The cistern breathes steam in the darkness. 

Deep beneath the city's foundations, where the weight of millennia has compressed the bedrock into something between stone and glass, this chamber persists. Water seeps eternal through hairline fractures in the ceiling—not rain, but the condensed breath of the industrial works far above, their heat engines bleeding warmth down through a thousand layers of pipe and stone until it meets the ancient coolness here and weeps.

The space is no larger than a modest room, yet it contains multitudes. Brass pipes thread through the air at all angles, some as thick as torsos, others thin as veins, their surfaces green with verdigris and slick with perpetual condensation. They cluster so densely that moving through the space requires contortion, ducking beneath one while stepping over another, turning sideways to slip between two more. Each pipe carries its own vibration, its own frequency of industrial thrum, and together they create a wall of sound—not quite musical, not quite mechanical, but something between a foghorn's drone and a tuning fork's pure tone, loud enough to feel in your bones.

The floor is a single piece of obsidian-smooth stone, worn by aeons of water into perfect flatness. Shallow depressions mark where droplets have fallen for so long that even stone remembers. The water collects in these hollows, no more than a finger's depth, perfectly still despite the acoustic chaos above. In the darkness—lit only by the faint phosphorescence that bleeds from certain pipes, a ghost-light remnant of industrial processes—these pools become black mirrors, reflecting nothing.

No moss grows here. No slime coats the pipes. The water that drips and pools contains no life, not even the simplest bacteria. The heat and the particular cocktail of industrial runoff have created conditions too specific, too narrow for biology. Instead, mineral deposits form in slow, crystalline patterns where water meets metal, building tiny cities of salt and copper sulfate that will never be inhabited.

The machinery above has run so long that no one remembers its original purpose. The pipes were laid according to plans now lost, carrying steam and stranger vapors between processes that continue purely through momentum. The cistern exists in a pocket of engineering that has achieved perfect stasis—water enters, water evaporates, minerals precipitate, pipes sing their endless chord. Nothing changes because everything is already exactly where it needs to be.

Time moves differently in the din. The sound is so constant, so overwhelming, that it becomes a kind of silence. The darkness is so complete that phantom colors bloom behind closed eyelids. The warmth and humidity are so uniform that the body forgets its own boundaries. This is a place outside of life, outside of death, outside of change—a mechanical womb that will never give birth, humming its industrial lullaby until the city above finally forgets to feed it steam, and maybe long after that, running on the ghost of habit alone.

The only way in is through a maintenance hatch that no one maintains, down a ladder whose rungs are worn smooth as the floor below. The only reason to come is to confirm that nothing has changed. Nothing ever has. Nothing ever will. The cistern breathes its steam and sings its chord and keeps its own counsel in the darkness, a perfect machine for producing nothing but its own continuation.
