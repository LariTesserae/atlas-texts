The plateau city spreads across ancient stone, a thousand feet of weathered granite worn smooth as skin by eons of patient ice. Here, where the air thins and bites with perpetual cold, generations have carved their settlement into the very bones of the mountain.

No trees mark this height—only the endless grey-white expanse of stone, pocked with pools of snowmelt that gather in the shallow depressions left by grinding glaciers. These pools freeze and thaw with the subtle seasons, their surfaces crackling with delicate geometries of ice. The water seeps through hairline cracks, feeding the only life that persists here: films of pale lichen that paint abstract patterns across the rock, and in the deepest crevices, colonies of phosphorescent fungi that pulse with wan light during the interminable twilights.

The city itself is more excavation than construction. Countless generations have hollowed out the plateau, creating a honeycomb of chambers and passages that descend into surprising warmth. The surface shows only the caps of this buried metropolis—stone chimneys releasing threads of smoke, covered walkways connecting domed entrance pavilions, and the great carved channels that direct the precious meltwater into cisterns below. Everything is built low, hugging the ground against the constant wind that keens across the tableland.

Within the rock, the city transforms into something organic despite its mineral nature. Tunnels branch and merge following the stone's natural grain, widening into markets and gathering halls, narrowing into private warrens. The walls are polished smooth by countless passing hands, and in places the original tool marks—simple iron chisels and patient hammering—still show the work of the first settlers who began this great hollowing.

The density of life here defies the barren surface. Every cubic meter of excavated space serves multiple purposes: sleeping alcoves carved into the walls of workshops, mushroom gardens in the damp lower levels, corridors that double as social spaces where neighbors gather around braziers of imported coal. The sound is constant—a reverberating chorus of hammering, conversation, footsteps, and water dripping through channels, all contained and amplified by the stone until the mountain itself seems to hum with habitation.

The fauna have adapted to this vertical world. Pale blind cave fish swim in the underground cisterns, their populations carefully managed. Colonies of white bats roost in the highest chambers, emerging at dusk to hunt the few insects that survive at this elevation. Most numerous are the stone mice—small, grey creatures with enlarged ears and eyes, that live on fungus and food scraps, their squeaking a constant counterpoint to the human sounds.

Time moves differently here. The city grows by increments—a new chamber excavated over months, a water channel deepened by a finger's width each year. The plateau itself changes imperceptibly, losing perhaps a hand's breadth of height each millennium to wind and frost. Even the weather follows ancient patterns: mornings bring mist that rises from the warmer depths to condense on the cold stone, afternoons see fitful snow or sleet, and the nights are clear and bitter, the stars seeming close enough to touch in the thin air.

The technology is simple but perfectly adapted. Iron tools, worn smooth with use, chip away at the stone. Clever arrangements of mirrors and polished surfaces channel what sunlight penetrates the depths. Water wheels, turned by the controlled flow from the cisterns, grind grain and pump air through the deeper passages. Everything is maintained, repaired, passed down—nothing is discarded in this place where every resource is precious.

At the edges of the plateau, the world falls away into cloud and distance. Other peaks emerge like islands from the white sea below, some showing their own signs of habitation—smoke, carved terraces, the glint of mirror signals on clear days. But each mountain-city is its own world, connected by treacherous paths that only the bravest traders navigate with their sure-footed pack animals.

This is a place of endurance made livable, where the human need for shelter and community has transformed dead stone into something almost alive, where the absence of conventional life has been filled with a different kind of ecosystem—one of carved spaces and adapted creatures, of patient craft and accumulated wisdom, all balanced on the ancient backbone of the world.
