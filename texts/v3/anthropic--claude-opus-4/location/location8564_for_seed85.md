The plateau city of Khora-Veth stretches across a vast mineral shelf, its primitive stone towers rising like broken teeth from deposits of crystallized salt and sulfur. The air shimmers with heat even in the perpetual twilight—the sun here is wrong somehow, a dim coal that never fully rises or sets, caught forever behind veils of mineral dust that spiral up from the planet's exposed mantle.

The settlement sprawls loosely across leagues of cracked stone, connected by rope bridges and carved channels rather than proper roads. Buildings cluster around natural chimneys where warm gases vent from below, their walls built from fitted blocks of compressed ash and salt-stone, mortared with a resinous paste the inhabitants harvest from certain caves. The architecture follows no master plan—structures lean at odd angles, buttressed by poles of calcified wood from the scattered thornbrakes that somehow draw moisture from the stone itself.

Between the building clusters, the landscape opens into vast courts of bare rock, some naturally smooth, others showing the scars of recent upheaval where the ground has buckled and split. Steam hisses from fresh cracks, and in places the stone glows dull orange where magma wells close to the surface. The inhabitants have learned to read these signs, abandoning whole districts when the tremors speak of imminent eruption, only to rebuild atop the fresh volcanic glass once it cools.

The fauna here defies easy categorization. Creatures of living mineral scuttle across the hot stone—things like crabs but built of articulated crystal, feeding on sulfur deposits and leaving trails of vitrified sand. Larger beasts lumber between the buildings: amalgamations of flesh and stone that the inhabitants have half-tamed, using them to haul blocks and turn the great grinding wheels that crush ore. In the thermal vents, colonies of something between coral and lichen grow with visible speed, harvested daily by workers who must constantly trim them back lest they overwhelm the settlement.

The dim light casts everything in gradients of ash and ember. Colors here tend toward the volcanic—blacks and grays punctuated by veins of yellow sulfur, patches of red iron oxide, and the occasional shocking blue of copper compounds. The inhabitants daub their buildings with these mineral pigments in patterns that shift and change, as different districts compete in silent, chromatic warfare.

Sound travels strangely in Khora-Veth. The dense air muffles distant noises while amplifying the close—the tap of a hammer might die within yards while the whisper of steam carries for miles. The settlement maintains an eerie quiet broken by sudden cacophonies: the crack of splitting stone, the shriek of crystal-crabs fighting over territory, the low harmonic thrum that rises from the depths during the frequent quakes.

The city exists in constant flux. Buildings collapse and are rebuilt. Entire neighborhoods migrate as the geological activity shifts. The rope bridges are taken down each night and restrung each morning along new paths as the ground warps and settles. There is a strange democracy to this chaos—no permanent authority can establish itself when the very ground refuses stability. Instead, the inhabitants follow an elaborate system of colored banners and carved markers, a primitive but effective way of coordinating their endless dance with the turbulent earth.

Water exists here only as vapor, captured in specialized chambers where the inhabitants have learned to condense it from the volcanic gases. They trade this precious distillate in tiny vials, and their most sacred rituals involve the sharing of a single drop placed on the tongue. Their bodies have adapted—skin like leather, eyes that barely need to blink, organs that recycle every trace of moisture.

The twilight never lifts, the ground never stills, and the city never sleeps. Khora-Veth pulses with a strange vitality, its primitive tools and simple structures constantly tested against a landscape that refuses to be tamed. It is a place of endless making and unmaking, where the boundary between the built environment and the volcanic chaos beneath grows thinner with each passing hour, and where life persists through sheer, stubborn ingenuity.
