The turbines never stop screaming.

High in the mountain col, where frost-shattered granite meets the endless dark, a grove of industrial windmills churns through air thick with condensation. Each tower stands alone, separated by hundreds of meters of sparse alpine meadow—tough grasses and cushion plants that grip the thin soil between weathered stone. The vegetation grows lush despite the darkness, fed by something other than sunlight, drawing sustenance from the perpetual mists that roll up from unseen valleys below.

The windmills are old, their concrete bases stained with decades of rust-bleed from the steel lattice towers. Some lean at subtle angles, foundations undermined by freeze-thaw cycles and the slow creep of saturated earth. But their blades still turn, still shriek metal against metal where bearings have worn through their housings. The sound builds and layers—a dozen turbines at different speeds, different stages of mechanical decay, creating a wall of noise that makes thought difficult, sleep impossible.

Between the towers, the meadow breathes with strange fertility. Phosphorescent mosses coat the north faces of erratic boulders, while thick mats of alpine herbs release clouds of spores when disturbed by the wind. The plants grow in radiating patterns around each turbine base, as if the vibrations traveling through the ground stimulate some deep metabolic process. Their leaves are unusually large for this elevation, fleshy and pale, adapted to harvest energy from sources unknown.

Water seeps everywhere despite the lack of visible streams. It beads on the tower struts, drips from blade tips, pools in granite hollows. The air tastes of iron and ozone. Sometimes the condensation falls as a fine rain that coats everything in a slick film, making the service ladders treacherous. Other times it freezes into hoarfrost that builds into fantastic crystalline structures on the windward sides of the turbines, only to shatter and fall when the blades' vibrations reach some critical resonance.

The darkness is absolute—not the darkness of night, which promises dawn, but something more fundamental. No stars pierce this black. No horizon lightens. The only illumination comes from the bioluminescent flora and the occasional spark where metal grinds against metal in the turbine nacelles. These brief flashes reveal the true scale of the installation: dozens more towers extending into the murk, their positions marked only by the doppler shift of their screaming.

A single service road, little more than crushed stone and determination, switchbacks up from somewhere below. Halfway up sits a concrete blockhouse, windowless, its steel door hanging open on broken hinges. Inside, banks of mechanical gauges and analog meters still twitch with readings—wind speed, RPM, kilowatt hours feeding into cables that disappear into the ground. The needles dance frantically, suggesting tremendous energy generation, though where that power goes and what it feeds remains obscure.

The turbulence here is not of wind alone. The very space seems unstable, as if the combined vibrations of all these machines have loosened something fundamental. Patches of meadow suddenly erupt with growth—plants shooting up meters in seconds before withering just as quickly. The mist forms impossible vortices, spiraling upward in defiance of air pressure and temperature gradients. Sometimes the sound cuts out entirely for a heartbeat, leaving a silence so profound it feels like falling, before crashing back doubled.

This is an ending place, or perhaps a beginning. A high mountain installation where industry met something it couldn't name, adapted to serve purposes no longer remembered. The turbines harvest more than wind now. They process the darkness itself, transform it into something the strange plants can use, create conditions where life persists in defiance of sunlight and sense. And through it all, that endless mechanical shriek—not a complaint, but a kind of singing, a hymn to the marriage of the technological and the inexplicable.

In the spaces between the towers, where the sound overlaps and cancels in rare nodes of quiet, you can almost hear something else. A deeper thrumming, geological in scale. The mountain itself responding to all these years of vibration, beginning to resonate at its own frequency. Beginning, perhaps, to wake.
