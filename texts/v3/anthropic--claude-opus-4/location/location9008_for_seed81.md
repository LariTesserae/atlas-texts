The Singing Plateau stretches beyond comprehension, a mineral expanse of weathered stone that has forgotten the meaning of life. At this altitude, the air is thin and cool, carrying a perpetual dampness that never quite commits to rain but manifests as shifting veils of mist that race across the endless rock face.

The city—if it can be called that—is carved from the plateau itself. Millennia of patient excavation have hollowed out a vast honeycomb of chambers and passages, leaving behind a skeletal framework of stone pillars and arches that stretch upward for hundreds of meters. The emptiness between these structures is profound; you could walk for hours through the carved canyons without encountering another solid wall. The builders are long gone, leaving only their negative space behind.

What makes this place alive despite its lifelessness is the wind. The plateau's immense scale creates its own weather systems—powerful currents that howl through the hollow city with voices that shift from bass rumbles to piercing shrieks. The ancient architects understood this: every carved surface, every arch and opening, has been shaped to catch and modulate these winds. The entire city is an instrument played by the atmosphere itself.

The sound is overwhelming. Deep thrums shake the stone beneath your feet while high keening notes spiral overhead. Harmonics build and clash as different wind systems collide, creating acoustic storms that can last for days. The mist carries these sounds even further, acting as a medium that makes the air itself seem to vibrate with phantom frequencies.

Despite the brightness—the plateau sits above most cloud cover, and the bare rock reflects light with startling intensity—there's something unsettling about this place. Perhaps it's the way the carved chambers seem to breathe with the wind, or how the acoustic patterns sometimes resolve into almost-recognizable rhythms, as if the city were trying to remember a song it once knew. Water collects in carved basins during the mistier periods, creating temporary mirrors that reflect the endless stone geometries before evaporating again.

The turbulence never stops. Wind-carved dust devils dance between the pillars, and the very air seems to churn with invisible currents. Sometimes the acoustic pressure builds to such intensity that it creates visible distortions, ripples in the air that make the distant structures waver like mirages. The city exists in a state of constant flux—not in its physical form, which erosion has long since stabilized, but in its sonic architecture, which rebuilds itself with every shift in wind direction.

This is a place of pure geometry and sound, where the absence of life has become its own kind of presence, where emptiness has been crafted into something monumental, and where the planet itself has become both the musician and the instrument.
