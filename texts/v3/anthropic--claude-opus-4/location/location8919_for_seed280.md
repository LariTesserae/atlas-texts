The Thermal Columns rise from an ancient seabed that never knew air. Here, two kilometers beneath the surface, pressure has sculpted water into something almost architectural—vertical corridors of heated brine that shimmer like pillars of molten glass, each one a perfect thermal gradient maintained by the engineered vents below.

The seafloor itself is bare calcite and compressed sediment, polished smooth by millennia of slow currents. No algae stains these surfaces, no coral builds upon them. The only interruptions in the mineral plain are the vents themselves—crystalline structures that pulse with a faint internal light, their geometries too perfect for nature, too ancient for any known civilization. Each vent stands alone, separated by hundreds of meters of empty seafloor, their isolation deliberate and absolute.

Between these columns, the water stratifies into distinct layers visible only where they meet. The boundaries shimmer like heat mirages turned liquid, each layer maintaining its own temperature, salinity, and viscosity through mechanisms that shouldn't persist in open water. The technology that maintains these impossible gradients hums at frequencies below hearing, felt only as a subtle wrongness in the bones.

Life thrives here, but not as surface dwellers would recognize it. Translucent ribbons of tissue drift between the thermal layers, their bodies essentially two-dimensional, feeding on temperature differentials themselves. Schools of creatures like living prisms refract the dim light from the vents, splitting the pale glow into brief rainbows that die in the darkness. Larger forms patrol the edges of the warm zones—serpentine things with crystalline teeth that hunt by detecting minute pressure changes in the stratified water.

The scattered structures that dot this landscape are minimal but profound. Smooth domes of unknown material emerge from the sediment, each no larger than a dwelling but connected by threads of light that pulse through the seafloor like neural pathways. Some have openings—not doors but perfect circles that maintain their shape despite the crushing pressure, holding back the water with fields that feel like stepping through thick air.

Inside these refuges, the water becomes something else—still liquid but behaving with different rules. Objects settle upward. Light bends in helixes. The temperature maintains itself at precisely blood-heat regardless of depth or season. Equipment left by long-gone occupants still functions, though its purposes remain opaque: panels that project three-dimensional mandalas of flowing data, surfaces that respond to touch with patterns of warmth and cold, containers that hold volumes larger than their exteriors suggest.

The silence here is profound but not empty. The water carries vibrations too low for human ears—the grinding of tectonic plates far below, the microscopic crackling of mineral precipitation, the coordinated movements of the ribbon-creatures that pulse in patterns too complex for coincidence. Sometimes, the vents emit harmonics that make the water itself sing in frequencies that bypass the ears entirely, resonating directly in the fluid of the inner ear, creating phantom sounds that seem to come from within.

Above, the water column extends into blackness. The surface might be a kilometer away or ten—distance becomes meaningless in the unchanging pressure and darkness. Time moves differently here, measured not in days but in the slow cycles of the thermal columns as they pulse warmer and cooler in rhythms that might span centuries. 

The landscape is both eternal and transitional, a place where advanced technology has become geology, where life has adapted to feed on physics itself, where the boundary between the built environment and the natural world has dissolved into something entirely other. It is a monument to a civilization that understood water not as a medium but as a canvas, and painted upon it with tools we can barely perceive, let alone comprehend.
