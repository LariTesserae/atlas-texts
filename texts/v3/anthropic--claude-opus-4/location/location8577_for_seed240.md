The salt flats stretch endlessly, their cracked polygons of white crust interrupted by seeps where dark water bleeds through. This is a drowned place that forgot how to stay drowned—an ancient seabed that rose and fell and rose again, carrying its memories of ocean in mineral veins that weep brackish tears through the earth's skin.

The heat shimmers wrong here. It bends not just light but sound, so that the distant becomes near and the near becomes distant. A bird's cry from miles away arrives before the bird itself appears—sometimes hours before, sometimes days. The creatures have learned to live with this temporal smearing, building their lives around echoes of events that haven't happened yet or happened long ago.

Water pools everywhere despite the heat, emerging from hairline fractures in the salt pan. These seeps never dry, fed by some deep aquifer that pulses with tidal memory. The pools are perfectly circular, each one exactly seventeen feet across—a measurement that corresponds to nothing, means nothing, yet repeats with disturbing precision across the entire expanse. In these pools, the water is so dense with minerals it holds objects at its surface like solid glass.

What vegetation exists grows in defiance of logic. Crystalline succulents with translucent leaves cluster near the water's edge, their roots extending not down but sideways, following the salt crust's surface in perfect spirals. They photosynthesize moonlight as readily as sunlight, their metabolism following rhythms that don't match any earthly cycle. During certain hours—never the same hours twice—they sing, their leaves vibrating at frequencies that make the pools ripple in geometric patterns.

The fauna here exists in impossible abundance for such sparse vegetation. Creatures emerge from the pools themselves—not born but precipitated, like salt crystals forming in reverse. Serpentine things with too many joints undulate across the flats, leaving trails that spell out mathematical equations in the salt. Birds with hollow bones filled with helium nest in the air itself, their eggs floating on invisible thermals, hatching mid-flight.

The largest inhabitants are the salt deer—pale, translucent things whose bodies refract light into rainbow halos. They graze on the mineral crust itself, their teeth grinding salt into finer salt, their stomachs performing unknown alchemies. When they run, they leave hoofprints that fill immediately with water from below, creating temporary pools that last exactly forty-nine minutes before seeping back into the earth.

Sound here operates by its own physics. The grinding of salt underfoot might emerge as orchestral music. The wind carries conversations from other times—sometimes future traders discussing routes across the flats, sometimes the dying words of ancient sea creatures settling into sediment. The loudest sounds come from below: a constant groaning and shifting, as if the earth itself is trying to remember what shape it should take.

Time moves strangely across this expanse. A journey that should take hours stretches into days, while weeks compress into moments. Travelers report arriving at their destinations before they left, or finding their own footprints leading in directions they haven't walked yet. The sun tracks across the sky in reliable arcs, but shadows fall according to different laws, sometimes pointing toward light sources that don't exist, sometimes pooling like liquid in defiance of any illumination.

Weather here is purely local. Rains fall in perfect columns, soaking single pools while leaving the surrounding salt bone dry. These rains taste of metals unknown to chemistry, and where they fall, the pools briefly become windows—showing depths far greater than their shallow basins should allow, revealing spaces that exist perpendicular to normal dimensions.

The salt flats pulse with a slow, geological heartbeat. Every ninety-three years, they exhale—a great sighing that lifts clouds of mineral dust into the air, where it hangs for months, creating a second sky of glittering particles. During these exhalations, the rules break down further. Gravity becomes negotiable. The pools flow upward in spirals. The fauna learns temporary flight, not through wings but through pure conviction.

This is a place between states—between sea and land, between past and future, between real and theoretical. It exists in the gap between what was and what might be, a landscape that treats possibility as a physical force as real as erosion or heat. Here, the world's fabric wears thin, and through the worn places, other orders leak through, leaving their salt-crystal signatures in the endless, weeping white.
