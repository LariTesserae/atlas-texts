The salt flats stretch endlessly beneath a sky so luminous it burns white at the edges of vision. This is a drowned world that refuses to admit it—water seeps up through crystalline crusts everywhere, creating a landscape of ten thousand shallow mirrors that reflect the harsh light back upon itself until the air itself seems to ring with brightness.

The ground is a tessellation of polygons where mineral deposits have dried and cracked and dried again over eons, each cell rimmed with ridges of accumulated salts—halite, gypsum, ancient borates—that rise like tiny mountain ranges no higher than your ankle. Between these ridges, the water pools mere millimeters deep, so perfectly still it seems solid until you step and send ripples racing outward in perfect circles. The water is wrong somehow—too clear, too heavy, with a surface tension that makes it bead and roll like mercury.

Sparse vegetation punctuates this mineral plain: lone stalks of some adapted grass that grows in perfect spirals, their leaves fused into tubes that whistle in harmonics when even the slightest air moves through them. But there is no wind here—hasn't been for centuries—so what makes them sing? The sound builds from nowhere, a chord that starts subsonic and climbs through registers until it becomes a physical pressure against your eardrums. The plants themselves are glassy, translucent, their internal structures visible like botanical diagrams made real. Moisture condenses on their surfaces and runs down in careful channels to pool at their bases, where the roots spread in perfect radial patterns just beneath the salt crust.

The deafening sound comes from the water itself. Each pool resonates at its own frequency, determined by its precise depth and mineral content. They sing to each other across the flats—a compositional language of overtones and interference patterns that builds into a wall of sound so dense it has texture, so loud it becomes a kind of silence. You feel it more than hear it, vibrating through your bones, making your teeth ache.

Scattered across this acoustic landscape are the remnants of something that tried to live here once. Not buildings exactly, but accumulations—places where someone or something gathered materials with intent. Pillars of fused salt rise here and there, their surfaces carved with erosion patterns that might be deliberate, might be natural. Some have hollow cores where the sound resonates differently, creating pockets of relative quiet or sudden amplification. Stone cairns mark spots where the water wells up stronger, their rocks worn smooth and impregnated with minerals that make them glow faintly in the overwhelming light.

The bright illumination comes not just from above but from below—the salt crystals trap and redirect light in ways that defy simple optics. Photons bounce between crystal faces, building in intensity, creating a luminescence that persists even when clouds pass overhead. The few shadows that exist are wrong, falling in directions that don't match any visible light source, as if the landscape remembers the shadows of things that no longer exist.

No animals move here. No insects buzz. No birds wheel overhead. The only motion is the slow seepage of water through crystal matrices, the only life these architectural plants that seem more mineral than vegetable. Even microbes find little purchase in the hypersaline pools, leaving the water preternaturally clear, sterile, perfect.

Time has stopped here, or rather, time moves in circles. The water rises and falls on rhythms measured in centuries, but always returns to the same level. The salt polygons crack and heal and crack again in identical patterns. The sound builds and releases, builds and releases, a breathing that never varies. This is a landscape caught in an endless loop of its own making, where every process has found its perfect equilibrium and locked into place.

The strangeness is in the details: the way certain pools reflect not the sky above but some other sky, purple-green and starless. The way the spiral plants sometimes catch and hold sounds from somewhere else—distant voices, mechanical noise, music in scales that hurt to perceive. The way your footprints fill not with water but with a fluid that climbs upward, forming temporary sculptures that hold your shape for exactly seventeen heartbeats before collapsing back to flatness.

This is erosion's endpoint, weathering's final form—a landscape worn down not to nothing but to its essential nature, where every grain and drop has found its place and stays there, singing its one note in the deafening symphony of stasis.
