The Cistern Quarter rises from the mountain's shoulder like a fever dream of iron and steam. Here, where the city climbed too high and the air grew too thin for proper rain, they built their water-catchers: a dense maze of collecting surfaces, condensation towers, and weeping walls that trap every drop from the perpetual mist rolling up from the valleys below.

The foundations are young volcanic stone, still sharp-edged and black, carved hastily into terraces when the lower wells ran dry just two generations ago. Each terrace bristles with infrastructure—pipes and gutters, steam vents and collection tanks, all pressed together in the urgent density of a place that exists for a single purpose. The industrial apparatus hums and clanks without rest: pumps drawing moisture from the air, boilers cycling condensation, the endless drip-drum-splash of water finding its level through a thousand channels.

Between the machinery, the fauna has adapted with startling abundance. Clouds of midges hover in the permanent humidity. Tunnel-weavers string their silk between warm pipes, their webs heavy with condensation that drips into collection trays below. The cistern newts—pale, nearly transparent—swarm through the shallow channels, their populations exploding in this artificial wetland. Moss-eating beetles clatter across the damp stones in such numbers that their collective movement creates a constant chittering susurrus beneath the mechanical din.

The sparse vegetation tells the story of altitude and industry: only the hardiest moisture-lovers cling to life here. Slick yellow lichens paint the volcanic rock. Thin ropes of fountain grass sprout from cracks where the steam-warmed water collects. A few stunted cloud-pines lean from gaps between buildings, their roots cracking through pipes, their needles black with soot.

The warmth is artificial but pervasive—waste heat from the condensation engines, steam leaking from badly sealed joints, the collected thermal mass of a thousand boilers running day and night. It creates its own weather: the natural mountain chill fights the industrial fever, birthing tiny thunderstorms that flicker between the towers, adding their water to the harvest.

Light barely penetrates this dense quarter. The mist dims the sun to a pale disk, while the narrow passages between collection towers create perpetual twilight at street level. Sputtering gas lamps mark the main thoroughfares, their yellow glow reflecting off wet surfaces in endless recursion. The workers navigate more by sound than sight—each pump station has its own rhythm, each overflow its particular splash pattern.

The soundscape is overwhelming: water in all its voices, from the soprano tinkle of condensation drops to the bass roar of main collection channels. Steam hisses. Pumps thump. Pipes groan under pressure. The fauna adds its own layer—the high whine of midges, the skittering of beetles, the plop of newts dropping into cisterns. Human voices, when they come, are swallowed by this aqueous cacophony.

Change comes slowly here despite the constant motion of water. The volcanic stone erodes grain by grain under the constant moisture. Pipes rust and are patched rather than replaced. The same families have tended the same pump stations for decades, passing down knowledge of which valves stick, which collectors work best in which weather. Even the fauna populations, while numerous, maintain steady equilibriums in their artificial niches.

This is a place of beautiful ugliness, where necessity has created its own ecosystem. The Cistern Quarter may be industrial, crowded, and forever damp, but it pulses with a life uniquely its own—a vertical marsh of metal and stone where the mountain's mist becomes the city's lifeblood, drop by precious drop.
