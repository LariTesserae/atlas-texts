The drowned city spreads beneath forty meters of clear, cold water. Its stone streets run between buildings of fitted coral blocks and carved limestone, their surfaces softened by centuries of submersion but still holding their essential forms. The architecture speaks of patient craft—walls built thick and solid, joined without mortar in patterns that lock each piece to its neighbors. Arched doorways and windows pierce the structures at regular intervals, now serving as passages for the slow currents that breathe through the city.

Kelp forests have claimed the upper stories. Their massive holdfasts grip the building tops where sunlight still penetrates, sending up thick stipes that sway in the submarine breeze. The fronds form a canopy twenty meters below the surface, filtering the light into constantly shifting patterns of gold and green. Below this living ceiling, smaller seaweeds carpet every horizontal surface—delicate red algae painting the old market squares, colonies of sea lettuce draping from balconies, gardens of anemones blooming in what were once public fountains.

The streets themselves have become canyon-like channels between the buildings. The original paving stones remain visible beneath a thin layer of sediment, their surfaces colonized by encrusting coralline algae that adds subtle pinks and purples to the grey stone. Where the streets widen into plazas, the remnants of careful urban planning reveal themselves—raised platforms that once held statues, circular depressions that might have been reflecting pools, the foundations of what could have been market stalls or gathering pavilions.

The water here maintains a steady coolness, hovering just above the point where it would become uncomfortable. It tastes faintly of minerals leached from the stone, with the clean salinity of deep ocean rather than the biological richness of shallows. Visibility extends perhaps thirty meters on clear days, when suspended particles settle and the kelp canopy parts to admit columns of sunlight.

Sound travels strangely in this submerged metropolis. The city holds a fundamental quiet—no voices, no footsteps, no clatter of daily life. Instead, there's the subtle creaking of kelp stipes flexing in the current, the occasional tick and pop of settling stones, the whisper of water moving through the countless openings in the architecture. These sounds seem both muffled and clarified by the water, creating an acoustic environment of intimate distances and surprising clarities.

The infrastructure reveals itself in subtle ways. Channels cut into the streets once carried fresh water or waste; now they guide the prevailing currents in predictable patterns through the city. Stone bridges arch over these channels, their undersides hosting gardens of filter-feeding organisms. Cylindrical structures that might have been wells or cisterns now serve as vertical habitats, their depths home to different communities at each level of light penetration.

The density of construction varies by district. Near what must have been the center, buildings crowd close, separated by streets barely wide enough for two people to pass. Multi-story structures show evidence of shared walls and interconnected spaces. Further out, the buildings space themselves more generously, with what might have been gardens or courtyards between them—spaces now thick with seagrass meadows that ripple in the current.

Few fish inhabit these streets. The occasional school of small silver fish flashes through the kelp canopy, and solitary predators sometimes cruise the deeper boulevards, but the city feels notably empty of mobile life. The true inhabitants are the sessile organisms—the barnacles studding the cornerstones, the sea urchins methodically grazing paths across the algae-covered walls, the ghostly sea pens rising from accumulated sediment in doorways.

Time moves differently here. The tides breathe through the city twice daily, but their effects feel muted at this depth. Seasons announce themselves through subtle shifts in water temperature and the reproductive cycles of the kelp. Years leave their marks in the slow accumulation of growth on the buildings—each structure becoming more itself and less itself simultaneously, as marine life adds new textures while gradually eroding the original edges.

The city's original builders left few clues about their departure. No sudden catastrophe seems to have befallen them—no scorch marks, no tumbled walls, no scattered belongings. The city simply appears to have been evacuated and then claimed by the rising waters, as if the submersion was anticipated, perhaps even planned for. The careful construction, built to last without metal fasteners or advanced materials, has served the city well in its aquatic afterlife. The buildings stand as solid beneath the waves as they must have stood in air, their stones gradually becoming substrate for new forms of architecture—the living, growing structures of kelp and coral that may, in time, completely transform this place while preserving its essential geometry.
