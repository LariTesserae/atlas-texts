You descend through layers of green twilight, where the distinction between water and vegetation has long since dissolved. This is the Drowning Garden—a vast aquatic jungle that spans continents beneath warm seas, where plant and liquid have merged into something neither fully one nor the other.

The water here is thick with life, so saturated with microscopic algae and dissolved plant matter that it moves like honey, tinted emerald and gold. Massive kelp-trees rise from the seafloor kilometers below, their trunks as wide as city blocks, their hollow cores creating vertical rivers that cycle nutrients between the depths and the shallows. These are not mere plants but living architecture—their tissues have evolved chambers and passages, galleries where the water moves in specific patterns, creating pressure differentials that sing.

The sound is overwhelming. The kelp-trees groan and whistle as they sway, their internal waterways creating organ-pipe resonances that travel for miles. Smaller vegetation—carpets of neural moss, forests of filter-fronds, meadows of pearl-weed—add their own voices: clicks and buzzes as gas bladders adjust to pressure, the crackling of rapid growth, the sussuration of billions of leaves processing the thick water.

Between the plant titans swim creatures that blur the line between animal and vegetation. Eel-vines undulate past, their bodies part root system, part muscle. School-flowers drift in synchronised bouquets, each petal a separate organism. The abundant fauna here have adapted to navigate the dense plant matter—some with bodies like living scissors, others that flow like liquid between the stems. Photosynthetic sharks cruise the middle depths, their skin green with symbiotic algae, while below them, spider-orchids walk across seafloor meadows on root-legs, hunting the crawling seeds that scuttle through the undergrowth.

The landscape is young in geological terms—these seas formed only thousands of years ago when the planet's ice caps collapsed catastrophically. The drowned mountains below still show sharp ridges and fresh rockfaces, though they're being rapidly colonized by the explosive plant growth. In places, you can see the remains of the before-times: a radio tower now serving as a trellis for climbing kelp, its metal slowly being digested by acid-secreting vines. A shopping mall, its skylights long since shattered, has become a nursery for juvenile forest-jellies. These scattered ruins are being absorbed, transformed into calcium and iron that feed the endless growth.

The warmth drives everything here. Volcanic vents on the seafloor—part of the same geological upheaval that brought the floods—maintain the seas at blood temperature. This creates constant convection cycles, vertical currents that the plants have learned to manipulate. The kelp-trees position their fronds to channel these flows, creating aerial highways for seed-dispersal and hunting grounds for the tendril-fishers that dangle sticky threads into the express lanes.

As you move through this space, the density becomes oppressive and nurturing at once. Every cubic meter contains thousands of organisms—from the microscopic phytoplankton to the occasional passage of a grove-whale, its barnacled back supporting an entire mobile ecosystem. The water itself feels alive, responsive, as if the boundary between organism and environment has dissolved. Currents shift as you pass, plants reaching toward the heat of your body, curious fauna investigating this strange visitor to their liquid forest.

In the dim green light, bioluminescence provides navigation. The neural moss flashes in waves, transmitting information across vast distances in patterns that might be communication or might simply be the ecosystem's way of regulating itself. Beacon-fruits glow from the branches of the kelp-trees, their light attracting clouds of pollinating fish whose scales scatter the glow into prismatic halos.

This is a world in constant motion—growth and decay, hunting and hiding, the slow dance of the kelp-trees and the quick dart of predators through their fronds. Detritus falls constantly from above like green snow, feeding the communities below, while gases bubble up from decomposition, creating false air pockets where unwary air-breathing visitors might gasp in methane and hydrogen sulfide instead of oxygen.

The Drowning Garden extends beyond the horizon in every direction, a planet-girdling ecosystem that has its own weather—plant-storms where growth explosions trigger chemical cascades, algae-blizzards that reduce visibility to zero, clear-water high pressure systems that allow shafts of sunlight to penetrate to previously dark depths, triggering feeding frenzies. Somewhere in the far distance, perhaps, there might be islands where the kelp-trees breach the surface, their crowns spreading into the air, creating floating forests where the rules change again.

But here, in the warm depths, surrounded by the choir of living sound, wrapped in water that breathes with the pulse of countless organisms, you float in the densest expression of life the planet has ever known—a place where the flood that ended one world has birthed something stranger and more vital than what came before.
