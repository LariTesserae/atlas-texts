The Singing Scarps rise impossibly high, their bone-white cliffs catching the relentless light that pours down from a sky too bright to look at directly. These are not mountains in any conventional sense—they are vertical prairies, great walls of porous stone that have been hollowed and channeled by millennia of wind until they've become vast organ pipes stretching up into the thin atmosphere.

The sound never stops. Each gust of wind sets the entire landscape thrumming, a deafening chorus that ranges from deep, chest-rattling bass notes in the largest channels to piercing whistles through the smaller perforations. The scarps have reached a kind of perfect erosion, where no new holes form and no old ones close—the stone has found its final song and will sing it forever.

Water exists here only in the morning, when clouds driven up from distant lowlands condense against the cold stone faces. For a few hours, thin rivulets trace down the vertical surfaces, pooling in tiny pockets before evaporating in the harsh light. These brief wet moments are everything.

In those pockets, clinging to crevices and ledges no wider than a hand, grows a sparse collection of primitive plants—leather-leafed things that can survive on morning dew alone, their roots working deep into the porous stone. They never flourish, never spread beyond their tiny domains, but they persist with a stubborn permanence.

The fauna has adapted to this vertical world in startling abundance. Clouds of membrane-winged creatures ride the updrafts, their bodies evolved to be living kites that barely need to flap. They nest in the holes of the singing stones, their calls lost in the greater symphony. Long-limbed climbers with suction-pad feet traverse the cliff faces, feeding on the pocket plants and the insects that pollinate them. Most remarkable are the filter-feeders—sessile animals that look more like sea anemones than anything terrestrial, their feathery appendages extended into the wind to catch whatever organic matter drifts by.

The brightness is overwhelming. With no trees or overhangs to provide shade, the white stone reflects and amplifies the sunlight until the entire landscape seems to glow. Shadows are sharp but small, existing only in the deepest recesses of the wind-carved channels. The animals here have either evolved transparent eyelids or learned to navigate by sound alone.

No one builds here. The constant noise would drive any thinking creature mad, and the vertical nature of the terrain defies conventional construction. Occasionally, one might find a climber's rope—woven from plant fibers and weighted with carved bone—left behind by some brave or foolish soul who attempted to traverse the scarps. These primitive tools are quickly bleached by the sun and incorporated into birds' nests.

The scale is dizzying. The scarps extend for many miles in either direction, a white wall dividing the world. From certain vantage points, you can see how they curve slightly, as if they were the rim of some unimaginably vast crater. The vertical prairie ecosystems repeat with subtle variations—here a section where the holes are larger and the notes deeper, there a stretch where the stone is slightly yellower and the vegetation even sparser.

Time feels strange here. The erosion has stopped, the ecology has stabilized, the sound never varies except in its endless improvisation on the same themes. A visitor could return after a hundred years and find everything exactly as they left it—the same plants in the same crevices, the same flight patterns of the same species of aerial fauna, the same deafening song pouring from the same white stones under the same brilliant sky. It is a place that has finished becoming what it is, and will remain so until the world itself changes around it.
