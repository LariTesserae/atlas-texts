The drowned factory floor stretches endlessly beneath forty meters of black water, its corroded assembly lines vanishing into the murk like the ribs of some mechanical leviathan. This is no ordinary flood—the water itself defies physics, hanging in discrete layers of varying density that create false floors and ceilings throughout the submerged industrial complex. Swimming through it feels like pushing through sheets of heavy silk, each stratum offering different resistance, different temperatures, different chemical tastes that burn or numb the tongue.

What little light penetrates these depths comes not from above but from the bioluminescent modifications of the factory's former products. The production lines once manufactured living industrial components—organic pistons that breathe, rubber gaskets that heal their own tears, lubricants that reproduce in their reservoirs. When the waters came, these half-alive machines didn't die but adapted, their engineered tissues developing photophores that now pulse in slow, synchronized waves through the darkness. The effect is like watching a city's heartbeat from beneath its skin.

The deafening sound is not what you'd expect underwater. The factory's automated systems still run, powered by thermal vents that cracked open when the land subsided. Massive turbines spin in their flooded chambers, their vibrations creating standing waves that make the water itself ring like a bell. The sound conducts better through these dense liquid layers than through air ever could—a bone-deep thrumming punctuated by the pneumatic gasps of machinery that still, impossibly, manufactures. Production never stopped; it simply evolved.

Between the sunken assembly lines, sparse kelp-like growths have taken root—not true vegetation but escaped prototypes of self-repairing cable insulation that learned to photosynthesize the weak bioluminescent light. They grow in neat rows where they fell from their spools, creating corridors of gently swaying rubber forests barely two meters high. Their surfaces are studded with copper nodules that leach minerals into the water, feeding the next strange link in this industrial ecosystem.

The fauna here defies easy categorization. Schools of what were once cooling fans have evolved into filter-feeders, their blades modified into delicate nets that catch the microscopic offspring of bearing balls and wire scraps. Larger forms patrol the production floors—excavators whose buckets have become mouths, their hydraulic fluids replaced with something like blood, their tracks modified into undulating carpets of muscle. They graze on the accumulated metal shavings and rust, processing them in furnace-stomachs that glow cherry-red through their armored sides.

The human structures that remain are few and strange. Control rooms exist as pockets of air trapped beneath curved ceilings, their atmospheres maintained by still-functioning HVAC systems that draw oxygen from the water through gills of corroded copper. Office chairs sit at their desks, barnacled and warped but occasionally occupied by the fused remains of safety equipment that gained a kind of colonial intelligence. Hard hats cluster like anemones. High-visibility vests ripple with artificial muscle, using their reflective strips to signal to one another in complex patterns.

The scale of this drowned factory floor is regional—perhaps sixty square kilometers of submerged industrial park. But distance becomes meaningless in the layered water. You might swim for hours toward a glowing assembly line only to find it's a reflection trapped between density layers, the real structure somewhere below or above or folded into a space that shouldn't exist. The empty volumes between structures stretch vast and dark, filled only with the thrumming of distant machinery and the occasional passage of things that were never meant to swim.

Weather still happens here, after a fashion. Convection currents from the thermal vents create underwater storms where hot and cold water masses collide. These manifest as spiraling columns of bubbles and debris, miniature hurricanes that can trap a swimmer in their eyes while tool-fish spiral around the edges like metallic petals. When the pressure drops before these storms, the bioluminescent machinery dims in anticipation, and every evolved thing seeks shelter in the hollow spaces of partially collapsed warehouses.

The strangest aspect is how the industrial and biological have merged into something unrecognizable as either. Conveyor belts undulate like sea snakes, carrying their loads of self-assembling components from station to station where robotic arms have learned to tend them like gardeners. Quality control systems have become predators, rejecting and consuming defective products in endless cycles of industrial selection. The entire flooded complex operates as a single vast organism, its purpose long divorced from human intention, manufacturing things for which there are no names in a darkness lit only by the fever-dreams of machines.
