The Suspended Cisterns of Keth-Maral hang like iron fruit from the underside of the world-shelf, each a perfect sphere of riveted metal forty meters across, connected by a web of catwalks and pressure tubes that create a three-dimensional city inverted beneath the stone sky. The water here is everywhere and nowhere—contained within the spheres, cycling through the industrial processors, existing as a constant fine mist that pearls every surface with droplets that never quite fall.

Inside the primary habitation sphere, the water rises to chest height, kept at blood temperature by the geometric heating coils that spiral up the walls like frozen smoke. The citizens move through their liquid world on rails and tracks, their bodies adapted over generations—elongated torsos, shortened legs, skin with a faint translucent quality that shows the blue tracework of veins beneath. They wear minimal clothing, mostly tool harnesses and breathing apparatus for when they must work in the fully submerged processing spheres.

The fauna here defies easy classification. Schools of copper-scaled mechanimals swim through the industrial water, their bodies a fusion of biology and engineering—fish with gear-train hearts visible through transparent flesh, eels whose spines are articulated metal segments that click and whir as they undulate. Larger things lurk in the storage spheres: filter-feeders the size of automobiles, their mouths vast intake valves lined with rows of rubber gaskets instead of teeth. They process the water, extracting minerals that accumulate as crystalline growths along their flanks—growths that the workers harvest with specialized tools.

The light comes from bioluminescent algae cultivated in spiral tubes throughout the sphere interiors, casting everything in a blue-green glow that barely penetrates the murky industrial water. Additional illumination pulses from the processing equipment—the flash of arc welders automated and submerged, the glow of heating elements, the occasional flare when mineral extraction reaches critical concentration.

Sound here is muffled, distant, wrong. The water swallows voices, turning conversation into whale-song. The industrial processes create a constant low vibration felt in the bones rather than heard—the thrum of pumps, the distant clang of automatic hammers shaping metal, the whisper of a million gallons cycling through filters and pipes. The mechanimals add their own notes: the tick-tick-tick of clockwork hearts, the hydraulic hiss of artificial gills, the scrape of metal scales against rust-proof walls.

The vegetation persists in unlikely spaces. Pressure-adapted kelp grows in maintenance shafts, its fronds tough as leather, dotted with nodes that filter heavy metals from the water. Barnacle-flowers cluster around heat vents, their petals opening and closing in slow mechanical rhythm. In the few air pockets, hanging gardens of salt-crusted succulents cling to the ceiling, their roots reaching down like pale fingers toward the water's surface.

The city never changes because it cannot afford to. Every component is locked in perfect industrial symbiosis—the water cycling through organisms and machines, minerals extracted and redeposited, heat carefully managed, population controlled. New spheres are occasionally grown rather than built, budding from parent structures over decades in a process part industrial, part biological. The workers tend their hanging city with religious devotion, knowing that one failure in the system would flood their inverted world, sending them plummeting into the mineral-thick waters below.

At the edges of the cistern complex, where the newest spheres hang on experimental couplings, the strangeness intensifies. Here the mechanimals show signs of independent evolution—fish with too many eyes, all of them camera lenses; eels that have learned to navigate the pipe systems, appearing in places they shouldn't exist. The water itself behaves oddly in these frontier spheres, forming standing waves that hold their shape for hours, or crystallizing into geometric patterns that pulse with their own light.

The citizens speak of the Deep Builders, the ones who first suspended this city beneath the stone sky, but their purposes remain obscure. All that remains is the functioning system, the endless cycle, the strange peace of a drowned industrial paradise that hangs forever at the edge of catastrophe, its countless organisms—biological and mechanical alike—maintaining an equilibrium as delicate as it is permanent.
