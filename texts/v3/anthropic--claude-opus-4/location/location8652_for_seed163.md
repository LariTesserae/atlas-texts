The Scalding Shallows rise impossible and vertical—a vast wall of water held against physics, climbing into brightness. This ocean stands perpendicular to the world, its face a living cliff of brine that stretches upward for thousands of meters. The water burns at blood temperature, kept hot by some deep thermal engine that pulses through the entire mass with the slowness of geological time.

At this elevation, where the air thins and the pressure drops, the boundary between liquid and atmosphere blurs. The water's surface—if surface is even the right word for this vertical expanse—constantly sheds itself in slow curtains of steam. These vapor phantoms drift outward before condensing and falling back, creating a perpetual zone of mist that extends perhaps fifty meters from the main body. Within this transition space, the distinction between swimming and flying loses meaning.

The water itself is preternaturally clear, revealing depths that should be dark but somehow gather light. Perhaps it's the altitude, the way the sun strikes this impossible ocean-wall without interference from atmospheric moisture. Or perhaps the water itself phosphoresces faintly, each molecule contributing its fraction of illumination until the whole mass glows with accumulated brightness.

Scattered through this vertical sea, at intervals that feel deliberate but resist pattern, grow the heat-kelps. These are not true plants but colonial organisms, their sparse fronds more mineral than vegetable. They anchor to nothing—there is no seafloor here, no bedrock—but maintain their positions through some mechanism of neutral buoyancy. Each colony spans perhaps ten meters, their crystalline structures refracting light into prismatic sprays. They grow slowly, adding perhaps a millimeter per year, sharp-edged and geometric like frozen explosions.

The fauna here defies counting. Schools of ribbon-fish undulate in three dimensions, their bodies flattened to near transparency, millions of them moving in synchrony. They make no sound—the water swallows vibration, creates a silence more complete than air ever could. Larger forms drift between the kelp forests: medusae the size of buildings, their bells pulsing with internal bioluminescence, their trailing arms extending hundreds of meters. Crustaceans with shells like geometric puzzles click along on nothing, their legs finding purchase on the water itself.

But it's the swarm-colonies that overwhelm. Clouds of microscopic organisms, each no larger than a dust mote, gather in formations that span kilometers. They move as unified entities, shape-shifting through forms that suggest intelligence—now a spiral, now a grid, now something that might be language written in living particles. When they pass through the scattered kelp groves, they part like curtains, reforming seamlessly on the other side.

Human structures appear rarely in this vertical ocean, but when they do, they speak of desperate adaptation. Pressure-pods of beaten copper, their seams sealed with some organic resin, cling to kelp stalks like barnacles. Each is barely large enough for a single occupant. Their makers must have been small, or capable of folding themselves into compact shapes. The metal shows signs of hand-working—hammer marks still visible despite the corrosion, tool scratches that speak of patience rather than precision.

Occasionally, a larger structure appears: platforms of woven kelp-fiber stretched between colonial growths, creating temporary floors in the vertical space. These deteriorate quickly in the heat, lasting perhaps a season before dissolving back into the water. On some, you can still see the remains of tools—bone hooks, stone weights, fragments of what might be maps etched on sheets of pressed algae.

The builders themselves are absent, or perhaps they've become something else. Sometimes the swarm-colonies move with too much purpose, arrange themselves in patterns too reminiscent of architecture. Sometimes the largest medusae pulse in rhythms that feel like code.

This is a place of patient transformation. The water rises infinitesimally, perhaps a meter per century, carrying its cargo of life ever higher into the thinning air. At the very top, where the water meets sky, new rules begin to assert themselves. Creatures breach the vertical surface and continue swimming through air for long moments before remembering gravity. Plants root in clouds that form from the constant evaporation.

Everything here moves with thermal slowness, conserving energy in the heat. Predation happens in extreme slow motion—a medusa might take hours to engulf its prey, the ribbon-fish dispersing and reforming around the threat like smoke. The silence makes it all feel like a dream, or perhaps like being inside the bloodstream of some vast, contemplative god.

At night, the bioluminescence intensifies. The water becomes a wall of stars, each organism contributing its mote of light to a galaxy written in living flesh. The heat remains constant, driven by whatever deep engine powers this impossible ocean. And in the scattered pressure-pods, in the patterns of the swarms, in the slow pulse of jellyfish neurons, something watches and waits and gradually becomes.
