The Resonance Plains stretch endlessly beneath a bruised twilight that never shifts, a continental expanse of crystallized silence turned to stone. Here, in the thin atmosphere of this high plateau, sound itself has fossilized into vast beds of acoustic mineral—purple-black sheets that ring like struck bells when the wind finds them, though wind is rare in this elevation where air barely clings.

The cold is absolute but dry, a preserving cold that turns breath to powder before it can form ice. What sparse vegetation exists has adapted to photosynthesize from vibration rather than light: tungsten-wire grasses that quiver at specific frequencies, their metallic strands harvesting energy from the endless industrial hum that pervades the plane. These knife-thin blades grow in precise mathematical patterns, spirals and grids that maximize their resonant capture, sprouting from cracks in the sound-stone where ancient earthquakes left their geometric scars.

The fauna here are magnificent in their strangeness—herds of creatures like living pipe organs, their bodies hollowed chambers of cartilage and horn. They move in slow migrations across the acoustic wastes, their footfalls creating the very vibrations they feed upon. Smaller beings skitter between their legs: crystal spiders that weave webs from solidified echoes, and bore-worms that tunnel through the resonant substrate, leaving behind networks of wind-carved flutes in the stone.

Scattered across this vastness are the remnants of the Tuning Stations—industrial monuments to some ancient attempt to harness the plane's acoustic properties. These factories, built from the same resonant mineral they sought to process, now stand as enormous sound-producing ruins. Their turbines still turn in the thin air, generating drones that carry for thousands of miles. Some have evolved their own ecosystems: colonies of filter-feeding organisms that strain nutrients from sound waves cluster around still-functioning amplification dishes.

The loudness here is not what surface dwellers would recognize as noise. It's a deep, structural sound—the planet's own voice channeled through massive geological formations that act as natural speakers. The ground itself carries these vibrations, creating standing waves that can lift dust into permanent, hovering patterns. In certain valleys between the sound-stone mesas, these waves create zones of crushing acoustic pressure where even the abundant fauna cannot venture.

The dim light comes not from any sun but from bioluminescent deposits in the mineral substrate itself, activated by specific frequencies. As sound waves travel across the plains, they trigger faint Aurora-like displays in the stone—purple and indigo flashes that ripple outward from each acoustic event. The industrial ruins glow brightest, their still-functioning machinery creating persistent halos of cold luminescence.

Time moves differently here, measured not in days but in the slow cycles of the Great Resonance—a planetary-scale standing wave that takes decades to complete one oscillation. The fauna migrate in harmony with this cycle, the vegetation grows and retreats according to its phases, and even the scattered structures seem to breathe with its rhythm, their metal frameworks expanding and contracting imperceptibly.

In the deepest basins, where the acoustic pressure has compressed the mineral substrate into mirror-smooth floors, abandoned vehicles sit perfectly preserved—industrial crawlers and resonance harvesters, their operators long gone but their engines still idling in the lowest registers of sound. Around them, opportunistic organisms have evolved to tap into their electrical systems: living cables that snake across the ground, their segments pulsing with stolen current, feeding gardens of electric moss that grow in perfect circles around each machine.

The horizon here is always singing—a thin line of vibration where the plains meet the darker sky, creating a phenomenon the scattered inhabitants of the remaining stations call the Edge Frequency. It's said that if you could travel fast enough to reach it, you would find the boundary where sound itself stops, where the great acoustic plains give way to perfect silence. But in this vast, slow world, nothing moves fast enough to catch that horizon. Instead, the fauna, the sparse wire-grass, the ruins, and the resonant stone itself exist in a state of permanent vibration, a world-sized instrument playing an endless, deafening symphony to an empty sky.
