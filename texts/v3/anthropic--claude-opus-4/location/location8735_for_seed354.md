The Drowned Works of Marellia stretch beneath two hundred meters of warm, green-black water. From above, nothing shows but the endless expanse of the Thermal Sea, its surface disturbed only by the occasional bubble-plume where ancient steam vents still exhale through the ruins.

Below, a city sleeps in perpetual twilight.

The light that filters down has traveled far through water thick with ancient sediments and colonies of heat-loving algae. By the time it reaches the first tower-tops, it arrives as a dim olive glow, barely enough to distinguish the massive industrial forms from the darkness between them. Everything appears in shades of verdigris and rust, shapes emerging from shadow only when you drift close enough to touch their encrusted surfaces.

The city was built for air, not water. You can see it in the architecture—vast factory halls with roofs that once channeled rain into cisterns, now gaping open to the weight of the sea. Catwalks and elevated rail lines snake between buildings at what were once dizzying heights, now forming a dense three-dimensional maze of corroded iron and brass. The builders packed every cubic meter with purpose: pipes threading between support beams, machinery nested within machinery, control stations tucked into the joints between massive pressure vessels.

Time and water have softened the harsh industrial edges. Surfaces that once gleamed with oil and metal polish now wear thick coats of growth—not animals, for no fish swim these depths, but vast colonies of thermophilic vegetation. Heat-loving kelp forests rise from every horizontal surface, their fronds swaying in the slow convection currents. Bacterial mats carpet the walls in velvet sheets of purple and orange. Where steam still vents from deep geothermal taps, specialized algae bloom in clouds of brilliant green, the only vivid color in this muted world.

The machinery continues its patient work. The city's builders engineered for eternity, and eternity is what they got. Deep beneath the seafloor, geothermal extractors pull heat from the planet's core. The energy moves through a circulatory system of pipes and exchangers, some as wide as tunnels, others hair-thin networks barely visible beneath their coating of growth. The entire city pulses with this slow metabolism—water cycling through vast radiators, steam condensing and re-condensing, thermal gradients maintaining themselves across centuries.

The sound is almost nothing. Without air, the industrial symphony plays muted: the deep throb of pumps felt more than heard, the whisper of bubbles rising through kelp fronds, the occasional groan of metal adjusting to temperature changes. Even these sounds carry poorly through the thick, warm water. Mostly there is silence, broken only by your own movements and breathing apparatus.

In the factory districts, the density reaches its peak. Here, the builders abandoned any pretense of open space. Conveyor systems thread through processing tanks, themselves built around reactor cores, themselves enclosed in monitoring stations stacked like honeycomb. Every surface sprouts valves, gauges, access ports—a mechanical coral reef where function dictated form with obsessive efficiency. The vegetation has made it even denser, filling every gap with fronds and filaments until the original structures seem to breathe with organic life.

The heat never relents. Even at this depth, the water stays blood-warm from the geothermal wealth below and the trapped heat of centuries of operation. In some districts, where the machinery runs hottest, the water approaches boiling. These zones shimmer with thermal distortion, and the specialized plants that grow there have leaves like leather and stems like cable.

The city has no edge, no moment where artifice gives way to nature. It extends beyond sight in every direction, tower after tower vanishing into the aqueous gloom. Somewhere out there, perhaps, the factories thin out, the pipes grow sparse, the careful engineering yields to bare seafloor. But here, in the heart of the Drowned Works, every cubic meter bears the mark of its makers' ambition—to build a world entire, to replace nature with engineering, to create a closed system that would outlast even their own extinction.

And it has. The city lives on without its builders, a vast machine dreaming its slow industrial dreams beneath the weight of an artificial sea.
