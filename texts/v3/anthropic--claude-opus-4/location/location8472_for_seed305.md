The Breath Columns rise from barren rock like a forest of stone lungs, each tower riddled with thousands of holes that wheeze and moan in the mountain wind. At this elevation—nearly vertical cliff faces climbing toward an unseen summit—the air is thin and scorching, heated by some geological anomaly deep below. The columns themselves are recent formations, perhaps only decades old, their surfaces still sharp and unweathered, formed when superheated gases punched through the mountain's crust in a catastrophic event that left this honeycomb architecture behind.

Each column stands forty to sixty feet tall, packed so densely together that moving between them requires turning sideways and squeezing through gaps barely wide enough for a human body. The stone is pale gray, almost white, and perpetually warm to the touch. In the brief morning hours, moisture condenses on the cooler rock faces, trickling down through the perforations to collect in small basins at the base of each tower. By midday, these pools have evaporated, leaving only mineral rings and the occasional cluster of heat-adapted mosses that cling to life in these temporary oases.

The sound is overwhelming. Wind funnels through the countless holes, creating a chorus of whistles, groans, and subsonic rumbles that shift constantly with changes in air pressure. During the rare storms that sweep across these heights, the columns shriek like pipe organs, their voices carrying for miles down the mountainside. The acoustic environment is so dense and disorienting that visitors often report vertigo, their sense of direction scrambled by the omnidirectional symphony.

Life here is scarce but remarkable. Tube-dwelling wasps have colonized many of the smaller perforations, their paper nests visible as gray plugs in the stone mouths. They emerge only during the cooler dawn hours, their bodies adapted to withstand temperatures that would kill most insects. Occasionally, a scaled swift—a bird with heat-reflective feathers—darts between the columns, hunting the wasps with precision strikes before retreating to cooler altitudes.

Near the center of this formation, partially crushed between two leaning columns, stands the remnant of what might have been a shrine or waystation. The structure is built from the same pale stone, its walls carved rather than constructed, as if someone long ago hollowed out a smaller column for shelter. Inside, the walls are covered with erosion patterns that might be natural or might be the remains of inscriptions, their meaning lost to the constant scouring of wind-blown grit. A shallow depression in the floor suggests a fire pit, though what fuel could burn in this airless heat remains a mystery.

The columns continue upward along the cliff face until they disappear into a layer of superheated air that shimmers like water, distorting everything above into writhing mirages. Sometimes, in the predawn darkness, observers claim to see lights moving through the upper reaches—perhaps some bioluminescent organism adapted to the extreme conditions, or perhaps just another acoustic illusion created by sound waves visible in the temperature gradients.

This is a place of paradoxes: young stone that sings with ancient voices, desert conditions punctuated by brief moments of moisture, crushing density in a vast vertical wilderness. The Breath Columns stand as a testament to the mountain's violent transformation, a pipe organ built by catastrophe, played by wind, and slowly colonized by the few forms of life ingenious enough to find opportunity in its hostile music.
