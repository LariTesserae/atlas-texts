The steam district breathes through its iron lungs, a maze of sweating pipes and condensation chambers built into what was once a shallow basin between hills. The air tastes of minerals and machine oil, thick enough to leave droplets on your skin within minutes. This is where the city's water converges—not in natural pools, but in the great collection systems that feed the turbines below.

Corrugated metal roofs stretch between rusted frameworks, their surfaces perpetually beaded with moisture that runs in constant rivulets toward the catch-gutters. The original ground is mostly hidden now beneath gratings and walkways, but where it shows through, the earth is dark and saturated, pooling with runoff that reflects the dim industrial lighting in oil-slick rainbows. Sparse tufts of heat-adapted grasses push through gaps in the infrastructure, their leaves thick and waxy, evolved to survive in this perpetual sauna.

The sound never stops—a layered symphony of dripping, hissing, clanking, and the deep thrumming of the condensers. Steam vents release periodic jets with sharp whistles, while somewhere beneath, the great pumps maintain their steady heartbeat. The machinery spans several city blocks but feels intimate, enclosed by the constant mist that limits visibility to perhaps fifty meters in any direction.

Between the pipes and platforms, the fauna thrives in unexpected abundance. Pale crabs scuttle along the warm metal surfaces, their shells adapted to absorb moisture from the air. Clouds of tiny moths dance around the sparse industrial lights, their wings evolved to shed water rather than scales. In the deeper pools beneath the walkways, eyeless fish dart between the support pillars, feeding on the bacterial mats that flourish in the mineral-rich runoff.

The workers who maintain this district move like ghosts through the steam, their rubberized coats glistening. They know the rhythm of the place—when the pressure builds, when the overflow channels will flood, when the automatic valves will cycle. Their shanties perch on the higher platforms, simple structures of corrugated metal and tarpaulin that channel the endless condensation into personal water supplies.

At the edges where the district meets drier ground, the transition is sharp. The moisture creates a distinct boundary, a wall of humidity that hits you like entering another world. Beyond this threshold, the city's normal sprawl resumes, but here in the steam district, the marriage of water and industry has created its own ecosystem—a place where the boundary between machine and nature has dissolved into something altogether different, running on its own logic, its own cycles, its own strange vitality.

The heat never breaks. Even in what passes for night, when the industrial lights dim to their lowest setting, the temperature holds steady, maintained by the massive thermal mass of water flowing through the system and the waste heat of the turbines below. It's a landscape of perpetual summer storm without the storm, all the humidity and pressure but none of the release, unless you count the constant gentle rain of condensation as weather—which here, perhaps, it is.
