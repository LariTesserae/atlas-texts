The plateau stretches endlessly beneath a pewter sky, its surface worn down to the fundamental bones of the world. What was once a mountain range has been ground into a vast tableland of metamorphic rock, shot through with veins of quartz that catch what little light filters through the perpetual overcast. The stone is polished smooth in places by eons of wind, cracked elsewhere into geometric patterns like the scales of some impossible reptile.

Moisture clings to everything here, though rain rarely falls. Instead, the dampness arrives as fog that rolls up from unseen valleys, condensing on the cold stone in beads that trickle into ancient channels worn finger-deep into the rock. These tiny rivers converge into pools that form in the natural depressions—perfectly circular basins scoured out over millennia, some no larger than a palm, others the size of mill wheels. The water in them is unnaturally clear and seems to vibrate with a frequency just below hearing.

Scattered across this primordial expanse are the remnants of industry—not ruins exactly, but machinery that seems to have grown into the landscape itself. Turbines rise from the stone like metal trees, their blades turning eternally in winds that follow patterns older than memory. The towers are pitted and worn, their original purpose obscured, but they continue their rotations with a grinding persistence that fills the air with a low harmonic drone. Sometimes the sound rises to a keen when the wind shifts, and the whole plateau rings like a struck bell.

The machinery follows the water. Where the carved channels converge, you find the remains of pump houses built from the same stone as the plateau, their walls so weathered they seem like natural outcroppings until you notice the precision of their angles. Inside, turbines still turn, drawing power from the trickle of water, feeding electricity through cables that disappear into bore holes drilled straight down through the rock. The cables hum with current, warm to the touch, heading somewhere deep.

Vegetation clings to existence in the sheltered spaces—stunted juniper analogues with bark like copper wire, growing in perfect spirals around the pooling basins. Their roots penetrate deep into the rock through cracks so fine they seem impossible. When the fog rolls in, these plants emit a faint phosphorescence, their needle-leaves glowing blue-green in the murk. Lichen grows on the north faces of the turbine towers, but it's unlike any earthly lichen—it forms perfect fractals, each tiny rosette a mirror of the whole colony, and where it grows thickest, you can hear it crackling with static electricity.

The fauna here exists at the edge of perception. Sometimes you glimpse something moving between the turbine towers—too large to be wind-blown debris, too regular in its movement to be shadow. The pools occasionally ripple without any apparent cause, and if you wait long enough by the larger basins, you might see something surface briefly—a smooth curve of shell or skin that doesn't match any known taxonomy. But mostly, life here has learned to be invisible. The real activity happens in the frequencies beyond human hearing, in the vibrations that travel through the stone itself.

The few structures that might once have housed human workers are reduced to foundations and partial walls, their corners rounded by ages of wind. But the industrial infrastructure persists with an unsettling vitality. Gauge boxes still register pressures. Transformers hum in their weatherproof housings. And in certain places where multiple turbines align, their combined resonance creates standing waves in the air—columns of dense vibration where the fog behaves strangely, forming spirals and lattices before dispersing.

At night, the phosphorescent plants provide the only light besides the occasional spark from the machinery. But on certain nights, when the fog is thick and the turbines sing in harmony, auroras appear in the sky despite the latitude—sheets of green and gold that seem to emerge from the plateau itself, as if the stone were dreaming in light.

This is a place where deep time and industrial ambition have merged into something neither natural nor artificial. The machinery has become geology. The geology has become a vast instrument. And somewhere far below, where all those humming cables converge, something waits with the patience of stone—drawing power, drop by drop, from the ancient watershed of this worn-down world.
