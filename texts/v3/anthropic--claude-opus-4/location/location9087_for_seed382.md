The Singing Hollow spreads beneath the earth like an inverted city, its bone-pale towers growing downward from the cavern's ceiling. Each structure is woven from something that might once have been wood but now exists as a crystallized memory of vegetation—fibrous, warm to the touch, humming with stored sunlight from an age when this place knew sky.

The towers hang like stalactites, their surfaces riddled with hexagonal cells that open and close in slow waves. Within each cell grows a different variety of air-plant: crimson tendrils that drink moisture from breath itself, bulbous sacs that inflate and deflate in tidal rhythms, feathery fronds that filter nutrients from the constant warm drafts rising from the depths. No water has touched these plants in ten thousand years. They sustain themselves on heat, on minerals carried in the air, on the resonance of sound itself.

For this is why they call it the Singing Hollow—the entire cavern thrums with a bass note too low for human ears, punctuated by crystalline chimes when the wind catches the hollow tubes that pierce each hanging tower. The sound comes from everywhere and nowhere, reflected and amplified by the peculiar acoustics of the space until it becomes a physical presence, a pressure that visitors feel in their bones.

The inhabitants have adapted their primitive tools to work with vibration rather than force. They shape the crystal-wood through careful application of tuning forks, coaxing new chambers to grow rather than carving them. Their hammers are more like bells, their saws are strings that sing matter apart along its natural frequencies. They build by encouraging the existing structures to dream new geometries into being.

The settlement clusters around the thickest stalactite-towers, connected by bridges of woven air-root that span the vast emptiness between. These bridges sway with each shift in the thermal currents, but their mesh is so fine and their design so cunning that a child could cross them safely in the dark. Which is fortunate, as true darkness is what passes for day here—the dim periods when only the faintest phosphorescence limns the edges of things, when the heat-glow from the depths below dims to ember-warmth.

During what the inhabitants call "bright-time," that glow intensifies to a ruddy gold, and the air itself seems to thicken with warmth and spore-light. The plants respond by opening their cells wider, breathing deeper, and the whole hollow fills with a pollen that glows like sparks. This is when the strangeness becomes most apparent—shadows that fall upward, gravity that seems negotiable near certain formations, spaces where sound becomes visible as ribbons of disturbed air.

The city-organism (for it is impossible to say where the built environment ends and the living architecture begins) extends through multiple levels of the cavern system. Some towers have grown so long they disappear into lower chambers where the heat becomes oppressive and the sound-pressure can damage unprepared ears. Others spread laterally, sending out runners that colonize new sections of ceiling, slowly expanding the settlement's reach.

At the center of it all, in the deepest accessible chamber, stands—or rather, hangs—the Resonance Garden. Here the oldest air-plants have woven themselves into a cathedral-space where sound becomes almost solid. The inhabitants gather here for their most important ceremonies, using the acoustic properties to share collective experiences that bypass language entirely. In this space, memory becomes music becomes meaning, all without a drop of water or a ray of natural light.

The whole system pulses with its own slow life, each tower breathing in century-long cycles, the bridges singing their stress-songs as they bear their loads, the tools humming in harmony with the materials they shape. It is a place that has found its own balance, its own way of being lush in the absence of water, its own form of abundance in apparent emptiness.
