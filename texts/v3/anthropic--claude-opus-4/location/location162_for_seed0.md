The darkness pulses with life—a vast shallow basin where the night never fully lifts, even at what should be midday. Here, beneath a permanent canopy of storm-pregnant clouds, the world exists in a state of perpetual twilight cacophony.

Water doesn't fall here so much as it breathes. The air itself seems to inhale and exhale moisture in great, irregular rhythms. One moment you're walking through what feels like warm fog, droplets condensing on every surface, and the next the air pulls back, leaving only a humid memory. During these "dry breaths," you can hear the water gathering somewhere above in the cloud ceiling—a distant, anticipatory rumble.

The vegetation has adapted to this breathing darkness. Massive fungal towers rise like pale fingers, their surfaces slick with condensation, glowing faintly with bioluminescent veins that pulse in response to the moisture cycles. Between them, thick carpets of what might be moss or might be something else entirely spread in overlapping layers—some growing atop others, creating a landscape that rises and falls in soft, organic waves. These living carpets release spores when the moisture peaks, filling the air with drifting motes that glow briefly before dimming.

But it's the sound that dominates everything. The fauna here exists in staggering density—creatures stacked upon creatures in the vertical spaces between the fungal towers, burrowing through the layered moss-carpets, gliding between the spore clouds. They've evolved beyond sight in this eternal dusk, becoming creatures of pure sound. Their calls create a wall of noise: deep thrumming from massive things that shake the ground, high-pitched trilling from clouds of smaller beings that move in coordinated swarms, percussive clicking from the middle reaches. The sound is so dense it has an almost physical quality, pressing against your chest, making thought difficult.

The young landscape still shows its rawness. The fungal towers crack and split as they grow, revealing sharp edges of fresh growth. New valleys open suddenly when the moss-layers grow too heavy and collapse, exposing steep-walled chasms that immediately begin to fill with new life. The ground itself seems unsettled, still deciding its final form.

Occasionally, primitive tools appear half-buried in the moss—crude stone implements worn smooth by countless small hands or paws, simple digging sticks made from the hardened stems of giant fungi. But these are not signs of civilization. Instead, various creatures have independently discovered tool use. You might see a cluster of palm-sized things with too many legs using rocks to crack open spore pods, or witness a serpentine creature twice your length manipulating fungal fibers into simple snares.

The whole landscape writhes with constant motion. Swarms rise and fall like waves, predator chains create rushing rivers of movement through the moss valleys, and the fungal towers sway in rhythms that have nothing to do with wind. Even the moisture cycles drive activity—creatures emerging and retreating, hunting patterns shifting, the very ground swelling and contracting.

In the rare moments when the sound drops to merely loud rather than deafening, you can hear other things: the crack of new fungal growth, the whisper of billions of spores settling, the wet sound of moss consuming something recently deceased. And underneath it all, a low vibration that might be geological or might be the collective breathing of every living thing in this teeming darkness.

The air tastes thick, alive, full of spores and pheromones and the humid breath of countless organisms. This is not a place built for human presence. It's a world still inventing itself, where evolution runs at breakneck speed in the permanent twilight, where every surface crawls with purpose, and where the darkness itself seems to pulse with ferocious life.
