The mercury plains stretch outward under a salmon-colored sky, their surface tension creating perfect, unbroken mirrors that extend for kilometers. These are not pools but a continuous sheet of liquid metal, perhaps two centimeters deep, resting on a substrate of fused glass that formed when this world was young. The mercury never evaporates, never flows, held in supernatural stasis by field generators buried beneath the glass—ancient technology that bends local physics to maintain this impossible equilibrium.

Sparse vegetation punctuates the reflective expanse: crystalline growths that might be plants or might be something else entirely. They rise in geometric clusters—hexagonal columns of translucent material that sweat beads of water continuously down their faces. The droplets fall to the mercury surface without creating ripples, absorbed instantly into the metal's mass. These crystal formations grow only where hairline fractures web through the glass substrate below, following the crack patterns with mathematical precision.

The air hangs thick with moisture despite the warmth, a greenhouse atmosphere that condenses on any surface cooler than ambient. Your breath fogs even in the heat. Water droplets form and reform on your skin, on your clothes, running in tiny rivulets that the mercury greedily drinks when they fall. The humidity carries a metallic taste, a slight electrical sensation on the tongue.

Sound dies here. The mercury surface swallows acoustic waves, creating pockets of supernatural quiet between the crystal groves. Even your footsteps make no noise—the liquid metal parts silently beneath your weight, held by its engineered surface tension from clinging to your boots. Only the faintest whisper comes from the perpetual weeping of the crystals, a sussuration like distant rain on glass.

The landscape's youth shows in its sharp geometries. The crystal formations haven't had time to weather or merge; each stands distinct with knife-edge faces and perfect vertices. The fracture patterns in the glass below spread in fresh, angular networks, some still warm to the touch where tectonic forces recently split the substrate. In places, you can see where new cracks are forming—not spreading, never spreading, but simply appearing fully-formed as the field generators adjust to accommodate new stress patterns.

The technology reveals itself in subtle impossibilities. Mercury should evaporate in this warmth, but doesn't. Water should pool atop the liquid metal, but instead merges seamlessly. The crystals should grow or shrink with environmental changes, but remain frozen in their current forms. Sometimes, at the edge of vision, you catch glimpses of the maintenance systems: gossamer nets of light that flicker through the mercury's depths, or the faint hum of processors calculating the exact field strength needed to maintain each impossible equilibrium.

Walking these plains feels like moving through a vast circuit board, where the mercury serves as conductor, the crystals as components, and the water cycle as coolant. The whole landscape hums with potential energy that will never be released, a planetary-scale device locked in eternal standby mode. The warm air presses close, thick with moisture that the crystals endlessly recycle, while overhead the salmon sky remains forever cloudless, its light reflecting endlessly between the mercury below and the crystalline faces that rise like frozen fountains from the geometric cracks of this young, strange world.
