The Furnace Plains stretch beneath a starless vault, a vast expanse of oxidized iron dust that has known no rain for ten thousand years. The darkness here is absolute—not the darkness of night, but the darkness of a world whose sun died so long ago that even its memory has faded from the rocks. What light exists comes from below: thin veins of bioluminescent bacteria that have adapted to feed on the heat itself, tracing delicate networks through the rust-colored substrate like neural pathways in a dying brain.

The heat is crushing, relentless. It rises from deep geological processes that never ceased when the surface died—tectonic friction, radioactive decay, the slow compression of planetary mass. The air shimmers with it, though there's hardly anyone to see the distortion. At 200 degrees Celsius, the atmosphere itself has taken on an oily quality, thick with metallic vapors that condense and re-evaporate in an endless cycle.

Scattered across this infernal plain stand the Siphons—towering industrial relics from the last age of habitation. Each one is a kilometers-tall needle of heat-warped alloy, their surfaces pitted and scarred by centuries of thermal expansion and contraction. They were built to harvest the planet's geothermal energy, converting heat gradients into usable power through processes their creators took to their graves. Now they stand abandoned, still functioning in their mindless way, drawing heat from the depths and bleeding it into the atmosphere in great silent plumes that disturb the heavy air.

Between the Siphons, the landscape is dense with the accumulated detritus of geological time. Layers of metallic dust have compacted into a kind of natural pavement, cracked into hexagonal patterns like the skin of some impossible reptile. In the cracks grow the only vegetation this world knows: thermophytic crystalline structures that blur the line between mineral and organism. They spread in sparse colonies, each "plant" a cluster of heat-absorbing filaments that convert thermal energy directly into growth, building their bodies from precipitated metals in the atmosphere.

The fauna that persists here has evolved past recognition. Tungsten-shelled arthropods the size of dinner plates navigate by infrared, moving in careful paths between the crystal groves. They're rare, these creatures—a sighting happens perhaps once in a hundred days. They feed on the metallic plants, their mandibles capable of shearing through iron-hard fibers. When they die, their shells add another layer to the accumulated depths of the plain.

In certain places, the abandoned infrastructure has created micro-environments of extraordinary strangeness. Where cooling pipes once ran, networks of tunnels now channel superheated air in predictable patterns, creating permanent cyclones of metal-laden wind that sing a low, almost subsonic tone—the only sound that regularly breaks the oppressive quiet. These thermal rivers have carved smooth channels through the dust plains, and along their banks cluster the densest concentrations of crystal vegetation, creating oases of alien life in the midst of desolation.

The most unsettling aspect of this landscape is its terrible stability. Nothing truly changes here. The Siphons will continue their purposeless harvest until their mechanisms finally succumb to entropy—a process that could take millions of years. The thermal cycles will continue their ancient rhythm. The sparse life will persist in its narrow niches, neither flourishing nor failing, locked in an equilibrium as perfect as it is meaningless.

Time moves differently in such a place. Without day or night, without seasons or weather, there are only the slow accumulations of dust and the patient growth of crystal forests. Occasionally, a Siphon develops a new harmonic in its operation, adding a subtle drone to the soundscape that might persist for centuries before friction silences it again. The arthropods trace their careful paths, following thermal gradients that shift by mere fractions of degrees over decades.

This is a world in aftermath—not dead, but dreaming in the heat-drunk darkness of its own transformed state. The industrial age that built the Siphons is so distant that their purpose has become purely abstract, their function continuing long after meaning has departed. They are prayers in steel and ceramic, offered to no god, maintaining rituals whose significance died with their makers.

In the deepest darkness between the Siphons, where even the bacterial light fails to penetrate, stranger things move. Heat mirages take on impossible geometries. The crystal plants sometimes emit sounds like whispers in no language ever spoken. The metal dust sometimes arranges itself in patterns too regular to be natural, too fleeting to be studied. This is a landscape that has been alone with itself for so long that it has begun to develop its own inscrutable purposes, its own slow thoughts expressed in thermal currents and mineral accumulations.

The Furnace Plains endure, locked in their dark fever dream, a monument to persistence without purpose, existence without witness, time without change.
