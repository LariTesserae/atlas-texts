The Drowning Forge hangs suspended in absolute darkness, a cathedral-sized chamber of hammered copper and bronze that has never known sky. Water presses in from all directions—not the cold crush of ocean depths, but something fever-hot and syrupy, dense with minerals that taste of blood and rust. The walls are beaten metal, every surface showing the marks of primitive hammers, the patterns spiraling inward like frozen whirlpools.

There is no floor, no ceiling—only six walls of worked metal forming a hollow cube perhaps three hundred feet across. The water that fills this space glows faintly from within, not with light but with heat that registers as a dull red pressure against closed eyelids. The temperature hovers just below scalding, turning the entire chamber into a liquid furnace.

Through this submarine forge drift the builders: blind crustaceans the size of house cats, their shells evolved into living anvils. Their backs are flat and scarred, their claws modified into hammers that ring against their own bodies in endless rhythm. The sound carries strangely in the hot water—not muffled as it should be, but amplified, each strike becoming a bell tone that reverberates through the metal walls until the entire structure sings with their labor.

They work in absolute darkness, following vibrations through the water, adding new plates of beaten copper to the walls from metals they excrete and shape. The sparse vegetation consists of wire-thin kelp made entirely of oxidized bronze, growing from the seams between plates. These metal weeds provide the only food, rasped away by specialized mouthparts and slowly regrowing from the mineral-rich water.

The chamber never changes its dimensions, but its surface constantly renews—new metal laid over old in paper-thin sheets, the builders maintaining their prison-sanctuary-universe for reasons lost to their eyeless generations. Sometimes a builder will hammer too hard and punch through to whatever lies beyond the walls. The hole seals immediately, growing closed like healing skin, but for a moment something else presses in—water of a different temperature, a different density, suggesting vast spaces beyond this small eternal forge.

The empty spaces between the creatures are vast relative to their size. They drift apart, following the thermal currents that spiral lazily through the chamber, only congregating when the harmonics of their hammering accidentally sync into specific patterns. When this happens, they freeze in place for hours, listening to something in the resonance that perhaps recalls an ancestral purpose.

In the absolute darkness, time becomes meaningless. The builders continue their work, their bodies slowly becoming more anvil than animal, their hammering songs echoing through water that tastes of copper and fever dreams. The chamber persists—young in its constantly renewed surfaces, ancient in its unchanging form, a small pocket of primitive industry floating in an undefined somewhere, a forge that builds only itself, forever.
