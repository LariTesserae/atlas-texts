The Grinding Halls stretch beneath the world's skin—a labyrinth of chambers worn so smooth by aeons that walls, floor, and ceiling blur into continuous curves. These are not natural caves but the hollowed veins of something impossibly ancient and built, though what original purpose they served has been eroded beyond memory.

Water seeps through the stone-that-is-not-stone in rhythmic pulses, following tides that obey no moon. The moisture collects in shallow depressions worn into the floor over millennia, creating thousands of temporary pools that appear and vanish with each unseen cycle. The walls sweat a thin film that tastes of iron and salt.

The scale defies comprehension. Each chamber could swallow cities whole, yet they are merely cells in an organism of architecture that spans continents underground. The passages between them narrow to mere hundreds of meters before opening again into vaulted spaces where the ceiling vanishes into darkness. Weather forms here—slow, strange weather. Mists rise from the periodic floods and condense into clouds that drift through the upper reaches, occasionally releasing gentle rains that patter down for hours.

Everything here was made, though by whom or what remains unknowable. The substrate appears to be a kind of concrete or compressed ash, ground down by time until it achieves the texture of worn bone. Primitive tools—stone hammers, copper chisels, implements of carved antler—lie scattered throughout, dropped by the countless generations who have attempted to maintain or modify this place. They work constantly, these unseen maintainers, their tools ringing against the ancient walls in cacophonous rhythms that echo and multiply through the branching tunnels.

The sound is overwhelming—not from any single source but from the sheer density of activity. The tap-tap-tap of ten thousand hammers. The scrape of metal on stone. The splash and drip of water. The groaning of the structure itself as it settles and shifts. Voices, too—chanting, calling, singing work songs in languages that have evolved in isolation down here for so long they barely resemble human speech.

Sparse vegetation clings to life wherever the dim light permits. Pale mosses fur the walls near the phosphorescent nodes that provide the only illumination—another built feature, these dull-glowing pustules of manufactured bioluminescence that pulse weakly, casting everything in sickly green-yellow hues. Thread-thin fungi drape between surfaces like cobwebs. In the larger chambers where the rains fall more regularly, ghostly ferns unfurl from cracks, their fronds nearly translucent.

The builders—if they can be called that—work in shifts that never end. They are everywhere and nowhere, their presence felt more than seen. A figure glimpsed at the edge of the light, hammering at a wall. A procession carrying bundles of tools through a distant passage. They repair and modify according to patterns that shift like living things, following instructions that seem to emerge from the architecture itself. One day they might be carving new channels for water flow; the next, sealing them up again.

The turbulence is not in the stone but in the purpose. The Grinding Halls are constantly being remade from within, their function shifting like a dream that can't quite settle. Workshops become cisterns become sleeping quarters become shrines become workshops again. The only constant is the work itself—the endless maintenance of a machine so old its operation has become its only goal.

Sometimes, in the deepest chambers where the sound reaches peak crescendo, you can feel it: the whole structure breathing. Expanding and contracting with geological slowness. The workers speed up their hammering in response, as if trying to keep pace with a heartbeat measured in centuries.

No animals live here—the constant noise and activity drive them away, and there's little to eat besides the pale vegetation. But the workers themselves have become something both more and less than fauna. They are part of the mechanism now, as essential as the water that seeps through the walls or the tools they wield with practiced monotony.

This is architecture as organism, maintenance as worship, erosion as construction. The Grinding Halls extend deeper and further than anyone has ever mapped, a built world that builds itself, ancient beyond measure yet somehow never quite finished. The work continues. The hammering never stops. The structure settles another millimeter, and ten thousand voices rise in a work song that echoes through chambers vast enough to have their own weather, their own gravity, their own patient, grinding logic.
