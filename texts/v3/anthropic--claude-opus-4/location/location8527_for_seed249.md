The vastness announces itself first in the heat shimmer. What appears to be horizon dissolves into another false edge, and another beyond that, until distance becomes meaningless. This is a young world still learning its shape—a continental shelf of crystalline substrate that emerged from some recent cataclysm, its surface sharp as broken glass in places, smooth as cooled metal in others.

The heat presses down like a physical weight, but it's not the dry heat of deserts. This warmth carries moisture in strange ways. The crystalline ground sweats—not with ordinary condensation, but with a kind of industrial perspiration that beads up in perfect spheres before rolling upward, defying gravity to collect in floating pools that drift slowly across the vast expanse. These aerial ponds never last long. The turbulence here is peculiar: invisible currents tear them apart, scatter them into mist, only for new ones to form elsewhere.

Vegetation thrives in this hostile nursery, though not as any botanist would recognize. The plants here grow in explosive spurts between the chaos—thick cables of something between vine and industrial piping that can extend hundreds of meters in minutes, sprouting from the sharp substrate wherever the floating moisture touches down. They bloom with mechanical precision: chrome flowers that unfurl with soft clicks and whispers, releasing spores that look like metal shavings. The foliage is lush but temporary. When the invisible storms sweep through, entire forests of this strange growth can be uprooted and flung skyward, only to rain down as seed elsewhere.

The built environment exists as interruptions in the organic chaos. A factory smokestack emerges from a tangle of the cable-vines, still warm, still humming with some deep mechanical life despite the vegetation that seeks to claim it. The sound it makes is almost below hearing—a throb you feel in your bones rather than your ears. Elsewhere, solitary structures dot the expanse: a single turbine spinning without wind, a concrete bunker half-melted and reformed into something arthropod, railroad tracks that climb impossibly up a vine trunk before terminating in midair.

The fauna, when it appears, defies easy categorization. Rare glimpses reveal things that might have been machines once, or perhaps organisms that evolved to mimic industry. A creature like a living crane arm delicately plucks chrome flowers, storing them in a belly that glows furnace-orange. Something that resembles a fusion of beetle and bulldozer trundles across the sharp ground, leaving smooth paths that the vines immediately colonize.

The strangeness runs deeper than surface oddities. The industrial structures don't seem abandoned—they're active, producing heat, generating that subsonic hum, occasionally venting gases that smell of ozone and hot metal. But they're not occupied. They're part of the ecosystem now, like volcanic vents in a deep ocean. The cable-vines tap into buried power lines, their chrome flowers opening and closing in response to electrical currents. The floating water pools sometimes align themselves along invisible electromagnetic fields, forming temporary rivers in the air.

The turbulence that defines this place isn't meteorological—it's systemic. Everything here exists in a state of constant renegotiation. The substrate cracks and re-forms. The vines grow and die in hours-long cycles. The scattered machinery sputters to life, performs some arcane industrial process, then falls dormant until the next triggering event. Even the rare fauna seems to appear and disappear according to patterns too complex to track.

In the relative quiet—for this place is eerily silent despite its activity—you can sometimes hear the deep breathing of the factories, the whisper of spores falling like iron filings, the distant crack of substrate splitting to birth new formations. The silence itself feels industrial, manufactured, as if sound is being actively suppressed by some vast noise-cancellation system.

This is a landscape in violent adolescence, where the boundary between the built and the grown has dissolved entirely. It's a place where industrial processes have become geological ones, where evolution happens at the speed of an assembly line, where the vast scale means that somewhere, at this very moment, a mountain of twisted metal and vine is being born while another collapses into constituent atoms, ready to be reconfigured again.

The light here carries its own quality—not quite natural sun, but the accumulated glow of a trillion tiny industrial processes, each chrome flower a little LED, each floating pool a lens that focuses and refracts. The shadows have weight. The air tastes of copper and chlorophyll. 

This is creation still warm from the forge, a world where the industrial revolution never ended but instead became the fundamental law of nature itself.
