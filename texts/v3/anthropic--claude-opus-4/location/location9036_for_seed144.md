The Brass Terraces of the Everforge

You stand at the edge of a vast amphitheater of metal, where industrial civilization carved itself into the bones of a young volcanic caldera. The heat rises in visible columns from copper-colored plateaus that step down into darkness, each terrace precisely machined from the native iron-rich basalt. No rain has ever fallen here—the temperature hovers perpetually around 140 degrees Fahrenheit, sustained by the geothermal vents that first drew settlers to this inhospitable place centuries ago.

The terraces crowd together, packed so tightly that the walkways between them are mere threads of darker metal, just wide enough for the maintenance crawlers that still patrol their programmed routes. Each plateau bristles with dormant machinery: injection towers, thermal extractors, crystallization chambers—all fused now into sculptures of rust and mineral deposits. The industrial infrastructure grows denser as you look deeper into the caldera, where the heat-shimmer makes the lower levels writhe like a living thing.

What little light reaches this place comes filtered through the perpetual haze of metallic dust and sulfur compounds. The sun, when visible, appears as a dull orange coin. Most illumination comes from below—the faint red glow of exposed thermal conduits and the occasional flicker of still-functioning indicator lights that stud the machinery like dying stars.

The air tastes of copper and hot stone. Sound barely carries in the dense atmosphere, muffled by the heat gradients. Only the occasional ping of contracting metal or the whisper of automated systems cycling through their eternal routines breaks the silence. Sometimes, deep in the maze of terraces, you might catch a glimpse of movement—one of the few adapted creatures that survived the industrialization, perhaps a heat-drinking silicon-mite or a metal-boring thermophage, creatures so specialized they exist nowhere else.

The entire landscape exists in perfect stasis. The volcanic activity that created the caldera stabilized eons ago into a steady, predictable heat source. The industrial systems, built to be self-maintaining, continue their purposeless cycles. Even the erosion happens at a pace measured in millennia—the young basalt too hard, the air too dry, the temperature too constant for any real weathering. 

Scattered across the terraces, you find the shells of control stations and monitoring posts, their hexagonal windows dark, their doors sealed. These small structures punctuate the mechanical landscape like punctuation marks in an unfinished sentence. Inside, if you could enter, you'd find everything preserved in the dry heat: chairs pushed back from desks, screens dark but uncracked, logs that end mid-entry on a day that seems both recent and impossibly distant.

The true strangeness reveals itself in the patterns. Look long enough and you realize the terraces aren't random—they form a massive spiral, a geometric perfection that speaks to some grand industrial purpose never quite achieved. The machinery, too, connects in ways that suggest function beyond mere resource extraction. Some say the entire caldera was being converted into a single vast machine. Others claim it already is one, running a program too slow and subtle for human perception.

At the rim where you stand, the heat is merely oppressive. But peer over the edge, down through the packed terraces with their frozen industrial dreams, and you can see where the geometry breaks down, where the ordered terraces give way to something older and stranger—the original throat of the volcano, still glowing with primal heat, around which all this careful engineering arranged itself like iron filings around a magnet.
