The Vapor Gardens crown this plateau like a fever dream of iron and orchids.

Steam rises perpetually through the grated floors—a thousand perforations in brass and copper that form the substrate of this entirely constructed place. The heat comes from below, from the ancient boiler hearts that were built into the mountain centuries ago, their coal-fires long since replaced by geothermal taps that drink from the planet's molten core. The original engineers are forgotten, but their machinery endures, patient and self-regulating, maintaining temperatures that would kill an unprotected human in minutes.

Yet life thrives here in forms both engineered and evolved. Condensation runs down every surface—rivulets tracing the riveted seams of iron pillars, gathering in channels designed to direct the flow into collection basins that overflow into other basins, creating a vertical watershed entirely of metal. The pillars themselves are overgrown with heat-loving vines that have learned to grip hot metal, their leaves perpetually beaded with moisture, their roots threading through gaps in the plating to drink from the steam pipes below.

The space is perhaps the size of a cathedral nave, though instead of stone vaults overhead there is only a lattice of pipes and catwalks, draped with hanging gardens of tropical abundance. Ferns cascade from every joint and junction. Orchids bloom in profusion—some original to the tropics far below, others bred specifically for this environment, their petals unnaturally large and fleshy, adapted to capture moisture from the air. There are flowers here that bloom only when the pressure valves release, triggered by the subtle percussion of steam that punctuates the quiet at irregular intervals.

Everything drips. The sound is subtle but constant—water finding its way down through layers of vegetation and machinery, each drop's journey a negotiation between leaf and pipe, moss and metal. Sometimes a valve will wheeze open with a soft hiss, releasing a fresh cloud of vapor that rolls through the foliage like morning fog. The plants seem to anticipate these moments, leaves turning to catch the moisture-laden air.

The walkways are treacherous with condensation and algae. Brass railings, green with verdigris, offer handholds slick with moisture. The flooring—expanded metal grating—reveals glimpses of the mechanical depths below: more pipes, more steam, more green things growing in defiance of their industrial substrate. Sometimes a bright flower peers up through the grating from below, having found some impossible foothold in the mechanical substrate.

In the center of this space stands the Accumulator—a great brass vessel like an inverted bell, thirty feet tall, its surface completely obscured by a climbing fig whose aerial roots have created a secondary structure around the metal, a living cage that pulses with collected water. The roots channel condensation down into a pool at the base where lotus-like plants grow in the warm water, their leaves perfectly circular, engineered to maximize surface area for transpiration.

The fauna here is minimal and strange. Occasionally a heat-drunk butterfly will materialize from the foliage—wings iridescent with moisture, moving slowly through the thick air. There are rumors of pale geckos that live exclusively on the hot pipes, their bellies evolved to tolerate temperatures that would cook other creatures. But mostly it is just the plants and the machinery, locked in their slow symbiosis.

The light filters down through the layers—sunlight diffused by steam and foliage until it becomes something aqueous and uncertain. The industrial lamps, mounted at regular intervals, create pools of yellow illumination that seem to float in the vapor. Their electrical hum is almost inaudible beneath the subtle percussion of dripping water and the occasional sigh of releasing steam.

Time moves differently here. The machinery operates on cycles measured in hours or days—building pressure, releasing, cooling, beginning again. The plants follow these rhythms, their growth spurts coinciding with the steam releases, their flowering triggered by temperature fluctuations that would be imperceptible to human senses. Even the corrosion happens slowly—brass turning green over decades, iron developing its patina of rust that somehow never compromises the structural integrity, as if the builders understood that decay itself could be incorporated into the design.

At the edges of this space, where the mechanical substrate meets the natural rock of the plateau, the boundary blurs. Pipes disappear into fissures lined with ferns. Steam emerges from cracks that might be natural vents or carefully drilled channels. The distinction between what was built and what was found has been lost to time and overgrowth, creating a place that is neither fully artificial nor entirely natural, but something altogether more strange—a garden that breathes with mechanical lungs, a machine that dreams in flowers and rust.
