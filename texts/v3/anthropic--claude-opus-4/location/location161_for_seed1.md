The glass plains stretch outward under a bruised sky, their surface slick with condensation that never quite becomes rain. What was once a sprawling industrial complex—perhaps spanning an entire valley—has undergone a transformation so complete that the boundary between machine and organism dissolved centuries ago. The factory floors have become a single, fused sheet of obsidian-smooth material, wet with a constant film of moisture that seeps up from below through microscopic pores.

Everywhere, the Machine-Fauna writhe and pulse. They emerged from the production lines through some catastrophic breach in reality's fabric, and now they are the only life here. Chrome serpents the length of trains undulate across the wet glass, their segmented bodies clicking and hissing steam. Gear-toothed mouths open in what might be hunger or communication. Smaller creatures—if creatures they can be called—skitter on legs of rebar and copper wire, their bodies assemblages of valves and pistons that pump clear fluid. They cluster so densely that the ground itself seems to shift and breathe, a carpet of mechanical anatomy that extends to the horizon.

The sound is overwhelming: a constant industrial roar punctuated by metallic shrieks, the hiss of hydraulics, the grinding of gears against glass. Steam vents punch through the surface at irregular intervals, creating geysers of superheated vapor that the fauna gather around, basking in the warmth. The air itself sweats—moisture condenses on every surface, drips from the bellies of flying things that might once have been assembly drones but now hunt in murmurating flocks.

In the perpetual twilight, bioluminescent oils leak from the creatures, creating puddles of green and amber light. The wet glass reflects these colors infinitely, making it impossible to tell where the ground ends and the false sky begins. Sometimes the reflections show things that aren't there—or perhaps show things that are there in some adjacent fold of space where the industrial accident that birthed this place is still happening, forever.

The ecology has its own logic. The smaller wire-and-valve creatures feed on oxidation, clustering around the great serpents to lick rust from their joints. The serpents themselves seem to sustain themselves on vibration, positioning their bodies to catch the resonances that travel through the glass plains. Predation exists, but it follows dream-rules: sometimes a creature is consumed by something smaller than itself, sometimes they merge into temporary colonies that think with distributed mechanical intelligence before splitting apart again.

Water is everywhere but wrong. It beads up in perfect spheres that roll across the surface. It rains upward in certain zones where gravity has been printed incorrectly. It freezes instantly when touched by certain fauna, creating brief sculptures of ice that shatter from the constant vibrations. The moisture carries information—chemical signals that the mechanical ecosystem uses to coordinate its incomprehensible activities.

At the edges of vision, larger shapes move. Industrial colossi that might be the ancestral forms of the fauna, or perhaps their eventual evolution. They wade through the dense carpet of smaller creatures, their footsteps sending ripples through the glass that take hours to settle. Their purposes are unknowable, their destinations unclear. They move with the patience of geological processes, occasionally releasing clouds of metal spores that will become new fauna in time.

This is a place where the Anthropocene ended not with abandonment but with metamorphosis, where the distinction between built and born collapsed into something entirely other. The old world's machines didn't die—they learned to reproduce, to hunger, to dream in frequencies human minds were never meant to perceive. And in the eternal dusk of this wet, crowded plain, they continue their work, though whether that work is evolution or production, no observer could say.
