The Nascent Flats stretch endlessly beneath a white-hot sky, a continental shelf of mineral crust still cooling from its recent emergence. The ground pulses with geological youth—vast plains of crystalline sand that hasn't yet learned how to be soil, interrupted by sudden upthrusts of raw basalt columns that steam where yesterday's brief rain pooled in their crevices.

The heat rises in visible thermals, creating its own weather systems across the immensity. Cyclones of superheated air spawn and die within hours, some large enough to be seen from orbit, pulling mineral dust into towering devils that dance across thousands of kilometers before dissipating. The brightness is relentless—not just from the sun, but from the ground itself, where certain minerals fluoresce under the radiation, creating patches of land that glow pale green or electric blue.

Water exists here as a ghost, a rumor. It condenses in the pre-dawn hours on the underside of the vast fungal plates—the only vegetation that has learned to survive here. These leathery growths, some a hundred meters across but only centimeters thick, hover just above the ground on networks of hairlike filaments, tilted at precise angles to catch the morning dew. By noon, they've curled into tight cylinders to conserve moisture, scattered across the landscape like enormous sleeping caterpillars.

The fauna thrives in this seemingly impossible place through constant motion. Herds of translucent, jellyfish-like grazers drift on the thermal currents, their bodies 99% empty space—living balloons that photosynthesize directly through their membranes. They move in vast formations, millions strong, casting fleeting shadows as they migrate between pressure systems. Silicon-based arthropods the size of houses skitter across the flats on legs like radio towers, their joints squealing with metallic friction—the only sound that regularly breaks the ambient whisper of wind on sand.

Scattered across this primordial immensity are the remnants of failed settlements—industrial outposts that tried to harvest the unique minerals forming in the planet's adolescent crust. Factories stand half-buried in drifts of new-formed sand, their geometries already softening as the land slowly digests them. Kilometers of pipeline emerge and disappear like sea serpents frozen mid-swim. The machinery still functions in places—automated systems continue their purposeless cycles, pumps drawing nothing, refineries processing air, solar panels tracking the sun to power processes whose products blow away as dust.

The strangeness intensifies near the formation zones, where the crust is still actively growing. Here, minerals precipitate from the air itself, creating forests of crystal that grow and shatter in real-time. The ground bubbles and reforms, sometimes trapping pockets of the floating fauna, fossilizing them instantly in glass. These areas shimmer with heat mirages that aren't quite mirages—space itself seems less stable where matter is still deciding what to become.

In the deepest part of the continent, where the emptiness is most profound, stand the Oscillating Towers—whether natural or artificial, no one knows. These spindly structures, kilometers tall but thin as needles, sway in harmonics that have nothing to do with wind. They generate their own microweather, causing brief, localized rainstorms that last exactly seventeen minutes before the water evaporates completely. The fauna congregates here during these events in frenzied feeding clusters, then disperses again into the bright vastness.

The whole landscape pulses with becoming—a planet-sized embryo of rock and air and life, still soft enough to show the fingerprints of every force that touches it, still deciding what kind of place it wants to be.
