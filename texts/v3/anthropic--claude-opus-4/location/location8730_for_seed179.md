The plateau spreads like a vast, warm tongue of stone, its surface riddled with thousands of shallow depressions where water gathers but never quite pools. Each depression holds a different depth of wetness—some merely damp with a sheen of moisture that seems to seep up from within the rock itself, others containing an inch or two of perfectly still water that reflects nothing, not even the sky above.

The stone here is young basalt, its edges still sharp enough to cut, formed perhaps mere centuries ago when something—not quite volcanic, not quite tectonic—pushed this table of rock up through older layers. The surface fractures in geometric patterns that shouldn't occur naturally: perfect hexagons nested within hexagons, each one weeping a thin film of warm water that tastes of minerals unknown to any periodic table.

Sparse vegetation clings to the cracks between these geometric pools—not quite moss, not quite lichen, but something that shifts between states. In one moment it appears as a dry, crystalline crust; breathe on it and it unfurls into something soft and plantlike, releasing spores that hang in the air far longer than gravity should allow. These floating particles create dense clouds that drift at ankle height, never rising, never settling, moving in patterns that suggest intention without consciousness.

The warmth here doesn't come from sun alone. The rock itself exhales heat in slow pulses, as if some massive circulatory system flows beneath. Where the water emerges from the stone, it steams gently, and in these wisps of vapor, shapes form that the eye refuses to properly resolve—not quite faces, not quite symbols, but something that makes the mind itch with almost-recognition.

Sound barely exists here. The water makes no splash, no trickle. The sparse plants rustle without wind. Instead, there's a quality to the silence that feels thick, as if the air has been replaced with something denser yet still breathable. Occasionally, from deep within the stone, comes a resonance felt more than heard—a thrumming that matches no earthly frequency, setting the water in each pool vibrating in patterns that spell out words in languages that won't be invented for another thousand years.

The horizon shimmers not with heat mirages but with something else—places where the landscape folds in on itself, where distant features appear closer than near ones, where the same cluster of vegetation appears at multiple points along sightlines that shouldn't intersect. Walking toward these anomalies, one finds the distance never quite closes; walking away, one discovers they've somehow circled back.

Time moves strangely across this expanse. Water evaporates upward in some pools while simultaneously condensing from nothing in others. The sparse plants age and youth in the span of minutes, their lifecycle a closed loop that never quite completes. Shadows fall at angles that suggest multiple light sources, though only one sun hangs in the sky—or perhaps it's the same sun viewed from different moments, all happening at once.

The only evidence of tool use appears in the patterns themselves—the too-perfect geometry of the pools, the deliberate spacing of the vegetation, the way certain stones have been placed (but by what? when?) to channel the seeping water into specific configurations. These arrangements serve no obvious purpose, yet their intentionality is undeniable. They feel less like construction and more like language, a vast statement written in landscape that awaits a reader who exists in the right configuration of spacetime to comprehend it.

In the densest areas, where the pools cluster so closely their edges nearly touch, the water forms a network of connection, and in these places reality grows thinnest. Stand at the right confluence and you might see through the ground to stars that shouldn't be below, or catch glimpses of this same plateau but forested, or frozen, or made of altogether different matter—all versions equally true, all happening here, now, always.
