The Vermillion Hollows

Deep beneath the crust, where stone gives way to something neither quite rock nor air, the caverns bloom with impossible gardens. Here, in these vast subterranean chambers, vegetation thrives without water—a paradox that defines this realm.

The plants are crystalline things, their "leaves" formed from paper-thin sheets of sulfur compounds that metabolize the intense heat rising from below. They grow in spectacular profusion: towering columns of ruby-red mineral fronds, cascading curtains of golden filaments that tinkle like wind chimes when disturbed by the thermal currents, forests of translucent spires that branch and rebranch in perfect mathematical spirals. The vegetation feeds not on water but on the chemical gradients in the superheated air—oxidizing iron vapor, reducing sulfur dioxide, building their bodies from the mineral breath of the deep earth.

The caverns themselves are young, geologically speaking. Their walls still bear the sharp, unweathered marks of their violent birth—some ancient upheaval that cracked the world open and left these immense voids. The ceilings arch hundreds of meters overhead, disappearing into heat-hazed darkness. The floors undulate in frozen waves of fresh basalt, still glassy in places where the molten rock cooled too quickly to form proper crystals.

Scale here defies easy comprehension. A single chamber might stretch for kilometers, connected to others by a labyrinth of passages—some vast as cathedral naves, others barely wide enough for the strange mineral forests to send their runners through. The emptiness between these gardens is profound. One might walk for hours through barren stone galleries, hearing only the whisper of one's own movement and the distant ping of thermal expansion in the rock, before suddenly emerging into another grove of the impossible vegetation.

Scattered throughout are the remnants of some ancient presence. Not ruins exactly, but deliberate arrangements—circles of fitted stone, channels carved to direct the flow of mineral-laden air, simple shelters built from stacked plates of shale. Tools lie abandoned: hammers of volcanic glass, chisels of harder minerals, ceramic vessels that might have held... what? Not water, surely. Perhaps they collected the condensed metal vapors that sometimes form on the cooler stones, or stored the edible crystals that grow like fruit on certain varieties of the mineral trees.

The builders are long gone, but their works remain perfectly preserved in this unchanging environment. No water exists here to rust or erode. No freeze-thaw cycles crack the stone. Even the vegetation, for all its lush abundance, grows with geological patience—a new frond perhaps every decade, a fallen crystal tree lying exactly where it toppled centuries ago, its body too alien to decay.

Light comes from the plants themselves. The hottest specimens glow with their own incandescence—deep reds and oranges that pulse with their strange metabolism. Others phosphoresce in cooler tones, drinking in the infrared and re-emitting it as ghostly blues and greens. The quality of light shifts as you move through the caverns, from ember-glow to foxfire to the occasional shaft of normal illumination where some natural light pipe brings down a reminder of the distant surface.

Rarely, very rarely, you might glimpse one of the few creatures that inhabit this realm. Silicon-based things that look like animate clusters of crystal, moving with painful slowness across the stone. Salamander-forms that swim through pools of liquid sulfur, visible only by the wake they leave in the glowing fluid. Something that might be insects or might be self-organizing mineral formations, creating brief, tinkling clouds as they scatter at your approach.

The silence is not absolute but close to it. The mineral forests make their subtle music—the ping of thermal stress, the whisper of chemical reactions, the occasional crash as an overgrown structure finally succumbs to its own weight and shatters across the stone. But these sounds are swallowed by the vastness, leaving long stretches where the only sound is the blood rushing in your own ears.

This is a place outside of time. The mineral gardens grow and die and grow again, but nothing truly changes. The abandoned stone circles wait eternally for their builders' return. The heat rises, the crystals feed, the vast emptiness between holds its breath forever. Even the air itself seems ancient—thick with metallic taste, heavy with particles that catch the colored light, moving in convection currents that have followed the same paths for millennia.

To walk here is to move through a dream of geology, where life has found an impossible foothold in conditions that should forbid it, where beauty emerges from the marriage of heat and stone and chemistry, where the normal rules are gently set aside in favor of something altogether stranger.
