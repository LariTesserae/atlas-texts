The Root Cathedral stretches beneath the world's skin, a vast hollow that breathes with chlorophyll and time. Here, in these dim galleries that extend for untold miles, the distinction between built and grown has long since dissolved into irrelevance.

The walls—if walls they can be called—are woven from root systems so ancient they've fossilized while still alive. Petrified wood and living tissue merge in seamless gradients, creating a substrate that is both stone-hard and supple, both mineral and vegetal. These root-walls arch overhead in Gothic curves, their surfaces slick with perpetual condensation that beads and runs in channels worn smooth by ten thousand years of the same drops following the same paths.

Everything here was crafted, but not by human hands. The builders were patient: moss colonies that learned to shape their growth into steps and platforms, fungal networks that hardened into bridges, root systems that knotted themselves into dwellings. Over millennia, these primitive organic architects created a total environment—rooms within rooms, galleries opening onto galleries, all grown rather than constructed yet undeniably *made*.

The fauna exists in overwhelming density, but you must adjust your eyes to see it. What first appears as bark texture reveals itself as clustered beetles, their shells evolved to match the root-wood exactly. What seems like hanging moss is composed of hairlike animals, millions of them, swaying in the perpetual tiny air currents. The "leaves" that carpet every surface are actually flat worms that photosynthesize in the dim phosphorescent glow that emanates from certain root nodes. Every surface crawls, though so slowly that the motion is nearly imperceptible—a living carpet of creatures stacked upon creatures, each adapted to feed on the waste of another, creating closed loops of consumption and production that have achieved perfect equilibrium.

Water seeps constantly but quietly through this living architecture. It weeps from root pores, pools in grown basins, runs through channels that are neither quite artifact nor quite natural. The sound is less than a whisper—just the faintest percussion of drops, the subtle movement of a billion tiny bodies, the almost-inaudible respiration of the root-walls themselves.

The air hangs thick and warm, saturated with moisture and the sweet-earth smell of eternal decomposition and growth. Spores drift lazily through the dimness, visible only when they pass through the occasional shaft of brighter bioluminescence. The atmosphere has a weight to it, pressing against exposed skin with humid insistence.

Time stopped mattering here eons ago. The same water cycles through the same routes. The same creatures are born, live, and die in the same niches their ancestors have occupied since before human memory. Even the slow growth of the root architecture has reached its ultimate expression—every possible space filled, every surface optimized, every relationship between organism and organism locked into unchanging patterns.

In certain deeper galleries, you can find the tools that started it all—stone axes with handles of grown wood, their blades worn smooth, embedded now in root-walls like holy relics. Clay vessels that once held water now serve as nodes where root systems converge. The primitive technologies that first shaped this place have been absorbed into it, becoming part of the living architecture they helped create.

This is not decay or abandonment. This is completion—a made environment that reached its perfect state and stopped, held in eternal equilibrium by the very organisms that comprise it. The Root Cathedral is less a place than a single vast organism pretending to be architecture, or perhaps architecture that learned to live. Down here, in the warm darkness below the world, the distinction hardly matters anymore.
