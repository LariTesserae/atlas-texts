The Singing Hollows stretch beneath the earth, a vast network of carved chambers where water weeps eternally from stone that has never known sunlight. This is a city built in the negative space of the world—not raised up but carved down, hollowed out over millennia by a civilization that understood stone and water better than they understood the sky.

The walls glisten with perpetual moisture, mineral deposits creating curtains of flowstone that divide the spaces into districts and neighborhoods. Everything here was carved, not built—the ancient masons worked by subtraction, following veins of harder rock and natural fissures to create a sprawling underground metropolis. Bridges span chasms as gossamer threads of worked stone, so thin they seem impossible, yet they've held for thousands of years. The sound of water is omnipresent: dripping, flowing, cascading through channels carved to carry it precisely where the builders intended.

The cold is bone-deep and permanent, a chill that comes from stone that has never been warmed by sun. Breath mists in the air, and the moisture condenses on every surface, making the carved steps treacherous and the handholds slick. Ice forms in the deepest chambers, creating palace-walls of frozen waterfalls that never melt.

What makes this place truly strange is what lives here now. The original builders are long gone, but their city has been colonized by creatures that need no light to thrive. Pale, blind things the size of cattle graze on the mineral deposits, their bodies translucent and shot through with veins of bioluminescence. They move in vast herds through the carved boulevards, their hooves clicking on stone, their low songs reverberating through the chambers in harmonies that make the walls themselves ring like bells.

These creatures—locals call them stone-singers—have no eyes but navigate by echolocation, their constant vocalizations creating a wall of sound that fills every space. Their songs bounce off the carved surfaces, creating interference patterns that somehow map the space in ways human senses can barely comprehend. The noise is overwhelming: not just the animals but the water, the echoes, the way sound travels strangely through spaces carved according to acoustic principles we no longer understand.

The city remains perfectly preserved, frozen in time. The water that seeps through its walls deposits minerals that repair any damage, slowly filling in cracks and breaks. The stone-singers polish the floors smooth with their passing. Nothing changes here—the same water follows the same channels it has followed for ten thousand years, the same herds follow the same paths their ancestors wore into the stone, the same echoes bounce between the same walls.

The only human inhabitants now are small bands who've learned to live in the upper galleries, where the sound is merely deafening rather than maddening. They've built simple shelters from leather and bone in the ancient alcoves, fish blind pale things from the underground rivers, and work copper they trade from the surface world. They cannot farm here—nothing grows in the eternal darkness save strange fungi that the stone-singers cultivate in their wake, and those are poison to human tongues.

The scale defies comprehension. Galleries open onto galleries, levels stack upon levels, and the carved highways run for miles through the darkness. Maps are useless—the sound changes the perception of space, makes near seem far and far seem near. Travelers speak of walking for days through identical corridors only to emerge where they started, or of taking a single turn and finding themselves in districts no living person has seen before.

In the deepest places, where the cold becomes something almost solid and the darkness has weight, there are carvings that hurt to look at—geometries that the eye refuses to resolve, patterns that seem to move when observed directly. The stone-singers avoid these places. Their songs go silent at the threshold, and they turn back to their familiar paths, leaving these depths undisturbed in their perfect, terrible preservation.

The water remembers, they say. It carries the memory of the builders in its mineral taste, deposits their thoughts as flowstone on the walls. Sometimes, in the harmony of the stone-singers' chorus, you can almost hear words—not in any language spoken now, but in the rhythm of water on stone, in the resonance of carefully carved chambers, in the echo that comes back changed from the deep places where even the herds won't go.

This is the Singing Hollows: a dead city that lives, a silence that screams, a preservation that transforms. Water and sound and stone locked in an eternal dance, while blind things trace the steps of architects who carved their own tomb from the living rock and filled it with music that will never end.
