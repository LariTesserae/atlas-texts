# The Tidepool Oubliette

Where sand becomes stone at the edge of forgetting, there is a pool that remembers too much.

It sits just beyond the reach of ordinary tides, carved into basalt that cooled wrong—too fast in some places, too slowly in others, leaving a basin whose depth changes depending on how you look at it. During spring tides, when the moon pulls hardest, seawater finds its way in through cracks that shouldn't connect to the ocean. The rest of the time, it holds only what falls from the sky and what seeps up from below: a brine so dense with minerals that touching it leaves crystal ghosts on your skin.

The pool is no larger than a dining table, if dining tables were made by someone who had heard of circles but never seen one. Its edges wander, following laws of their own devising. The water is the color of old mirrors.

---

Things collect here.

Not driftwood or kelp or the usual wrack of shorelines—those belong to the everyday ocean, the one that behaves itself. This pool gathers other things. Forgotten things. A button made of horn, its holes still threaded with something that was once blue. A key to a lock that rusted away three hundred years ago. Seeds from plants that never evolved, suspended in the dense water like possibilities in amber. Each object sinks to a different level and stays there, as though the pool were not one depth but many, all existing simultaneously.

The water sorts memories by weight.

Light ones float: the taste of bread you'll never eat again, the specific temperature of your mother's hand when you were very small. Heavier memories sink deeper: the moment you understood betrayal, the exact quality of light the afternoon someone died. At the very bottom—if there is a bottom—lie the memories no one alive remembers. The pool keeps them anyway. Just in case.

Sometimes, when the angle of light is precisely wrong, you can see all the way down. Through the suspended keys and buttons and seeds, through the layered memories rippling like heat mirages, to something that might be stone or might be the pearl nacre of forgetting itself. Looking too long makes your teeth ache.

---

The Archivist lives beside the pool, though *lives* implies choices that were made rather than circumstances that congealed.

She might have been human once—there's a humanness to her shape, viewed slantwise, a suggestion of shoulders and spine and the place where a face would go. But the years of tending the pool have translated her into something else. Her skin has taken on the quality of the basalt: smooth in places, porous in others, with a darkness that drinks light. When she moves, she flows more than walks, as though her bones have learned to bend like water finding its level.

She no longer remembers arriving, but she knows she must have. The pool does not make its own archivists. Someone has to choose it first, has to kneel at its edge and offer something to its keeping. Then the staying happens. The slow adaptation. The gathering of other people's forgotten things, the careful maintenance of the levels, the patient work of ensuring that what needs keeping is kept.

Her hands—she still has hands, mostly—are always damp. The mineral water has left its mark: her fingernails are crystalline now, and when she holds something up to examine it, light passes through her fingers like they're made of clouded quartz.

She speaks, but rarely. Her voice sounds like water moving through narrow spaces.

---

Each day—though day is an approximation, for the pool exists in a fold of coastline where fog makes its own time—the Archivist performs the necessary work.

First, she skims. Things arrive in the pool by their own methods: falling from seabirds that don't exist anymore, bubbling up from springs that connect to nowhere, sometimes simply appearing in the early morning as though they had always been there. Not everything that arrives belongs. Some memories are too fresh, still claimed by the living. Some objects still serve purposes. These she removes with a net made of something that might be silk or might be the shed wing-scales of extinct moths, placing them gently on the rocks to wait for the tide or the wind or whatever brought them to take them away again.

Then she sorts. The pool tries to organize itself, but entropy is always at work. A button sinks too far and disturbs the sediment of early grief. A key rises beyond its proper level and tangles with the taste of honeycomb. She reaches in—her arms can extend deeper than they should, joints multiplying as needed—and makes small adjustments. Places things where they can rest without disturbing the neighbors.

Sometimes she must dive.

The pool accepts her the way it accepts its treasures: completely, without question. She slips beneath the surface and the water closes over her without ripples, and down here the sorting is more delicate. These are the older things. The heavier things. A bronze pin from an empire that left no other trace. The word for snow in a language that died before writing was invented. The specific shade of green the sky turned during a volcanic winter no human eye survived to record, but which the pool remembers anyway, just in case.

She can hold her breath for hours. Or perhaps time moves differently beneath the surface. Or perhaps, in the pool, breathing is just another kind of remembering, and she has forgotten how to need it.

---

The pool changes her, as it changes everything it holds.

Her memories have begun to sort themselves the way the pool sorts its contents. Recent things float—the taste of this morning's fog, the particular ache in her crystallized fingers. Older memories sink. She can no longer recall her name without diving for it, and when she surfaces with it clasped in her translucent hands, it sits there for a moment like a caught fish before slipping away again, back to its proper depth.

She doesn't mind. Names are for people who might be called. She is the Archivist. The pool knows her; that's enough.

Her body has adapted in other ways. Her eyes have developed a third lid—transparent, nictitating—that lets her see clearly in the mineral-dense water. Her spine has learned to curve in ways that accommodate the pool's non-Euclidean depths. When she sleeps (she still sleeps, sometimes, curled on the basalt beside the pool like a question mark made of dark glass), she dreams in sediment layers: newest on top, oldest below, all of it settling according to its weight.

---

Visitors come, occasionally. The pool is hard to find—it exists in a place where maps disagree with themselves—but sometimes someone stumbles across it. A child following tidepools farther than tidepools go. A grief-stricken wanderer looking for edges. A researcher who took a wrong turn in their data and ended up here, where information takes physical form.

The Archivist watches them from angles that make her hard to see. Most visitors peer into the pool, disturbed by the clarity that shows too much, and leave quickly. But some stay. Some have brought things that need keeping.

A woman brings her grandfather's fishing weight, worn smooth by years of use, and drops it in. It sinks to precisely the right level, settling among objects from the same weight of love.

A teenager throws in a handful of beach glass, each piece a different shade of letting go.

An old man kneels at the edge for a long time, saying nothing, then spits into the water. His spit carries something—the Archivist can see it, dense and dark as it descends. The memory of a lie, perhaps. Or the truth he told instead. Both are heavy.

She does not interfere with these offerings. The pool accepts what it accepts. Her job is only to tend, to sort, to maintain the levels where everything can rest according to its proper weight.

Once, a child asked her what it was all for. Why keep these things? Who needs a button without its coat, a key without its lock, the memory of bread that no one living has tasted?

The Archivist considered this. Her voice, when it came, sounded like water through stone: "Nothing is ever really lost. Only waiting at its proper depth."

The child left, apparently satisfied. The Archivist wasn't sure she had answered the question, but it was the only answer she had.

---

At night—or what passes for night in this place where fog makes its own schedule—phosphorescence blooms in the pool. Not from any living thing, but from the memories themselves. The forgotten things glow softly, each at its own frequency. The seeds pulse green-gold. The keys shine rust-red. The buttons emit a light that has no name but feels like afternoon.

The Archivist sits beside the pool during these hours and watches the light show of forgotten things. Sometimes she dips a crystalline finger into the water and stirs gently, watching the lights swirl and settle, swirl and settle. The patterns they make are almost like writing, almost like music, almost like meaning.

She is changing still. Each year (or whatever passes for years), she becomes a little more translucent, a little more mineral. One day, she knows, she will be indistinguishable from the basalt, from the pool itself. Another formation of wrong-cooled stone at the edge of the impossible sea.

When that happens, someone else will come. The pool will wait—it's good at waiting—until someone kneels at its edge and offers something so precious and useless that they can't bear to keep it but can't bear for it to be lost. And then the staying will happen. The slow adaptation. The patient work of tending depths that don't behave, of keeping things that have no purpose except to be kept.

But that's later. For now, the Archivist skims and sorts and dives. She maintains the levels. She ensures that the bronze pin doesn't disturb the volcanic green, that the lost language rests peacefully among its peers, that the taste of bread and the weight of betrayal each have their proper place in the taxonomy of forgetting.

The pool remembers too much. That's what it's for.

And she tends it, because someone has to.

Because some things, once sunk to their proper depth, must never be allowed to surface accidentally.

Because the world needs a place where forgotten things can rest according to their weight, where memories settle into sediment, where the lost and the left-behind can finally, finally be still.

The fog rolls in. The phosphorescence fades. Tomorrow there will be more things to sort.

The Archivist settles beside her pool and waits.
