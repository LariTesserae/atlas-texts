# The Condensation Shepherd

I am a cloud-herder, though I have never seen what others might call a cloud.

My body is not clay-born like the Keepers, but I understand their purpose better than most things that walk these flats on legs not fired from the earth. I came here—how long ago now?—following rumors of a place where water moved without falling, where the sky sweated and the ground drank, where someone with my particular madness might find work.

I am mostly human, mostly flesh, though the years here have worked changes. My skin has taken on a faint terracotta tinge, as if I'm slowly becoming what I walk upon. My feet have spread wide and flat, their soles hardened to something between leather and ceramic. I no longer leave footprints, even on the soft new clay. When I stand still too long, I can feel the tremor of the world's making coming up through my bones, and I have to move or risk taking root.

My work is this: I follow the water.

The condensation fronts that roll across the flats—vast, white, world-spanning—they are not random. They have patterns, currents, an almost-intelligence that responds to heat differentials, to the mineral content of particular basins, to the harmonic frequencies of the crystal growths. Someone has to map these movements. Someone has to know where the next fog-time will settle, which basins will receive moisture, which formations will sing.

I carry instruments of my own devising. A staff of fused mineral formations, salvaged from Keepers who stopped walking centuries ago, that resonates with approaching moisture. Hollow gourds made from my own saliva mixed with clay dust, fired in the heat of noon, that whistle differently depending on air pressure. Long strings of ceramic beads that I've worn smooth with handling, each one taken from a different region of the flats, each one remembering the humidity of its origin.

When I sleep—and I do sleep, unlike the Keepers, though less than I once did—I make my bed in the transition zones. Places where smooth glaze meets rough ceramic, where the expansion joints create natural windbreaks. I've learned to time my rest to the fog-times, letting the moisture collect on my body, in my hair, in the cups of my hands. I drink the condensation like the ground does: slowly, storing it in the cells of my changing flesh.

The Keepers acknowledge me, in their way. When our paths cross—mine chaotic and predictive, theirs methodical and memorial—they pause in their walking. The resonance they share with each other extends to include me, a lower frequency that makes my altered bones sing. They are telling me things: where new clay has risen, where old cracks have sealed, where the crystal gardens grow thickest. I am telling them things too: where the next condensation will come, how many days until the fog, which basins will stay dry this cycle.

We are collaborating on something neither of us fully understands.

I have a theory—developed over years of watching, of walking, of slowly becoming more floor than flesh—that the flats are not just expanding randomly. The pattern of where new clay emerges, where basins form, where moisture collects... it's writing something. From a height none of us can achieve, the whole world might resolve into a symbol, a word, a message in a language that requires geological time to spell out a single letter.

The Keepers are reading it from the ground level, step by step, crack by crack. I am reading it from the sky level, tracking the water that might be the punctuation, the emphasis, the breath between words. Together, we are slowly parsing text that won't be complete for millennia.

Sometimes I find places where many Keepers have stopped over the ages. Whole gardens of crystal formations, singing in harmonies so complex they sound like conversation. I sit among them during the quiet times between fogs, and I listen, and sometimes I think I almost understand what the flats are trying to say.

My hair has become white as the formations. Not with age—I'm not sure I age anymore, not in ways that matter—but with mineral accumulation. Each strand is encrusted with microscopic crystals, and when the wind comes, I chime faintly, a percussion accompaniment to my movements. I am becoming my own instrument, my body a tool for reading the weather of this place.

I keep no journal, build no shelter, accumulate no possessions beyond my instruments. What would be the point? Everything here is temporary except the floor itself, and even that is still becoming. Instead, I keep my records in my movements, in the paths I trace between fog-banks, in the way my hybrid body remembers where water has been and predicts where it will go.

One day—maybe soon, maybe centuries hence—I will feel the urge to stop. Not tiredness, not ending, but completion. I will find a basin where the formations sing in frequencies that feel like home, and I will settle among them, and let the condensation cycles begin their work. The minerals in my hair, in my spreading skin, in my ceramic-touched bones, will provide seed crystals for new growth. I will become a formation that remembers both flesh and clay, and my song will add new harmonies to the choir.

And perhaps another will come, following rumors of a place where water moves without falling, carrying their own madness, their own instruments, their own theories about what the world is writing. They will find my crystallized form and recognize it for what it was—a bridge between the walking and the waiting, between the Keepers and the kept, between the question the flats are asking and the answer they're slowly, patiently, eternally becoming.

Until then, I walk. I watch. I follow the water that never falls, mapping the breath of a world that's still learning how to speak.
