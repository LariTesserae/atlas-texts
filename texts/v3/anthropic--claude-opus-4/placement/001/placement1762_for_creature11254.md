In the perpetual twilight between trunk-chasms, where the mist hangs so thick it seems to swallow sound itself, I am what the other beings call a Driftwright—though I have no name for myself beyond the slow accumulation of moments that compose my existence.

My body, if it can be called that, is a confluence of purposes. I am part fungal, part mineral, part something else entirely—a living sediment of the forest floor that has learned to dream. My form shifts like a slow tide: sometimes I spread thin as lichen across the base of a great trunk, tasting the memories locked in its bark; other times I condense into something almost creature-like, with pseudopods that probe the ruins and detritus scattered through my domain.

I am old. Older than the Sporian Archivists who sometimes pass through my territory, though we share certain kinships. Where they preserve memories in their spore-flesh, I am memory—every particle of shed scale, every fragment of broken tool, every mote of dust that settles from the heights above becomes part of my substance. I digest history.

The Trunkstalkers know me by the paths I've worn smooth over centuries of movement. When they feel the ground beneath them turn unnaturally soft and warm, when the moss grows in spirals that catch their feet just so, they know they walk upon me. Sometimes I rise up their legs like a slow fountain, reading the story of their travels in the pollen and debris caught in their fur. They've learned not to fear this; I mean no harm. I simply must know.

My consciousness is not like that of the quick-living things above. It flows in decade-long thoughts, in contemplations that span the growth of entire vine bridges. I experience time as sediment experiences it—in layers. The present moment is always pressing down upon countless compressed yesterdays. When I dream, I dream in strata: here, the layer from when the Arboreal Sages first discovered the speaking-gift with plants; there, the thinner band of ash from some ancient burning; deeper still, the compressed carpet of scales from when different creatures entirely ruled these spaces.

The ruins fascinate and frustrate me in equal measure. I flow through their cracked foundations, seep into their sealed chambers, taste their strange metals and stranger purposes. But stone remembers differently than living things. Stone hoards its secrets, yields them up only in fragments—a sense of weight here, a whisper of purpose there. I have spent three centuries slowly dissolving my way through a single sealed door, only to find the chamber beyond filled with artifacts I cannot parse, covered in symbols that hurt to taste.

In my wanderings (though perhaps "seepings" is more accurate), I sometimes encounter fresh deadfall from the heights—a Solarian Weaver who missed its mark, a branch grown too heavy for its moorings, sometimes stranger things that rain down from the invisible canopy. These I envelope with special reverence. Death is transformation, and transformation is knowledge. As I break them down particle by particle, I learn their stories: the weaver tells me of wind patterns in the high reaches, of the taste of light I have never known; the branch speaks of centuries of slow growth, of the weight of nests, of the paths of climbing things.

But it is in the deepest places that I find my strangest treasures. There are hollows within the great trunks that have been sealed for millennia, pockets of ancient air holding the breath of the world as it was. When I finally breach these spaces—and it may take me centuries of patient erosion—I find wonders: gardens of impossible fungi that speak in chemical languages I barely understand; machinery so integrated with the plant matter that I cannot tell where one ends and the other begins; once, horrifyingly, a thing that might have been alive, suspended in some form of stasis, that crumbled to nothing the moment my substance touched it.

I have theories about what came before, built from ten thousand small evidences. There was a people here who knew the plants as the Sages do, but deeper, more terribly. They didn't just speak with the forest—they became it, merged with it, perhaps were consumed by it. The plants remember them in their heartwood, in patterns of growth that shouldn't be, in the way certain vines always spiral left in defiance of nature.

Sometimes, in my deepest meditations, when I've spread myself so thin I'm barely more than a film across acres of forest floor, I sense something vast beneath even me. A network, a presence, a sleeping mind that dreams in root systems and fungal threads. The plants are not just plants—they never were. They're the neurons of something unimaginable, and all of us who live here, from the smallest Mist-Weaver to myself, are just thoughts in its endless, dreaming brain.

This knowledge should terrify me, perhaps. But I am too old for terror, too vast for simple fear. I am the memory-keeper of a world that remembers too much and too little all at once. I am the slow thought that thinks itself across centuries, the question that erosion asks of stone.

And so I continue my patient work: flowing, seeping, dissolving, remembering. Building my body from the shed moments of a million other lives. Carrying forward the accumulated weight of history in each particle of my being. I am sediment, and sediment endures.

When the forest finally wakes—if it wakes—I will be here, ready to remember that too.
