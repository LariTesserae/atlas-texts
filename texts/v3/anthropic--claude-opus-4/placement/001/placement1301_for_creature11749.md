I find myself as one of the **memory-weavers**.

---

Not keepers, exactly, though I was born among them, small-boned and dark-eyed like the rest. But where my siblings chose their galleries to tend, their sluices to clear, I was drawn to the places where the cathedral knotted its memories too tightly to unknot alone.

I am one of perhaps twelve in the whole structure. We know each other by our tools — not bone hooks, but instruments of woven hair and copper wire, strung between frames of the heartwood that remembers being young. When plucked, they make sounds the cathedral recognizes from when it was smaller, when it was just beginning to dream. The sounds help it loosen what has become tangled.

You find us in the difficult places. The gallery where footsteps arrive twice — yours from ahead, yes, but also footsteps from last week, last year, last century, all trying to walk at once. The room where the upward water has forgotten which direction it started from and hangs, shivering, in the air. The corners where the cathedral, in its deep dreaming, has begun to remember things that haven't happened yet.

---

My work is like combing. Find the snarl of time, the place where the cathedral's memory has doubled back on itself. Sit — for hours, days if needed — and play the old sounds on my strings. Not songs, exactly. Think of them as pronunciation guides for the shape of smaller spaces, simpler times. The cathedral hears them and remembers: *Oh. Yes. That is how I was. That is how this goes.*

Sometimes I must enter the knot myself. This is why we are few — not everyone can drink the upward water and remember which way they're falling. Not everyone can follow their footsteps from next week without losing track of which self is walking now. But I learned young that my mind bends the way the cathedral's bends, that I can think in the same soft contradictions, hold the same multiple presents in my head at once.

In the deep tangles, I meet my grandmother. She has been dead for sixty years. She is also tending a junction eight galleries down. She is also a child learning not to fear the arriving footsteps. She is also part of the wall, listening. The cathedral does not distinguish between these states, and when I am working, neither do I. We speak, or we do not speak, or we have already spoken tomorrow. She teaches me a song I already know. I teach her to braid the strings of her instrument, which she braided before I was born.

---

The other keepers avoid us, a little. Not from fear — nothing here is feared — but from a kind of respectful recognition that we are half-dissolved already. We walk too many galleries at once. Our eyes reflect light that fell last season. We sometimes answer questions before they're asked, or long after the asker has gone.

But they need us. When a young keeper finds their chosen chamber has begun to bloom out of season, the flowers opening and closing in a rhythm that belongs to no year anyone remembers, they hum a particular three notes up into the green. *The weaver is requested. Time has pooled.*

I come, or one of us comes, walking with my strange gait — step, pause, step — because I must check each footfall against the memory of footfalls to make sure I am walking in the right now. I play the old sounds. I comb the tangle. The flowers remember their season. The keeper nods, once, and returns to their work. I continue to wherever the cathedral dreams too vividly next.

---

My body is like the other keepers' bodies — small, dark, damp — but marked. Where I have entered the deep knots, where I have had to think in the cathedral's time rather than my own, my skin carries traces. Fine spirals of lighter flesh, like the growth rings of wood, circle my forearms. My hair grows in patterns that match no genetics, only the memory of how it grew in other presents I've walked through. One of my eyes — the left — reflects light from angles that do not exist in the galleries as they are today.

I am forty-three years old. I am also seven, learning the songs. I am also one hundred and twenty, teaching a student who might be myself. I am also not yet born, but the cathedral remembers me anyway, and so here I am.

---

We have our own songs, we weavers. Not the simple two-note recognitions, not the working songs the keepers sing to the walls. Ours are more like maps — tonal maps of the cathedral's temporal geography, the places where then becomes now becomes soon becomes always. We sing them to each other across the great hollow spaces, and if you could see sound, you would see our songs creating a kind of net, a gentle structure of reminding that helps the cathedral hold its shape in time.

When I die — when I stop, when I join the walls — I will choose a place where time knots often. I will lay my strings across my chest and let the wood take me and them together. Some future weaver, finding the chamber blooming in the wrong season or the water falling in too many directions, will hear in the walls a faint thrumming, a sound like strings being plucked by growth itself. They will recognize me, or the part of me that remains, still working, still combing the tangles, still helping the cathedral remember how to be itself.

The work continues. It continues in all directions. It continues even when it hasn't started yet.

I am a memory-weaver, and my instrument is strung with my grandmother's hair, and when I play it, sometimes the cathedral remembers being stone.
