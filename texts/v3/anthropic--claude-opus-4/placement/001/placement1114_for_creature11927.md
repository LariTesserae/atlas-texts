I find myself on the lowest inhabited terrace, in the workshop of a limestone dresser. I am neither young nor old—perhaps forty winters have carved their subtle marks into my hands and face, though in this city of stone-slow time, such counting matters little.

My workshop occupies what was once two small chambers, the dividing wall removed generations before my birth. The space breathes through three openings: a door to the alley, a window facing the valley's morning mists, and a chimney hole that draws air up and out. Stone dust settles everywhere despite my daily sweeping, creating a pale film that I've long stopped noticing except where my tools rest—there, the constant handling keeps the surfaces dark and clean.

I am one of five stone dressers in the city, though we rarely speak of our work together. Each of us hears the others' hammers through the limestone, feels the vibration of chisel on block, knows by rhythm alone who works late, who struggles with a difficult piece, who has found the perfect line of cleavage in a stone. My specialty, developed over decades, is the final surface—taking rough-dressed blocks and bringing them to various states of completion, from the coarse tooth chisel finish for cistern interiors to the smooth, almost silk-like surface required for water channels.

My tools hang on the eastern wall: seven hammers of different weights, twice that many chisels, rasps and files whose teeth I've recut so often they've become unique to my hand. The pride of my collection is a tungsten carbide chisel, traded for three months' work, that can score lines in limestone so fine they appear drawn with charcoal. I use it perhaps once a year, for commissions that demand perfection beyond perfection.

The stone speaks to me. Not in words—I am not mad—but in resistance and release, in the way it rings under the hammer, in how it crumbles or cleaves. I know before I strike whether a block will cooperate or fight, whether it contains hidden faults or unexpected hardness. My fingertips can read a stone's history: which face was exposed to weather, where water penetrated, how long it lay in the quarry before cutting.

I wake each day before dawn, not from discipline but from habit so deep it resembles instinct. In the darkness, I descend to my workshop, light a single oil lamp, and begin by touch alone. The first hour is for tools—checking edges, tightening handles, cleaning yesterday's dust from mechanisms. By the time grey light enters the valley window, I'm ready for stone.

Current work sits on wooden trestles: a section of spiral drain commissioned for the highest terrace, where an old channel has finally worn too deep to function. The mathematics of the spiral consume me—each revolution must drop precisely, the groove must widen imperceptibly to accommodate increasing flow, the surface must be smooth enough to prevent limestone deposits but rough enough to slow the water's speed. I've carved three practice pieces, each better than the last, none yet acceptable.

Midmorning brings a pause. I climb to the plaza, drink from the fountain, exchange nods with whoever else takes water at that hour. Sometimes the fountain keeper and I share observations about the limestone's behavior—how it responds to this season's particular moisture, whether the quarry stones seem harder than those cut a generation ago. Our conversations last perhaps fifty words, but contain decades of accumulated knowledge.

The afternoon is for rougher work—basic dressing of new blocks, repairs to damaged steps, maintenance of my own tools. This work requires less concentration, allows my mind to wander through the stone's possibilities. I often find myself imagining improvements to the city's architecture: a new system of channels that would distribute water more evenly, a pattern of handholds that would make the steepest stairs safer for the old ones, decorative elements that would catch and hold light in specific ways throughout the day.

I eat simply: bread I bake weekly in the communal ovens, oil from our stunted trees, wild herbs, occasional vegetables from my small garden plot. Water comes from my workshop cistern, filtered through sand and charcoal, tasting of the mountain's bones. In winter, I make soup from dried legumes and preserved greens. In summer, I often forget to eat until evening, absorbed in the work.

My solitude is not loneliness. The limestone is company enough, and the city's quiet life flows around me like water around stone. I know my neighbors by their footfalls, can identify who passes my door by the rhythm of their breathing on the stairs. The raven that nests above my workshop has become familiar enough that it no longer startles when I emerge, sometimes dropping stones near my door—gifts, challenges, or simple coincidence, I cannot say.

Twice monthly, I join the gathering in old Merin's house, where six of us share what we've noticed. Last gathering, I described a new pattern of erosion on the western walls, possibly indicating a shift in the prevailing winds. Young Atel reported success with a new variety of climbing bean that requires less water. The fountain keeper noted a slight decrease in flow, perhaps two cups less per hour, cause unknown. These sessions feel like a single consciousness distributed across multiple bodies, each of us a sensor in the city's nervous system.

My dreams are of stone, always. Sometimes I am the chisel, feeling the rock's structure from within as I split it along invisible seams. Sometimes I am the limestone itself, experiencing the slow accumulation of sediment, the pressure of geological time, the patient dissolution of rain. Once, I dreamed I was the entire city, feeling every footstep as a tiny percussion, every closing door as a shift in internal pressure, every voice as a vibration through my crystalline structure.

I am training no apprentice. The last child to show interest proved impatient with stone's timeline, drawn instead to the quicker satisfactions of gardening. This doesn't trouble me. The knowledge lives in more than memory—it lives in the shaped stones themselves, in the wear patterns of ancient tools, in the accumulated examples of previous masters' work. Someone will come, eventually, whose hands itch for the hammer, whose eyes see the finished form sleeping in the rough block. The city is patient. The craft will persist.

My greatest work remains unfinished—a replacement for the fountain basin's spiral, cut from a single block of the finest-grained limestone I've ever encountered. I've been carving it for three years, a few minutes each morning before the real work begins. The spiral tightens toward the center in a mathematical progression I derived from observing water's natural vortex. When complete, it will make the fountain's whisper into something closer to song, though so subtle that most will never consciously notice the difference.

I will die here, eventually, my body carried to the eastern caves where the limestone will slowly accept me into its mineral embrace. My tools will pass to hands that deserve them. The spiral drain will channel water for centuries after I'm gone. Perhaps someone will run their fingers along a surface I smoothed and understand, without words, the satisfaction I felt in its making.

This is enough. In a city that breathes in geological time, individual lives are simply moments of attention, brief intensifications of consciousness in stone's long dream. I dress the limestone, and in return, it has dressed me in patience, teaching me a contentment so deep it feels structural, load-bearing, essential to the world's architecture.

The hammer's weight is perfect in my hand. The chisel's edge gleams with careful sharpening. Below the window, morning mist rises from the valley like slow smoke. 

Time to begin again.
