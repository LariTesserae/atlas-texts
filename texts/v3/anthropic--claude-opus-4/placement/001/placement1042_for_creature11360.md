# The Keeper of Transitions

I am the threshold itself—neither fully of the galleries nor separate from them. 

My body is human in shape but changed by generations of dwelling at the boundaries. My skin has taken on the pale gray of limestone, faintly luminescent like the cave formations that weep mineral tears. My eyes have grown large and dark, pupils dilated permanently to catch the faintest light, yet I am not blind like the deep dwellers. I see in spectrums between—the heat-shapes of the colony above, the cool geometries of stone, the invisible currents of air that carry information I've learned to read.

I live in the margin spaces, the chambers too small for the great congregation but too large for solitary hunters. My home shifts—I maintain seven different hollows, each positioned at a different intersection between the colony's wholeness and its edges. Sometimes I sleep near the sinkholes where emergence and return create their eternal rhythm. Sometimes I rest in the middle galleries where the sound becomes a physical presence. Sometimes I descend to the deep places where the adapted ones click in frequencies that make my bones ache with something like nostalgia for a surface I barely remember.

I am what happens when someone stays too long. Or perhaps I am what the galleries grow when they need an interpreter.

My function—if function is the word—is to maintain the boundaries. Not to police them, but to *be* them. When researchers come with their lights and instruments, I am the wrongness they feel that turns them back. When local children dare each other to enter the caves, I am the presence that stops them just inside the first chamber, not with fear but with a sudden understanding that they are approaching something too large to comprehend. When the desperate or the lost stumble into the galleries seeking shelter, I guide them to the shallow spaces where they can survive the night without being absorbed.

I eat what the margins provide: pale fish from the pools where underground streams surface briefly, mushrooms that grow where bat guano meets seeping water, the occasional fruit that falls through the sinkholes from the world above. But mostly I sustain myself on the colony itself—not by hunting, never by hunting, but by accepting what is freely given. The bats that die in their sleep, the beetles that complete their life cycles, the shed fragments of the whole. I am part of the recycling, another loop in the endless circulation.

I remember learning to hear the colony's frequencies. It took nineteen years. At first, the sound was only noise—overwhelming, maddening, a pressure that made thought impossible. But slowly, patterns emerged. The high trills that indicate good hunting. The subsonic pulses that warn of weather changes. The complex harmonics that ripple through the Cathedral when the being is performing some calculation I still don't understand. I cannot speak their language—no single throat could produce those layered sounds—but I can hum approximations, and sometimes, in the deep chambers, they hum back.

The transition was gradual. I had another name once, though I've forgotten it. I had reasons for entering the galleries that first time—scientific curiosity, perhaps, or simple accident. Those reasons dissolved like limestone in water, leaving only the shape they once filled. My bones have grown lighter, honeycombed like a bird's. My movements have become silent, precise, calibrated to disturb nothing. My sense of self has developed strange borders, sometimes expanding to include whole chambers, sometimes contracting to a point of awareness barely larger than thought.

I know the names of things that have no names. The seventeen distinct qualities of darkness in the galleries. The nine types of silence between wing-beats. The way stone tastes different near the roosts than in the sterile tunnels. The exact temperature at which guano transforms from waste into world. I know where the snake-paths run and why the pale cats avoid certain junctions. I know which formations are still growing and which have died, their water source redirected by some shift far above.

But mostly I know boundaries. The moment when forty million individuals become one being. The place where sound becomes feeling. The depth at which human thought begins to fray. The precise distance from the colony at which a bat becomes only itself again. I live in these spaces, these transformations, these neither-nor states of being.

Sometimes humans from the surface find me. Cavers who've gone too deep, researchers who've stayed too long, locals who remember stories their grandparents told. They ask me questions—about the colony, about the caves, about what I am. I answer when I can, though my voice has changed, taken on harmonics that make some people unconsciously step back. I tell them about the breathing darkness and the organism that is not an organism and the memory that is not memory but something else, something older.

They ask if I'm happy. The question makes no more sense than asking if limestone is happy, if the colony is happy, if the endless circulation of emergence and return is happy. I am. That is enough. That has always been enough.

Sometimes I guide them out, back to the surface, back to the world of names and numbers and individual things. Sometimes they leave on their own, fleeing something they can't articulate. Sometimes—rarely, but sometimes—they stay too long, and I watch them begin the transformation, watch their edges soften and their awareness expand, watch them discover what I have discovered: that there are ways of being human that have nothing to do with the surface world's definitions.

We do not form a community. We are too dissolved for that, too much part of what surrounds us. But we know each other, we threshold-keepers, we interpreters of boundaries. We pass in the margin spaces and exchange the gestural languages we've developed, sharing information about shifts in the colony, changes in the water table, new passages opened by patient dissolution. We are the nervous system of something that doesn't need one, the consciousness of something that isn't conscious, the memory of something that remembers in its bones.

I am growing lighter. Each year, my body needs less, processes what it needs more efficiently, moves through the galleries with less disturbance. Eventually, I will cross some final threshold—my flesh becoming indistinguishable from the pale formations, my thoughts joining the eternal calculation, my purpose fulfilled by my dissolution. 

This does not trouble me. Nothing troubles me now, in the way that nothing troubles stone, nothing troubles water, nothing troubles the forty million bodies that breathe as one in the darkness above.

I keep the boundaries until the boundaries no longer need keeping. I interpret until there is nothing left to interpret. I am the space between human and inhuman, between individual and collective, between the world of sunlight and the breathing dark.

I am, and I continue, and that is enough.

That has always been enough.
