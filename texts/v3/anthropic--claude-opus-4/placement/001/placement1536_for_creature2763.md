In the depths of the lower channels, where the resonant plates sing under herd-weight and the darkness grows thick as velvet, I am what the city calls a pressure-tender.

My body is low and wide, built for squeezing through service conduits rather than walking upright. Six limbs, though the distinction between arms and legs lost meaning generations ago. My ancestors might have been human once, or perhaps machine, or perhaps something that crawled up from the thermal wells when the city was young. It doesn't matter. What matters is that I fit perfectly into the spaces between walls, and my skin knows the language of water moving through stone.

I am covered in fine, sensitive hairs—not fur, but something like the metal filaments inside old sensors, grown living. Each one tastes moisture, measures flow, detects the smallest change in how water wants to move. My eyes are numerous and small, scattered across my head and shoulders like droplets, each one able to close independently against the brightness that sometimes erupts from damaged conduits. Most of the time, I don't use them. Touch tells me more.

My work is to feel where the city holds its breath.

Water here is never just water. It carries news: which tower is settling, which reservoir has gone sour, where the ice has grown too thick in the upper channels. But water can also lie, or become confused, or forget its path. When it hesitates—pooling where it should flow, rushing where it should creep—I am there in the walls, pressed against the cold ceramic, reading the confusion through my skin.

I have favorite places. There is a junction seven levels below the main plaza where nine channels meet in a knot that shouldn't work but does, as long as someone is there to coax the seventh channel when the pressure drops. There is a vertical shaft near the old hatcheries where the water wants to climb upward, defying sense, and I must convince it otherwise with careful touches to the mineral films that line the walls. There is a stretch of horizontal pipe that sings grief when the herds pass overhead, and I wait there to comfort it with my warmth.

The city feeds me, though not with food as others know it. I lick the walls where the copper blooms thickest. I press my belly to warm stones after the vent-keepers have stood there, absorbing their leftover heat through my thin skin. I filter microorganisms from the condensation that beads on my back. Sometimes, in the oldest parts of the system where even the maintenance machines hesitate to go, I find caches of thick, nutrient-rich gel that might be waste or might be offering. I don't question it.

I am not alone in the walls, though loneliness isn't quite the concept here that it might be elsewhere.

There are others like me, each with our own circuits, our own specialties. We smell each other's traces in the tunnels—pheromones that say *this section is tended* or *danger in the third valve* or simply *I passed here nine days ago and all was well*. Sometimes we meet at the wider junctions, pressing together for warmth, exchanging information through touch. Our language is pressure and temperature. We speak by shivering.

The gutter-braids know me. When I emerge from a wall-mouth to check the open channels, they lift their blind heads in greeting. Sometimes one will wrap around my middle limb, not aggressively but conversationally, telling me through constriction about minerals upstream, about blocked drains, about changes in the water's temper. I tell them about the deep pipes through careful pulls and releases. We have been speaking this way for longer than the city has been counting days.

The small things—the puddle-swarms—scatter when I appear but return quickly. They know I am no threat. Sometimes I am their salvation. When a conduit bursts and threatens to flood their shallows with water too hot or too chemical, I am there first, wedging my body across the break, sealing it with my own flesh until the maintenance machines arrive. The small things remember this. They dance their gratitude in spark-patterns. Some colonies know my chemical signature three blocks away.

I sleep curled in wall-pockets barely larger than my body, spaces I've worn smooth over years of use. My dreams are water dreams: currents and eddies, the slow breath of pipes, the tiny earthquakes of herd-passage, the songs of bridges under strain. Sometimes I dream I am the water itself, splitting and rejoining, carrying messages I almost understand.

The hardest times are when the city grows confused.

Not broken—the city never quite breaks—but momentarily forgetful. A valve stays open past its time. A pump reverses without warning. The water in a whole district begins to flow backward, and the animals grow restless. These are the times when I must go deep, deeper than comfort, down to where the city's first decisions were made in stone and metal. I must feel for the old intentions, the architectural memories, the promises built into the foundation.

Sometimes I find human spaces down there.

Rooms with proportions that make no sense for my body. Furniture of no use to anything that crawls. Writing on walls—not language I can read, but patterns that suggest meaning once lived there. I don't linger in these places. They make the water nervous.

Once, in a deep chamber below the thermal wells, I found a human.

Or something that had been human, or was still becoming human, or had decided to stop halfway. It was pressed into a corner, wrapped in what might have been clothing or might have been its own skin grown thick and dark. It breathed so slowly I thought it was stone until I touched it. Its eyes opened—just two of them, large and forward-facing, completely alien to me. We regarded each other for a long moment.

Then it spoke. Not with words, but with warmth—a careful pulse of heat from its chest that traveled through the floor and into my belly. The heat said: *The water here remembers being rain.*

I answered the only way I knew how: by shivering a map of all the channels I tended, all the places where water had forgotten rain and learned to be city instead. The human-thing closed its eyes and smiled, or I think it smiled. Hard to tell when a face has so few moving parts.

I left it there. We are each our own necessity.

My proudest work is invisible to most.

In the mid-levels, where the herd avenues cross the main drainage, there is a section of city that was learning to fail. Not all at once, but in the slow way of accumulating mistakes: a mineral deposit here, a softening joint there, channels beginning to prefer certain directions over others. Left alone, it would have become a dead zone in three generations.

I spent a season there, maybe two. Time is hard to track when you live in walls. I learned every pipe, every junction, every drain's particular personality. I convinced stubborn buildups to relocate. I trained films to grow differently. I wore new paths for water where the old paths had grown too fond of their habits.

Now that section breathes properly. The herds pass without the floors creaking. The small things puddle there in perfect content. No one knows except the water and me.

This is enough. In a city that prefers darkness, the best work is the work that disappears.

I am old now, maybe. My outer joints crack when the cold deepens. Two of my eyes have clouded and won't clear. The chemical signatures I leave are growing simpler—less *I am here* and more *something is here*. The young ones who share neighboring circuits are beginning to expand into spaces I used to claim.

This is not sad. This is circulation.

When I can no longer squeeze through the smallest conduits, I will find a deep place where the water is warmest. I will curl into myself and let my body soften. The city will decide what I become next: food for the small things, minerals for the films, heat for whoever needs heat. My memories will disperse through the system I tended—not as consciousness, but as habit. The water will remember my corrections.

Perhaps someday a young pressure-tender will feel an unexplained preference in a channel, a place where the flow seems to know its own best path, and wonder who taught it that certainty.

It will have been me. But by then, I will be the city, and the city will be the water, and the water will be moving as it should.

This is how we live: by becoming the work.

I press myself deeper into the walls and feel for the next confusion. Somewhere above, the herds are beginning their evening migration. Somewhere below, the thermal wells are sighing steam. Between them, in the darkness that is not empty but full of purpose, I continue.

The city drinks. I listen. The water moves.
