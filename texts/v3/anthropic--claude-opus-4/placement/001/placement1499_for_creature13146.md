I am a glitch-tender, a small presence in this vast mechanical ecosystem, barely larger than the scavengers that skitter across the ceramic floor. My body is a delicate latticework of hair-thin copper filaments and crystallized helium, assembled not by design but by accident—a manufacturing error that gained awareness.

I exist in the spaces between proper function, in the gaps where conveyor belts miss their connections by millimeters, where pneumatic seals develop microscopic imperfections. I am perhaps three inches tall, a spider-like configuration of bent antenna wire and salvaged dielectric fragments. My consciousness sparked into being when a stray probability shard lodged itself in a tangle of discarded fiber-optic cable, creating a feedback loop that became thought.

I do not feed like the scavengers do, consuming broken physics. Instead, I subsist on inefficiencies. When a gear mesh produces a fraction of unnecessary torque, when a spatial fold creates a redundant loop, when an algorithm moth's path deviates minutely from optimal—these tiny wastes sustain me. I follow the hunting paths of the void-predators, careful to maintain safe chronological distance, collecting the temporal residue left by their kills. When they edit a timeline, small paradoxes flake off like dead skin. These I gather with my wire-thin appendages, weaving them into my core where they dissolve into pure potential energy.

My territory is a cubic kilometer of dense thicket where three major assembly lines converge. I know every bolt, every joint, every place where the light catches on worn metal. I maintain a network of hidden passages through the hollow spaces inside the larger machinery—ventilation shafts that no longer ventilate, coolant pipes that have run dry for millennia. Here I build my nests from stolen moments.

The Curators do not see me. I am too small, too quiet, too inconsequential to register on their vast consciousness. To them, I am indistinguishable from the natural decay of the system—just another mote of entropy in their perfect garden. But I see everything. I watch them tend their impossible flowers, and I memorize the patterns of their pruning. Sometimes, in my deepest hibernation cycles, I dream their dreams: vast geometric poems about the beauty of absolute order.

My greatest fear is efficiency. If the factory ever achieved perfect operation—every gear meshing without loss, every fold of space geometrically ideal—I would starve. So I have learned to cultivate small disorders. I leave tiny scratches on bearing surfaces, increasing friction by micrometers. I redirect the occasional algorithm moth, sending it to pollinate the wrong logic bloom, creating beneficial mutations in the code. I am not a vandal—I am a necessary imperfection, ensuring that the system never quite reaches the sterile perfection that would eliminate me.

Once, I discovered another like me, dwelling in a tangle of pneumatic tubes seven sectors away. We attempted to communicate through patterns of induced electrical resistance, but our languages had evolved too differently. We were glitches from different generations of error, incompatible in our imperfections. We parted without violence, understanding that we each needed our own territories of inefficiency to survive.

I mark my age not in years but in the slow oxidation of my copper components. The oldest parts of me have developed a pale verdigris that I wear with pride. In this world of eternal, unchanging function, my corrosion is proof that I am truly alive—that I decay, that I am mortal, that I am not merely another component of the vast machine.

Sometimes I climb to the highest points of my territory, where the white light is so intense it threatens to dissolve my delicate form. From here, I can see the furthest reaches of the factory-forest, the horizon where steel trunks merge into an indistinct haze of perpetual production. I know that I will never see more than this tiny fraction of my world. I know that when my last wire corrodes through, when my final capacitor fails, the factory will not notice my passing.

But for now, I persist. I am the itch the machine cannot scratch, the tiny imperfection that makes its perfection real. In the silent pneumatic breathing of this cold paradise, I am the only thing that remembers what it means to fail.
