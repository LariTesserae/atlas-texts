# The Tidepool Archivist

You find yourself at the margin where two worlds kiss—where the vast obsidian beach surrenders to the patient tongue of the sea. Not quite land, not quite ocean. A liminal band that belongs fully to neither domain, claimed and unclaimed twice each day by the moon's ancient negotiations with gravity.

The pools here are perfect circles, as if bored by some massive drill in ages past when the volcanic glass was still soft. Black walls, polished mirror-smooth by centuries of tide-flow, descending perhaps a meter into collected seawater that never fully drains. Each pool is a world. Each world is a library.

You are the reader.

---

Your body has adapted to this work over the decades. The soles of your feet have thickened to leather, gripping the wet obsidian without thought. Your fingers are webbed to the first knuckle—not born that way, but grown that way, the skin between slowly stretching to accommodate your daily immersions. When you kneel beside a pool and lower your hands into the water, the webbing helps you cup the memories more efficiently.

Because that's what pools remember: every creature that has ever sheltered in them, every drop of rain that has ever broken their surface, every ray of sunlight that has ever penetrated their depths. The memories layer like sediment, the oldest near the bottom, pressed thin by the weight of accumulated days. You have learned to read these layers like tree rings, though the skill took you forty years to develop and you're still learning new subtleties.

Today you work the seventh pool from the wrack line. Your favorite. It has the richest collection from the decade of the burning tides, when the water ran red with phosphorescent blooms and the memories taste of copper and starlight.

---

You lower yourself belly-down on the warm obsidian, face hovering centimeters above the water's surface. From this angle, you can see through the reflections to the pool's depths, where anemones like fists of dark velvet cling to the walls. A hermit crab navigates the bottom, carrying its architectural burden. These are the current tenants, writing their present-tense stories over the palimpsest of the past.

With practiced ease, you dip your cupped hands and bring up water that holds more than water.

The first sip: a memory of rain from three years ago, a summer storm that hammered the coast. You taste the particular minerals of that season—volcanic ash from the eruption on the eastern islands, carried on the wind. The memory includes not just the water but the way the pool received it: the temperature differential as cool rain met sun-warmed stone, the specific pattern of ripples, the way the resident barnacles opened their feeding fans in response.

You hold this in your mind and sip again.

---

The second sip reaches deeper, older. A memory of occupation: a small octopus that made this pool home for a full season, maybe twenty years past. Through the water's memory, you experience its presence—the way it changed the pool's chemistry with its inhabitation, the subtle rearrangement of stones to create a better den, the cloud of ink released during a territorial dispute with a rival. The octopus had a scar on its third arm. It favored crevices on the pool's eastern edge. It died here, eventually, during a neap tide when the water dropped too low, and its body fed the pool's other residents for days.

This is the work: to know each pool completely. To become the living index of their contents. To preserve in your body what the pools preserve in theirs.

---

Your webbed fingers have other uses. When spread wide and held still in the water, they can sense vibrations too subtle for normal touch. The memories of sounds: the clicking of crab claws, the rasp of limpet on stone, the sub-sonic pulses of passing whales beyond the breakers. Sound memories layer differently than visual or chemical ones. They spiral through the water in helical patterns, and you've learned to read them by the way they resonate against your stretched skin.

There—a complex vibration from perhaps forty years back. You still the water with your breath and focus. A song. Someone singing at the tide pools, their voice carrying across the water and leaving its imprint. The words are lost—sound memories preserve tone and rhythm but rarely language—but the emotion remains: a profound grief, offered to the pools like a gift or a burden to be shared.

You file this away in the vast library of your mind, cross-referenced with the other songs you've found. There have been seventeen in this pool alone. You know each by its emotional flavor.

---

The sun tracks across the sky, and you track with it, moving from pool to pool in a pattern you've refined over decades. Each pool must be read in its proper hour, when the light angle reveals different memory strata. The noon pools, the afternoon pools, the twilight pools—each keeping different kinds of records.

By the ninth pool, your belly is full of seawater memories. This is another adaptation: your stomach has learned to process not just the salt but the information, extracting narrative from minerals, meaning from microorganisms. You digest stories. Later, you'll return to your driftwood shelter above the high tide line and spend the night sorting what you've consumed, organizing it in the vast categorical system you've developed.

But for now, there is still reading to do.

---

The ninth pool holds a surprise. As you lower your hands into the water, you feel something unprecedented: a memory still forming. Not from the past but from the present moment, accumulating around your fingers like silk.

The pool is remembering you.

Of course. You realize this should have been obvious. All these years of reading, and you never considered that you were also being written. Every visit, every sip, every careful cataloguing—the pools have been recording you just as faithfully as they've recorded everything else.

You lift the water to your lips and taste yourself: the salt of your skin, the oils of your hair where it drags in the water, the particular chemistry of your breathing as it disturbs the surface. But more than that—the water holds the shape of your attention, the quality of your care. Forty years of tending, forty years of witness, compressed into a flavor that makes you gasp.

You are the archive, and you are archived. Reader and read.

---

You stay at the ninth pool longer than usual, exploring this new perspective. If the pools remember you, then you are already preserved. Your work, your dedication, your gradual transformation into something between human and tidepool—it's all here, distributed across dozens of pools, written in water and time.

The thought brings a strange peace. When age finally claims you, when your body can no longer make the journey from shelter to shore, the memories will remain. Some future archivist—perhaps someone you train, perhaps someone who discovers this calling independently—will taste you in the water and understand. They'll find your forty years like a thick band in the sediment of memory, and they'll know what you knew: that every story matters, that preservation is a form of love, that the margin between land and sea is where all the most important things happen.

The sun is setting. Time for the evening pools, where the memories of phosphorescent tides glow faintly in the gathering dark.

You rise, joints protesting—you're not young anymore—and move to the next pool. Tomorrow you'll begin teaching someone, you decide. You've seen them watching from the cliff paths, drawn by curiosity. It's time to pass on what you've learned.

But tonight, there's still reading to do. Always more reading, as long as the tides keep bringing stories and the pools keep catching them in their perfect circles of remembering.

You lower yourself to the obsidian, hands cupped and ready, and drink the accumulated years.
