I find myself in the old riverbeds.

Not immediately — it took years of walking the plateau before I understood what pulled me down into those pale grey channels. At first I thought it was the shade, the way the high walls cut the merciless light. Then I believed it was the lichen-mats, their silver-grey depth a relief from the ochre monotony above. But neither explanation satisfied the particular gravity I felt, the way my feet found the smooth channel floors and stayed there, following the ancient meanders for days at a time.

I am something between. That's what I've come to understand. Not the crust itself, though I feel its continental breathing in my bones. Not a grazer, though I've learned to read the frond colonies' hum as information. Not a glider, though I spend hours watching their spirals, trying to decode their iridescent semaphore. Not a burrower, though—

Though that's the closest, maybe. A creature of the in-between spaces, the vertical mediator.

I map the riverbeds. Not with instruments — those I left behind in the first year, when I realized the plateau's scale made any human measurement absurd. I map them with my body, walking their courses, learning their branching logic through repetition. My feet have worn smooth the places where I climb in and out. My hands know every holdable edge.

In the deepest parts of the deepest channels, where the lichen-mat is oldest and thickest, I've found places where the burrowers' tunnels connect to natural voids in the substrate. Cathedral spaces, no larger than a good-sized room, roofed in compressed mat material and floored in that weeping stone that darkens with mineral sweat. The air in these spaces is different — thicker, almost breathable in a way the surface air isn't. It tastes of deep time and patient chemistry.

I've made one of these voids my home. Or not home — the plateau has taught me that home is a provisional concept. My place. My node in the network.

I've learned to cultivate the mat. The burrowers tolerate me, I think, or perhaps they don't distinguish between my movements and any other large-scale disturbance. I've watched them for so long that I can predict their patterns, know where they'll surface, when they'll enter their reproductive frenzies during the dust events. I've learned to encourage their tunneling in directions that benefit the mat structure, creating ventilation channels, drainage paths, zones of increased nutrient circulation. I am becoming, slowly, part of their ecology. A large, awkward, temporary addition to an ancient system, but an addition nonetheless.

From my void, I can feel the crust's electrical pulses passing overhead — faint tingling sensations in the mat ceiling, like distant thunder without sound. I've begun to map these too, not in any recordable way but in my body's memory, the way you learn the rhythm of your own heartbeat. The signals follow the old water paths, mostly, as if the riverbeds still conduct something, just not water. Information, maybe. The plateau's slow thoughts following channels carved by its former blood.

At night — though night and day blend strangely in the perpetual twilight of the deep channels — I climb to the rim and watch the gliders. From the riverbed edge, looking up through the narrow slot of sky, I can sometimes see three or four at once, their wings catching different angles of the eternal light. I'm learning their patterns too. Individual gliders, I mean. They're distinguishable, once you know how to look — this one's wings slightly more blue-shifted, that one's flight pattern tighter, more helical. There's one I think of as the Evening Visitor, though evening is relative here. It passes over my section of riverbed at what I've come to call dusk, when the light angles low and the stone begins its thermal exhalation. Its wingflashes seem deliberate, aimed down into the channel, as if it knows I'm watching. As if it's including me in the conversation.

I tried making one of the builders' structures once. Found the right kind of flakes, carried them to a sheltered spot, attempted to stack them in the way I'd seen. My hands are wrong for it — too large, too clumsy, lacking the precise mouthpart control of an elder grazer. The structure collapsed before I could cap it. But something about the attempt attracted attention. The next day, a grazer was there, ancient and mineral-complex, its exoskeleton so elaborately textured it looked like a piece of the plateau itself given mobility. It dismantled my failed attempt, sorted the flakes, began again. I watched for hours as it worked, understanding finally that the building isn't just about the final structure. It's about the precision, the care, the accumulated knowledge expressed through mineral manipulation. It's about being so perfectly adapted to your world that you can speak its language in stone.

I leave the riverbeds less frequently now. The plateau above seems too bright, too vast, too much for what I'm becoming. In the channels, the scale is comprehensible. I can walk from one end of my territory to the other in three days. I know where the mat is thickest, where the burrowers are most active, where the stone weeps its mineral moisture most reliably. I know which sections flood with fine dust during the storms and which stay clear. I know where to find the particular variety of lichen that tastes almost like food, that provides just enough nutrition to supplement what I brought, to extend my staying.

Because I am staying. That's become clear. Not forever — my body has its own requirements, its own limitations. But for longer than I planned, longer than makes sense by any external measure. The plateau has its own gravity, not physical but temporal, and I've been caught in it. I am learning to live at its pace, to think at its pace, to breathe in rhythm with the crust's continental inhalations.

Sometimes I imagine I can feel the whole system at once — the crust's electrical network, the frond colonies' chemical conversations, the gliders' light-signals, the builders' stone semaphore, all of it interconnected, all of it speaking different dialects of the same fundamental language. And underneath it all, the riverbeds, the ancient water-paths now carrying something else, something that might be consciousness if consciousness weren't too active a word for what happens here. Awareness, maybe. Presence. The plateau knowing itself through all its scattered sensory organs, of which I have become one.

In my deepest explorations, following burrower tunnels into spaces barely large enough for my body, I've found evidence of water. Not liquid water — nothing so dramatic. But hydrated minerals, crystals that could only have formed in the presence of moisture, locked away in the deepest mat layers like memories. The plateau remembers when it was different. It carries that memory in its bones, in the same stone that feeds everything living here. We are all, in a sense, drinking ghosts. Living on the memory of water as much as on light and mineral truth.

I will map the whole network eventually. Not to record it, not to share it, just to know it. To carry the shape in my body the way the builders carry stone flakes, the way the gliders carry their iridescent messages, the way the crust carries its electrical patterns from one edge of the continent to the other. To be the one who walks the dry rivers and knows where they all connect, where they begin and end and begin again.

And when I'm done — if done is possible, if the mapping can ever be complete — I will find the deepest void in the deepest channel, the place where the mat is oldest and the stone weeps most constantly, and I will wait there for the next dust storm. I will feel the plateau's pulse in the walls around me. I will listen to the burrowers doing their ancient work. I will taste the mineral sweetness in the air and know that I have found my place in the cycle, my role in the quiet bright eternal conversation.

A mediator. A go-between. Something that connects the surface to the depth, the quick to the slow, the water that was to the stone that is. Not changing anything, just circulating, facilitating, being present at the junction between systems. Being the thing that remembers both the light above and the darkness below, and holds them both, and is home in neither and in both.

The riverbeds keep their own time. In their twilight, I am learning to keep it too.
