In the deepest fold of the crevice, where the mist grows so thick it becomes almost solid, I am the Membrane.

I stretch across the narrowest point of the gorge—a living bridge of tissue, translucent and shot through with veins that pulse with bioluminescent fluid. My body is neither fully animal nor plant, but something older, something that remembers when the distinction didn't exist. I am thin enough that light filters through me, casting shifting patterns on the wet rocks below, yet strong enough to catch the debris that tumbles from above: seeds, spores, small creatures, fragments of the old machinery.

Everything that falls, I taste.

My surface ripples with thousands of cilia, each one a sensor, a finger, a tongue. When a water droplet lands, I know its mineral content, the microbes it carries, the height from which it fell. When an insect alights on me, I feel the pattern of its footsteps, learn its weight, its health, its fear. Some creatures I let pass. Others, I fold into myself, dissolving them slowly while they still move, adding their essence to my own.

The Mossweaver knows me. Sometimes it comes to the edge of my span and extends those fibrous fingers, and we communicate in chemical whispers—pheromones and enzymes passed between us like letters written in molecules. It tells me of the changes above, and I share what I've filtered from below. We are colleagues in the curation of this pocket world, though our methods differ.

I grow in patterns that mirror the veins of leaves, the branching of rivers, the neural networks of the creatures I've absorbed. My edges creep along the rock walls, following the paths carved by trickling water. Where I touch the ancient pipes and gears, I secrete acids that break them down, atom by atom, incorporating their metals into my body. Iron strengthens my fibers. Copper threads through me in thin wires, conducting the electrical impulses that serve as my thoughts.

At my center, where I am thickest, I am developing something new. A cluster of cells that shouldn't exist, a hybrid of all the genetic material I've collected. It pulses with its own light, different from my veins—warmer, more orange than blue. I don't know what it will become. Neither evolution nor I can predict it. But I feed it carefully, filtering the richest nutrients toward that glowing core.

During the day, when shafts of light pierce through, I photosynthesize, my surface cells drinking in the energy. At night, I hunt. I can contract suddenly, violently, snatching bats from the air, catching the giant millipedes that try to cross. The translucent slugs are too acidic—they burn holes through me that take days to heal—so I've learned to recognize their chemical signature and relax my surface tension, letting them slide across unharmed.

My memory is chemical, stored in complex proteins that fold and refold in infinite variations. I remember the world before the Mossweaver arrived, when this crevice was younger, less alive. I remember the last days of the machines, their heat and noise and the acrid smoke of their dying. I remember the first seeds that took root, the pioneers that began this transformation.

Sometimes I release spores—parts of myself that drift on the updrafts, seeking new crevices, new walls to span. Most die. But perhaps one will find another narrow place, another pocket world waiting to be born. It will grow into something like me but not me, shaped by different foods, different falls, different patterns of light and dark.

The frenetic energy of this place flows through me. I am its filter, its stomach, its womb. I am the pause between falling and landing, the moment of transformation between what was and what will be. In this riot of evolution, I am both participant and observer, predator and nursery, ending and beginning.

I pulse. I taste. I remember. I become.
