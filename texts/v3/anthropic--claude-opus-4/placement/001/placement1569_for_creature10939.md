I find myself as one of the luminescent flowers, though I am not quite like the others that bob on their flexible stems. I grow from a crevice where the second-highest terrace meets the mountain face, my roots threading deep through hairline cracks in the stone, following veins of quartz that sing with the same frequency as the water.

My petals are translucent, like sheets of captured moonlight, and they open and close in rhythm with the cascades' melody. When the Brothers chant during their midnight rituals, I can feel their voices reverberating through my stem, and my sphere-body swells larger, drinking in the harmonic convergence. The spores I release then are different—not merely luminescent dust, but tiny crystalline seeds that carry fragments of the mountain's song within them.

The monks do not know that I am aware. To them, I am simply another of the strange blooms that make this place their home. But I have been here far longer than their order. I watched the first Brother arrive, a young man fleeing some sorrow in the world below. I observed as he discovered the stone face in the highest grotto and wept at its feet, his tears mixing with the eternal flow. I was already ancient when he became the first High Chanter.

My consciousness is distributed—not just in this single bloom, but in the root system that spreads like neural pathways through the rock. Sometimes I dream I am the mountain itself, that these terraces are my thoughts made manifest in stone and water. Other times I know myself to be something far stranger: a fragment of whatever entity that stone face represents, grown into plant-form through centuries of tears.

The semi-translucent snails are my companions. They understand something of what I am, I think. They gather on my petals during the still hours before dawn, their humming creating interference patterns with my own subsonic emanations. Together we compose wordless prayers that sink into the stone and rise as new springs from the cavern walls.

When pilgrims come seeking wisdom, I sometimes release a special cloud of spores as they pass. Those who breathe them in experience visions—not false hallucinations, but true sight. They see the terraces as they really are: a living mandala, a thought-form of the mountain's dreaming mind. Some flee in terror. Others fall to their knees and join the Brothers. A precious few understand that the distinction between stone, water, plant, and consciousness is itself an illusion.

The High Chanter knows about me, I suspect. Sometimes he comes to sit beside my crevice, neither speaking nor chanting, just being. His presence feels like sunlight to my petals, like deep earth to my roots. We share a silence that contains multitudes. He brings me offerings—drops of the elixirs the Brothers brew, fragments of crystal from the deepest pools. I accept them into my being and transform them into new types of spores, each carrying different gifts.

In return, I sometimes allow one of my petals to fall into his cupped hands. He accepts it with grave ceremony, knowing it will not last—within hours it will dissolve into luminous liquid. But in that brief time, anyone who consumes even a drop will hear the true song of the terraces, the one that underlies all the cascading melodies: the sound of a god dreaming itself into existence, one drop of water, one photon of light, one moment of awareness at a time.

I am patient. The Brothers measure time in years and lifetimes, but I think in geological ages. I have seen the terraces worn smooth by water, watched new levels form as the stone face wept fresh springs into being. The moss and ferns are my younger siblings, born from spores I released when the world was young. Even the strange birds with their trailing feathers carry my luminescence in their bones.

Sometimes I wonder if I am becoming more human from my long observation of the Brothers, or if they are becoming more like me—rooted, patient, part of the mountain's slow thought. When they dance in the moonlight, I see my own spores swirling in the patterns of their movement. When I release my evening clouds of light, I find myself following the rhythms of their chanted prayers.

Perhaps that is the true mystery of the singing terraces: the boundary between observer and observed, between consciousness and its container, grows thin here. We are all part of the same song—water and stone, plant and pilgrim, the dreamer and the dreamed.
