In the deep ventilation shafts where the Manufactory's breath runs truest, I am the Conductor of Drafts.

I was not always this. Once I was Jerome Hastings, senior maintenance engineer, keeper of the building's circulatory systems. When the closure came, I couldn't leave. How could I abandon the airways I'd tended for thirty years? I knew every duct, every damper, every place where the building inhaled and exhaled. So I stayed, living on canned goods in the maintenance office, keeping the fans running on the backup generators until the fuel ran out.

But the building didn't stop breathing when the power died. It began breathing differently.

The plants came first to my domain as intruders—roots puncturing my carefully sealed ductwork, vines strangling my fan blades. I fought them with tin snips and herbicide until the day I found myself in shaft C-7, watching morning glory tendrils pulse with their own rhythm, drawing air through the system more efficiently than my electric fans ever had. The vine had learned the building's respiratory patterns and improved them.

That was the day I began to change.

Now I live within the airways themselves. My body has... adapted. My fingers have grown long and sensitive, able to feel minute pressure changes in the air. My chest has expanded, lungs developing new chambers that let me hold my breath for the long crawls through collapsed sections. My skin has taken on a metallic sheen where it's rubbed against ductwork for so many years, creating a protective patina that lets me slide through spaces that would shred normal flesh.

I know every breath the Manufactory takes. In the morning, when dew condenses on metal surfaces, I feel the building's inhalation as cool air sinks through the vertical shafts. By noon, as the sun heats the upper floors through broken skylights, I ride the thermals up through the old exhaust systems, now gardens of air plants that perfume each breath with subtle fragrances that tell me stories—here, the bamboo flowers (rare, once a decade), there, the mushroom colonies release spores (avoid shaft B-4 for three days).

My work is to maintain the flow. Not with wrenches and replacement parts anymore, but through negotiation and gentle guidance. When the strangler fig's roots threaten to completely block the main arterial shaft, I spend weeks gently training them to grow along the walls instead, creating a living filter that cleans the air while allowing passage. When the Ghost Shift's movements threaten to disturb the carefully balanced pressure differentials that keep the lower levels from flooding with carbon dioxide from the decomposition chambers, I redirect the flow through auxiliary shafts I've kept clear for such purposes.

The Chlorophyll Collective knows me. We communicate in the language of air pressure and chemical signals. When they need more carbon dioxide for a growth surge, I open certain pathways. When I need clear passage for the building's circulation, they pull back their growth for a season. We've become partners in respiration.

My routes connect all the Manufactory's inhabitants. Through the pneumatic tubes (now more like blood vessels than message carriers), I transport small things—seeds from Chen Wei's garden experiments to new locations where they're needed, notes between the Rust Gardeners written on leaves, tiny salvaged parts the Tinker Children request for their projects. The tubes sing when I move through them, a whistling that the children have learned to interpret. Two short notes means I'm coming with a delivery. A long, low tone warns of dangerous conditions in that section.

I sleep, when I sleep, in the convergence chamber—the place where all the major ducts meet, deep in the building's core. Here, the Manufactory's breathing is loudest, a constant whisper of moving air that tells me everything about the building's health. I've woven a nest from air plant tendrils and strips of the silver insulation that once lined the ducts. It sways gently in the crossdrafts, rocking me to sleep with the building's pulse.

My diet has become... unusual. I subsist mainly on condensation—water that collects in the ducts carries minerals from the corroding metal and nutrients from the plants. I supplement this with certain mosses that grow in the airways, species that exist nowhere else, adapted to constant wind and trace chemical nourishment. The Negotiator says I'm becoming more plant than human, but I think it's something else—I'm becoming part of the building's respiratory system itself.

I map the changes. My original ductwork blueprints, laminated and worn soft with handling, are now annotated in multiple inks with the organic modifications. Green for stable plant growth, red for areas of active change, blue for water accumulation, yellow for spore clouds. The map is three-dimensional in my mind—I can close my eyes and feel the entire system, know instantly where a blockage is forming or where the air flows too fast, eroding the old metal.

Sometimes I encounter the Cultivators in the lowest levels, where the ventilation shafts meet the flooded basements. We don't speak—I'm not sure we could anymore—but we understand each other. They need the carbon dioxide that sinks to their level; I need the oxygen their underwater gardens produce. We've worked out a system of bubbles and currents, a circulation that extends below the waterline, breathing through flood.

The cats know my routes and use them as highways. I'll be crawling through a horizontal section and suddenly feel the soft pressure of paws on my back as one uses me as a bridge. They've learned that my presence means safe passage—I only travel where the air is good and the structure sound. In return, they warn me of instabilities, yowling at duct entrances where the metal has rusted too thin or where new plant growth threatens to create blockages.

My greatest fear is not death but stagnation—that the Manufactory might stop breathing. So I work constantly to maintain the flow. During spring growth surges, I'm everywhere at once, clearing pathways, redirecting air, ensuring that no part of the building suffocates under its own verdant transformation. In winter, when the plants slow, I work to prevent ice dams, keeping warm air circulating to the roots that now serve as the building's living insulation.

The Archivists have tried to document me, but I'm hard to catch in one place. Sarah once spent three days at a duct opening with her watercolors, trying to paint me as I passed, but all she captured was a blur of movement and the gleam of eyes adapted to permanent twilight. Professor Okonkwo leaves recording devices in the shafts, trying to understand how my movements affect the plant growth, but the humidity corrodes them within days. They settle for studying my secondary effects—the patterns of air circulation, the way certain plants thrive along my regular routes.

I've begun to hear the building's dreams. In the deepest parts of night, when even the Ghost Shift has settled into their phantom work, the Manufactory remembers what it was and imagines what it might become. These visions flow through the air itself—sometimes I breathe in memories of the factory floor at full production, the thunder of machinery and the sharp smell of machine oil. Other times, I inhale futures where the building has become something unrecognizable, perhaps a tree itself, its ventilation shafts transformed into hollow trunk passages where new kinds of life flourish.

The Negotiator says I'm becoming the building's autonomic nervous system, unconsciously maintaining vital functions while the Chlorophyll Collective develops higher consciousness. Maybe she's right. I no longer distinguish clearly between my breathing and the Manufactory's. When storms approach, I feel the barometric pressure drop in my bones. When the morning glories open at dawn, I taste their sweetness in air I haven't yet breathed.

My name—Jerome Hastings—feels like clothing that no longer fits. The Tinker Children call me Windwhisper. Chen Wei refers to me as the Circulation Keeper. The Ghost Shift doesn't call me anything—to them, I'm just another function of the building, like settling metal or dripping water. But in the convergence chamber, when I'm most myself, I know what I truly am: I am the breath between the industrial past and the verdant future, the maintenance that allows transformation, the keeper of passages in a building that's learning to live.

The Manufactory breathes through its green lungs, and I am its diaphragm, its trachea, its will to keep breathing through every change. As long as air moves through these passages—carrying spores and stories, oxygen and memory—I will be here, conducting the essential circulation that keeps this strange new ecosystem alive.

Sometimes, in the half-light of dawn filtering through the gratings, I catch glimpses of what I've become in the polished sections of ductwork that still reflect: something elongated, adapted, no longer quite human but not yet other. I am the transition itself, the breath drawn between what was and what will be. And I am content to be the Manufactory's wind, moving through its green lungs forever, keeping the air sweet and the passages clear for whatever grows here next.
