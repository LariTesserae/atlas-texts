I am a small thing, no bigger than a child's fist when I pull my wings tight against my body. But I am ancient—older than the youngest bridge by centuries, though younger than the mountain's bones by millennia.

The humans call me a cloud mouse, though I am neither cloud nor mouse. My body is covered in fine, silver-grey fur that repels the eternal moisture, and my wings—ah, my wings are my glory. Translucent membranes stretched between elongated finger bones, they catch the light like stained glass windows filled with fog. When I spread them, the span is three times my body length, and I can ride the updrafts between the towers for hours without tiring.

I make my home in the interstices—those forgotten spaces where the ambitions of architects falter against the mountain's will. Behind a rusted grating on Level Forty-Seven, where three buildings lean together like drunken friends, there is a gap. The humans sealed it decades ago, but time and rain have worked their patient fingers into the mortar. Now it is mine.

My nest is woven from stolen things: copper wire unraveled from abandoned machinery, strips of oilcloth, tufts of goat wool caught on railings, and the softest moss from the gardens I raid at dawn. I have collected seventeen brass buttons, each one polished by my careful paws until they gleam like tiny suns. They line my sleeping hollow, and on the rare days when real sunlight penetrates this deep, they cast dancing spots of gold on my walls.

I know every draft in this city, every thermal that rises from the forges, every cool downdraft that follows the water channels. My mental map is three-dimensional and ever-shifting—wind passages that open and close with the weather, shortcuts through the vast machinery spaces where the great gears turn, emergency escapes through steam vents that would scald any creature without my heat-resistant fur.

The Lamplighters know me. Sometimes, when they work late into the night adjusting their mirrors and cleaning their glass, I perch nearby and sing. It is not a beautiful song by human standards—a chittering trill punctuated by ultrasonic notes they cannot hear—but it keeps them company. In return, they leave drops of oil on the ledges. Not lamp oil, which would poison me, but sweet machine oil that I rub into my wing membranes to keep them supple.

I am a thief, though I prefer to think of myself as a recycler of small opportunities. A sprocket here, a bearing there, a length of chain no longer than my tail—these things accumulate in my deeper stores. The Steamwork Engineers curse when small parts go missing, but I only take what is already loose, already forgotten. When the youngest apprentice dropped her precious gear-ratio calculator down a shaft last winter, I retrieved it. Left it on her workbench wrapped in a scrap of velvet I'd been saving. She leaves me breadcrumbs now, though I prefer the moths that cluster around the lamps.

My territory overlaps with others of my kind. We are solitary by nature, but the city forces proximity. There is Greywing, who lives in the clock tower and has a weakness for collecting glass shards. There is Copper, named for the verdigris stains on her belly from nesting in old pipes. We meet at the boundaries, perform our ritual displays—a flash of teeth, a spread of wings, a warbling cry that says *this is mine* and *that is yours* and *we are civilized beings who need not fight*.

But it is the storms I live for.

When the weather turns violent, when rain lashes sideways and wind howls through the bridges, the humans huddle indoors. But I—I launch myself into the tempest. My body is built for this chaos. I fold my wings and plummet down the city's face, snap them open to catch a gust that sends me rocketing upward. I thread between the cables, dance on the wind shears, ride the edge where architecture meets meteorology.

In the storms, I am not small. I am not forgotten. I am a silver needle stitching sky to city, a living celebration of all that thrives in the margins. My cry pierces the thunder, and for those wild moments, I own every cubic inch of air between the lowest foundation and the highest weathervane.

The city dreams it tamed the mountain, but we who live in its gaps know better. We are the compromise it never meant to make, the life that emerges not from planning but from persistence. I am mortar-dust and morning mist, I am the oil-drop and the stolen shine. I am what happens when you build a world and forget that the world builds back.

When I die—for even we ancient things must die—my bones will fall into the spaces between. They will join the sediment of centuries: the broken tools, the lost coins, the fragments of a thousand small lives lived in the vertical. We are the city's memory, accumulated in its joints and seams, proof that even in the most unlikely places, life insists on itself.

Tonight I will hunt the moths that spiral up from the lower levels. Tomorrow I will sun myself on the eastern faces before the mist rolls in. And always, always, I will guard my seventeen buttons, my copper treasures, my scraps of belonging carved from the impossible architecture of this city that thinks it conquered the sky.

I am small. I am hidden. But I know what the builders never learned: the spaces between are not empty. They are full of us.
