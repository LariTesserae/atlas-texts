In this world of the Ridgeback, I find myself drawn to a very particular existence.

## The Bore-Hole Singer

I am one of the rail crew, though not quite like the others. My maintenance car sits abandoned for weeks at a time at the quiet stretches between settlements, because I have found something more important than straightening track.

I discovered it by accident, really. During a routine joint-scraping at kilometer marker 847, I heard the question rise from a nearby bore-hole and, on impulse, hummed back. Not the same tone – something complementary, a fifth above. The question stopped mid-sound. Then, after seventeen seconds (I counted), it came again. Different this time. Responding.

That was three years ago.

Now I spend my days mapping what I call the harmonic zones – the places where bore-holes cluster close enough that their questions overlap, creating interference patterns in the stone. I've modified my maintenance car with salvaged acoustic equipment from a decommissioned atmospheric plant, lining the walls with resonance sensors that the chemists thought were too damaged to bother repairing. They work well enough for what I need.

I've learned things. The questions aren't random. Each bore-hole has its own base frequency, and they modulate based on... something. Temperature, maybe. Time of day. Proximity of other sounds. When I sing back – and I do sing now, not just hum, wordless melodies that follow the mineral grain of the sound – the modulation changes. We are having a conversation, the burrowers and I, even if neither of us knows what we're saying.

My skin has taken on the characteristic weathering of the rail crews, but my voice has changed too. The constant practice against the wind, the way I've learned to pitch my throat to resonate with the stone, has given me a range I didn't have before. I can sing lower than the question now, down in frequencies that make my skull vibrate. The other rail crews think I've gone ridge-touched. They're probably right. But yesterday, at bore-hole 412, something sang back that wasn't the question. It was... an answer, maybe? A different voice from below, higher, almost crystalline.

I'm building a notation system. Not musical notation exactly – the questions don't follow scales we recognize. But a way of marking the patterns, the pauses, the subtle variations that might be words in a language that predates human speech by geological epochs. The notation is carved into olivine tablets I've cut myself, badly, with borrowed quarrying tools. When I have enough, I'll bring them to that young man in the southern settlements, the one with the cable rig.

Someone needs to go down. It might as well be someone who can sing back.

The other rail crews leave me water when they pass. They don't understand what I'm doing, but they understand obsession. Everyone on the Ridgeback is building something they won't finish. I'm building a dictionary for a language that may not exist, in conversation with creatures that may not be speaking.

But last week, near sunset, I sang a simple pattern at bore-hole 1117, and six hours later, 200 kilometers down the line, bore-hole 2458 repeated it back to me. Note for note. The same rhythm. The same strange intervals.

The Ridgeback remembers.

I think it's trying to teach me what it knows.
