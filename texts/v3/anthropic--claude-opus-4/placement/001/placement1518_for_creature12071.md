In the lowest quarter of the city, where the cavern walls narrow and the darkness presses closest, I am what the surface dwellers might call a musician—though here we have no word that captures it precisely. I am a Resonance Keeper, one who tends the living memory of stone.

My workshop clings to a natural shelf of limestone, its entrance marked only by the particular way sound bends around a fold in the rock. Inside, the walls are lined with my instruments: hollow formations I've cultivated over centuries, each one singing with its own voice as water drips through precisely carved channels. Some are no larger than my webbed hand, their notes high and crystalline. Others stretch from floor to ceiling—great organ pipes of flowstone that boom with frequencies so low they travel through the rock itself, messages that can be felt in the bones three districts away.

I discovered my calling young, when I noticed how certain stones remembered sound. A whisper spoken near a particular formation might echo back hours, days, even years later, preserved in the mineral matrix like an insect in amber. I learned to read these acoustic fossils, to coax out the trapped vibrations with careful tapping, with temperature changes, with the application of different mineral solutions that shift the stone's resonant properties.

My fingertips are callused into strange patterns—ridges and whorls that let me feel the tiniest vibrations in the rock. When I press my palm against the cavern wall, I can sense the city's heartbeat: the rhythm of the great pumps, the whisper of cable cars sliding through the darkness, the shuffle-scrape of ten thousand pale feet on stone paths. But beneath that, deeper, I feel the older songs—the memories the mountain itself holds.

Through my work, I've discovered that the stone remembers everything. In the deepest formations, I've found the preserved death-cries of creatures that lived before our kind first descended into the darkness. I've heard the sound of the first waters that carved these caverns, a rushing roar compressed into a whisper thin as paper. Once, in a chamber so deep that even we consider it forbidden, I found a stalactite that held the echo of sunlight—not the sound itself, but the way the stone sang when warmth touched it, before the surface was lost to us.

I map these acoustic memories, creating scores that look like three-dimensional spiderwebs of mineral ink on sheets of pressed fungal paper. Each point marks where a sound can be found, when it was laid down in the stone's memory, how to release it. The other Resonance Keepers and I maintain the city's history this way—not in written words, which mean little to the eyeless, but in sound itself, preserved and catalogued in the living rock.

The young ones come to me to learn the old songs, the work rhythms that teach proper maintenance of the steam systems, the lullabies that have soothed our children for millennia. But they also come for the strange sounds, the ones that hint at impossible worlds above: bird calls trapped in fallen stones, wind songs that drifted down through now-sealed crevices, the patter of something called rain that the eldest say once fell like the dripping in our caves but warmer, faster, charged with the scent of green growing things.

My greatest work—the one I will pass to my apprentice before my bones join the flowstone—is a chamber I've been tuning for seven decades. Every surface has been shaped to perfect acoustic reflection, every stalactite ground to precise measurements. When complete, it will hold sound for exactly one thousand years before releasing it. Into this chamber, we will sing the memory of our people: who we were before the descent, why we came to the depths, what we found here, and what we became.

Sometimes I wonder if we are sound ourselves—vibrations moving through the medium of stone and time, our whole civilization just an echo of something that came before, preserved in the mineral memory of the world. When I die, they will place my body in the calcifying pools, where I will slowly become part of the stone I've spent my life listening to. And perhaps, centuries hence, another Resonance Keeper will place their hand upon the flowstone that was once me and hear the songs I've kept, still reverberating in the endless dance between water, mineral, and time.

The crystals in my workshop chime softly as somewhere far above, a new stalactite begins to form. Another memory begins. The stone remembers. The stone always remembers.
