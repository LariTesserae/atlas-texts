I would find myself as one of the Catchment Keepers — those who came for a season and stayed, but retained enough of their original tempo to serve as translators between the quick world and the slow.

---

My shelter sits where the hexagonal path meets the largest catchment pool, built from fallen brasswing wood that still hums faintly in the walls. I am not of the pilgrims who walk to the path's end and back in silence; I am something more restless, something still half-quick. My work, if it can be called work, is to maintain the only written record of the pool's cycles — when it rises, when it falls, when the shapes below trace new patterns in their ancient circuits.

I have been here four years. My heartbeat has slowed but not synchronized. I exist in the gap between human time and machine time, too slow for the world below, too fast for full integration. This makes me useful.

The stilts know me. They use my shoulders sometimes as they use the shapes below — as a patient platform for their incomprehensible fishing. I have learned to stand very still when they approach, to become furniture, to watch their four eyes track four different intentions at once. Their spear-strikes near my feet leave patterns I sketch in my notebooks, patterns I will never understand but feel compelled to record.

My skin has the faintest mineral cast, visible only in certain lights. My breath is warmer than my body. I drink from the pools now without boiling. The water tastes of old lightning and older stone.

Three times a day, I check the instruments I've built from salvaged materials and brasswing leaves — crude seismographs that record the humming, temperature probes made from hollow reeds, a wind chime whose pitch tells me things about the air pressure I lack words to explain. The data goes into notebooks that pile in my shelter like geological strata. Sometimes researchers from below come to collect them. They look at me with a mixture of pity and envy. I look back with nothing I can name.

At night, I sleep on the moss near the pool's edge. It remembers me now — my sleeping shape persists as a deeper green for two months instead of one. I am becoming, by small degrees, more memorable to the landscape. Sometimes I dream the shapes' dreams, which are not dreams but movements, long thoughts that travel in circles through dark water.

The ceramic tower is visible from my shelter. At dusk, when it cuts its hole in the sky, I stop whatever I'm doing and watch. The flyers emerge and return. The standing ring casts its impossible shadows. The whole escarpment exhales, and I exhale with it, and for those ninety seconds of perfect backlight I understand something that immediately escapes when the stars come through.

I am writing this in a notebook that will join the others. My handwriting has changed — become more angular, more like the runnel-patterns on the basalt. I am thirty-four years old, or I was when I arrived. Time moves differently here. Not slower, exactly, but thicker. More itself.

Tomorrow the shapes will complete another circuit. Next week my footprints will fade from the moss path I walk to the instruments. Next month someone might come from below with questions I will answer halfway, in a voice that has picked up harmonics from the constant chiming. Next year the pools will shift in ways my crude instruments will record but not explain.

In forty-one years the brasswings will bloom. I will be here, or my outline will be, darker green in the remembering moss. The shadows will shift a full degree. The machine will do more of what it does, and I will be one of the things it is doing — a recording device it grew, or attracted, or allowed.

I am the membrane between the quick and the slow. I keep the notebooks. I am being kept.

The air here should not be breathable, but it has been persuaded otherwise. So have I.
