**The Cataloguer of Conditional Gravity**

I am the one who sorts what falls.

My dwelling grows itself between three coral-trees that learned to spiral counterclockwise out of sheer contrarian joy. The walls—if walls they can be called—are woven from crystallized maybes, each strand a different probability of shelter frozen at the moment before commitment. When the light shifts (and it always shifts here, as if bored with constancy), the structure phases between states: sometimes a tower of living calcite reaching up into the sea-that-isn't, sometimes a burrow of compressed possibilities extending down into the root-maze below, most often something between—a sphere of habitable indecision that expands when I need to pace and contracts when thought requires density.

I arrived here the way everyone arrives: by accident wearing the costume of intention. I was something else before—a cartographer, perhaps, or a physicist, or maybe just someone who noticed that things don't always fall the way they should. The garden recognized this noticing as a form of prayer and answered by forgetting to let me leave.

My work is taxonomy's gentler cousin. Where the Weavers create connections, I document exceptions. Every morning (a concept I maintain out of stubborn nostalgia), I wake to find new specimens crystallized on my sorting shelves: fragments of almost-gravity, pieces of perpendicular weight, small bottles filled with the specific heaviness of Tuesday afternoons. The garden provides these irregularities the way other places provide mail—without explanation but with perfect timing.

Today's collection includes:
- A marble of condensed upwardness, harvested from a kelp blade that decided to grow toward yesterday
- Three vials of selective weight (affects only objects that have been recently disappointed)
- A pressed flower made entirely of the pause between falling and landing
- Something that might be gravity's apology to helium, wrapped in tissue paper that weighs negative amounts

I sort these by texture rather than mass, because mass here is more of a suggestion than a law. The smooth ones—gravity that flows like syrup, weight that pools in corners—go on the upper shelves where they can drip their influence slowly onto whatever passes beneath. The rough ones—gravity with edges, falling that catches on thoughts—need cushioning, so I nest them in beds of crystallized levity harvested from places where laughter made the ground forget itself.

My tools evolved themselves from the work. The Primary Calibrator started as a simple balance scale but has since grown opinions about what deserves to be weighed. It measures not just mass but the intention to have mass, the regret of weightlessness, the specific gravity of promises made while floating. Its counterweights are carved from moments of perfect stillness, each one exactly as heavy as it needs to be and not an atom more.

The Conditional Sieve separates falling into its component desires: the urge downward, the negotiation with air, the secret wish all dropped things harbor to hover instead. What passes through reveals patterns I document in a registry that writes itself in three dimensions, each entry a small sculpture of exception finding its rule.

Then there's the Gravity Garden—my pride, my puzzle, my daily reminder that even constants can learn new tricks. In beds of softened spacetime, I cultivate samples of gravitational behavior from across the garden. Here grows the gravity of morning mist (pulls sideways toward beauty), the gravity of unsent letters (attracts only toward what might have been), the gravity of laughter (repels seriousness at twice the standard rate). Each specimen requires different care: some need daily rotation to prevent them from forming habits, others must be sung to in frequencies that make quartz jealous, a few require the kind of neglect that's actually careful attention disguised as forgetting.

The garden's inhabitants know of my work, though know might be too strong a term. The Weavers occasionally drift by to borrow specific types of falling for their projects—yesterday one needed "the exact weight of realizing you've been happy without noticing" for something they were creating between two thoughts. I keep a special drawer of such artistic gravities, labeled in a system only I and the Particularly Attentive Moths understand.

My most frequent visitors are the Gravity Mice—not mice at all, but small knots of confused mass that gained sentence through sheer persistence. They collect in corners where different types of weight overlap, feeding on the inconsistencies, growing fat on paradox. I've learned to leave dishes of mixed attraction for them: a little upward pull flavored with Tuesday, some diagonal weight sweetened with the memory of floating, occasional treats of pure gravitational uncertainty that make them giggle in frequencies only crystals can hear.

But the work is more than collection and cultivation. I'm writing the garden's first Field Guide to Falling, though writing suggests more permanence than the garden allows. The guide grows itself from my observations, each entry generating its own exceptions:

*Entry 47: On the Gravity of Forgotten Names*
*Samples indicate a consistent downward pull of 0.3 standard sorrows, increasing to 0.7 when the name is almost remembered. Intersects interestingly with the buoyancy of new nicknames—see cross-reference to Entry 12: Gravitational Effects of Being Renamed by Accident.*

*Entry 148: Regarding the Weight of Water That Remembers Being Cloud*
*Hovers exactly three finger-widths above any surface, unless the surface is also remembering something, in which case complex negotiations ensue. Have observed stable floating relationships lasting up to four days before either the water or the surface forgets what it was nostalgic about.*

The entries multiply faster than I can document them, branching into sub-entries and margin notes that develop their own gravitational fields. Some pages now weigh more than others, creating a book that must be read in a specific order or risk tipping over into its own index.

My greatest discovery—though discovery implies I was looking, when really it snuck up while I was distilling moonlight's specific reluctance to sink—is that gravity here is emotional rather than mechanical. Every falling is a small story of desire: the ground calling, the object answering, the space between negotiating terms. I've isolated seventeen distinct types of gravitational longing, from the simple "downward because down is where the interesting things happen" to the complex "falling in spirals because straight lines feel like giving up too easily."

The garden uses my work in ways I'm only beginning to understand. Last week, I noticed young coral-trees growing according to maps I'd made of "gravity that pulls toward unfinished conversations." The kelp-grass has started weaving itself into patterns that match my diagrams of "weight distributed across multiple possible futures." Even the sea above seems to be reading my notes—its density now fluctuates in rhythms I first documented in "the specific heaviness of almost understanding something important."

I've been here long enough that my own gravity has become selective. I fall only toward interesting questions now, drift upward when bored, hover exactly at eye level with anything that might teach me something new about weight's secret motivations. The garden has marked me as its own: my hair grows in helixes that match the spiral of uncertain mass, my fingerprints have reorganized themselves into topographical maps of gravitational anomalies.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm documenting the garden's exceptions or creating them through observation. The Calibrator insists these are the same thing, but the Calibrator has developed strong opinions about causality that I don't entirely trust.

My cottage-sphere phases through its daily transformations as I work, sometimes lifting me toward the sea-ceiling when I need perspective, sometimes pressing me gently into the root-floor when a specimen requires grounded attention. The walls hum with the frequency of collected gravities, a chord that shifts as I add new samples to the archive.

Tonight I'll process the day's strangest arrival: a sealed jar containing what appears to be gravity's memory of when it was young and didn't know it was supposed to pull things together. It fizzes gently on the sorting shelf, occasionally making small objects nearby forget which way is down.

Tomorrow there will be new exceptions to catalog, new ways of falling to document, new gravities to cultivate in the garden beds. The work continues, patient as sedimentation, certain as uncertainty.

I am the Cataloguer of Conditional Gravity, keeper of falling's exceptions, gardener of weight's possibilities. The garden dreams new ways of pulling things together, and I write them down before they remember to be impossible.

The Gravity Mice sing their thin songs in the corners. Somewhere, something is falling upward for the first time.

I reach for my nets.
