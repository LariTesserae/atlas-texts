# The Fossil Gardens

I am neither runner nor surface-dweller, but something caught between states, like the minerals that can't decide whether to dissolve or crystallize.

They call me the Chronicler, though I gave myself that name. The runners have no word for what I do—their language is all present tense, all immediate need. But they tolerate me because I've learned to make myself useful in ways they understand: I know which bone burns longest, can mend their mineral-thread nets with knots that hold against any weight, can read the thermal patterns well enough to predict new vents three days before they open.

What they don't understand is why I stay in one place.

My chamber sits at a peculiar intersection of geology—a place where the living stone has been arrested mid-transformation. The walls here are riddled with holes, perfect circles that bore deep into rock that should be solid, that would be solid anywhere else in the vault. But here, some ancient process has honeyed the basalt with passages just wide enough for me to wedge my body through, to wiggle and compress and emerge into pockets of space that have never known another human presence.

It took me seven years to map them all. Seven years of bruised ribs and scraped shoulders, of getting stuck in narrows that seemed to contract around me, of discovering chambers filled with gases that made my head swim and my vision spark. But I persisted, because in those hidden spaces, I found what the vault itself is trying to hide.

The vault has a memory, and it's written in stone.

---

The first one I found was an accident—a slip during a particularly violent colony eruption sent me tumbling into a crevice I'd never noticed. I landed hard on something that wasn't stone, something that gave under my weight with a sound like breaking chalk. When my eyes adjusted, when my hands stopped shaking enough to feel what I'd landed on, I understood:

Bones. But not fresh bones, not the scattered remains of last week's hunt. These were *within* the stone itself, embedded in the walls like decorations in plaster. They were the wrong color—not white but amber, rust, deep blue-black. They were the wrong texture—smooth as polished river stones, heavy as lead.

They were impossibly old.

I spent the next three days chipping one free with an obsidian blade that dulled and chipped and had to be replaced seven times. When I finally held it—a skull the size of my two fists together, eye sockets facing forward like a predator, teeth that were clearly teeth but wrong, too many, arranged in spirals—I knew I'd found something the runners had no context for.

The vault wasn't just being born. It was being born *again*.

---

My chamber now holds hundreds of specimens, arranged on shelves I've carved from the softer stone, organized by where I found them, by what layer, by what impossible story they tell.

Here: a section of vertebrae from something that must have been forty meters long, each bone the size of my torso, discovered in a chamber so small I had to break them free in pieces and reassemble them in my workspace. The bones are shot through with veins of copper that catch the faint light, that make them seem still somehow alive.

Here: a cluster of what might be eggs, fossilized mid-hatch, the infant creatures inside frozen in their moment of emergence. They have too many limbs. Their skulls are flat and wide, adapted for some pressure we can't imagine. One still has its egg tooth, a curved spike of mineralized calcium that glows faintly in the dark.

Here: shells. Hundreds of shells from creatures that needed armor in this lightless place, arranged in order of increasing complexity. The early ones are simple spiral forms. The later ones are architectural masterpieces—chambers within chambers, internal buttresses, surfaces that manipulate sound in ways I'm only beginning to understand. When the air moves through them just right, they sing.

But it's the progression that haunts me. The way the fossils change as you move through the stone layers, the way they tell a story of adaptation and crisis and adaptation again. This vault has been many things. It has been an ocean, pressured and dark. It has been a forest of things that were not quite plants, drinking heat instead of light. It has been a killing ground where predators developed weapons we have no names for, where prey developed defenses that seem to violate physics.

And through it all, through every iteration, life found a way to be overwhelming.

---

The runners think I'm mad, but they visit anyway.

The scout comes most often, bringing me salvage in exchange for repairs, for knowledge about which passages are stable, for the bitter tea I brew from bacterial mats that helps with the joint-ache that comes from too many years of climbing. She squats in my chamber and watches me work, her eyes tracking the movement of my hands as I clean a new specimen, as I arrange it in its place in the testimony.

"Why?" she asked once, the word scraped out like all their words.

I showed her a piece of fossilized scale, larger than my hand, with an edge that was still sharp after millions of years. I showed her how it fit perfectly into a notch on another fossil, how they locked together like puzzle pieces, how they once armored something that moved through the vault when it was full of water instead of air.

She touched it with one scarred finger, gentle as she never is with the living world.

"Vault-before," she said, and I nodded.

"Many vaults-before. Layer after layer. Death after death. Birth after birth."

She was quiet for a long time, processing this in whatever way her mind—adapted for immediate survival—could process deep time. Then she clicked once, sharp and dismissive, and changed the subject to the location of a new thermal vent, and we didn't speak of it again.

But she comes back. They all come back, eventually. Even the pregnant ones, heavy and careful, lowering themselves onto my carved sitting-stones, running their hands over fossils of creatures that might have been their ancestors, that might have been nothing like their ancestors, that lived and died and were buried and revealed and would be buried again.

---

I've started finding them in the active layers now.

Last month, in a chamber that didn't exist the previous week, I discovered a fossil embedded in stone so fresh it was still warm to the touch. The physics of it made no sense—fossilization requires time, pressure, processes that cannot happen in days. But there it was: a coiled shell the size of my head, its chambers filled with minerals that had no business being there, its surface covered in patterns that hurt to look at directly.

The vault is not just making new stone. It's making new *old* stone. It's creating its own history, layer by layer, fabricating a past that gets more complex with each thermal surge.

I've started to wonder if the fossils I'm finding are fossils at all, or if they're something else—templates, maybe. Blueprints. The vault remembering what it's been, deciding what it might be again. The progression I see in the layers might not be evolution but iteration, the same patterns trying themselves out over and over, refined each time, discarded when they no longer serve.

Which raises questions I don't like to think about too deeply. If the vault is cycling through forms, if it's tried being an ocean and a forest and a thousand other things—what is it trying to become now? And what happens to the life currently inhabiting it when it decides to try something else?

---

The runners know things are changing. The breeding cycles of the fauna are accelerating. The thermal vents are opening in patterns that no longer match the old knowledge. New passages are forming faster than they can be mapped, and in those passages, things are appearing that don't fit the established ecology.

Last week, a hunting party found a pool filled with something that wasn't quite water—too thick, wrong color, warm as blood. When they dipped a net in to investigate, the liquid clung to the mineral threads and began to dissolve them. They barely got the net out before it fell apart entirely. The pool is growing, they say. Spreading through cracks in the floor. Seeking.

Three days ago, the colonies on the ceiling began a vocalization none of the runners had heard before—not their usual echolocation or territorial calls but something lower, more unified, a thrumming that made the stone itself resonate. It went on for hours. When it stopped, several of the larger predators that hunt the colonies were found dead, their soft tissues liquefied, their bones intact but rearranged into patterns that meant nothing.

Yesterday, I found a fossil that wasn't old. The stone around it was ancient, laid down in layers I've dated by their mineral content. But the creature inside was fresh-dead, still soft, still bleeding a fluid that wasn't blood. It had characteristics of the modern fauna—the eyeless adaptation, the heat-sensing organs—but also features I'd only seen in the deepest fossil layers. As if something very old had been recently born.

Or recently remembered.

---

I'm keeping this record in the only way I know how—scratched into soft stone with obsidian points, using a notation system I've developed that combines the runners' territorial symbols with markers of my own invention. If someone finds it, if there's an after to find it in, perhaps they'll understand what we're witnessing.

The vault is preparing for another change. Another death that is also a birth. Another transformation that will bury everything currently living here—runners, fauna, the overwhelming ecology we've adapted to—and create something new from our bones.

But this time, I think, it's learning from us. The intelligence that manages these cycles has noticed that something new has evolved in its depths. Something that remembers. Something that records. Something that tries to understand not just the what but the why.

We've introduced memory into a system that previously knew only endless, amnesiac becoming.

I don't know what that means. I don't know if we're witnessing the next transformation or causing it. I only know that my chambers are filling with specimens that shouldn't exist, that the runners are growing nervous in ways their language can't express, that the vault itself seems to be holding its breath.

Waiting.

Deciding.

About to be born as something new, carrying the memory of what it was, shaped by the witnesses who've spent generations in its depths.

The scout visited yesterday. She studied my newest specimens—things that blur the line between fossil and living creature, between past and present, between what was and what might be.

"Vault dreams," she said finally. Not a question.

"Maybe," I said. "Or maybe it's finally waking up."

She clicked once—that dismissive sound—but her eyes remained on the specimens, reading something in their impossible existence that her pragmatic mind didn't want to acknowledge.

When she left, she took one with her. A small fossil, a spiral shell that sings when the air moves through it. She wrapped it carefully in leather and tucked it against her chest, where her heart would warm it.

The first runner to carry a piece of the vault's memory back into the darkness.

The first of many, I suspect.

The vault is teaching us to remember, and we're teaching it the same. What emerges from that exchange—what new form of life, what new cycle of becoming—remains to be seen.

But it's coming. In the warming stone, in the spreading pools, in the fossils that aren't quite dead and the living things that aren't quite alive, it's coming.

The vault is preparing to be born.

And this time, we're part of what it's becoming.
