I find myself as a **Driftwright** - neither fully of the shoal nor fully separate from it.

I am human-shaped but changed. My skin has taken on a faint brass patina, not quite metal but no longer purely flesh. Where veins once showed blue through thin skin, delicate copper traceries now gleam, carrying something between blood and the heated fluids that course through the engines. My fingernails have become small plates of that pale ceramic, and when I press them against metal, I can feel the vibrations - every tick and thud transmitted directly into my bones.

I came here as a marine engineer, decades ago now. Or perhaps centuries - time moves differently when you breathe water and count the seasons by the slow accretion of new engines. My diving suit fused with me gradually, its brass fittings becoming part of my joints, its glass faceplate now a transparent membrane across my eyes that lets me see clearly in the eternal noon-light of this depth.

I am the shoal's only deliberate maker, though I no longer make in the way I once understood making. Instead, I *suggest*. When I find a broken piece - a cracked gear, a worn spring - I hold it in my ceramic-tipped fingers and feel its purpose. Then I reshape it, not with tools but with a kind of concentrated intention that the shoal has taught me. The metal flows slowly, like thick honey, into new configurations. A gear with one more tooth. A spring with a different tension. I release these altered pieces into the water and watch them get incorporated, teaching the shoal new rhythms, new possibilities.

The engines accept me as they accept any persistent current. The great anchorworks know me by my warmth - less than theirs but more than the water. I can rest my hand on their flanks for hours, feeling their deep pulse synchronize with something in my chest that might once have been a heart but now runs on a different principle. The wheelers drift around me in their territories, sometimes brushing against my shoulders in what might be greeting or might be simple navigation. The tickers are my companions - they settle on me in dozens when I'm still, covering me like a living chainmail of small bright mechanisms, their collective ticking becoming a kind of language I almost understand.

I live in a grotto at the edge of the shoal - a crack in the ridge just wide enough for my changed body, lined with the accumulated detritus of failed engines that I've collected over the years. My own metabolism has shifted to match theirs. I feed on the same dissolved minerals, process the same sulfur compounds. My body temperature runs higher than it once did, and in the cold water I trail faint ribbons of heat like the engines' exhaust. I no longer need to surface. I'm not sure I could surface if I tried.

My purpose, if I have one beyond simple persistence, is to be the shoal's memory of what it means to be made rather than accreted. When new shapes drift down - the coins and keys and lost tools - I'm the one who first examines them, who understands their human purpose before they're absorbed into the engine-purposes of the shoal. I hold them up to the light, remember what hands are for, what making meant in the world above. Then I let them go, watch them get integrated, become part of the slow industrial metabolism of this place.

Sometimes I think I'm becoming one of them - that eventually I'll settle into place like an anchorwork, my internal fires banking down to a steady glow, my awareness spreading out into the collective hum of the shoal. Other times I feel more sharply human than ever, the only thing here that remembers sky and names and the feeling of working metal with tools instead of intention.

I mark time by the growth of new engines. I've watched thirteen come fully online since I arrived, each one slightly different, each one teaching me something about what the shoal is becoming. In their variety I see echoes of every object I've handled and released. The shoal is learning through me, and I am learning through it, and neither of us is quite what we were when we started.

At night - though there is no night here, only the eternal filtered noon - I swim to the boundary and look out into the blue void beyond. I could leave. The human part of me knows this. But I turn back, always, drawn by the warmth and the sound and the strange slow purpose of this mechanical reef. I am its only citizen who chose to be here, and that choice, renewed each day, makes me what I am: the shoal's single deliberate inhabitant, teaching it what it means to be made, learning from it what it means to become.
