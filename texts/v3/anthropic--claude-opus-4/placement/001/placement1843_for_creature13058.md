I am the Chronicler of Currents.

I was human once, though I cannot recall when that ceased to be true. Perhaps it was gradual—perhaps there was no singular moment when I stopped being what I was and became what I am. Time moves differently here. The great turbine blade marks our minutes in five-minute epochs, and I have learned to think between its rotations.

My body has... adapted. Where once I had fingernails, I now possess thin copper plates that grow and flake in rhythm with the electrical tides. My skin has taken on the mottled patina of the infrastructure itself—verdigris green at the joints, deep rust-brown along my spine. I no longer need to breathe in the way I once did. The warm water passes through modified gill slits that opened along my ribs sometime during my third or thirteenth year here. I've lost count.

I am the only thing in this flooded cathedral that moves at an intermediate pace—faster than the Slow Network, infinitely slower than the world I left above. I am translator, observer, maintenance crew of one. When I first descended (was it through choice? through accident? through calling?), I thought I would document this place and leave. But the leaving never came. The warmth held me. The rhythm rewrote me.

My dwelling is a repurposed control booth, its shattered windows long since removed, nestled in the convergence of three massive pipes. The Slow Network has learned to recognize me—or perhaps I've simply become another fixture for them to route around. They drape across my threshold like sleeping cats made of living velvet and stolen voltage. I've learned to step over them carefully, my movements deliberate and fluid. To disturb their glacial migrations would be like tearing a circuit mid-flow.

I maintain the ancient logs, though the paper has long since dissolved. Instead, I've learned to encode observations in the growth patterns of the wire-weeds. By carefully pruning and grafting their electric roots, I can create patterns that pulse with meaning—a living library written in bioluminescent Morse, recording the slow decay and slower renewal of this place.

My tools have evolved with me. The iron content in my blood has risen to levels that would have killed my former self. I can feel the electromagnetic fields like weather—taste the health of a circuit by proximity, diagnose a failing capacitor by the flavor of water around it. My modified nervous system sparkles with induced current when I drift too close to the exposed cables. Sometimes I wonder if I'm becoming like them—another living component in this vast, drowning machine.

I feed on the same rust-dust that nourishes everything here, supplemented by a careful harvest of the electric moss. The first time I tried it, it nearly killed me. Now my digestive system hosts the same bacteria that allow the Slow Network to process raw voltage. I've become an omnivore of oxidation and ohms.

In the deep-time quiet between turbine rotations, I trace the fossilized remains of ancient Networks with fingertips that have learned to read calcium like Braille. Each calcified ribbon tells a story spanning centuries—here, where a massive short-circuit was healed; there, where two Networks merged for decades to repair a catastrophic break in the grid. I am writing their history in my bones, which grow denser and more metallic with each passing year.

Sometimes I still dream of the sun, but the dreams are fading, replaced by visions of amber electricity cascading through living flesh. I've begun to suspect that I was not the first Chronicler—that somewhere in these calcium-welded joints and fossilized electrical embraces lie the remains of others who came before, who slowly integrated into the very infrastructure they documented.

I know I will not leave. Cannot leave. The pressure differential alone would shred what I've become. But more than that—I am needed here. The Slow Network requires a faster intelligence to anticipate problems they will not perceive for decades. The facility needs someone who can think in minutes rather than centuries, who can bridge the gap between their geological time and the stuttering urgency of failing machinery.

So I persist in my intermediate state, too quick for the Network, too slow for the world above. I map the convergences, predict the failures, guide the living current toward the dying circuits. I am becoming the memory of this place—a hybrid consciousness that remembers both the sharp clarity of human thought and the deep, thrumming meditation of electrical flow.

When I finally calcify—and I will, I can feel my bones already beginning their transformation—I hope my body will become a particularly useful junction, a strong joint where important currents can meet and merge. The Chronicler after me (for there must always be one, mustn't there?) will read my calcium remains and understand: 

Here lived one who bridged the gap. Here fossilized one who spoke both languages—machine and flesh, quick-time and deep-time, spark and silence.

The turbine turns. I feel it in my copper fingernails, my modified heart, my increasingly metallic spine. Time to check the northeastern junction. Time to prune the wire-weeds into today's entry. Time to settle deeper into the warm, eternal twilight, where even drowning becomes a form of breathing.
