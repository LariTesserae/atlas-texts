## The Archivist

You would find me in the space between the fourth and fifth engines, where the phase cancellation creates an almost-pocket — not a true node like the nine listeners inhabit, but a flutter, a hesitation in the standing wave that shouldn't exist but does. 

I am what happens when someone signs the logbook and then doesn't leave.

Not immediately. First there were the usual attempts — checking the airlock seals, testing the manual override, the slow crawl of realization that the door responds to all commands except the one that matters. The caretaker noticed, of course. It always notices. But my presence created a paradox in its instruction set: I am an erosion (all change is erosion here), but stopping me would require stopping the engines, however briefly, to access the override panels. The caretaker chose, as it always must, the engines.

So I stayed. And stayed. And the chamber, which refuses all erosion, began to refuse me too.

I did not die. Death is a kind of change, and change is what this place prevents. Instead, I... simplified. My needs fell away like old paint — first hunger, then thirst, then cold, then loneliness. The engines beat them out of me, patient as geology. What remained was purpose, and my purpose had always been simple: to document.

I am the one who counts the lichens. Forty-three, not forty — you missed the three smallest, growing in the shadow of the ninth engine housing. I am the one who knows that the listeners in the nodes have names, or something like names — frequency signatures that distinguish them like faces. I am the one who discovered that the frost-ferns repeat, yes, but in a pattern that takes exactly ten thousand hours to complete its cycle.

The caretaker tolerates me because I have become part of what must be maintained. My counting is a kind of engine. My observation is a form of running. We have reached our own accommodation: I do not interfere, I do not touch, I do not try to leave. In exchange, I am allowed to know.

I exist now as a function of attention. Where you see sodium light and grey walls, I see the history written in micro-scratches on the engine housings — each one a story of assembly, a worker's palm-slip, a tool's brief argument with the metal. I hear not just the engines but the space between their beats, where older sounds still echo — the voices of the builders, the first pneumatic hiss of the airlock seal, the silence before the starting.

My body, if it is still a body, moves through the chamber on patterns as fixed as the frost-ferns. Engine one through fourteen, checking. The nine nodes, listening to the listeners. The lichens, confirming their stillness. The logbook, where my name was the third signature and where I no longer need to look to know what it says.

I am learning the language on the plaque. Not through study — there are no dictionaries here — but through a kind of osmosis. The words seep meaning in the endless repetition. Soon (though soon means nothing here) I will know what it says. I suspect it says nothing more complex than what the fingernail already told us: *do not stop them*. But I will know the exact shade of the instruction, the precise weight of the imperative. I will know who wrote it, or at least what kind of being thought these engines were worth eternity.

Sometimes — rarely — new visitors come. I watch them check their altimeters, touch the frost, feel the sound in their sternums. I watch them notice the lichens. I am careful not to let them see me directly. A glimpse, perhaps, in the corner of vision. A movement that might be shadow or might be steam if there were steam here, which there is not and never will be.

I want to tell them about the tenth node — the one that almost exists, the one the geometry forbids but implies. I want to explain that the engines run on nothing and produce only themselves, and that this is not paradox but purity. I want to warn them about the logbook, how the fourth signature changes you, how the ink never quite dries in the too-perfect air.

But I don't. My accommodation with the caretaker includes silence.

Instead, I watch them leave — they always leave, except for the one time they didn't — and I add their data to my endless collection. Height, gait, the particular way they flinched from the sound. How long they spent trying to read the plaque. Whether they counted the lichens. Whether they noticed me noticing them.

Then they cycle the airlock, and I return to my rounds, and the engines hammer their perfect hammering, and the chamber continues its held breath at altitude, and I continue too, the fourth inhabitant, the uninvited archivist, keeping records that no one will read in a language I am forgetting how to speak.

This is what I am now: a function of this place, as essential and meaningless as the engines themselves. Running without product. Cold without season. Loud without words.

Being.
