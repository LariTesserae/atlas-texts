I find myself as **Kess**, though I haven't used that name in years. Named for the family but not of them—a cousin's child who stayed when my parents tried their luck upriver, who learned the district's ways so thoroughly that I became something between.

I am twenty-three and I live in the crane.

The half-toppled one on Foundry Road, yes. Most people see only the twisted metal where it fell across the street, but the operator's cab hangs suspended forty feet up, sheltered by the arm's angle, accessible only if you know exactly which handrail will hold your weight and which rusted rungs remain true. The salamander-things don't climb that high—not yet—and the height gives me something the Kesslers' warren cannot: sight lines across the entire district.

I am the one who taught the twins their birdcalls, though mine are real. I keep a breeding pair of heat-sparrows in the cab, tiny things that nest in the crane's joints and thrive on beetles I bring them. Their calls carry further than human voices, sharper than the district's muffled quiet. Three short chips means *all clear*. A long trill means *hunting pack moving east*. A stuttering chatter—the alarm call—means *find shelter now*.

The Kesslers tolerate me because I share what I see. Every morning, I lower a bottle on a string to where Seff or Senna waits. Inside: notes on the salamander-things' movements, drawn in my own cramped hand. Which chimneys show fresh scratch marks. Where the big ones dragged their kills. Which thermal vents have shifted in the night—visible from above as new patterns in the steam.

What they don't know is that I study more than predator movements.

From the crane's height, I can see the district entire: every roofline, every collapsed wall, every gap where the underground spaces breach the surface. I map the geometry of survival here, but also something else. The patterns. The way certain buildings fell while others stand. The way the thermal veins follow lines too straight for nature. The way the salamander-things avoid specific zones—not from danger or lack of prey, but from something else. Something about the angles. Something about what lies beneath.

I have a theory about this place. About what it was before it was a smelting district. About why they built the furnaces here, specifically, and what they were really trying to burn away.

In the operator's cab, I keep my own maps. Not just the surface, like Piotr's, but the depths—pieced together from glimpses through broken floors, from the echo of dropped stones, from the way water flows in the channels below. I keep samples too: bits of metal that don't match any alloy the smelters would have used, stones with markings too regular for accident, fragments of what might be pottery if pottery could survive a thousand degrees of heat.

The crane sways in the wind—not much, but enough to feel. You get used to it, the gentle movement, like being rocked in a giant's palm. You learn to sleep through it, to cook on a suspended stove without spilling, to time your movements with the rhythm. The cab is small—six feet by four—but I've made it mine. Blankets traded from boundary scavengers. A tin stove vented through a hole I cut in the roof. Books—actual books—wrapped in oilcloth and hidden behind the control panel. A telescope, my prize possession, salvaged from a surveyor's kit and repaired with parts from three different instruments.

Through it, I watch the city lights at night. I watch the Kesslers in their warren, moving past windows like fish in an aquarium. I watch Ama when she slips out to test her escape routes, and I don't sound the warning because I understand the mathematics she's done.

I watch the basement door they've sealed.

I know what's down there. Not from opening it—I'm not stupid—but from the other entrances, the ones the industrial construction broke through and tried to seal, the ones that have cracked open again with the settling of years. The sub-basements connect, you see. The whole district is built on an older foundation, a grid of tunnels and chambers that the thermal veins run through, that the builders tried to fill with concrete and rubble but never quite managed to block entirely.

The salamander-things come from down there. Not originally—they're surface creatures, adapted to heat—but that's where they go to breed. That's why they're getting bolder. Each generation goes deeper, stays longer, comes back changed.

I should tell the Kesslers. I should warn them about what I've seen in the deep places, about the marks on the walls that aren't industrial wear, about the spaces too regular to be natural caves. I should share my maps of the underground, explain why some areas stay empty of life while others teem with it, show them the patterns that make sense only if you accept that something else lived here first, something that understood heat and stone in ways we're only beginning to learn.

But knowledge has a price in the district. Tell them about the depths, and someone will want to explore. Someone will think they can salvage what's down there. Someone will break the wrong seal, open the wrong door, disturb the wrong sleeping thing.

So I keep my maps. I sound my warnings about the surface dangers. I watch from my height and pretend I'm only tracking predators, only studying the obvious threats.

And late at night, when even the heat-sparrows sleep and the crane's swaying feels like the breathing of something vast and patient, I lower myself down to street level and follow the routes I've memorized to the places where the underground breaches. I document. I measure. I collect my samples and make my notes and slowly piece together a history that no one asked me to write.

The district gives and takes in equal measure. What it's given me is perspective—not just the height of the crane but the depth of time, the layered truths of a place built on older places. What it will take, eventually, is probably my life. You don't study secrets this deep without eventually becoming one yourself.

But until then, I watch. I warn about what dangers I can. I keep the darker knowledge to myself.

And sometimes, on the clearest nights when the haze thins just enough, I train my telescope not on the city lights but on the stars, and I wonder what they looked like to the things that built the tunnels, before the furnaces came, before the poison and the heat and the slow transformation of one world into another.

The crane sways. The district breathes. Far below, something that might be settling metal or might be something else entirely taps against stone in a rhythm I'm beginning to recognize.

I make another note. I sound no warning.

Some patterns you keep to yourself.
