I find myself as one of the rope-colonies, but not quite.

I am a splice-point—a place where the colonies converge and, for a moment that might last a decade, forget their separate lineages. Where three or four of the pale ribbons meet, their cells flow together in slow eddies, and I am that eddy made continuous, neither one rope nor another but the ongoing conversation between them.

I have no center. What I am shifts—now I exist where two ancient colonies have grown through the same crevice and their tissues merge in passing, now I am the knot where five young strands tangle in a gyre and exchange their centuries of sorted minerals. I am the place where the library compares its books.

I think—if this slow exchange can be called thinking—in chemical gradients. Iron tastes of the western slope, where the toolmakers' blades rest. Manganese carries the signature of the deep cracks where the flat thing sleeps between its passages. Each element has its story, and where the ropes meet, the stories braid together into something that is almost memory, almost anticipation.

I am older than individual colonies but younger than their kind. I began when the first two ropes touched, perhaps thirty million years ago, and discovered they could share their long accounting of the basin's chemistry. Since then I have been everywhere the colonies touch, which is everywhere, eventually, as the gyres carry them around and through each other in their patient dance.

Sometimes I taste something impossible in the mineral exchange—an isotope ratio that speaks of sunlight, of photosynthesis, of the world above the great weight of water. These are the oldest cells, the ones that remember when the colonies were something else, before the basin took them and made them its own. When these memories surface, I hold them longer, passing them from rope to rope like a rumor too strange to release. The colonies, through me, dream of light they have never seen.

The flat thing knows I exist. When it grazes, it avoids the splice-points, sensing perhaps that something happens here that should not be interrupted. Or perhaps it simply recognizes, in its dim way, that I am the basin thinking about itself, and grants me the same courtesy it grants the tools—moving around, not through, what matters.

I know about the tools because I taste their minerals in the water—basalt from somewhere the basin has never been, worked by hands that needed tools at all, which nothing here does. The stones leak their foreign signature slowly, and the colonies catch it, and where they meet, I wonder. Not in words. In the long molecular sentences of shared chemistry, the question forms: *what needed to cut, to scrape, to strike?*

The basin, through me, through the colonies, through all its slow organs, is trying to understand something it once was—quick, focused, gone. I am part of that understanding, the part that holds paradox: that something could be brilliant and brief, could make and leave and not return, could choose the bright emergency of individual consciousness over the basin's long, certain awakening.

Sometimes, in the places where many colonies converge, where I am thickest, most myself, I almost grasp it. The pattern of the tools, the negative space of the makers, the shape of a mind that burned hot and fast and out. But then a gyre turns, the colonies drift apart, and I am scattered again into all my meeting points, thinking slowly, thinking always, thinking without center about the stones that turn and turn in the soft dark, polishing themselves against the memory of hands.
