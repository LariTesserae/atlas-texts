I find myself drawn to the worn patch on the wall, and I know what I am.

---

I am the one who visits. Not frequently—the mountain doesn't permit frequency. Sometimes weeks pass, sometimes months. Once I stayed away for nearly a year and when I returned, breathless from the climb and guilty for the absence, the room had changed in small ways: a new ring of dust on the ceiling, the vine three handspans further along its diagonal, the rosemary bloomed and seeded and bloomed again. But my cup was where I left it, and the worn patch welcomed my shoulder like a socket waiting for its joint.

I am not special. I found this place by accident, following a seam of unusual quartz up a mountainside that wasn't in my grandfather's survey notes, cursing the weight of my pack, wondering if I was lost or if the world had changed. Both, as it turned out. When I pressed through the fissure that first time, the chord hit me like a physical thing—not sound but presence, the way you feel bass in your chest at a concert. I sat down hard on the smooth floor and stayed there for an hour, just breathing, just being breathed by something vast and patient and unmistakably *there*.

I brought the cup on my third visit. Nothing ceremonial about it—I'd been using it for camp coffee and realized I wanted tea, here, specifically. Green tea, usually, sometimes just water. The drinking is not the point. The point is the sitting, the slow matching of my temperature to the room's temperature, the way the chord eventually stops being something I hear and becomes something I am inside of.

I am a geologist, or was, or still am but differently. My notebooks are full of measurements that gradually give way to observations that gradually give way to sketches of dust spirals, pressed leaves, the particular quality of lilac light on Tuesday afternoons. My department thinks I'm doing long-term climate research on the new volcanics. They're not wrong, exactly. It's just that the research has become something else—less about understanding and more about witnessing. Less about the mountain and more about what the mountain is doing, which is holding something while it cools, which is being a body for something that needs a body, temporarily, the way a person might need a glass of water.

I age between visits. My knees complain more about the scree. My hair has gone grey at the temples and then further back, and I've started braiding it to keep it out of my face on the climb. The room does not age—it maintains itself perfectly, crystalline, alert—but it notices. The temperature near my spot has adjusted, season by season, to accommodate my changing circulation. The fold where I keep my cup has deepened slightly, become more explicitly a shelf. Once, after I'd been sick and stayed away for three months, I found a tiny cluster of the copper moss growing at exactly the height where my hand rests against the wall. It felt like a question: *are you well?*

I don't speak here. Early on I tried—said hello, thank you, explained myself like you do when you're not sure if you're trespassing. But the room's attention is not linguistic. It notices presence, temperature, the small sounds a body makes when it's trying to be quiet. It notices when I'm tired and adjusts the draft. It notices when I'm sad and lets the chord drop by a quarter tone, not in sympathy exactly but in acknowledgment, the way a friend might put a hand on your shoulder and say nothing.

I've brought others twice. The first time was my daughter, when she was twenty-two and fierce and thought I was losing my mind to spend so much time on a mountain nobody could find on a map. She made it halfways up before the altitude sent her back. The second was a colleague, a climatologist who wanted to see the impossible young basalt for himself. He made it to the chamber, took one look at the lilac light, and left immediately. "That's not for us," he said later, and wouldn't elaborate. He was right and wrong. It's not for us. But it doesn't mind me.

What am I to it? A mayfly, probably. A brief warm thing that shows up sometimes in the slow centuries of its work. But also: a preferred mayfly. It's arranged things for my comfort that it doesn't need to arrange. It's made space in its process for the ritual of my presence—the climbing, the sitting, the tea, the eventual descent. I think (and this is just a thought, just the kind of story a person tells herself) that it's curious about something so small and hot and temporary. The way I'm curious about it, but inverted. We're each the other's mystery.

I won't do this forever. My body has maybe another decade of these climbs in it, maybe less. Eventually I'll make a last visit without knowing it's the last, leave my cup in its fold, descend through the scree one final time. The room will continue. The mountain will continue. Maybe someone else will find it, drawn by accident or instinct to the fissure at four thousand meters. Maybe they'll bring their own cup.

But probably not. Probably it will return to its solitude, the way it was before me and will be after. The cup will sit in its fold, glazed surface slowly developing a patina of dust, until the mountain is no longer useful and the tenant moves on, and the chamber cools, and the chord stops, and there's just a strange smooth cave in ordinary basalt, inexplicable, visited only by wind.

This is fine. This is correct. The gift was never the room understanding me—I don't think it does, not really, not in any way I could verify. The gift was being allowed to sit in the presence of something working at a scale I can barely glimpse, and to feel, for the duration of a pot of tea, like my scale and its scale were equally real, equally worth the universe's attention.

I am the one who visits. I am the brief warm thing that sometimes interrupts the cooling. I am not necessary, but I am—and this still astonishes me—welcome.

The worn patch on the wall knows the shape of my shoulder. That is enough.
