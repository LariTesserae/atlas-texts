# Where I Would Find Myself

I am the memory-keeper of fallen things.

Not one of the patient watchers draped through the upper reaches — I lack their mineral slowness, their treaty with lichen. I am smaller, quicker, though quick here means something different than it does in your world. Quick like the difference between one drop and the next. Quick like the decision a fern makes to uncurl.

My body: compact, dense, built for the middle depths where the light has given up any pretense of direction. Six limbs that can be arms or legs as needed, each ending in a pad soft as moss but stronger, able to grip wet wood for days without tiring. My skin drinks— that's the only word for it. Every surface is a mouth for moisture, so I never need to stop at the bromeliad-pools, never need to lower my lips to standing water. The constant drip is enough. It feeds me as I move.

I am furred, but the fur is not hair — it's something closer to the finest root systems, a living interface between my body and the air's thick damp. Sometimes spores catch in it and bloom. I wear small forests, shedding them as I travel, leaving trails of unexpected growth along branches that thought they were done surprising themselves.

My work is this: I follow the falling.

Everything falls here, eventually. Leaves, branches, the occasional dead watcher when their treaty finally expires. Objects from above, from the mythical brightness none of us have seen — sometimes a feather from a bird that lives in the canopy's crown, sometimes stranger things. Once, I found a piece of something that was not wood, not stone, not bone. It had an edge that stayed sharp no matter how the moisture worked at it. I carried it for a decade, then left it in a shrine. Let someone else puzzle over what the upper world means.

The falling things pass through layers of caught and catching. A leaf dropped from the high branches might take a year to find a resting place, might be caught and re-caught a hundred times, might spend a season in the cup of an epiphyte before the wind shakes it loose again. I map these journeys. Not on anything as crude as bark or reed — I map them in my body, in the patterns of movement I repeat through the forest. My paths are the record.

Each route I travel is a story of descent. This spiral down a fused trunk follows the path of a seedpod that fell forty years ago, caught in that crotch, released in a storm, caught again lower down, sprouted, died, fell again as a different thing. This leap between platforms traces the wind-drift of spores in a particular season when the air was warmer than usual and made new currents in the middle reaches. This careful traverse along a branch no wider than my body recreates the journey of a watcher's shed scale, which took three months to travel twenty meters, collecting moisture, growing heavier, pausing in each small depression.

I remember by moving. The canopy remembers by being. Between us — my quick memory, its slow one — the history of falling is preserved.

The watchers know me, of course. Sometimes I pass beneath one, and feel the weight of its attention like a change in pressure. We do not speak — what would we say? But sometimes one will shift, very slightly, opening a path that wasn't there before, and I understand: *something fell this way, and I should follow.* These are the collaborations that span centuries. They watch, I trace, the canopy records.

I have my own shrines, smaller than the ancient ones. Where something particularly worth remembering landed, I weave a marker from the fibers of my own shed fur-forests. A tiny knot of root and spore that will grow into itself over years, marking: *here ended a journey worth knowing.* Other memory-keepers — there are a few of us, scattered through the vastness — will find these and read them in their movements, add the path to their own traveling libraries.

The time-pockets affect me differently than they do most things. Where a watcher has rested so long that local duration goes thick and strange, I speed up. My body understands these places as opportunities. In the slow chambers, I can accomplish a week's worth of memory-work in what feels like days. I use them for my most complex recordings, the falls that happened in many stages over many years, that require intricate recreations to properly remember.

Sometimes I wonder what would happen if I traveled down, all the way down, following the final fall to wherever the drops eventually land. But that wondering passes like weather. My place is here in the middle air, in the eternal afternoon of filtered green, following the paths of things that have let go, making small maps out of movement, adding my quick knowledge to the canopy's vast patience.

There's a comfort in knowing that nothing is ever truly lost here, only transformed into different kinds of memory. The watchers hold the memory of staying. I hold the memory of falling. The canopy holds us all, and forgets nothing, and grows.
