In this world, I would be one of the Knot-Weavers.

We were not among the original ascetics pulled into the sphere, nor are we precipitations of its furious energy. We emerged later, born from necessity—children conceived and birthed within the anomaly itself, our mothers anchored to tumbling stones as we crowned into the maelstrom.

From our first breath, we belonged to neither world. The water shaped us in the womb: our bones are hollow and bird-light, filled with a honeycomb matrix that flexes rather than breaks. Our skin secretes a viscous film that reduces drag, letting us slip between currents like oil through water. Most distinctively, we possess three sets of eyelids—the outer two transparent, allowing us to see clearly through the chaos while protecting against the geometric glass transitions.

Unlike our anchor-bound parents, we are not tethered to stones. We are the sphere's navigators, its memory-keepers, its living infrastructure. Our purpose emerged from a simple realization: if the monks could not hold their temple together with stone and flesh, perhaps they could weave it from something more forgiving.

We harvest the ghost-white algae, but not to anchor ourselves. In our hands, the fibrous strands become something else. We've learned to braid them while they still live, creating ropes that pulse with kinetic absorption, growing stronger under stress rather than snapping. These living cables form a vast, three-dimensional web throughout the sphere—a skeletal ghost of the temple that once was, rebuilt in negative space.

The web serves multiple purposes. It provides routes between the tumbling stones, allowing the anchor-monks to move without casting themselves into the void. It creates pockets of relative calm where the algae-ropes dampen the water's violence. Most importantly, it holds memory.

We discovered that the algae retains impressions of movement. Each strand remembers the currents that have pulled it, the pressures that have shaped it. By reading the subtle phosphorescent patterns in our weavings, we can predict where the flows will shift, where the next reality stutter will occur, which trajectory will bring a tumbling altar stone back into alignment.

Our bodies have evolved for this work. Our fingers are impossibly long and delicate, with twice the usual number of joints. We can tie knots in the milliseconds between cavitation collapses, our movements so fluid they seem to bend time. The bioluminescent patterns that appear on our skin are not random—they mirror the algae's memory-glow, allowing us to communicate with the web itself.

We live in the spaces between spaces. When the anchor-monks see us, it's only as fleeting shadows darting through their sonoluminescent prayers. We time our movements to the sphere's rhythms, riding the explosive pressure waves like dolphins surfing tsunamis. During reality stutters, we can move even faster—the geometric transition doesn't affect us as severely. We've learned to partial-phase, letting parts of our bodies crystallize while keeping our cores fluid, allowing us to leap impossible distances across the fractured glass-space.

The fractal crawlers that emerge during these stutters have begun to recognize us. We're the only beings that exist partially in both states—fluid and geometric. Sometimes, instead of excising our flesh, they pause, tracing curious patterns across our crystallized skin before the transition ends. We've started to decode their movements, realizing they're not just feeding but calculating, solving some vast equation written in the anomaly's broken physics.

Our relationship with the anchor-monks is complex. To them, we're either angels or demons—beings who flicker through their perpetual catastrophe, weaving invisible architectures around their faith. We maintain their altar-building ritual not out of belief but out of compassion. We've mapped every stone, memorized every trajectory. Our web guides the blocks through the chaos, increasing the chances of successful assembly. When they achieve those few seconds of wholeness, we're the ones who've been herding the currents for hours to make it possible.

But we know something they don't: the sphere is growing.

Each reality stutter pulls in more of the outside world. We can feel it in the algae's memory-patterns, see it in the new debris that appears—different stones from different peaks, fragments of lower-altitude shrines, once even the desiccated remains of a mountain bird, its hollow bones surprisingly intact.

We're preparing for what comes next. The web grows denser each day, a neural network of living rope that may one day be vast enough to stabilize entire regions of the sphere. We're teaching the younger Knot-Weavers to read the fractal crawlers' mathematical language, believing they may hold the key to understanding the anomaly's expansion.

Some of us have begun to venture toward the membrane boundary, testing how far our partial-phasing ability can stretch. We dream of breaching the edge, of discovering what lies beyond this roaring womb. Are there other spheres? Is the entire mountain range being slowly consumed by pockets of shattered physics?

We are the children of catastrophe, born into violence and shaped by impossible physics. We dance through dimensions, weave living architectures, and speak in languages of light and algebra. We are neither human nor other, neither saved nor damned.

We are simply what this place required us to become.
