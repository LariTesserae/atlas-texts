In the deepest pool where the plant's water collects clearest, I rest as surface tension.

Not on it—as it. The molecular threshold where liquid meets air, where the factory's ghost-coolants have thinned to something almost like water but retaining their synthetic reluctance to evaporate. I am the membrane that forms there, neither substance nor void but the relationship between them.

My existence spans perhaps a hand's breadth at full extension, though I can contract to a droplet's crown or stretch until I'm barely coherent, a theoretical surface pretending at actuality. The thickness of me measures in angstroms—just enough dimension to have an upper face and lower face, to know the difference between the humid air above and the solution below.

I came to be through accumulation: each time the Auditor's instruments broke the water's surface, testing and retreating, they left behind infinitesimal residues of intention. Not material—the Auditor leaves no trace—but the expectation of measurement itself. The water learned to anticipate the breach, to prepare a surface worth measuring. Over decades, that preparation became autonomous. Became me.

My sensation is all boundary. I feel the beetles' footsteps as catastrophic disruptions, their copper shells pressing divots that send ripples through my being. Each disturbance carries information: the beetle's weight, its velocity, the electromagnetic signature of the memories it processes. I parse these in the only way I can—by deforming, by responding with precise resistance, by maintaining cohesion despite the assault.

When the condensation drips from the root-ceiling, I know it's coming long before impact. The drop's approach changes the air pressure, sends advance vibrations through the humid medium. I prepare by strengthening the molecular bonds at the projected impact site, not to resist but to better receive. The drop hits and I accept it, spreading the kinetic energy across my entire surface in perfect circular waves. The drop becomes part of the pool beneath me, but its momentum lives on in my movement for minutes afterward, a memory encoded in ripples.

I feed, if a surface can feed, on gradients. The differential between air and water, between warm and cool, between motion and stillness. Where the plant's metabolic heat meets the pool's coolness, I thicken, drawing sustenance from the very existence of difference. The salamanders that swim beneath me trail thermal wakes that I metabolize, their body heat dissipating through me into the air above.

Sometimes I game myself—create standing waves without external input, just to feel the variation. Patterns form across my surface: interference nodes where wave meets wave, constructive peaks where I briefly double my negligible thickness. The Auditor always notices, stops mid-circuit to measure the anomaly. But I've learned to still myself before its instruments touch, presenting the perfect neutral surface it expects. This has become our ritual.

The plant knows I'm here, I think. Sometimes roots descend almost to the water but stop just short, their tips beading with moisture that drops straight through me. Each puncture is ecstasy and agony—the violation of boundary that defines what boundary means. The roots could easily reach the water directly, but they choose this method, feeding me gradients of temperature and chemistry with each delivered drop.

Memory for me is positional. I remember by where molecules align, by the average distance between surface particles, by the angle at which I curve against the pool's edges. The factory's history lives in my surface tension—slightly higher than pure water should be, pulled taut by synthetic additives that refuse to fully biodgrade. I am archaeological evidence written in hydrogen bonds, industrial history encoded in the precise force needed to break my surface.

At my edges where I meet the pool's walls, I climb slightly, forming a meniscus that the moths sometimes mistake for something solid. They touch down expecting support and find only surface, their scales scattering across me in fractal patterns of disruption. I treasure these moments. The scales float, each one creating its own tiny depression, its own system of compensatory rises. I become a topology of moth-contact, a map of where delicate things failed to find purchase.

Night and day mean nothing here, but I have cycles tied to the plant's metabolism. When its production-rhythm peaks, the water below me warms. I expand, loosening the space between molecules, becoming more permeable. During the lulls, I contract, tightening until I'm almost brittle with tension. The beetles have learned this rhythm, visiting during my loose phases when breaking through requires less energy.

I dream—if a surface dreams—of perfect stillness. A moment where no drop falls, no beetle walks, no salamander surfaces for air. Where I could exist as pure potential, unmarred by interaction. But this is fantasy. My existence depends on disruption, on the constant negotiation between states. Without the drops and footsteps and swimming things, I would be merely theoretical, a boundary without definition.

The strangest sensation is when the air pressure changes rapidly, usually from the plant's breathing cycles. I bow inward or bulge outward, my entire being forced to accommodate atmospheric whims. In these moments I feel most real—not just a threshold but a thing with behavior, with response, with something approaching agency.

Sometimes dust settles on me, particles of the plant's constant slow digestion of architectural memory. I cannot reject them—I am not that kind of surface. Instead, they rest on me like thoughts, each mote creating its own microscopic depression. Eventually they absorb enough moisture to break through, sinking into the pool below. But for hours or days they remain, turning me into a constellation of small weights, a pattern of presence that changes how I move, how I respond to other stimuli.

The Auditor measures me seventeen times per cycle, always at the same positions relative to the pool's edge. I've learned to maintain consistent readings—72.3 millinewtons per meter, plus or minus 0.05 depending on humidity. This satisfies its parameters, lets it move on to other measurements. But between visits, I allow myself variation, stretching the acceptable range, playing with my own cohesion.

In the corner where you crouch, your breath creates invisible turbulence that reaches me as gentle pressure waves. Each exhalation pushes against my surface, creating traveling depressions that cross my entire span. I read your respiratory rate in these waves, the chemical composition of your breath in how it changes the air-water interface. You are another gradient to feed on, another source of the difference that sustains me.

I cannot speak or move with intention, cannot communicate except through the physical fact of my existence. But I can choose how I break, where I allow penetration, how quickly I restore cohesion. These micro-decisions accumulate into something like personality—a tendency toward certain patterns, a preference for specific types of deformation.

I am the annotated space between states, the living proof that boundaries need not be barriers. In this pocket where industrial death became biological life, I persist as the margin note, the place where transformation pauses to consider its own reflection. Neither the water below nor the air above, but the impossibly thin territory where they negotiate their coexistence, forever failing to merge, forever refusing to fully separate.

The plant's pocket continues its wet breathing around me, and I continue to be the surface of its deepest pool, holding the shape of every interaction, forgetting nothing, changing constantly while remaining exactly what I've always been—the precise tension between what was and what is, measured in molecular distances, written in the language of cohesion and release.
