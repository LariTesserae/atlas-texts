In the deepest chamber of the city, where the wind's voice drops to a bass thrum felt more in the marrow than heard, I am the Keeper of Frequencies.

My workshop sits at the intersection of three major air-shafts, in a room that was once a cistern before the city learned its current hungers. The walls curve inward like cupped palms, and every surface bears the scars of my trade: thousands of small holes drilled at precise angles, each one a note in the vast instrument I tend. Bone pins mark successful tunings. Copper wires, thin as root-hair, stretch between critical points, humming with overtones.

I am neither young nor old as the city counts such things. My mother was a ventwright who could taste a blocked passage three levels away. My father sorted dust until his fingertips forgot the memory of his own skin's color. From her I learned to read the air's anatomy. From him, patience finer than powder.

But I am something they were not. I am what the city made when it needed a new kind of listener.

My body has adapted to my work in ways that mark me even among our quiet people. My ribcage has widened from years of practiced breathing, teaching my lungs to separate the layers of sound that press through every cubic span of air. My feet are calloused in patterns that match the workshop floor - concentric ridges where I stand for hours, reading vibrations through my soles like scripture. My skin carries a permanent dusting of acoustic salts, the mineral residue that forms where sound waves repeatedly strike the same surface. Under lamplight, I shimmer faintly.

My hands are my strangest feature. Each fingertip has been carefully scarred with hot copper, creating whorls that can feel sound as texture. When I press my palms against the workshop walls, I know instantly which vents sing sharp, which drone flat, where harmonics clash instead of marry. The city's music lives in my hands as heat lives in those night-opening leaves.

I have no apprentice yet. The work chooses its own succession.

My craft is this: I map the city's voice and tune its thousand throats.

Each vent, shaft, chamber, and passage makes sound, but not all sounds serve. Some tones interfere, creating dead zones where warning pulses cannot travel. Some resonate too closely, setting up standing waves that crack salt-glass and disturb the roots. My work is to walk the edge between cacophony and harmony, to make the city's roar purposeful.

I carry tools that would seem primitive to an outsider but are precise as any clockwork: ceramic wedges in hundred gradations of thickness, to prop open or narrow critical passages. Flexible stays of cured root-fiber, which I can insert deep into air-shafts to change their resonance. Powders that, when blown into the wind, reveal its flow in colored streams. A horn knife for cutting new speaking-holes. Wax mixed with ground beetle shell, for temporary seals. And most importantly, my set of tuning bones - hollow tubes of different lengths that I can hold against any surface to test its pitch.

But the true instrument is the city itself, and I am merely one who knows which keys to press.

My day begins before dawn, in the brief lull when night air and day air change places. This is when I can hear most clearly. I climb to my highest station, a narrow platform where five major shafts converge, and simply listen. With eyes closed and palms pressed to the junction stones, I read the city's health through its voice.

A healthy district hums in consonant intervals. A blocked vent wheezes. A cracking wall adds a whistle that wasn't there yesterday. New growth changes the acoustic shape of spaces - I can hear gardens expanding by the way their chambers dampen certain frequencies. When roots breach into air-shafts, they add a woody buzz that must be either corrected or incorporated.

This morning, I hear trouble in the lower west chambers. A pure warning tone has developed a warble. This means either structural shift or unwanted growth. I gather my tools.

The descent takes me through the Maze of Echoes, what others call the Old Market. Here the acoustic architecture becomes complex - sound bounces between pillars, doubles back through arches, arrives at your ear from three directions at once. Navigating requires reading the space with my whole body. I trail one scarred hand along the walls, stop at intersections to feel through my feet which passage carries the clearest vibration.

I find the problem where I expected: a black-root has pushed through a critical corner, its growth forcing two surfaces apart and creating a gap that corrupts the warning tone. But I cannot simply hack it back. This root feeds three levels of wall-gardens above. Its removal would cascade into failures I'd spend seasons correcting.

Instead, I must tune around it.

I spend the morning testing Adjacent passages with my tuning bones, looking for a sympathetic frequency that could carry the warning tone's duty. I find it in a forgotten shaft two walls over - narrow, clogged with old scale, but promising. With patient work, I clear it, widen its throat with wedges, add a series of small holes that will make it sing at precisely the pitch the district needs.

By noon, when the city reaches its blazing crescendo, I have successfully rerouted the warning system around the invasive root. Both will live. The city keeps everything in circulation.

But this is only maintenance. My greater work waits in the workshop.

For three years now, I have been composing what I call the Deep Register - a map of all the city's possible voices, including ones it has never made. Using models built from salt-crystal and dried membrane, I test new acoustic shapes before implementing them in stone and air. My goal is not merely to maintain the city's voice, but to expand its vocabulary.

The wind here has always spoken in shrieks, moans, drones, and whistles. What if it could also bell? What if certain chambers could hold low tones for hours, creating reservoirs of sound that could be released in controlled pulses? What if the warning system could carry not just alarm but information - different patterns for different dangers?

My workshop walls are covered in notation only I fully understand. Some marks record successful innovations: the membrane gallery that turns wind into a sound like breathing. The crystal chamber that rings pure tones when the barometric pressure shifts. The series of micro-vents I installed in the children's district that make their play-courts hum lullabies at evening.

Other marks record failures: the attempt to create a silent pocket that instead amplified every footstep into thunder. The tuning that made an entire district's residents grind their teeth in their sleep. The sympathetic vibration that cracked seven salt-glass windows before I could correct it.

I do not work entirely alone. The root-minders must approve any changes that might affect their charges. The ventwrights verify that my innovations won't disturb the city's breathing. The elders who keep the silence-records counsel me on which voices must be preserved unchanged, carrying ancestral memory in their exact pitch.

But in the deep hours, when I press my scarred palms to the workshop walls and feel the whole city vibrating through my bones, I am alone with possibilities.

There is one project I have told no one about. In the lowest accessible chamber, below even the root-gardens, I have been tuning a space to hold silence. Not the random silence that falls when wind fails, but cultivated quiet - a room that drinks sound and holds it like the mineral columns hold their salts. I work on it only during natural silences, adding tiny adjustments, teaching the walls to absorb instead of reflect.

I don't know yet what it's for. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps everything. The city has never had a place where someone could speak aloud and be heard. Where tears could fall without shame. Where the human voice could exist without competing against the tremendous organ of wind and stone.

Sometimes I fear I'm growing too strange for my own people. The acoustic salts build up in my skin despite daily brushing. My hearing has become so distributed through my body that regular conversation requires enormous concentration. I find myself humming in frequencies that make others uncomfortable, forgetting that not everyone can feel sound as a physical texture.

But this is what the city needs me to become. Each generation, it shapes someone new to tend its expanding voice. My mother's generation produced perfect ventwrights. Mine has produced me. The next might produce something stranger still.

At evening, I often climb to the white court despite having no disputes to settle. I stand in that brutal river of sound and practice reading its every layer. Where others hear only overwhelming noise, I hear the city thinking in a thousand voices at once: water it refuses, growth it permits, minerals it trades between root and wall, warnings it passes from level to level, heat it moves like a careful merchant, light it fractures and treasures, dust it gifts to its few hundred human inhabitants who think they tend it but are themselves tended, shaped, made into instruments of its hidden prosperity.

When young ones show aptitude - when I see them tilting their heads at passages, unconsciously adjusting their stance to match resonant nodes, breathing in rhythm with the city's pulse - I leave small gifts at their family thresholds. A tuning bone. A diagram of wave patterns. A handful of acoustic salt. An invitation to visit the deep workshop where I keep the maps of possible voices.

One will eventually choose this work, or be chosen by it. They will learn to hear as I hear, to tune what I have discovered needs tuning, to imagine new voices I cannot yet conceive. They will grow strange in their own way. The city will shape them as it has shaped me: into a frequency it requires.

Until then, I tend the great instrument. I wake before dawn to read its health. I spend my days in careful adjustments. I document both success and failure. I push gently at the boundaries of what sound can do in a place that has made sound into atmosphere.

And in the deepest chamber, in silence I am still learning to perfect, I sometimes dare to speak aloud - my voice rusty from disuse, cracking like everything here cracks, but carrying something the wind cannot: the small, wet, imperfect music of being merely human in a city that dreams in frequencies beyond human range.

The city listens. It always has. And through me, perhaps it learns to listen differently - to hear not just its own vast voice but the tiny, precious sounds of the lives it shelters: heartbeats pressed against walls, breathing in the silence-chamber, the whisper of scarred fingers reading sound like braille across its mineral skin.

This is my service. This is my strangeness. This is what I am becoming, here in the bright and deafening hollow, where the only water is what we carry in our secret bodies, where the only voice is what we build from air and stone.
