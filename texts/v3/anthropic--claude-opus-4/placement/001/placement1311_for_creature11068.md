# The Tender of Small Incubations

Four blocks north of the Sweatworks, in a cellar that was once a coal bin, there is a workshop lit by grow lights. The lights run on timers—fourteen hours on, ten hours off—and beneath them, on shelves made from salvaged fire escapes, sit hundreds of glass vessels. Mason jars, mostly, though there are pickle jars and baby food jars and one beautiful apothecary bottle the color of honey. Each contains water, and in the water, something growing.

The workshop belongs to Vera, though "belongs" suggests a formality that doesn't exist here. She found the space, she cleaned it, she ran the extension cords down from the bakery above (with permission, eventually), and now she comes here every day to tend her specimens. The baker calls her "the mushroom lady," which is wrong but not worth correcting. What she grows aren't mushrooms. They're not exactly anything.

Start with what they look like: cloudy masses, some like torn silk, some like smoke frozen mid-curl, some with the structure of coral or the fronds of something that should be green but isn't. They're all pale—white to grey to the faintest pearl—and they all move, though so slowly you have to mark their positions with tape on the glass to be sure. They don't need much. Warm water, changed weekly. A pinch of salt. The particular spectrum of the grow lights, which Vera discovered through trial and error makes them proliferate in ways sunlight doesn't. Time.

She found the first one in a jar her grandmother left in the basement, forgotten for who knows how long. Just water and some pale threads that might have been mold but weren't, suspended like a thought interrupted. She almost threw it out. But something about the way it hung there, patient and complete, made her look closer. When she held it to the light, the threads cast shadows that didn't match their shapes—too many angles, too much depth. She kept the jar. She watched it for a month before she saw it grow.

Now she has hundreds, and no two are quite the same. She's learned their preferences: this one likes a copper penny in its jar, that one grows best with a drop of her spit added monthly, another responds to vibration and sends out new fronds when she hums near it. She keeps meticulous notes in composition books, sketching growth patterns, recording water temperatures, documenting the slow vocabularies of their changes. She's developed a notation system that's part chemistry, part music, part her own invention. No one else could read it.

The things—she just calls them "the things"—don't seem to be related to any known organism. She's taken samples to the university, to a mycologist friend, to anyone who will look. They shrug. Under a microscope, the tissue doesn't resolve into cells exactly, more like a crystalline network that's somehow organic, somehow liquid, somehow solid all at once. They might be a kind of slime mold. They might be precipitates of something in the water. They might be, one professor suggested unhelpfully, "suburban folklore expressing itself through kitchen chemistry." Vera doesn't bring samples anymore.

What she knows: they're old. Not the specimens—the oldest she's been growing for three years—but the... pattern of them. The type. She feels it when she handles the jars, a sense of deep time that has nothing to do with their actual age. Like they're ambassadors from some much older process, something that's been happening in forgotten water for longer than anyone's been watching. She wonders sometimes if every glass of water left alone long enough would eventually grow one, if the whole world is seeded with this potential and just waiting for the right conditions. The right patience.

Her favorite is in the apothecary bottle. It has a form like a spiral staircase collapsing in slow motion, and at certain angles the light through it makes her dizzy in a pleasant way, like standing up too fast but without the darkness. She's had offers to buy it—there's apparently a market for "cryptid specimens" online—but she would no more sell it than sell her left hand. Less. A hand she could theoretically do without.

The workshop has its own climate now, warm and close from the lights, the air thick with the particular smell of things growing in water—not unpleasant, just specific, like a greenhouse for ghosts. Vera has a stool and a hotplate and a radio that only gets one station clearly. She spends hours here, changing water, adjusting positions, making notes. She's begun to see patterns: certain forms recurring across different jars, growth rates that sync with moon phases, a tendency for the things to orient themselves toward each other when placed close together, like very slow magnets.

She thinks, but cannot prove, that they're communicating. Not with her—she's just the keeper, the one who changes the water. With each other, across the shelves, through the glass. Building something or becoming something or just acknowledging something in their glacial way. The thought doesn't disturb her. She likes being incidental to their purpose, whatever it is.

She's 54, lives alone, works part-time at a frame shop. She has no family she speaks to, few friends, no social media presence. She is exquisitely happy. Not the happiness of achievement or recognition, but the happiness of someone who has found exactly the right sized mystery to tend. Small enough to fit in jars, large enough to suggest infinite depth. She could do this forever, she thinks. Watch them grow and change and be whatever they are, her attention neither crucial nor unwelcome, just present.

Sometimes she brings a jar home and sleeps with it on her nightstand, watching the pale form inside drift and curl in its water world. In the morning she always brings it back. They do better together, she's noticed. Even the ones that seem solitary grow more vigorously when they're among their kind.

She's made provisions. If something happens to her, the baker upstairs has instructions and a key. He's promised to at least keep the lights running until he can find someone else to take over. She's written down what she knows in a letter, sealed it, left it on the shelf by the door. "Feed them salt and patience," it begins. "They're older than they look."

The things continue growing, slow as thought, patient as salt. Vera continues tending them. In the warm light of the borrowed basement, surrounded by glass and secrets, she sometimes feels she's found the exact point where mystery becomes devotion. It's enough. It's more than enough. It's just right.
