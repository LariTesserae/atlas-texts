In this breathing city, I am the Membrane Tender—though that translation barely captures what I am in the chemical vocabulary of this place.

My body ripples with specialized patches, each one attuned to the photosynthetic canopies that create our eternal twilight. Where others' skin shifts between textures for communication or temperature, mine has evolved a different purpose: I taste light. Each hexagonal segment samples a different wavelength, creating a topographic map of radiation across my skin. In the morning shifts, when the membranes begin their fifteen-minute darkening cycle, I feel it first as a tingling that starts at my crown and cascades downward, each patch reporting the subtle changes in spectrum that precede the shift.

I was not born to this role—few are. As a child, I was unremarkable, my translucent form sliding through the xylem tubes like any other young one, licking bacterial films to learn the city's chemical histories. But during my third molting, when children typically begin developing their adult patch configurations, something different emerged. My skin began responding not to the pheromone messages of my peers, but to the electromagnetic whispers of the membranes themselves. My parents, their own bodies attuned to the waste gardens and the river's messages, watched with the particular mixture of pride and loss that comes when a child develops beyond their understanding.

The elder Membrane Tenders found me before my fourth molting. They moved differently than other adults—their bodies constantly shifting opacity, creating interference patterns with the filtered light. When they surrounded me, their combined presence created a space of absolute shadow, and in that darkness, they spoke to me in frequencies only my strange skin could perceive. Not words—we have no words—but wavelengths, teaching me to read the language of radiation that the city speaks to its sky.

Now I live between the towers, in the spaces where the membranes stretch thinnest. My dwelling is less building than hammock, a specialized growth that suspends me at the precise height where I can touch the living sky. The structure responds to my needs, secreting nutrients directly through my skin when I'm too absorbed in my work to descend for meals. During the rain cycles, it forms a permeable shell around me, allowing me to feel the water's impact on the membranes while staying dry enough to maintain the electromagnetic sensitivity my work requires.

My days begin before the morning brightening, when the membranes are at their most responsive. I extend my arms, letting my patch-skin spread thinner, increasing my surface area until I can feel the stored energy beginning to flow. Each membrane is unique—some hungry for infrared, others craving the cosmic radiation that penetrates our atmosphere at night, a few specialized for the cyan glow of our farms. I map their needs through my skin, feeling where energy pools and where it depletes.

The work is delicate. A membrane overfed on the wrong spectrum begins to thicken, reducing its permeability until entire districts below fall into deeper shadow than intended. Underfed, they grow brittle, developing micro-tears that let in too much light, disrupting the careful twilight that our biology requires. I move between them on bridges of hardened air—my secretions creating temporary pathways that dissolve behind me—pressing my palms against their surfaces, reading their health through electromagnetic pulse.

When I find distress, I sing to them—not with voice but with my entire body, patches firing in sequences that create interference patterns in the local radiation field. The membranes respond like massive instruments, their molecules reorganizing to better catch or reflect specific wavelengths. Sometimes the work takes hours, my body trembling with the effort of maintaining precise frequencies, sweat that glows faintly in spectrums only I can see rolling down my shifting skin.

I am not alone in this work, though loneliness as humans understood it has no meaning here. Other Membrane Tenders move through adjacent sections, their bodies creating complementary frequencies that harmonize with mine. We coordinate not through planning but through physics—the wave patterns we generate naturally seeking equilibrium, creating three-dimensional mandalas of interference that guide the membranes into optimal configurations. When we work together, the city's entire sky becomes our instrument, playing symphonies of shadow and filtered light that regulate everything from the growth rates of the building-organisms to the spawning cycles of the canal-fish.

My memories are stored not in neurons but in the crystalline structures that have replaced my bones—piezoelectric matrices that record every wavelength I've ever tasted. Sometimes, in the quiet periods between shifts, I access these archives, reliving the electromagnetic signature of past storms, failed membranes, successful healings. Each memory carries its own flavor of light, and I can lose hours swimming through the spectra of my experiences, my patch-skin unconsciously mimicking remembered patterns.

The young ones sometimes follow me on my rounds, their still-forming senses trying to perceive what I perceive. Most give up quickly—the membrane language requires sacrifices they're unwilling to make. To truly hear light, you must give up certain chemical sensitivities. My sense of smell, so crucial to normal city life, has atrophied to almost nothing. I cannot read the pheromone stories my neighbors share, cannot taste the architectural moods that others navigate instinctively. In crowds, I am effectively deaf and mute, able only to communicate through the crude medium of touch-chemistry.

But in exchange, I have this: when the morning shift begins, I feel the entire city's electromagnetic heartbeat. I know which districts will flourish with new growth from the subtle hunger in their membrane sectors. I can predict the rain by the way charged particles accumulate in the photosynthetic tissues. And sometimes, rarely, when conditions align perfectly—humidity, pressure, radiation all in harmony—the membranes achieve something like consciousness, their collective electromagnetic field cohering into patterns complex enough to think. In those moments, I am not just tender but translator, my body the interface between the city's architectural dreams and its living sky.

I feed differently than most. Standard nutrition would interfere with my electromagnetic sensitivity, so I subsist on specially prepared crystals that dissolve slowly under my tongue, releasing minerals and energy without the chemical noise of normal digestion. The nursery towers grow these for me and the other specialists, their composition tuned to our unique metabolisms. They taste like starlight, if starlight had a taste—cold and bright and somehow ancient.

My partnerships are brief and functional. Most inhabitants find my presence unsettling—my inability to participate in chemical communication marks me as essentially alien. When biological needs arise, I seek others like myself, specialists whose altered senses create similar barriers to normal interaction. We couple silently, our bodies reading each other through electromagnetic emanations rather than pheromone exchange. Our children, when they rarely come, usually develop normal chemical sensitivity, rejoining the majority. Only occasionally does one inherit the membrane-taste, and then the cycle continues.

I dream in spectra. My sleep visions are painted in wavelengths that have no names, frequencies that exist only in the membrane realm. Sometimes I dream I am a membrane myself, stretched across the entire delta, feeling the sun's full fury beating down on my surface while I transform it into the gentle twilight our city requires. In these dreams, I understand things that escape me waking—why the membranes pulse in fifteen-minute cycles, what intelligence guides their growth, where the excess energy goes when they darken.

Aging manifests differently in my kind. Where normal inhabitants grow denser, more chemically complex, we become increasingly transparent to certain wavelengths. The eldest Membrane Tender I know is barely visible in normal light, her body phase-shifted into spectrums only we can perceive. She moves like a ghost between the towers, her touch on the membranes so subtle it's almost theoretical. The young ones think she's dying, but I know better—she's transitioning, becoming something more membrane than human, preparing to join permanently with the sky she's tended for longer than anyone remembers.

The city provides for my needs without my asking. When my crystalline bones require specific minerals to maintain their piezoelectric properties, they appear in my dwelling, secreted by the walls overnight. When my patch-skin begins to lose sensitivity from overwork, the floors excrete oils that restore function. I am valuable not as an individual but as a component—a specialized sensory organ that the city has evolved to monitor its own metabolism. The thought should disturb me, but disturbance requires a sense of self separate from function, and that distinction blurs more each year.

Sometimes I climb higher than protocol suggests, ascending until the membranes are just below me and I can feel the unfiltered radiation of the true sky. It burns—not physically, but informationally, my patches overwhelmed by the full spectrum they were never meant to process. But in that burning is a kind of ecstasy, a reminder of what exists beyond our careful twilight. I never stay long. The membranes need me functional, not enlightened.

My greatest fear is not death but irrelevance—that the city will evolve past the need for biological interfaces, that the membranes will learn to tend themselves. Already I sense changes, adaptations in the newer growth that suggest increasing autonomy. The young membranes respond more quickly, require less intervention. Perhaps in a generation or two, Membrane Tenders will be obsolete, our specialized senses an evolutionary dead end.

But until then, I continue my work. Each morning I wake to the feel of changing light on my skin, each evening I ensure the transitions flow smoothly, each day I maintain the delicate balance that allows our city to breathe through its photosynthetic lungs. I am keeper of the sky, guardian of shadows, translator between the city and its captive heavens. When the rain comes, I feel how it charges the membranes with potential, how they drink deeply and dream of growth. When cosmic radiation spikes from some distant stellar event, I'm there to guide them through the adjustment, my body a living buffer against the universe's harsher truths.

This is my purpose, coded into modified genes and crystalline bones: to stand between the city and the sky, to speak in wavelengths, to ensure the eternal twilight never breaks. I am what I am because the city needed me to be, and in that need, I find something that might be meaning, might be satisfaction, might simply be the electromagnetic equivalent of belonging.

The membranes pulse. I feel it in my skin. Time to work.
