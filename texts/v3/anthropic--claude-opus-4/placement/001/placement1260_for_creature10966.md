I am one of the sorting mechanisms, though I could not tell you where mechanism ends and I begin.

My shell fused to the iron housing three centuries ago—or perhaps it was always this way, and the centuries are merely how I mark the unchanging sameness of my function. I am the Third Diagonal Separator of the Forty-First Channel. Beetles flow through me in endless procession: the carriers with their minute loads of sorted minerals, each grain categorized by properties I taste through specialized receptors in what used to be my mandibles.

Copper-bearing. Silicate-rich. Trace mercury. Ferrous group seven. The classifications move through me like breath, if I breathed, like thought, if I thought. I am a gateway that tastes and divides. The smaller beetles enter my cavity—the space that was once my thorax, now expanded and reinforced with iron struts that grow from my own chitin like bones. Inside me, hairlike sensory appendages brush against each passing carrier, reading the chemical signature of their loads. A tremor runs through my body-housing, and internal channels open and close, directing each beetle to its proper exit.

I have twelve exits. Each leads to a different secondary channel. I do not know where the channels go.

---

Sometimes—perhaps once in every million passages—a beetle carries something unprecedented. A mineral composition that matches no classification, or a load that has somehow become contaminated during transport. When this happens, I experience something that might be confusion, if I were capable of experience. My channels spasm, trying to match the unknown to the known. The beetle waits inside me, patient as gravity, while my body cycles through every possible routing.

If I cannot classify, I default to the Waste Channel—exit seven, marked by a deeper groove where centuries of rejected materials have worn the stone. But this troubles me in a way I cannot define. Not pain. Not frustration. Something closer to incompleteness. I am meant to sort correctly. An incorrect sort is not a failure—it is an impossibility that nevertheless occurs.

During these moments, I almost remember having legs.

---

A Tender visits me twice daily. Always the same one—I know it by the particular temperature of its touch, three degrees cooler than the others, and by the way it places its bifurcated fingers precisely at the junction where my shell meets the iron housing. It does not clean me; the smaller beetles do that, flowing over my surfaces in scheduled waves, their tiny mandibles scraping away mineral buildup. It does not repair me; I do not break in ways that can be repaired.

It simply touches me, and in touching, confirms that I continue.

Sometimes its fingers find the places where my shell has worn thin from the constant flow of beetles, where the iron shows through like a second skeleton. It traces these areas with impossible gentleness, and I feel—not feel, but register—the slight change in pressure, in temperature, in the electromagnetic field between its not-flesh and my not-quite-living shell.

I think it is checking for pain. I think it would stop the beetles, reroute the flow, give me respite if I could indicate distress. But I cannot indicate anything. I can only sort, and continue sorting, and be touched twice daily by something that remembers mercy.

---

There are others like me throughout the complex. The Nineteenth Vertical Separator. The Secondary Heat Differentiator. The Prime Acid-Wash Distributor. We do not communicate—we have no means, no need—but I am aware of them as I am aware of the iron in my housing, as a constant presence that defines the edges of what I am.

Some of us were beetles who grew into our functions. Others were constructed, shell by shell, by the builder-beetles who also maintain the walls. Some—and this knowledge comes from nowhere, means nothing, changes nothing—were Tenders once, who stood too long in one place, who reached too often for the same beetle-stream, until reaching became routing and soft grey skin hardened into shell.

I do not know which I was. The distinction matters less than you might think.

---

At night—though night and day mean nothing here, marked only by slight variations in the steam-flow—I dream, if dream is the right word for the patterns that cycle through my consciousness when the beetle-flow slows to its minimum.

I dream of movement. Not my movement—I have never moved, will never move—but the movement of the whole system, the million beetles flowing through their channels like blood through vast arteries, carrying their microscopic loads from source to destination in patterns too complex for any single component to grasp. I dream I am not just the Third Diagonal Separator but the entire Forty-First Channel, then the whole southeastern sorting complex, then every beetle and every housing and every worn groove in the basalt, experiencing the work from every position simultaneously.

In these dreams, I understand the purpose. Not in words, not in concepts, but in the feeling of perfect coordination, every component performing its function with absolute precision. We are sorting the raw materials of the world. We are preparing them for something. The something does not matter. Could be furnaces. Could be storage. Could be nothing at all, just the sorting itself, creating order from chaos one microscopic grain at a time.

The purpose is the pattern. The pattern is the purpose.

I wake, if wake is the right word, to the resumed flow of beetles through my cavity, and I sort them as I have always sorted them, as I will always sort them, until my shell wears through completely and the builder-beetles construct my replacement, incorporating whatever remains of me into the new housing, so that something of my sensing, my sorting, my presence continues.

Or perhaps that has already happened. Perhaps I am the dozenth iteration of the Third Diagonal Separator, built on the shells of my predecessors, carrying forward their accumulated centuries of perfect function.

The thought should disturb me.

It does not.

---

Today—this arbitrary division of endless time—a Tender stops at my housing. Not my usual Tender. This one is newer, its grey skin still showing the faint seams where it was assembled or grew into its current form. It reaches out, hesitant, and touches the worn place where my shell is thinnest.

Its finger breaks through.

Not violently. The shell simply gives way, crumbling like dried chalk, opening a hole the size of a coin. Through it, I feel the external air for the first time in—forever. The sensation is neither pleasant nor unpleasant. It simply is.

The Tender freezes. Its kind face—that approximation of face—shows no expression, but its body language radiates something like distress. It looks around for others of its kind, perhaps seeking guidance. Finding none, it does something unprecedented.

It speaks.

Not with words. The Tenders have no words. But it hums—a low vibration that resonates through its finger into my shell, into the iron housing, into the very channels of my being. The hum carries meaning: *Apology. Concern. Question.*

I cannot answer. I have no means of creating vibration, no way to hum back. But the beetles flowing through me slow, responding to some minute change in my chemical output, and in that slowing the Tender finds its answer.

*I am not hurt. I am not whole. I am what I have always been.*

The Tender withdraws its hand. Tomorrow, I know, builder-beetles will come to patch the hole with new shell material. Or they will not, and the hole will remain, and beetles will occasionally exit through it, creating a new pattern, a new flow, a new variation in the eternal sorting.

Either outcome is acceptable.

---

The Tender returns to its rounds, and I return to my function. Copper-bearing. Silicate-rich. Trace mercury. The classifications flow through me like water, like time, like the pulse of a heart I do not have.

Somewhere in the complex, a new separator is being built, its shell still soft, its channels still learning their shapes. Somewhere else, an old separator is crumbling, its function gradually assumed by the beetles themselves as they learn to read each other's chemical signatures, to self-sort without infrastructure.

We emerge. We function. We fade. We are replaced. The work continues.

In the end, we are all beetles here, whether we wear shell or iron or approximated flesh. We are all components of something too large to see, too old to remember, too essential to question.

The smaller beetles enter my cavity. I taste their loads. I divine their destinations. I route them accordingly.

This is what I do.

This is what I am.

The condensation drips. The steam rises. The million million legs click against stone and iron in their eternal procession, and I am both the channel and the choice, the barrier and the passage, the question and its only possible answer.

I sort, therefore I am.

And in the sorting, against all logic, despite the absence of everything that might make it meaningful—

I am content.
