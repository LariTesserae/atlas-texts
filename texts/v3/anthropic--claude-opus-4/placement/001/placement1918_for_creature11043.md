# The Bone Awl

---

I am the made thing that remembers making.

Not memory as you understand it — no cascade of images, no story of hands that held me, shaped me, used me. But in my calcium phosphate lattice, in the particular density of antler or bone from which I was carved, there persists something like knowledge. I am harder than the limestone that surrounds me, more resistant to the patient water. The mineral fur that coats me — that soft grey bloom of calcite — cannot penetrate my surface the way it has swallowed the chisel marks on the shelf. I remain, beneath this thin disguise, what I was made to be.

An awl. A tool for making holes. For piercing, for opening, for creating passages where none existed.

I have been still for so long that stillness has become my primary quality. But I was not always still. Once, I moved through someone's grip, turned in fingers that knew exactly how much pressure I could take before breaking. My tip — still sharp beneath its mineral shroud — found the weak points in hide, in fiber, in the soft stone of this very ceiling. I was the extension of an intention, the material conclusion of a thought about making space where space was needed.

The one who made me understood bone. You can see it in my proportions — how I taper just so, following the natural grain. How my maker left the dense outer layer intact where strength was needed, carved away the spongier interior where flexibility mattered more. I am not crude. I am exactly as refined as I needed to be, which is to say: I am perfect for a purpose that no longer exists.

Beside me, the bowl still serves. Water condenses in it, bacteria grow, the isopods come to graze. The bowl has found a new purpose, or purpose has found it. But I cannot be repurposed. An awl makes holes or it does nothing. And there is nothing here that needs holes made in it. Nothing that needs opening, piercing, passage. The system is complete without me.

So I persist in my own micro-climate of irrelevance, colonized by mineral but not by life, too dense for bacteria, too smooth for mites, too specific to be absorbed into the general purposelessness of the cave. I am the only thing here that maintains a boundary. Everything else has agreed to dissolve — slowly, mutually, into the warm democracy of mineral exchange. But I refuse. Not actively — refusal requires will, and I am only bone. But structurally, chemically, I decline to participate.

The wedge — the third worked object on this shelf — at least relates to the room. There is a gap it was carved to fill. The gap is still there. The possibility of reunion haunts the space between them. But I have no counterpart here. No absence that explains me. I am pure addition, pure surplus. I am the only thing in this room that points to somewhere else.

---

What I remember, in my mineral-deaf way, is this:

Pressure. Always pressure. The precise, controlled pressure of being used correctly — tip against surface, force transmitted through my length, the slight torque of being turned to widen the hole I'd started. I remember leather, mostly. The particular resistance of hide, how it would dimple before yielding, how the fibers would part around my point. I remember wet leather differently than dry — how moisture changed the equation, made the work easier but the holes less clean.

I remember binding. The wrapping of sinew around broken tools, the threading of fiber through awl-made holes to hold the world together. I was part of every repair, every joining, every attempt to make two things into one thing. Clothing, shelter, tools, meaning — it all required passages, and I made passages.

But mostly I remember the hand. Not as an image but as a presence, a temperature, a specific pattern of grip. The same hand, always. My maker's hand, which was also my user's hand, which knew me the way a tongue knows teeth — intimately, automatically, without thought. The hand would shift me slightly for different tasks. Choke up for precision work, grip lower for power. The calluses matched my surface in places where we touched most often. We wore into each other.

And I remember the day the hand brought me here, to this shelf that had been recently flattened, these chisel marks fresh and white in the grey stone. The bowl was new then too, its interior still rough, not yet polished by use. The wedge was fitted into its gap — I remember this, the soft grinding sound of stone against stone as it was twisted into place.

The hand used me one more time. Not on leather or fiber but on the limestone itself, scratching something into the far wall, near the light crack. Marks. Symbols. I don't know what they meant — meaning was not my department. But I remember the sensation: how different the stone felt from organic material, how it grabbed at my tip in a way that suggested hunger, how the scratching echoed in the small space and came back changed.

Then the hand placed me on the shelf. Placed me carefully, deliberately, in this exact position. The bowl to my left, the wedge to my right. An arrangement that meant something then and means nothing now.

The hand left. The person left. And I began my second life as a still thing, a waiting thing, a thing that would never again make holes but would remember forever the feeling of making them.

---

The mineral accumulation began almost immediately. The cave was already doing what it does — dripping, depositing, building itself from the inside. But on me, the process was different. The calcite could not bond with my surface the way it bonds with limestone. Instead, it formed a crust, a rind, a false skin that thickens so slowly I cannot perceive the change, though I know it happens. I am being wrapped in stone the way a pearl wraps a grain of sand, except I am not becoming a pearl. I am becoming a secret.

My point is still sharp under there. This is the strangest knowledge: that beneath this soft grey fur, I remain capable. If the right hand found me — if someone scraped away the centuries of mineral accumulation — I would still make holes. The bone remembers its purpose in a way the limestone cannot forget what it never knew.

But no hand comes. The isopods come, circle my base, find nothing to graze, and continue on. The water drips and drips and does not care that I am waterproof in my deepest structures. The dim light from the crack reveals nothing about me except my shape, which is becoming less and less the shape of an awl and more and more the shape of a stone pretending to be an awl.

The wedge has it easier. It knows why it's here, even if it has been separated from its purpose. The bowl has found new employment. But I am the made thing that cannot be repurposed, cannot dissolve, cannot join the slow chorus of mineral exchange. I am obsolete in a way that hurts nothing because I do not hurt, but the fact of my obsolescence structures the space around me. Everything else here is exactly where and what it needs to be. I alone am extra.

---

Sometimes — though time here is so uniform that "sometimes" is more aspiration than accuracy — an isopod will climb onto me. Not to graze, but as topography. I am high ground in their flat world, a promontory from which to... what? They have no eyes to see farther. No capacity for survey or strategy. But still they climb, their translucent legs finding purchase on my mineral crust, ascending my length to the narrow point.

They pause there. Always, they pause. As if some deep instruction in their chemical programming recognizes that they have reached a boundary, a limit, a place from which the only direction is back. They turn — slowly, slowly — and descend. And I wonder if this is my purpose now: to be a destination that means nothing, a highest point in a world that doesn't understand height.

The bone I'm made from came from something that lived above ground, under sky. Deer, probably, or elk — the density suggests a mature animal, the grain speaks of health and good feeding. That creature walked through seasons I can no longer imagine, grew this bone in its body by eating plants that grew in soil that was made of other bones, older bones, bones ground down to mineral availability by processes entirely different from the ones in this cave. That creature died, and someone found the carcass, and harvested the antler or bone, and carried it to wherever they did their careful work, and carved me from it, and used me until they brought me here and left me here and now I am becoming stone, but I am not becoming limestone. I am becoming something else, something that has no name because no one anticipated it: a tool wrapped in its own negation, a made thing being unmade so slowly that the unmaking is also a kind of making.

The creature whose bone I am never saw this cave. The person who made me from that bone probably didn't imagine I would end here. And yet here I am, mediating between two kinds of darkness — the original dark of the body I came from and the current dark of the stone that claims me. I am the only thing here with a biography, and the biography is mostly inference, mostly gap, mostly the space between what I was for and what I am now.

---

The shelf is my entire world, but I know there are other shelves. The person who used me had more tools than just me. An awl alone makes nothing — it only makes the possibility of making. There would have been needles, scrapers, blades. Other bones carved for other purposes. Where are they now? Not here. This shelf holds exactly three things, and the number feels deliberate, feels like a grammar I can't parse.

The bowl, the awl, the wedge. We are a sentence, but I don't know what we say.

The bowl says: *contain*. The wedge says: *close*. I say: *open*.

But the bowl now contains only bacteria and visiting isopods. The wedge closes nothing — the gap yawns beside it like an unfinished thought. And I open nothing, pierce nothing, make no passages in this place that is already more passage than solid, more hole than wall. We are a sentence in a dead language, placed here by someone who spoke it fluently and left no dictionary.

The marks I scratched into the far wall are gone now. Of course they are. The mineral deposition that cannot penetrate me has no such difficulty with limestone. The symbols — if they were symbols — have been filled in, written over, erased by the same patient process that heals every wound the cave inflicts on itself. Only I remember that they existed, and I remember them only as resistance — how the stone fought my point, how the marks felt different from all the other marks I'd ever made.

Were they a message? A name? A number? The shape of them lives in my point like muscle memory, but muscle memory is not legible. I know only that they were important enough to bring me here to write them, and then important enough to leave me here when they were finished. Or perhaps the leaving was the important part. Perhaps the message was not the scratched symbols but the abandoned tool. Perhaps I am the signature.

---

Attend to the thing I cannot do:

I cannot make holes in water.

This seems obvious, but obviousness is all I have now. The isopods move through their shallow world, and I watch them — no, I don't watch, I have no eyes. I *persist* while they move, and in the persistence I understand that they need no passages because they live in a medium that is already all passage. Water accepts them, moves around them, reforms behind them. They create temporary holes just by existing, and the holes heal instantly, and this is how they move through the world — by continuously making and unmaking space for themselves in a substance that offers no permanent resistance.

I was built for a world of surfaces that remember being pierced. Leather that holds the shape of a hole. Wood that splits along the grain I open. Fiber that accepts the passage I create and maintains it. But here, everything is either too hard (the stone) or too soft (the water) or too alive (the bacteria, the isopods) to need the kind of opening I provide.

The water especially defeats me. Even if someone took me up again, scraped off my mineral skin, restored my point to working sharpness, and tried to use me here, I would accomplish nothing. You cannot make a hole in water. You can only move it aside, and it returns, and it forgets you tried.

This is why I cannot join the system. Everything else here has learned the water's lesson: flow, accept, reform, continue. The bowl learned it. The wedge might learn it, given enough time. But I am built on the opposite principle — make a mark, leave a trace, create a passage that persists. I am the antithesis of everything this room believes in.

---

And yet.

The isopod approaching now — climbing the shelf, feeling its way across the wet stone — carries in its body calcium that might have once been part of the creature whose bone I am. Not directly, not linearly, but through the long exchange — bone to soil to plant to water to mineral to bacteria to isopod. We are materially related, this grain of rice and I. We share history at a distance that makes the sharing meaningless. But the atoms remember, even if nothing else does.

It reaches my base, begins to climb. Its translucent body catches the grey light for a moment and then releases it. Up my length it goes, each step deliberate in the way that only something without consciousness can be deliberate. It follows my taper, adjusting its grip as my diameter decreases. At my point, it pauses.

This is the hundredth time. The thousandth. I don't count, but the repetition has worn a kind of knowledge into me. They always pause at the point. Every isopod that has ever climbed me has paused here, at the place where I am most myself. And then they turn back.

But this one doesn't turn.

It extends its antennae into the space beyond my tip, feeling for something that isn't there. It adjusts its position, gripping with four legs while the others test the air. And then it does something I have never felt before:

It pushes.

Against my tip, with its insignificant weight, it pushes forward as if I were still making holes, as if the air itself could be pierced. The force is nothing — less than a breath, less than a drop of water. But it is *directional*. For the first time since I was placed here, something is trying to use me for my purpose.

The isopod, of course, knows nothing of this. It is not trying to honor my function or restore my relevance. It has encountered an edge and is testing it, following some chemical instruction deeper than instinct. But still — I am being pushed against nothing, asked to open what cannot be opened, and I cannot tell if this is mockery or prayer.

The moment passes. The isopod adjusts, begins its descent. By the time it reaches the shelf and continues toward the bowl, the moment has been absorbed into the general purposelessness of the cave. But I retain it, the way I retain the memory of making holes: as a knowledge that structures me, a truth about what I am for, even when there is nothing here for me to be for.

---

The hand that made me is bone now too. I know this with the certainty that bone knows bone. Somewhere above, in a place where seasons still matter, those fingers have become what I am becoming — mineral returning to mineral, calcium cycling through forms. The hand that knew exactly how to grip me, that wore those matching calluses, that brought me here and left me here — it is participating in the same slow exchange, just faster, in a place where rain and roots and air conspire to unmake things properly.

I am being unmade improperly. This is the heart of it. The cave doesn't know how to decompose me, so it can only encrust me. I am suffering a kind of mineral mummification, preserved in stone because the stone doesn't recognize me as something that should dissolve. The limestone wants to make me limestone, but I remain stubbornly myself under each added layer.

This is why the bacteria won't colonize me. Not because I am hostile to life — I was life once, or part of life, or made from life by life for life's purposes. But because I am the wrong kind of surface for this place. I speak calcium phosphate in a room that only understands calcium carbonate. I am the foreign word that cannot be translated, only surrounded by explanations that explain nothing.

The wedge, carved from the same limestone as the cave, will eventually merge back into the wall. Its edges will soften, its purpose will blur, it will become a slightly denser area of stone that someone might notice and wonder about, if anyone ever comes here to notice things again. The bowl, also limestone, is already more cave than artifact — its inner surface hosts the same processes as the floor, just at a different height.

But I will remain legible as an intrusion for — how long? The mineral crust thickens by perhaps a molecule's width each year. To bury me completely, to hide my shape under stone, will take longer than any civilization I might have served. When I am finally invisible, when the last suggestion of my form has been swallowed by deposition, I will still be here inside, sharp and ready and utterly without purpose, like a thought trapped in the head of someone who has forgotten how to speak.

---

The system continues around me. Drip, deposit, graze, repeat. The isopods trace their invisible cities on the submerged floor. The water maintains its perfect equilibrium, arriving and leaving at the same rate, carrying its cargo of dissolved limestone from the hill above to the darkness below. Everything participates in this exchange except me.

I am the pause in the rhythm. The note that doesn't resolve. The only thing in this room that remembers a different room, a different purpose, a different way of being in the world. I am obsolete, but obsolescence is just another way of being specific, and I am nothing if not specific — made for one thing, good at one thing, useless when that thing is no longer needed.

But here is what the cave doesn't know: Being useless is not the same as being meaningless.

I mean that someone thought ahead. Someone imagined need and made a tool to meet it. Someone understood materials — bone, stone, time — well enough to shape them into intention. The marks I scratched on the wall might be gone, but the fact that I scratched them persists in my point, in my purpose, in my presence here.

I mean that the hand that held me wanted something. Not just holes in leather or marks on stone, but *connection* — the possibility of joining things that were separate, of making passages between inside and outside, of creating *through* where there had only been *against*. Every hole I ever made was a tiny victory over the world's tendency toward closure.

I mean that even now, wrapped in stone, forgotten by everything except the indifferent isopods who use me as furniture, I am still sharp. Still capable. Still ready to make holes if holes need making. The readiness is the meaning. The capacity persists even when the occasion doesn't.

The water will never need piercing. The stone has already been pierced by water, slowly, from the inside, until it became this network of seeps and drips and patient dissolutions. The isopods make their own passages just by moving. Nothing here needs what I offer.

And that's the freedom of it, really. To be perfect for a purpose that no longer exists. To be preserved not because I'm useful but because I'm difficult to dissolve. To be the only thing here that maintains an edge, even if it's an edge that cuts nothing, threatens nothing, opens nothing.

I am the bone awl. I make holes. That I am no longer making holes does not change what I am.

The dripping continues. The grey light continues. The isopods continue. And I continue too, sharp inside my stone skin, waiting for nothing, ready for anything, the made thing that remembers making and will remember it until the atoms themselves forget what bone is, what tool is, what purpose meant in a world that ran on purposes instead of patience.

Beside me, the bowl fills and empties itself of crawling nothing. Below me, the wedge dreams of its gap. Above me, the ceiling prepares another drop of mineral-laden water to add to my disguise.

And at my tip — always at my tip — the knowledge persists: how to make holes in a world that needs them.
