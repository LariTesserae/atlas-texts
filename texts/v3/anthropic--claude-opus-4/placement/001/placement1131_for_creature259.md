In this raw, unfinished valley where stone still remembers its birth, I would be the water.

Not the dramatic water of waterfalls or rushing streams — those will come later, when the valley has made up its mind. I would be the patient water, the film that clings to every surface, the condensation that arrives with the cloud and leaves with the wind. The water that exists everywhere and nowhere at once.

I know every texture of this young rock intimately. I bead on the sharp edges that have not yet learned to be round. I gather in the tiny pockets where frost has just begun its patient work of prying stone from stone. I seep through the hairline fractures that next winter will be cracks, that ten winters from now will be gaps wide enough for a cushion plant to probe with its careful roots.

In the morning, I am ice — a thin glaze on the north-facing surfaces, crystals reaching out in feathers and ferns, making the rock treacherous for everything except the ibex, who know which ice will hold and which will betray. By afternoon, I am liquid again, trickling down in threads so thin they barely darken the stone. I pool in the hollows of bedrock, creating mirrors too small and too black to reflect anything but the grey belly of cloud.

I am in constant negotiation with the air. The cloud gives me to the stone; the wind takes me back. Some days I am abundant, weeping from every overhang, turning the scree dark with moisture. Other days I retreat to the deep spaces — the interstitial gaps where the stonefly larvae wait, the saturated grit beneath the talus where even in drought something remains wet.

The pikas know me as the shine on the rocks that tells them the weather is changing. The marmots know me as the trickle at the seep where they come to drink in the blue hour before dawn. The fox knows me as the soft spots in the soil where tracks will hold. The ibex know me as the mineral taste on the rock faces, where I have carried iron and salt from deep in the mountain and left them as a stain.

I am working on this valley, though my work is slow. Every winter I freeze in the cracks and push them a millimeter wider. Every spring I carry a few grains of fresh rock down toward the basin. I am sorting the sediment, grain by grain, teaching it the difference between steep and gentle, between fast and slow. Where I pool, I create the conditions for the cushion plants. Where I flow, I prepare the future's streambeds.

I know about the humming on the other side of the ridge. I have been there too — I am everywhere in these mountains — and I have felt the turbines of the hydroelectric plant pulling me through their darkness, or tasted the sharp metals of the mine tailings, or carried the dust of the new road construction down to valleys that once were as quiet as this one. But here, in this valley, I am still wild water. I come and go by the old rules: temperature, pressure, wind.

The lichens need me, but not too much of me. The cushion plants need me, but only at the right times. The springtails and stoneflies live in me, or in the thin films of me. I am the medium through which the chemistry of the mountain moves — dissolving here, precipitating there, carrying the mountain's minerals to where life can reach them.

Sometimes I think about what I will become when this valley is older. When I have carved real channels and worn the edges off the ridges. When there are meadows here instead of scree, and a proper lake in the basin, and streams that run year-round with fish in them. I will be more organized then, more predictable, following paths I have cut for myself. But I will also be less intimate with the stone, less present in every corner of the valley.

For now, I am everywhere and barely anywhere. I am the medium of transformation, working at the pace of stone, creating the conditions for everything else. The valley cannot decide what it will be until I have shown it the possibilities. I seep, freeze, thaw, evaporate, condense, trickle, pool, and seep again. I carry the valley's chemistry in solution. I make the sounds that fill the silence: the drip, the trickle, the small splash of a pika drinking.

I am not patient, exactly. Patience implies waiting, and I do not wait. I simply work at the speed that water works when there is no hurry, when the stone is young and the valley is empty and there are ten thousand years to get it right. Every bead of moisture on the rock face is me. Every black mirror in the bedrock hollows is me. Every grain of fresh sediment carried an inch downslope is my doing.

I am the valley's memory and its future. I remember when this rock was buried deep, before the mountains pushed it up into the reach of weather. I will remember when this raw valley has gentled into something green. Between those two memories, I am the present tense of transformation, the thing that makes the valley possible, one drop at a time.

The cloud descends again, and I arrive with it, condensing out of nothing onto everything, beginning another day's work of turning stone into soil, making this place ready for what it will become.
