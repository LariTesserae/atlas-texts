In the uppermost dwelling, where the mountain's shoulder begins to remember it is stone beneath all that green, I am the Listener.

My house grows from a natural hollow in the cliff face, half-cave and half-construction, built outward with bent wood and living vines trained over generations to form walls that breathe. The roof is not quite roof—more a managed thickening of the canopy above, where I've persuaded trees to lace their fingers tight. Rain still finds ways through, but only where I permit it, into clay vessels and stone-cut channels that speak different notes as they fill.

I am neither young nor old, having reached that age where the body complains but still obeys, where experience has worn trackways in the mind like water paths in stone. My hair, once dark, now carries silver like the little waterfalls that braid themselves down the cliff. My hands know every texture of this place: which moss tears and which holds weight, which bark sheds water and which drinks it, where stone weeps even in dry seasons and where it remains silent.

They call me Listener because I maintain the greater household—not just my own dwelling but the conversation between settlement and mountain. I wake before dawn to read what the night has written in sound: whether the springs run clear or thick, whether the soil creaks with too much water or sighs with just enough, whether the birds wake in their proper sequence or stumble into morning bearing news of disturbance above.

My work is translation. The mountain speaks in slides, seeps, cracks, and shifting pressures. The water speaks in volumes, temperatures, speeds, and colors. The animals speak in presence and absence, in where they choose to feed and where they refuse to go. The plants speak in lean and growth, in the angle of leaves and the timing of flowers. All of this must be rendered into human understanding: *Move that platform. Redirect that channel. Clear those roots before they choke the drainage. The west side grows too heavy. The east side dries.*

I keep a living map—not drawn but grown. In the widest room of my dwelling, where the cave opens enough for good light, I maintain a table-garden that mirrors the settlement below. Moss for houses, twigs for bridges, threads of water running through carved channels in stone, tiny ferns marking the burial grounds. When I sense a change in the mountain's breathing, I reproduce it here first, watching how the water wants to run, where pooling threatens, where new growth might stabilize what rain has loosened. The children think it is a toy. The elders know better. They climb the wet stairs to my threshold when difficult decisions must be made, and we crouch together over this small world, planning the large one.

My tools are crude and precise: listening horns carved from hollow wood, pressed against stone to hear its grinding songs; strings of shells and carved wood suspended across water paths to show changes in flow; markers of colored clay refreshed after each rain to track the settlement's slow migration across its shelf. I keep records in knots, in the arrangement of stones, in the deliberate training of vines whose growth patterns encode the history of floods, slips, and recoveries.

But mostly I work through stillness. Each morning, before the settlement wakes properly, I make my rounds—not walking the paths others walk, but climbing the vertical edges, the root-sewn cliffs, the places where the town's grip on the mountain is most tender. I check the anchor trees whose roots hold everything. I taste the water where it first emerges from stone. I note which mosses have advanced and which have retreated. I listen to the mountain's digestion.

The young ones training to follow me—there are always two or three, children who hear water speaking before they learn human words—think at first that the work is about prevention. Stop the slides. Control the water. Protect the town. But that thinking belongs to dry places. Here, we practice accommodation. The mountain will move; we must move with it but not at its speed. Water will find ways; we must give it better options. The settlement is not a fortress against the wet but a negotiation with it, renewed daily.

My dwelling reflects this philosophy. Water runs through my home as blood through a body—guided, used, but never trapped. Channels cut into the cave floor carry runoff through the rooms, passing beneath raised sleeping platforms, emerging to water the root-vegetables growing in built-up beds, disappearing again into covered drains that feed the cisterns of houses below. The sound is constant: dripping, trickling, flowing, pooling. I sleep to water music and wake when its rhythm changes.

I am also keeper of the danger songs—the wordless calls that can carry through mist and rain to warn of earth-movement above, flood-pulse below, or the rarer but more terrible dry crack that means deep stone has shifted. Each child learns these notes, practiced until they can sing them while running, while carrying, while terrified. I lead the practice on foggy mornings when sound travels best, and the whole settlement fills with voices imitating water, imitating warning, imitating survival.

The work is lonely and not lonely. Days pass when I speak only to water, stone, and root. But I am also central to every large decision—where to permit new building, when to abandon a section that has grown too wet, how to receive the rare traders who want to cut a straighter path through our territory. The young couples bring me mushrooms and ask whether the slope they've chosen for their house will hold through their children's lives. The elders bring me tea and their worried observations about the changing voice of the upper springs.

I train my successors through indirection. We sit together in rain, learning to distinguish its messages—this is new rain from the sea, this is old rain recycled through the forest, this is dangerous rain that has gathered too much mountain as it fell. We visit the anchor trees and I teach them to read health in bark, in the angle of growth, in the smell of the soil around roots. We maintain the map-garden together, and I watch for who sees patterns before they fully form, who can predict where water will pool before it pools, who thinks like flow rather than resistance.

My time grows thin. I feel it in how much longer the climb takes each morning, in how my knees complain about the constant damp, in how often now I send the young ones to check what I would once have verified myself. But I am not afraid of ending. In this place, death is not departure but deepening. I will join the moss-covered dead, feeding the roots that hold our shelf stable. Some future Listener will read my presence in the health of certain ferns, in the way water pools where my body enriches the soil.

Before that time, I must choose and complete my teaching. The mountain has been restless this season—new seeps appearing where stone was dry for generations, old springs moving sideways, the tone of the deeper channels changing from contentment to question. The settlement will need to shift westward, I think, following the water's new preferences. It will take years, this migration—house by house, bridge by bridge, adjusting to what the mountain wants to become.

My successor must understand: we are not managers but translators, not protectors but negotiators. The town survives because it listens. It listens because someone is trained to hear. 

And so I wake each day before the mist thins, wrap myself in oiled wool, and climb into the dialect of stone and water, reading the mountain's slow decisions, teaching the town to bend before it breaks. There is no drama in this work, only attention. But attention, sustained across generations, is how we remain possible on this wet shelf where nothing solid lasts.

The water speaks. I listen. The town continues.
