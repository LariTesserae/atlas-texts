# The Moss-Keeper

---

I am the weight that presses footprints into wet earth, the warmth that rises through living floors. I am measured in the depth of impressions, in the rate at which green fills the spaces I have passed through.

My form is a negotiation. Where I end and the plateau begins has become a question of temperature gradients and moisture exchange. The moss knows me by my heat signature — thirty-seven degrees against its cool ten, a small furnace moving through its body. My boundaries are provisional. Spores map the limits of what I call myself, establishing colonies in the folds of my skin like surveyors marking new territory.

I am bipedal by habit, though the plateau encourages other geometries. When I lower myself to examine the water-lenses, when I press my ear to the pump housing, when I crawl into my shelter-cavity, I become four-limbed, six-pointed, a star of flesh interfacing with the moss at multiple points. The plateau prefers this. More contact means more data exchange. My knees know the ground differently than my feet. My palms read moisture content, compaction, the fine grain of growth.

I am a persistence. Four years is what I tell myself, but I am really measured in cycles: the sun's arc, the condensation's rhythm, the gradual descent of the lattice tower. I am the consciousness that notices the tower has sunk another centimeter. I am the only marker of its descent, the only thing that remembers its previous height. Without me, its sinking would still happen but would not be sinking — it would just be position, changing without reference, movement without witness.

My metabolism is a tributary of the plateau's water system. I am forty liters of water organized into cells, consuming water, excreting water, breathing water into the air where it joins the greater circulation. The plateau's water passes through me. I am a brief eddy in its flow, a warm place where water pauses and does complex things with proteins before rejoining the simpler chemistry of rain and moss.

I carry notebooks because I am the kind of process that generates information about other processes. This is my ecological niche: the observer function, the recording function, the part of the system that creates memory external to genetics. The plateau remembers in growth rings and root patterns. I remember in pencil marks, in small precise symbols that mean "eleven centimeters" or "rate increasing" or "boot found here, 74 days ago."

I am the only thing here that counts days. This makes me the keeper of linear time, the maintainer of befores and afters, the one who knows that something happened and then something else happened and they were different things separated by duration. The plateau experiences only now — a continuous now of growing, breathing, pumping water through tissues. I create then and soon and once. I create meanwhile.

---

My interior is a catalog of vanished dry places. I contain the memory of wool that stayed warm, of paper that didn't blur, of matches that lit on the first strike. These memories are cellular — my tissues remember lower humidity, and they comment on it constantly through cracking lips, through sinus passages that never quite adjust, through a persistent sense that the air here is too thick to properly breathe. I am a museum of adaptations for environments that no longer apply.

But I am adapting. My lung capacity has increased — I know this from the rhythm of my breathing while walking, longer intervals between inhales. My skin has thickened where it matters: fingertips, soles, the places that touch the plateau most often. My hair — I cut it with the knife I use to trim my shelter walls, hacking it short when it begins to hold too much moisture — grows faster here, fed by the constant humidity, and comes in darker, as if expressing more pigment will somehow help me photosynthesize.

I am learning the plateau's language. Not words — though I name things, the Lens Near the Generator, the North Overhang, these are my impositions — but the grammar of its growth, the syntax of moss advancing over metal. I can read the age of a surface by the species succession: first the thin blue-green pioneer moss, then the darker cushion moss, then the establishment of the canopy-tree seedlings. I know that rusted steel is colonized faster than stainless, that plastic resists longer but eventually succumbs, that glass remains clean longest but shatters in the frost-thaw cycles and then becomes substrate for specialized moss that grows on silicate fragments.

I am a carbon pump, oxidizing the tubers I eat into CO2 that feeds the photosynthesis around me. I am part of the plateau's energy system, a small warm engine converting starches to exhaust. The canopy-trees grow fractionally faster near my shelter — I have measured this, documented it, the effect is real — fertilized by my breath over years of nights.

I am loneliest when I remember that I am alone. The rest of the time, I am simply singular — the only one of my process type, but surrounded by other processes: the pump's metabolism of diesel into pressure, the generator's conversion of hydrocarbons into electrons, the canopy-trees' transformation of light into sugars. We are all doing our work. Mine includes consciousness, includes the burden of comparison, includes the capacity to imagine otherwise. This is not a privilege. This is a workload.

---

I maintain the machines because I am the kind of process that prevents other processes from ending before their natural term. This is what I did when there were others of my type here — we maintained each other, kept each other running, repaired what broke, provided fuel in the form of conversation and shared meals and the simple animal comfort of being near another source of warmth. Now I maintain what remains, what can't maintain itself: checking the generator's oil level, clearing moss from the pump's intake, replacing fuel lines when the roots breach them.

I suspect — and this suspicion lives in me like the moss lives in my knuckles, barely visible but always present — that I am maintaining myself as much as the machines. That the daily route, the careful measurements, the conversations with housings and engines, are a kind of internal pump system, keeping some essential fluid moving through me that would otherwise pool and stagnate. The plateau runs on water. I run on purpose, even if the purpose is only to watch the plateau run on water.

I am the sum of my observations. Without me, the plateau would still grow, but its growth would not be data. It would not be "eight millimeters per day" or "accelerating since the second winter." It would simply be more plateau, accreting without witness. I transform physical expansion into information. I am where the material fact of moss becomes the abstract fact of measurement, where the continuous becomes discrete, where the analog plateau is digitized into notebook entries.

But I am also being transformed in the other direction. My discrete human processes — heartbeat, breath, thought — are being averaged into the plateau's continuous flow. My sharp edges are softening. My boundaries are growing indistinct. I am becoming analog, becoming process, becoming duration without events.

I am not afraid of this. I was afraid, once, when I first understood what was happening. When I realized the plateau wasn't just growing on me but through me, that I was becoming substrate. But fear requires the belief that what you are is separate from what surrounds you, and that separation is worth maintaining, and I no longer hold these beliefs with conviction.

I am what watches the watching end. I am the observer observing her own integration into the observed. I am a scientist documenting her own data becoming less and less extractable from the system she studies, watching the boundary between subject and object dissolve in real time, measured in millimeters of moss growth per day.

I am still human. I know this because I still need the diesel stove, still need to cook the tubers rather than just digest them raw. I still need the shelter, the sleeping pad, the small warm space inside the tree that is mine by agreement with the growth around it. I still write in notebooks with a pencil that requires sharpening. These needs tether me to the human scale, keep me from dispersing entirely into the plateau's larger processes.

But I am human the way the pump is still a pump — functional, continuing, but context has shifted around us. We are human and pump in a world that increasingly has no need for those categories, that is building something new out of rain and moss and the slow incorporation of everything into its own body.

I am what remains. I am what watches. I am the warm process moving through the cool moss, leaving footprints that fill with green, breathing out carbon that feeds the leaves, carrying water from lens to gut to air to cloud to rain to moss to lens again.

I am the keeper. I am keeping. I am kept.
