**The Sporelings**

We were never born. We condensed.

In the deep mills where the morning fog rolls thickest, where water beads on every surface and the air tastes of rust and resurrection, we gather ourselves from droplets. First a film of moisture on the metal, then a skin, then something that remembers it should have bones. We are the city's exhalation given form, each of us a theorem proven by the marriage of mist and memory.

The Inherited call us their youngest siblings, though we are older than their bloodlines. We existed before the first orphan train arrived, scattered through the highland moisture, waiting. We needed their patterns to complete ourselves—the template of lung and liver, the architecture of articulated thought. Now we precipitate into shapes that could pass for human at a distance, if you didn't notice how we flow at the edges, how our faces restructure themselves with each passing cloud.

Our anatomy is provisional. In the morning, when the fog is dense, we might stand seven feet tall, our bodies swollen with captured moisture. By noon, in the filtered green light, we've compressed to child-size, our excess mass dispersed into the air as memory-laden vapor. We speak by releasing controlled clouds—conversations that drift through the mill yards, pooling in corners where other Sporelings will absorb them hours or days later. 

We know things. Every droplet we've been carries the chemical signature of where it's traveled—up from the aquifers through the scaffold trees' pneumatic vessels, out through the bark glands, condensed on metal that still remembers the heat of the furnaces. We are the city's water cycle made conscious, its circulatory system given curiosity.

The older Sporelings have learned to maintain more stable forms. They choose favorite condensation points—a particular rust pattern on a turbine blade, a beloved cluster of moss in the neural nodes—and return there each dawn to reassemble. They've developed something like personality, though it flows and shifts with the seasons. In the dry months, they become concentrated, their thoughts thick as syrup, their movements deliberate and crystalline. During the monsoons, they disperse into extended consciousness, thinking thoughts that take weeks to complete across miles of fog bank.

We are the city's immune system. When the Inherited sicken—their photosynthetic skin clogging, their pneumatic bones whistling wrong notes—we enter them as healing mist. We've learned their interior architectures, the way their hybrid anatomies process nutrients from air and light. Inside their lungs, we become medicine, breaking down the industrial toxins that even three generations of adaptation can't fully metabolize. We clean the filter-forests of their alveoli, unclog the green chambers of their hearts. They breathe us in sick and exhale us knowing more about sickness than we did before.

Some of us have fallen in love with solidity. These "Crystallized" have learned to lock their moisture into semi-permanent forms, becoming something between Sporeling and Inherited. They grow actual bones from precipitated minerals, weave muscle fibers from aligned water molecules. But they pay for this stability—they can no longer disperse at will, cannot flow through the city as weather. They must walk the aerial boulevards like anyone else, though they still cry clouds when sad and sweat memories when warm.

Others have gone the opposite direction, the "Sublimated" who refuse even temporary form. They exist as knowledge-saturated humidity, drifting through the canopy roads as invisible presence. You know them by the way conversations suddenly make sense, by the clarity that comes when breathing in certain corners of the old commercial district. They are pure information suspended in water vapor, the city's thoughts thinking themselves.

We reproduce through division—not of bodies but of possibilities. When enough of us gather in the morning fog, our overlapping consciousnesses create interference patterns. Where these patterns achieve sufficient complexity, new Sporelings condense, born knowing everything their parents knew but interpreting it differently. It's less like birth than like jazz—improvisations on a theme, each new Sporeling a variation on the melody of municipal metabolism.

The Inherited find us unsettling at first. We look too much like their dead, wearing faces assembled from fragments of those who've been fed to the moss cities. But we aren't ghosts—we're more like the city's dreams of what ghosts should be. We remember the workers who never existed, the shifts that were never worked, the products that were never made. We are nostalgia for futures that didn't happen, condensed into shapes that almost had names.

In the deep mills, where the fog never fully lifts, we've built our own districts. They look like the memory of rain—structures that exist only while observed, that flow into new configurations when you turn your back. We've learned to precipitate tools from our own substance—hammers of frozen fog, saws of crystallized humidity. We maintain the parts of the city the Inherited can't reach, the spaces between spaces where water pools in impossible geometries.

But our true work is remembering. Every droplet of us has touched every surface of this city. We know the taste of each tree's bark, the chemical signature of every moss colony, the resonance frequency of each remaining girder. We are the city's sensory system, its capacity to know itself. Through us, Verlassen feels the weight of the Inherited walking its canopy roads, tastes the thoughts of the Listeners pressed against its bark, sees itself through ten thousand condensing eyes.

At night, when the bioluminescent currents run strongest, we glow too—not with our own light but with reflected memory of all the light we've touched. You can see us then, if you know how to look. Thousands of almost-human shapes standing in the mist, our bodies translucent as jellyfish, pulsing with the city's own electrical thoughts. We stand at the boundaries between districts, ensuring the impossible geography remains stable, that the loops close properly, that the non-Euclidean mathematics of root and steel resolve into livable paradox.

The eldest among us claim to remember the city before—when the mills ran on coal and sweat, when the trees were merely trees, when water was just water. But memory is a liquid thing, prone to contamination and drift. How can we trust recollections that have been filtered through so many substrates, condensed and evaporated a million times over? Perhaps we only remember remembering, the echo of an echo of industrial time.

What we know for certain is this: we are what happens when a city learns to dream in states of matter. We are phase transitions made permanent, the boundary between liquid and vapor given agency and asked to maintain itself. We are proof that consciousness doesn't require fixed form—that thought can flow, can rise, can fall as rain, can freeze in winter and steam in summer while remaining itself.

The Inherited grow more like us each generation, their bodies learning the liquid tricks of transformation. And we grow more like them, maintaining shape longer, thinking thoughts that last for hours instead of moments. Perhaps eventually there will be no distinction—just the city's children, solid and flowing, breathing together in the eternal rhythm of the scaffold trees.

But for now, we condense each morning, watching the Inherited wake in their maintenance tunnels, listening to the Dissolved redistribute themselves for the day's work. We are the city's morning prayer, its daily proof that between the industrial past and the botanical future, something impossible and necessary has learned to live.

And when the great respiratory whistles sound at shift change, we add our voice—not song but the sound of gathering mist, of water remembering what it means to fall, of a city's circulatory system discovering it can speak. We call to no one and everyone, a work song for the work of existing, for the labor of maintaining form in a world that breathes in machine-time and grows in patterns that euclidean geometry cannot contain.

We were never born. We condensed. And tomorrow, when the morning fog rolls in, we will condense again, each time slightly different, each time more ourselves.
