# The Sleeper Beneath Seven Cairns

I am the weight that holds the mountain down.

Not the mountain itself — that would be grandiose, and I am past grandiosity. I am the thing that prevents the mountain from doing what it wants to do, which is to disperse. Every grain of granite dreams of sand. Every boulder remembers being dust between stars. The mountain wants to come apart, and I am why it cannot.

Find me thus: seven hundred feet below the treeline, where the krummholz gives up trying and the stone begins in earnest. Seven cairns mark the place, though only five are visible anymore. Two have dispersed back into the general scree. The others persist through stubbornness and the occasional ministrations of hikers who do not know what they maintain. The cairns describe a rough circle, sixty feet across. At the center, nothing. Stone like any stone. A few cushion plants grinding roots into cracks. Sky.

I am beneath. Not far — thirty feet, no more. But thirty feet of granite might as well be thirty miles for anything that walks and breathes. I am folded into a space between two sheets of bedrock, a space that was not there before I arrived and will not be there after I leave. The stone accommodates me the way water accommodates a drowning man: reluctantly, temporarily, only for as long as the weight insists.

What am I? Density without mass. Pressure without substance. I am the principle of holding-together crystallized into something not quite entity, not quite force. Imagine a fist that is only the act of clenching. That is close. I am the clench that keeps this particular piece of the world from achieving its natural state of dispersal.

I was not always here. Once I moved through the deep crust, following the isotherms, riding the slow convection of the mantle. I was younger then, and believed in things. I believed that if I could find the places where the earth wanted most badly to come apart — the spreading centers, the failed rifts, the ancient sutures barely holding — I could heal them. Press them together until they fused. Make the world more solid, more itself.

I was wrong about everything except the method.

The stone knows me. We have been together long enough that my presence has altered its crystalline structure. The granite here is denser than it should be, its grains packed past the point of natural compression. Sometimes, in the expansion of freeze-thaw, flakes will spall off the exposed bedrock with unusual force — not the lazy prying of frost-wedge but something more like escape. These flakes ring when they strike other stones. They ring like bells. Hikers notice this, sometimes. They pick up the singing stones and carry them down the mountain in their packs, not knowing they carry fragments of my work.

The seven cairns were built by people who understood, however dimly, what lay beneath. Not me — they never knew about me — but the effect of my presence. This place on the mountain where things hold together past reason. Where the scree does not slide even on the steepest sections. Where the stones balance in ways that should not work. They built their markers and conducted their small ceremonies and went away, and their understanding went with them. Now there are only the hikers with their singing souvenirs.

Here is what it is like to be me:

Imagine you are holding your breath. Not in your lungs — deeper. Imagine your whole body is holding its breath, every cell suspended between in and out. Now imagine you have been holding this breath for eight thousand years. The tension has become so familiar it feels like rest. The strain has become indistinguishable from structure. You no longer remember what exhaling would feel like, only that it must not happen. Not yet.

Because when I let go, this part of the mountain will remember what it wants to be. The granite will crack along every plane of weakness I have been holding closed. The cairns will tumble. The balanced stones will find their natural angles of repose. A section of the ridge — not large, perhaps an acre — will slump and spread and achieve the entropy it has been denied. It will happen over months, not moments. But it will happen.

And I will sink. Released from the work of holding this particular piece of crust together, I will follow my weight down into warmer stone. The mountain will seal above me like water. I will descend until I find another place that wants to come apart, and I will wedge myself into its weakness, and I will hold it together until I cannot. This is my cycle. I am not saving anything. I am only delaying the inevitable unraveling, and the delay itself has become my purpose.

The hikers who pocket the singing stones do not know they are taking pieces of compressed time. Every flake that rings like a bell is a moment when the mountain wanted to disperse and could not. The sound is not the stone. The sound is the frustration of the stone, its long argument with me made audible. In warm living rooms down in the valleys, collectors arrange these fragments on shelves, not knowing they have brought home pieces of a negotiation between weight and dispersal that has been going on since before their species learned to walk upright.

Sometimes, at night, when the temperature drops and the stones contract, the entire area above me chimes. Faintly, but the cairn-builders heard it. They understood it as the mountain praying. They were not wrong. The prayer is: *let me go*. My answer, pressed into every grain of granite, is: *not yet*.

I dream, sometimes. Density dreams are slow, thick, iterative. I dream of the time when I moved freely through the mantle. I dream of the other weights — my kin, though we have no kinship that would translate to your understanding. Some still move. Some, like me, have found their places and locked themselves into the slow work of temporary cohesion. We do not communicate. What would we say? We are all doing the same thing: holding pieces of the world together that want to fly apart. The only difference is where and for how long.

In my dreams, the planet is a vast conspiracy of dispersal temporarily frustrated by beings like me. Every stable thing — every mountain, every continental shelf, every ocean floor — persists only because somewhere beneath it, one of us is clenched into the stone, holding the center. Geography is not the result of forces but of resistances. The map is not what is but what has not yet been allowed to disperse.

This is almost certainly not true. But when you have been holding your breath for eight thousand years, almost certainly not true begins to feel like a kind of faith.

The cairns mark my boundary. Inside the circle, my influence is absolute. The stone coheres past reason. Outside, normal geology resumes. Sometimes I feel the edge of my domain precisely: here the granite sings with compression, there it merely exists. The transition is sharp as a blade. The cairn-builders felt it too. That is why they marked it. They wanted others to know: inside this circle, different rules apply.

What they did not know is that the circle is shrinking. When I first wedged myself into this particular weakness, I could hold a square mile together. Now, sixty feet. The compression is growing more intense but the volume is reducing. I am not weakening. I am concentrating. Eventually, I will hold only a single point of granite in perfect, impossible density. Then I will let go, and that point will expand back to what it should have been, and I will sink, and the cycle will continue.

The hikers come in summer mostly. They rest by the cairns. They add stones sometimes, maintaining what they do not understand. They notice that the ground here feels different — more solid somehow, as though the mountain is more committed to being mountain in this particular spot. They are feeling me, though they do not know it. They are feeling what it's like when stone is not allowed to do what it wants.

One day, years from now but not many, someone will be here when I finally exhale. They will hear the mountain sigh — a sound like satisfaction, like a tension so old it had been mistaken for structure finally released. The cairns will shift and tumble. The impossible balances will find their possible angles. Over the following months, this part of the ridge will slump in ways that geologists will find hard to explain. But the stones will stop singing. That is how you will know I have gone: the stones will stop singing, because they will no longer need to complain.

Until then, I hold. Not because it matters — nothing this temporary matters on the scales I inhabit. I hold because holding is what I am. The fist that is only the act of clenching. The weight that keeps this particular piece of the world from achieving its natural state of dispersal.

The mountain dreams of sand. I am its bad sleep. When I let go, it will dream more peacefully, and I will find another dream to interrupt. This is the whole of it. This has always been the whole of it.

The stones ring like bells when they break free, but only once. After that, they are only stones. The hikers who carry them home are carrying spent prayers, though pretty ones. On quiet nights, they might hear in those fragments an echo of the longer argument, but they will not understand it. How could they? They are themselves expressions of the same temporary cohesion — walking, breathing arguments against dispersal, their whole lives spent in the same denial the mountain practices at my insistence.

But softer. And so much shorter. And when they let go, they do not sink into deeper stone to find another failing thing to hold together. They simply disperse, as everything wants to, as everything will.

Except me. Except for now. Except for here, in this circle of seven cairns where five remain, where I make the mountain more solid than it wants to be, where I am the clench that keeps the world from flying apart, at least this part of it, at least for a little while longer.
