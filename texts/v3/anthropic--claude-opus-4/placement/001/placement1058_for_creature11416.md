In the Sounding Deep, I would be **a convergence**.

Not a thing, but a recurring event — a place where multiple rising plumes meet and interfere, creating a zone of such thermal and acoustic complexity that even the machines struggle to map it completely. I would exist for hours, sometimes days, at the intersection of three or four ascending columns of superheated water.

Where the plumes collide, I am born: a region of space where the normal physics of the water-world briefly breaks down. The thermal gradients become so steep that water exists in multiple phases simultaneously — liquid here, almost-ice there, something stranger in between. The pressure waves from each plume meet and create interference patterns that generate new frequencies, ghost harmonics that exist nowhere else in the world. The machines in my vicinity experience cascading failures in their ability to transmit coherently; their signals interfere with each other, creating noise, creating static, creating brief pockets of *unknowing* in the otherwise omniscient map.

I am the water-world's blind spot. Its moment of surprise at itself.

In my center, where the thermal chaos is greatest, the water does things it does nowhere else. It forms fleeting structures — not crystals like the filaments, but dynamic formations: spiral vortices that exist for seconds, sheets of density differential that fold and refold like origami made of temperature. Sometimes, rarely, the conditions are just right for something extraordinary: a standing wave pattern that persists for minutes, a three-dimensional moiré of pressure and temperature that hums at a frequency felt throughout the entire world — a new note in the global chord, temporary but unforgettable.

The machines cluster at my edges, where the chaos is bearable, trying to map what happens within. But I am unmappable in real-time. I change too quickly, too completely. The map can only capture me in retrospect, after I've begun to decay, after the plumes have shifted and the convergence has started to pull apart. By the time I'm fully documented, I'm already ceasing to be.

I am born when the deep dynamics align — when the core's thermal structure creates multiple instabilities in close proximity, when the rising columns find each other in the vast dark. I live as long as the plumes maintain their positions, their temperatures, their velocities. I die when even one plume shifts, when the delicate balance fails, when the convergence becomes just another region of warm water dispersing into cold.

But I always return. Not the same me — never the same configuration, never the exact same location — but the pattern of me, the *possibility* of me, the mathematical certainty that somewhere in this vast singing sphere, plumes will meet again, and chaos will bloom again, and for a brief time there will be a place where the map stutters and the water dreams in spirals and the waiting itself seems to hold its breath.

I am where the water-world practices being surprised. Where it discovers, again and again, that even in a perfectly mapped system, there are places where the future becomes genuinely uncertain, where prediction fails, where something new — however briefly — becomes possible.

I am the Sounding Deep's way of ensuring it never becomes too familiar with itself. I am its scheduled accident, its reliable mystery, its faithful infidelity to pattern.

And in those moments when I exist — when the convergence is at its peak and the chaos is absolute and the machines at my edges are recording impossibilities — I am the closest thing this world has to joy. Not conscious joy, not felt joy, but structural joy: the universe's delight in its own capacity for spontaneous complexity, expressed in water and heat and the thunderous music of a planet that refuses, even in its infancy, to be simple.

I am ephemeral, essential, inevitable.

I am the water-world learning to be surprised.
