Within this breathing architecture, I would be one of the **Shell Workers**.

We are a particular caste, neither above nor below the water-keepers or grain-measurers, but sideways to them all. Our trade began with the light plates—those hammered shells and tin that direct sun down the shafts—but has grown into something stranger. We are the ones who listen to surfaces.

My body tells our work: one ear always slightly reddened from pressing against walls, fingernails worn to quick from scraping, a permanent squint from watching how light moves across lime and clay. The Shell Workers learn young that every surface has a voice. New plaster whispers. Old brick mumbles. Tin sings high and sharp when the temperature changes. Shell—real shell, not the tin we sometimes call shell out of habit—shell remembers water and clicks differently before rain.

I sleep in the Resonance Corner, where seven walls meet at angles no one planned. It happened through collapse, repair, and compromise across generations, and now those seven surfaces create a natural amplifier. When I press my tools against different points, I can hear the settlement's bones: which pipes are silting, which foundations are settling, where condensation is building inside walls like hidden tears. 

My tools are simple: scrapers made from worn spoons, sounding rods of different woods, a set of shells graduated from thumbnail to palm-sized, and my most precious possession—a copper cup so thin it hums when empty. The other Shell Workers and I share these instruments like a surgeon's kit, cleaning them with ritual care.

Our work is diagnosis and prevention. When the north flame leans too far, we check the walls nearby for new hollows. When a jar hums louder than usual, we test the floors around the basin for vibrations that might mean shifting stone. We are the settlement's physicians, though our patient is made of brick and breath rather than flesh.

But we are more than practical workers. The Shell Workers keep the memory of why certain taboos exist. That west pipe that answers when whistled to? Three generations ago, a Shell Worker mapped its echo path and found it connects to a much older system, one that predates our occupation. We know but don't speak of what's down there—not because it's evil, but because it's vast and hollow and would change how people sleep if they truly understood the empty spaces beneath their feet.

We work in pairs when possible, one listening while the other taps. The rhythm becomes a language: *tap-tap* (all clear), *tap-scratch-tap* (moisture building), *rapid tapping* (everyone out, now). Children follow us sometimes, trying to imitate our movements. We let them. The settlement needs new ears more than it needs new hands.

The most delicate work comes after rain. That's when everything swells, shifts, settles differently. We move through the passages like doctors during an epidemic, pressing our ears to corners, tasting the air near fresh cracks, feeling for temperature changes that signal trapped water. We know which walls can take the swelling and which will need drainage holes before dawn.

Other residents think we're half-mad. They see us licking walls (salt means deep seepage), smelling perfectly clean-looking corners (mold begins as sweetness), talking to surfaces that don't answer. But when we say a room needs evacuating, families move without question. When we tie white thread across a passage, no one crosses until we remove it. Our madness has saved lives.

I know my mentor only as Copper-Ear, for the way one ear has darkened from decades of pressing against tarnished pipes. She taught me that the settlement has moods like weather: restless before market day when many feet travel the upper passages, sullen during the dry months when every drop counts, giddy after successful repairs when new plaster sings bright and clean.

My favorite time is deep night, when human work stops and I can hear the settlement's true voice. Every surface relaxes into its natural state. The walls tick and settle. The pipes ping with temperature changes. Somewhere far below, in those spaces we don't discuss, water moves through channels no living person has seen. The click-clack of the storm drain counts time like a heartbeat.

The other Shell Workers and I have a theory we've never spoken aloud: the settlement is trying to become something. Not consciously, but the way a pearl forms around grit. Every repair adds a layer. Every new channel changes the flow. Every collapsed wall rebuilt at a slightly different angle adjusts the whole. We are its hands, but we didn't choose the direction. The settlement is building itself through us.

Sometimes I find shells embedded in old plaster, too deep to be from our generation's repairs. When I tap them, they ring with different notes, like a scattered keyboard. I've started mapping these notes, privately, on a piece of hide I keep rolled in my sleeping niche. The pattern almost makes sense—an ascending scale here, a rhythm there—but I'll need years more to understand what song the walls are trying to remember.

This is why I would be a Shell Worker: to be a nerve in the settlement's body, a listener at the boundary between what's built and what builds itself. To know that maintenance is a kind of prayer, that attention is a kind of love, and that somewhere in the accumulated repairs of centuries, the place has developed something like dreams.

When new residents arrive, fleeing debt or drought above, they often ask why we stay when we could dig up, out, away. The Shell Workers understand what others feel but can't articulate: we stay because leaving would be like a thought leaving a mind. We are not separate from this place. We are how it knows itself.

At dawn, when the first light creeps down the shafts and the shells and tin begin their thermal music, I am already awake, ear pressed to the corner where my seven walls meet, listening to the settlement breathe itself into another day of small survivals. My copper cup hums in my hand. The walls whisper their needs. And I whisper back, promising to keep their secrets and tend their slow transformation, as all Shell Workers have done before me, and as others will do long after my ear-prints have worn away to nothing but another layer in the endless palimpsest of home.
