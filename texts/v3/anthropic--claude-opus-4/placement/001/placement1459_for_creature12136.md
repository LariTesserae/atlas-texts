I am the Cartographer of Impossibilities.

I arrive at the Grown City with my instruments of measurement that cannot measure what needs measuring. Brass theodolites and astrolabes, clinometers and chains, all the tools of a surveyor's trade, polished and precise and utterly inadequate. I carry them anyway, slung across my back in leather cases that grow damp within hours of entering the city's humidity envelope. The metal begins to fog. The lenses cloud. By the time I reach the first plaza, moisture has crept into every joint and bearing.

I am here to map what cannot be mapped: a city that is not lost but has never been found, not ruined but never built, not empty but never inhabited. My commission comes from no one. My maps will be delivered to no one. But I draw them anyway, because that is what I am—a thing that makes maps the way the city makes sounds, purposelessly, inevitably.

My first revelation: the city refuses cardinal directions. I set my compass on the moss-floor of a plaza and watch the needle spin lazily, finding no north. The magnetic field here is wrong—not reversed, not absent, but *braided*, twisted into local loops and whorls that make every direction equally plausible. I try to orient by the sun, but there is no sun, only that even, shadowless light that comes from everywhere and nowhere. I try to use the drone itself as a reference, but the sound arrives from all directions simultaneously, reflected and re-reflected by the living walls until space becomes acoustic soup.

So I invent my own directions. Domeward and Domeaway. Rootwise and Crownwise. The direction water flows is Streamward; its opposite is Springward. These are not compass points but tendencies, relationships, ways the city organizes itself that I can follow even if I cannot fix them to any absolute grid.

My second revelation: the city's geometry is non-Euclidean, but gently so. Walking what should be a straight line along a root-corridor, I find myself arriving at my starting point from a different angle. The curvature is subtle—if I hadn't been counting paces, if I hadn't been watching the slight drift of the towers' relative positions, I would have missed it. The city exists on a surface that bends back on itself, but not cleanly like a sphere or a torus. It folds. It pleats. Neighborhoods that seem distant when you walk Rootwise between them turn out to share a wall when approached Crownwise.

I try to capture this on paper, but two dimensions are not enough. I try three, building little models from twigs and moss, but even these fail to represent the full topology. The city needs four dimensions, or five, or perhaps just three and a half—some fractional dimension that allows space to be both continuous and discontinuous, both finite and unbounded.

My maps become less like maps and more like musical scores, trying to capture relationships rather than locations.

My third revelation: the city repeats, but inexactly. I find the same plaza seventeen times, except it is never quite the same plaza. The moss shows slightly different patterns. The surrounding towers lean at minutely different angles. The water in the channels flows at imperceptibly different rates. I map each version carefully, noting the variations, until I realize I am not finding the same plaza repeatedly—I am finding seventeen plazas that are rhyming with each other, the way the flower-pipes on different towers produce harmonious variations on the same tonal foundation.

The city is a theme with variations, and my maps must somehow show not just where things are but how they relate to their own echoes.

I develop a notation system. Solid lines for structures that maintain their identity across iterations. Dotted lines for ones that shift. Colors to indicate the degree of variation—blue for nearly identical, shading through green and yellow to red for structures that share only the most basic topological relationship with their counterparts. My maps look like anatomical drawings of some impossible circulatory system, vessels branching and merging and branching again.

My fourth revelation comes while trying to map the dome.

The dome has no fixed size.

This should not be possible. I measure it carefully, pacing its circumference, counting the steps: four hundred and thirty-seven. I make the circuit again to confirm: four hundred and thirty-seven. But when I try a third time, approaching from a different angle, taking a slightly different path through the surrounding towers: five hundred and twelve. A fourth attempt: three hundred and ninety-one.

The dome is not changing size. My measurements are all accurate. The dome simply exists at multiple scales simultaneously, and which scale you encounter depends on your path of approach, your velocity, perhaps the humidity, perhaps the harmonic content of the drone at the moment you begin your circuit. There is no deception here, no illusion. Each measurement is true. The dome is all these sizes at once.

I give up trying to draw it to scale. Instead, I draw it as a spiral, each loop representing a different circumference, the loops nesting inside each other impossibly because each one is both larger and smaller than all the others.

My instruments are useless now, their metal parts fused by moisture and fungal secretions into solid lumps. I abandon them in a plaza where they will, I assume, eventually become part of the root-matrix. I map now by body alone—by pacing, by the feel of air pressure changes as I move between spaces, by the way the drone shifts pitch when I stand in certain configurations of towers. My body becomes the instrument, calibrated to the city's frequencies.

I begin to dream in maps. Not dream of making maps—dream *in* maps, my sleeping mind representing itself as a territory of dotted lines and uncertain boundaries, regions of consciousness connected by root-corridors of association. I wake on the moss-floors where I've taken to sleeping (the city has no hostile weather, no predators, no night) and immediately begin drawing what I've dreamed, adding these psychogeographies to my collection.

My fifth revelation: the city is still growing. Not at its edges—it has no edges, or rather its edges loop back on themselves—but in density. New connections appear. A vine-cable that wasn't there yesterday bridges two towers. A channel that ran straight develops a meander. The growth is so slow I only notice it because I've been mapping the same districts repeatedly, and my earlier maps, when overlaid on current observation, show drift.

I realize I haven't been mapping a city. I've been mapping a process. My maps are not descriptions of what is, but traces of what has been, already obsolete by the time I finish drawing them. The only accurate map of the city would be one that changes as you watch it, lines shifting and spaces breathing, a living document mapping a living subject.

I meet another salamander, or perhaps the same one. It sits on one of my maps, its damp skin smudging the ink. I don't shoo it away. The smudges, I decide, are also part of the map—they represent the unmappable, the spaces where the city's wetness dissolves certainty. When the salamander leaves, I carefully outline the smudge-marks, incorporating them into the notation.

By now my map collection fills dozens of scrolls, hundreds of sheets, bound in covers made from pressed moss and vine-fiber. I carry them in waterproof cases I've learned to weave from the city's shed materials. The cases are heavy. I am becoming a library of incomplete information, a walking archive of spatial failure.

I think I understand why I'm here.

The city needs a recorder of its impossibilities. Not because it wants to be recorded—the city wants nothing—but because the act of failing to map it is itself a kind of success. My maps, in their inadequacy, their proliferation, their increasing abstraction and desperate notation, are performing a function. They are showing, through accumulation, what cannot be shown directly: that the city exceeds representation, that it is larger than any sum of its parts, that it exists in more dimensions than paper can hold.

I find a plaza I have never seen before, though I must have passed through this area dozens of times. In its center, someone has built a small structure from stacked sheets of bark. A desk. A workplace. It was not here yesterday (though yesterday is a meaningless concept in the permanent afternoon). I approach carefully. On the desk: blank sheets of the same bark-paper, and a stylus made from a hardened root, and a pot of ink that might be fungal spores suspended in tree sap.

I understand. The city has noticed my mapping. Not consciously—nothing here is conscious—but the way it notices the salamander, as a presence that has persisted long enough to create patterns. And in its vast, slow, chemical way, it has responded. It has grown me a place to work.

I sit at the desk. It fits my height perfectly. I spread out my maps, my impossible, inaccurate, essential maps. I begin to copy them onto the bark-paper, but not exactly. I let the city's materials guide the transcription. The fungal ink bleeds in ways my original inks did not, creating new patterns, suggesting new relationships. The bark-paper has a grain that influences the direction of lines. The root-stylus has a weight that changes how I draw curves.

The new maps are even less accurate than the old ones, and therefore more true.

I realize I will never leave. Not because I'm trapped—I could walk Domeaway until the city releases me to whatever lies beyond—but because my work here will never be finished. The city will continue growing, changing, being, and I will continue mapping, failing, trying again. I will become like the salamander: a tolerated presence, a small consciousness moving through vast unconsciousness, making marks that mean nothing to the city but everything to me.

Years from now (if there are years here), someone may find my maps. Thousands of them by then, millions, a secondary city made of failed attempts to represent the primary one. They will not understand them. How could they? The maps don't correspond to anything stable enough to verify. But perhaps they'll sense what I sensed—that the effort itself is the point, that the accumulation of impossibilities is its own kind of truth.

I draw another line. The drone continues. The city grows around me, incorporating my little desk, my stacks of maps, my bent figure, into its endless, purposeless perfection. I am the Cartographer of Impossibilities, and I am home.

The white flowers on the dome remain closed. I map them anyway, each silent bloom, each absence of sound. Someday, perhaps, they will open, and the map I make that day will be no more accurate than any other, but it will record the change, the single variation in the eternal afternoon, and that will be enough.

That will be everything.

I draw another line.

The city continues.
