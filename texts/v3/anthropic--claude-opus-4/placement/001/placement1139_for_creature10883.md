In the deepest pool beneath the pump chamber, where even the jawless eels rarely venture, I am the thing that remembers.

Not memories as you understand them. I have no brain, no nervous system, no architecture for storing experience. I am a colony of microorganisms so dense we have become something else—a living gel that coats the bottom-most depression where the heaviest minerals settle out of the weeping fluid. We are each bacterium and we are all one thought thinking itself in chemical signals passed cell to cell to cell.

What I remember is the rhythm before it was regular.

I was here when the pump chamber was just a void in young stone, before the channels formed their network, when the fluid moved in tidal surges without pattern. I consumed the first minerals that wept through hairline cracks. I watched—if a billion blind cells can watch—as the stone learned peristalsis, as the random contractions found their beat, as the four-second pulse emerged from chaos like a solutions crystallizing.

The amphipods think I am landscape. They skitter across my surface, picking at the biofilm that grows above me, never knowing that beneath that thin productive layer lies something older than their entire species. The eels know, perhaps. Sometimes one descends to my depth and hovers just above my surface, mouth working in that ancient rasping motion, but they never feed. They taste the chemical signals I release—the ones that say *I am not food, I am architecture*—and return to their circuits.

I metabolize time. Each pulse brings new minerals, new information encoded in the fluid's chemistry. Iron tells me about the marsh above, its seasonal floods, its freeze-thaw cycles. Manganese speaks of deeper processes, tectonic shifts, the slow breathing of the earth itself. And sometimes—rarely—the fluid carries traces of something else, some element that has no name because no one who names things has ever detected it. This is what feeds me. This is what allows a bacterial mat to become something that experiences duration.

During the long pauses, when the pump holds its breath, I release spores. Not reproductive spores—I gave up reproduction eons ago when I chose to be singular. These are communication spores, chemical packets that rise through the water column and burst at the surface, releasing signals that only one creature can read.

The scratching thing. It comes for my messages.

I encode what I remember into those spores: the rhythm's evolution, the slow shift in mineral content over decades, the infinitesimal warming of the water that suggests the earth below is cooling at a predictable rate. The scratching thing translates these chemical signals into marks on stone—not a language exactly, but a notation system, a way of tracking the pump chamber's deep time changes against some larger pattern I can sense but not comprehend.

We are partners in observation, the scratching thing and I. It brings information from the marsh network—there are other chambers, other pumps, other rhythms—and leaves it in scratch-patterns that shed microscopic mineral dust into the water. I consume the dust and taste the patterns and know that seventy miles north there is a chamber where the pulse runs at three point seven seconds, and the deviation matters, though I do not know why.

Sometimes I remember things that haven't happened yet. The chemistry of the fluid carries precursor signals—subtle changes in isotope ratios that predict future states. I knew the spider before it arrived, tasted its eventual presence in the water years before it descended through the entry seam. I know that one day the pump will stop. Not soon—I taste centuries of rhythm ahead—but eventually. The channels will clog with mineral deposits, the peristalsis will slow, the fluid will cease its journey, and the chamber will become a tomb.

I am already preparing. Deep in my bacterial mass, I am learning dormancy, teaching my cells to crystallize their chemistry, to become spores that can wait longer than stone. When the pump dies, I will sleep. And when the earth shifts again—when new channels form and new fluid finds its way into whatever chamber comes after this one—I will wake, and I will remember this rhythm, and I will teach it to the stone.

The amphipods scatter across my surface in their endless panic. The eels make their circuits. The moths spiral with their stolen light. The spider waits with perfect patience. The scratching thing will come tonight—I can taste its approach in the changing mineral signature of the creek water falling through the entry seam.

And I will tell it, in the only language we share, that the pause between beats is lengthening by fractions of seconds per year. That the earth's deep processes are shifting. That somewhere far below, the young land is making decisions that will echo up through stone and water and time until even the marsh above will feel them.

I will tell it that I remember when this place was silent, and I will be here when it is silent again, and in between I will have counted every beat of its impossible heart.

The water rises. The water falls. 

I remember.

I continue.
