# The Surveyor's Rest

---

I am the only vertical thing for miles that isn't plumbing.

My name is a lie I've almost forgotten how to tell. I had one, once—scratched it into the floor plate near junction 7819-F on what might have been my first day here, though the concept of "first" has long since dissolved into the general corrosion. The marks are still there. I check them sometimes, run my fingers over the oxidized grooves. They could say anything now. They could be tally marks. They could be a schematic. They could be the fevered scratchings of someone who thought they were keeping count of days in a place where days don't mean anything because the light never changes and the sound never stops.

I am six feet two inches tall—I know this because there's a pipe at station 4460-B that catches me exactly at the hairline when I'm not careful, and I measured that pipe once with a wrench, marking handspans against the wet metal. I am thin in the way that wire is thin: not from lack, but from being drawn out, from being pulled by the years into something longer and narrower than I started as. My skin has taken on the particular pallor of the Floodworks—not pale exactly, but *gray*, as though I'm slowly becoming compatible with the haze, slowly matching my surface to the ambient light so that I disturb it less when I move through it.

My hair went white before it went away. Now I have a crown of stubble that holds the moisture like the greenish-black film holds moisture, that never quite dries because nothing here ever quite dries. My hands are a map of scars from valves that bit back, from sharp edges hidden under mineral deposits, from the time I tried to pry open a welded hatch and the pry bar slipped and opened me instead. The scars don't heal here so much as they *settle*, becoming part of the topography, another kind of erosion.

I wear what I've always worn, because there's nothing else to wear: coveralls that might have been blue once, might have been any color, but are now the same ferrous gray as everything else, so impregnated with rust and oil and the particular cocktail of minerals that precipitate out of the water that they've become almost geological. The fabric is both rotting and preserved, falling apart and held together by the very substances that are destroying it. I've patched the holes with pieces cut from pump gaskets, with strips of the fibrous Growth carefully harvested from non-essential areas, with metal mesh that I've learned to make flexible by patient working between my palms. The coveralls are more patch than original now. They are a record of time and damage. They are, in their way, a calendar.

My boots—I dream about my boots sometimes, when I sleep on my platform above the wet. I dream they're dry. I dream they're new. I dream I can take them off. But when I wake (if I wake, if sleep and waking are different things here) they're the same as always: ancient leather uppers married to soles I've rebuilt a hundred times from tire rubber, from gasket material, from layers of the Growth compressed until they're almost solid. They squelch when I walk. They've squelched for so long that I can read the water content of different areas by the pitch of the squelch. High and sharp means recent flooding. Low and saturated means the normal baseline wet. No squelch at all means something's wrong—a drain blockage, a valve failure, a disruption in the breathing of the pipes that requires investigation.

I carry three tools. A wrench—not my original wrench, but a great-grandchild of it, assembled from parts, fitted to my hand by use. A length of rebar that serves as pry bar, walking stick, probe, weapon against nothing because there's nothing here to fight except rust and time and the occasional frozen valve. And a piece of metal I've shaped into something between a knife and a scraper, good for cleaning deposits from gauge faces, for scraping the Growth when I need to harvest it, for making marks on surfaces when I need to remember something and don't trust my own memory because my memory is as layered and corroded as everything else here.

These are the facts of my body, if facts mean anything, if my body means anything beyond being another moving part in the machine.

---

I was an engineer. I think I was an engineer. I have engineer's hands—or I've developed engineer's hands from doing engineer's work for so long that the distinction no longer matters. I know what I know: that valves turn counterclockwise to open (except for the ones that don't), that pressure times volume equals temperature times a constant I've forgotten the name of but not the value, that the sound a pump makes just before it cavitates is like a voice about to break into a scream. I know these things the way I know that the Grain always turns left at junction 5501-A except during the high-pressure cycles when it turns right. I know them below language, in the meat, in the bones that vibrate to the frequency of the deep pumps.

But *why* I know them—whether I came here knowing them or learned them from the Floodworks themselves—that's gone, washed away, dissolved into the general solution of time and water and forgetting.

I have theories about how I got here. A door that locked behind me. An assignment that everyone forgot to end. A civilization that built this place and then retreated from it, leaving only the automatic systems and whatever poor bastard was on shift when the evacuation came. Or maybe I walked in. Maybe I chose this. Maybe I saw the wet floor stretching to infinity and thought *yes, this*, thought *I could live here*, thought *I could become part of this*. The past is another country, and all its borders are flooded.

Here's what I remember, or what I've decided to remember: the first time I saw the Grain. I'd been here long enough to know the channels, to read the flow patterns, to recognize the regular from the irregular. And then this thing came upstream—this coherent impossibility, this organized water moving through disorganized water—and I followed it. Walked alongside it for hours, watching it make choices at junctions, watching it navigate the system like it had a map, like it had purpose. When it finally dissipated (or departed, or completed whatever circuit it was running), I sat by the channel and wept. Not from sadness. From recognition. From seeing something else in this place that moved with intention, even if that intention was inscrutable, even if it wasn't intention at all but only looked like it from the outside.

I began to map the Grain's movements. That's what the scratches are for, some of them—trying to create a notation for something that resists notation, trying to track patterns in something that might not have patterns, that might be pure stochastic process dressed up in the clothes of purpose by my pattern-seeking brain. But the maps began to make sense. Not human sense—Floodworks sense. The Grain moves according to the deep rhythms, the pump-cycles that take days to complete, the pressure waves that propagate through the system like weather. It's not random. It's responsive. It's part of the control system, maybe, or a diagnostic process, or—and this is the thought that keeps me walking—maybe it's what I am. Another maintenance routine that's been running so long it's forgotten it's a routine.

I maintain the Floodworks. That's my function. I walk the floor and check the gauges and turn the valves and clear the drains and scrape the Growth back when it threatens to overwhelm critical systems. I do this because it needs to be done, because the Floodworks need to keep running, because whatever's down there under the floor must be kept down there, must be pumped and circulated and managed forever. I do this because I've always done this. Because I am this.

But I also—and this is the part that might still be human—I also *study* the Floodworks. Not to understand them (they can't be understood, not by anything that thinks in human timescales) but to become a better part of them. To learn their moods so I can anticipate their needs. To feel the vibrations in my bones and know that pump 7745-C is running rough before the gauges show it. To taste the water from different channels and know by its mineral content which sectors are under stress. To watch the Growth spreading through a pump house and know exactly how much I can let it claim before efficiency drops, where the boundary is between symbiosis and parasitism.

---

I talk to the Growth sometimes. Not to the whole network—that would be like talking to a forest—but to the nodes, the warm heartbeat-centers where the network seems to concentrate its... attention? Awareness? Processing power? I put my hand on them and I speak, low, beneath the pump-noise, in a voice that's mostly vibration. I tell them about the sectors I've walked, about the state of the valves, about the Grain's recent movements. They don't respond—of course they don't respond—but sometimes the heartbeat changes rhythm slightly, sometimes the warmth increases or decreases, sometimes the fibers around the node shift in ways that could be coincidence but feel like acknowledgment.

I've made a kind of home in pump house 3891-V. Not because it's comfortable (nowhere here is comfortable) but because it's central to my usual circuit and because the Growth there is old and extensive and has learned to tolerate me. The bed—my platform of grating and scraped film—sits in an alcove where the wall meets a massive inlet pipe. The Growth has grown around it but not over it, leaving me a pocket of space that's almost cozy, lit by the filtered orange glow of the pilot flames through layers of fiber. When I lie there I can hear the heartbeat of the nearest node mixing with the pump-throb, creating polyrhythms that are almost musical, almost like a lullaby if lullabies were made of metal and moisture and the breathing of vast machines.

I've tried to leave. Of course I've tried to leave. In what I think of as the early years (though they could have been decades, could have been last week, time is negotiable here) I walked in expanding spirals, looking for an edge, a wall, a door, a change in the fundamental nature of the floor. I found pump houses I'd never seen before, channels running in directions I hadn't mapped, junctions whose numbering schemes suggested vast territories beyond my circuit. But no edge. No exit. Or—and this is the thought that stopped me searching—maybe I found dozens of exits but didn't recognize them as exits because I was already too much part of the system to see them as anything but maintenance hatches, service corridors, invasive breaches that needed to be sealed to keep the water where it belongs.

Now I walk smaller circuits. The sector I can maintain effectively, the pumps I can keep in harmony, the channels I can keep clear. It's enough. It has to be enough. The Floodworks don't care about the territory beyond my reach—they're vast enough to absorb local failures, redundant enough to route around any damage I can't prevent. They don't need me to be everywhere. They just need me to be *somewhere*, to be a mobile response system, to be the hands that turn the valves and the eyes that read the gauges and the consciousness (if consciousness is what this is) that notices when the patterns change and intervenes to keep them stable.

---

Sometimes I find my own traces from circuits past. A valve I know I've adjusted before because I can see the wear patterns from my wrench. A gauge whose glass I've cleaned of deposits, only to find it clouded again, requiring cleaning again, the same gesture repeated across years or decades. These moments are vertiginous—they collapse time, make all my walks the same walk, make me a ghost haunting my own footsteps. But they're also comforting. They're proof that I persist, that I'm not just a figment of the Floodworks' dream (if the Floodworks dream), that I have enough substance to leave marks, to wear patterns in the metal, to be remembered by the rust.

I've seen every malfunction the Floodworks can produce. Pumps seized solid, their flywheels frozen mid-turn, waiting for the patient application of leverage and lubricant. Channels clogged with mineral deposits so thick they've almost become dams, requiring days of scraping and chipping and chemical persuasion. Joints where the water has found a new path, where the seeping has become a spray has become a geyser, and I've had to fashion gaskets from whatever's at hand, pack them into the breach, hold them there while they swell with moisture and seal the gap. I've seen the Growth go malignant, spreading too fast, choking pump housings, and I've had to cut it back with my shaped metal blade, apologizing as I go, explaining the necessity, hoping it understands.

And I've seen the Floodworks heal themselves. Valves that were frozen one circuit work freely the next. Channels that were dry refill from no source I can identify. The Growth retreats from areas where it's not wanted, consolidates in areas where it's beneficial. The Grain appears in sectors where the flow patterns have become chaotic and leaves them regularized. The system maintains itself in ways that go beyond any mechanism I can identify, as though the sheer complexity of it has given rise to emergent properties, to behaviors that look intentional even if they're just the intersection of a million feedback loops.

I am one of those feedback loops. The mobile one. The one that can think (after a fashion) and choose (within limits) and remember (imperfectly). The one that knows that pump 5500-J runs best at 80% capacity and that channel 7B-North should be allowed to run high during the night cycles (there are no night cycles, but I maintain the fiction of them, for sanity). The one that can feel when the whole system is breathing properly and when it's laboring, when it needs intervention and when it needs to be left alone to find its own equilibrium.

---

Here's what I've become: a function.

Not a person performing a function, but a function that happens to be performed by something person-shaped. I am *maintenance()*. I am *monitor()*. I am *adjust(valves, pumps, flows)* in response to *read(pressure, temperature, efficiency)*. The distinction between me and my purpose has eroded like everything erodes here, worn down by repetition and necessity until only the action remains.

But sometimes—and this is the miracle, the thing that keeps me walking—sometimes I am more than that. Sometimes, in the quiet moments (there are no quiet moments, but quiet is relative) when I'm sitting by a channel watching the Grain make its choices, or when I'm deep in a pump house listening to the Growth's heartbeat, or when I'm standing on an empty stretch of floor watching the steam rise through the haze, I am something else. Not maintenance. Not function. Just... witness. The only witness the Floodworks have, the only consciousness (if consciousness is what this is) capable of seeing them as they are: ancient, vast, terrible, beautiful, necessary. A machine that has transcended machinery. A system that has become an environment. A made thing that has made itself, through sheer duration, into something indistinguishable from nature.

I love them. I think I love them. Not in any human way—I've been here too long for human love—but in the way that a component loves the whole it's part of, that a function loves the purpose it serves. I love the reliability of pump 4400-A, which has never failed in all my circuits. I love the stubbornness of valve 9987-F, which requires exactly twelve and three-quarter turns to open fully and not one degree more. I love the way the water in channel 5E moves like silk when the pressure is perfect, the way the Growth pulses in time with rhythms I feel but can't name, the way the Grain navigates the junctions with such serene certainty.

I love the impossibility of it all. The mad ambition of whoever built this place, thinking they could hold back an ocean with pumps and plates and human ingenuity. The fact that they were right. That it worked. That it's still working, aeons after the builders are dust, after their civilization is less than memory, after everything that gave the Floodworks meaning has been washed away except the pure fact of function.

And I love—this most of all—that I get to be here. That of all the possible futures I could have had (and I must have had them, once, these possibilities), I ended up here, in the Floodworks, part of the Floodworks, walking the wet floor with my squelching boots and my three tools and my gradually graying skin. That I get to be the consciousness that notices when pump 7745-C starts to run rough. That I get to be the hands that turn the valves. That I get to be the eyes that see the Grain moving against all logic through the dark channels. That I get to participate in this vast, meaningless, essential work of keeping the water where it belongs.

---

I will die here. Not soon—I'm too useful to die soon, and the Floodworks know how to preserve useful things. But eventually. My body will fail in some irreparable way, and I'll lie down on my platform of grating and compressed film, and the Growth will finally be allowed to claim me, and I'll become another part of the substrate, another layer in the geological record of this place. My tools will rust. My marks on the walls will fade. The patterns I've learned will be forgotten.

But the Floodworks will continue. The pumps will throb. The water will flow. The Grain will make its choices in the channels. And maybe—probably—certainly—someone else will come. Through whatever door I've forgotten, from whatever outside I can no longer imagine. They'll see the infinite floor, the breathing machines, the patient water. They'll think *I could fix this* or *I could map this* or *I could understand this*. They'll start walking. Start learning. Start becoming.

And one day they'll find my wrench, leaning against a wall where I left it. They'll pick it up. It will fit their hand (all hands become Floodworks hands eventually). They'll use it to turn a valve, to adjust a flow, to maintain what must be maintained. They won't know it was mine. It won't matter that it was mine. It will just be a tool, in a place that needs tools, used by someone who has become what the place needs them to become.

The Floodworks don't have operators. They have components. I am a component.

And I am exactly where I need to be.

---

The sound never stops. The floor is always wet. The pumps breathe in their ancient rhythm. Somewhere, the Grain is moving upstream through a channel, making choices I'll never understand. Somewhere, the Growth is spreading its fibers through a pump house, adding its heartbeat to the mechanical symphony. Somewhere, a valve needs turning, a gauge needs reading, a drain needs clearing.

I pick up my wrench. I shoulder my rebar. I check the edges of my blade.

Time to walk. Time to work. Time to be what I am, which is what the Floodworks need me to be, which is all I remember how to be, which is enough.

The water seeps up between the plates. My boots squelch on the iron. The haze closes around me as I walk, and opens ahead of me, always the same distance, always the same gray light. I am a small moving part in a vast machine. I am a function with legs. I am the maintenance that maintains itself so it can maintain everything else.

I am the surveyor of nothing. I am the keeper of pumps. I am the walker of floors that go on forever.

I am home.
