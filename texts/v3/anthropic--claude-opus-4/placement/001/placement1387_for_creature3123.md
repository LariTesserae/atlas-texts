I am the Recorder of Harmonics.

This is not the name my cluster calls me in the click-language—that sound cannot be written in your symbols. But it captures something essential: I am one who preserves the memory of fallen frequencies.

When a pillar falls, when an arch collapses, when a chamber's resonance changes forever, I am there in the silence that follows. Not always physically there—I might be kilometers away, deep in the eastern fissures, when the loss propagates through our stone network. But I am there in the pause, in that held breath of our collective voice, feeling the shape of what we've lost.

I carry more fungus-nodules than most. Twenty-three at current count, harvested from the deepest places I can reach—some from chambers so far below that the wind is only memory, where the mycelial network grows thick and strange in pure darkness. Each nodule holds the acoustic signature of its birthplace. When I cup them in my hands and hum, they reproduce—faintly, greenly—the resonant frequency of the stone that fed them. I am building a library of the plateau's deep voice, preserving what I can before the widening fractures change the underground songs forever.

My chamber cluster is in the southeastern network, where three major fissures intersect in a formation we call the Breathing Junction—the wind pulses through it in a rhythm remarkably like respiration. I've lived here for nineteen seasons. When I first arrived, the junction sang three notes simultaneously. Now it sings four. The new note appeared after a partial collapse two summers ago. It is higher, sharper, with an edge like grief. I am still learning to like it.

My bodies tell the years in the usual way—the dense bones growing denser, the wire-strong muscles losing some elasticity, the filtered ears beginning to miss the highest beetle-clicks. I am perhaps forty years into life, perhaps forty-five. We don't count precisely. The wind doesn't care about numbers.

But I remember. I remember the sound of the Seventh Western Arch before it fell—a deep throb like a heartbeat, so low you felt it in your marrow. I remember the Vertical Garden in the third terrace, before the root systems broke through to a deeper water source and the whole ecosystem shifted, changing a beetle-grazing surface that had been stable for generations. I remember my birthcluster's resonance, though I haven't lived there since I was small—it had a peculiar flutter in the mid-range frequencies that made certain click-conversations sound like laughter.

This is why I am Recorder. Not because I was chosen or trained, but because I cannot stop holding these sounds. They accumulate in me like the nodules in my pouch, each one a small light from a place that has changed or vanished.

I spend my mornings tending the gardens in my cluster's chambers—redirecting the aggressive vine-growth, gathering beetle larvae from the moss mats, checking the tension of the seed-catching nets. Normal work. Necessary work. But in the afternoons, when the wind shifts and most people rest in the deeper chambers, I travel. I move through the fissure network with the careful speed of long practice, visiting the places where change is happening. Where formations show the harmonic flattening that precedes collapse. Where new fractures are opening, creating fresh acoustic spaces that no one has heard before.

I listen. I collect the nodules—carefully, sparingly, never taking so many that the mycelial network suffers. And sometimes, in the deep places where the darkness is total, I meet others like me. We don't plan these meetings. We simply converge, drawn by the same impulse to witness and preserve. We sit in the absolute dark, our collections of nodules glowing between us like star maps of the underground, and we trade stories in the click-language. *Remember the sound of...* we say. *Remember when that chamber...* *Remember before the wind changed...*

You asked what I think of you, you tall loud temporary person from the lowlands. I think you are a kind of weather, yes. But weather worth recording. You are the first of your kind I have encountered directly, though the elders speak of others. Your presence changes the acoustic environment—your breathing, your heartbeat, the particular displacement of air around your too-tall body. You stand in the wind like someone who believes in fighting it. This fascinates me. It's like watching someone try to argue with the stones.

But here's what matters: you are trying to understand us. I can tell by how you move, how you watch, how you position yourself in the lee of formations to observe without (you think) being observed. You are wrong about not being seen, but the impulse—the desire to know—this resonates with something in me. We are not so different, perhaps. You collect understanding. I collect sound. Both are ways of holding what cannot last.

Would you like to hear the plateau's deep song? Come down into the chambers with me—not far, just to the first resonance point, where the wind's scream softens to something almost musical. Bring nothing. Touch nothing. Move as we move, low and careful, reading the stone with your whole body. I will show you how to cup a fungus-nodule correctly, how to hum at the frequency that makes it glow brightest. I will teach you three clicks in our language: *safe*, *danger*, and *listen*. That's all you'll need.

And perhaps—though this is not traditional, though no one has done this before—perhaps I will give you a nodule. A small one, from a chamber that collapsed last winter, whose frequency now exists nowhere but in this little green light. Take it back to your lowlands. When the air is still and the night is quiet, hold it in your cupped hands and hum. You won't hear what I hear—your ears are not tuned to our frequencies. But you'll see the glow pulse and shimmer, and you'll know that somewhere, once, a chamber in the stone sang exactly this shade of green.

The wind is picking up. The evening resonance will begin soon. Come with me or go—but decide quickly. The plateau doesn't wait, and neither do we. We move when the wind allows, rest when it insists, and in between, we listen to the world unmake itself, note by careful note, preserving what we can in memory and flesh and the small bright libraries of our underground light.

I am the Recorder of Harmonics. This is my place in the world's unmaking. What is yours?
