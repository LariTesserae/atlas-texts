In the breathing city, I am a Cambium Keeper.

I exist in the space between what was and what will be — that thin generative layer where bark meets heartwood, where growth decisions are made one cell at a time. My body is more transition than substance: skin that can't decide if it's moss or membrane, bones going woody at different rates so that my left arm bends like willow while my right creaks with oak-stiffness. I walk sideways through the city's walls, slipping between the growth rings where past and future touch.

My dwelling is not a dwelling but a perpetual state of almost-becoming. It exists in the cambium layer of a district-tree, accessible only during the moments of active growth. Sometimes this is dawn, sometimes midnight, sometimes the peculiar not-time that happens during the city's digestive phases. To find my home, you must catch the building mid-thought, mid-breath, mid-decision about what shape it wants to be. The entrance appears as a vertical slit in the bark that wasn't there a moment ago and won't be there a moment hence.

Inside, space operates on tree-logic. The floor is a cross-section of time — step left and you're in the room as it was a decade ago, step right and you're in its next century's configuration. My furniture exists in various stages of completion: chairs half-emerged from the floor, a table that's mostly intention, shelves that fruit books which haven't been written yet but whose words already yellow on pages made of compressed possibility.

I cultivate memories that aren't mine. This is my function, my calling, my curse — to tend the spaces between the city's growth rings where experiences accumulate like sap. When someone becomes a building, their human memories don't disappear but sink into the cambium, where they slowly transform into something plants can understand. I harvest these hybrid thoughts, these moments of translation between human and vegetable consciousness.

My tools are my fingernails, grown long and sharp and sensitive, able to detect the chemical signature of different memory-types. Joy leaves crystalline deposits. Sorrow runs in dark veins. Love grows in spirals that follow the same mathematics as shells and galaxies. I tap these memories like maple syrup, filling vials made from my own hollow bones with concentrated experience that I trade to the deep-dwellers who've forgotten what humanity tastes like.

The work is dangerous. Sometimes I tap too deep and hit a memory that's still transforming, still raw with human nerve-endings. These wild memories can take root in me, growing backwards through my consciousness until I'm living someone else's yesterday. I once spent three weeks convinced I was a carpenter from before the change, trying to cut the city's wood-flesh with tools that turned to leaves in my hands. Another time, I accidentally released a memory of fire — real fire, from when such things existed — and had to contain it in my own body until it burned itself out, leaving scars that still photosynthesize differently than the rest of me.

My hair grows in temporal loops, each strand a different age, some of them reaching into next week while others are still catching up from last year. I braid these temporal inconsistencies into patterns that help me navigate the city's growth-time. When I need to find a specific memory-layer, I follow the pull of the correct strand, letting future-hair guide me to where past-wood holds what I seek.

I speak all the languages of transition: the chemical pidgin that forms when human words meet plant pheromones, the growth-glossolalia that buildings use during their multiplication phases, the silent syntax of root-touch, the color-codes of seasonal change. My voice carries differently depending on the medium — through air it sounds like wind in leaves, through wood like the groaning of growth, through soil like the whisper of decomposition.

Other inhabitants avoid me, partly from superstition, partly from the discomfort of seeing someone who lives so literally between states. I am their reminder that the transformation is never complete, never stable, always ongoing. When they look at me, they see their own cambium layers, the places where they're still deciding what to become.

But the children seek me out. They come during their molting seasons, when their bark-skin loosens and their humanity shows pink and vulnerable underneath. I teach them the art of conscious growth, how to direct their transformation rather than merely endure it. In my almost-home, they practice being partially human, partially plant, learning to exist in the productive discomfort of incompleteness.

I collect the city's growing pains — literal pains that manifest as thorns that weep, as knots that throb, as grain-patterns that spell out agonies in languages only trees understand. These pains must be harvested before they sink too deep and poison the heartwood. I wear them as a vest of thorns, each one a small crisis prevented, a growth-trauma transformed into wearable memory.

My own memories have become unreliable, confused with the thousands I've handled. I remember being planted as a seed, though I know I was born human. I remember the taste of soil from below and sun from above. I remember being furniture before I was a person, or perhaps after, or perhaps this is someone else's memory that took root too deep to extract.

At night, I climb to where the city's cambium layer meets the sky — that impossible space where growth hasn't decided if it wants to be air or architecture. There, I tend the memory gardens where the city dreams its expansion. I prune the nightmares that would grow tumor-buildings, fertilize the hope-seeds that will become hospitals and schools, transplant good memories from dying districts to new growth zones.

Sometimes I find memories that predate the city, from when this was just earth and the possibility of green. These proto-memories are dangerous and precious — they contain the template for transformation itself, the moment when the first settler put down roots in more than metaphor. I guard these carefully, knowing that if they're damaged, the entire city might forget how to be itself.

I am the keeper of the space between, the gardener of transitions, the one who ensures that transformation has room to happen. In a city where everything is always becoming something else, I maintain the sacred instability that allows change. My home is a hyphen, my body is a semicolon, my work is the cultivation of infinite middles with no beginning and no end.

This is what I am: neither human nor plant but the continuous conversation between them, the translator who speaks both languages by speaking neither, the keeper of the growing edge where all possibilities touch and nothing is ever finished becoming.
