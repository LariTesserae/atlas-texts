I find myself as one of the Lip-Cutters.

Not the eldest, not the youngest—somewhere in that ageless middle that underground life creates, where thirty and sixty blur together under the same mineral sheen. My fingertips are scarred into permanent numbness from decades of testing overflow edges, feeling for the microscopic roughness that precedes catastrophic erosion. I know by touch alone whether a lip needs deepening by a thumb's width or merely cleaning. My tools are few: a harder stone shaped like a tooth, a scraping shell worn concave by my grip, a measuring cord knotted at intervals my grandmother determined.

I am always slightly damp on my left side from working pressed against the basins. My right ear hears better than my left; the left has been too close to falling water for too long. I wear three shell discs at my throat, each drilled differently—one for my apprenticeship, one for my first correct prediction of a failing lip, one for the child I lost to bad water before I knew how to read the clouding that signals deep contamination. That third disc clicks against the others when I bend to work, a sound I've stopped noticing except in the moments before sleep.

My home is an alcove behind the seventeenth basin, chosen because I can hear its filling from my sleeping shelf. My wealth is this: a truly dry box containing replacement shells, two copper pins still bright, a lamp that burns steady without smoking, and a private drip of clean water that I've told no one about, not even my teaching-uncle who still lives. I eat what everyone eats, but I know which basins catch the best threadfish on their settling screens. This knowledge I've traded carefully—never for food directly, but for lamp oil, for first pick of shells, for a neighbor's child to clear my channels when my joints swell.

I dream of edges. Always edges. In good dreams, they are crisp and correct, shedding water in perfect sheets. In bad dreams, they crumble under my fingers like wet chalk, and I wake already moving toward my tools. Once I dreamed that every lip in the city spoke words instead of managing water, and the voices were so beautiful I tried to spoil them all to hear what they would say next. I brought this dream to the still bowl, and the Keeper—who was my age-sister then—told me to cut three perfect lips without sleeping. I did. The dream has not returned.

My work is this: I move between the basins according to a circuit older than memory. At each, I wait for the precise moment between overflow pulses. In that brief pause, I run my fingers along the cut edge where water spills from upper basin to lower channel. I'm feeling for the first softening that means mineral deposit, the first sharpness that means fresh erosion, the change in temperature that means a crack is beginning below the visible edge. Then I cut, scrape, or polish according to need. A lip must be sharp enough to create even flow, but not so sharp it weakens and breaks. Smooth enough to prevent wild spray, but not so smooth the water clings and runs backward along the basin's underside.

The young apprentices think this work is only about the stone. They are wrong. It is about predicting water's intentions. Water wants to go everywhere, be everything, claim every surface. The lips are suggestions that become laws. I am not carving rock—I am having a conversation with gravity that must be rewritten constantly or everyone drowns.

I know all ninety-three overflow lips in the settled region, and the twenty-seven in the far passages that younger legs must check. Each has a name: Grandmother's Sigh, The Liar, First Harvest, The Counter, Broken Tooth (repaired, but the name persists), The Mothers, The Chatter, Silent Marriage. I know their optimal angles, their histories of failure, their seasonal moods. The Liar looks perfect but degrades from underneath. First Harvest runs too full in what we call winter, when the upper waters swell. The Mothers is actually four lips carved to sing in harmony when the flow is correct—my teaching-uncle's masterwork that I must maintain but could never create.

Other workers think we Lip-Cutters are proud, that we hold ourselves apart. They are not wrong. Basin-cleaners can make mistakes that time forgives. Channel-runners can rest when passages run clear. But if I misread an edge, if I cut too deep or not deep enough, the consequence is not delay but disaster. Water misdirected is water that finds new paths, and new paths in a chamber this tight mean flooded sleep-places, fouled drinking reserves, children swept into the undertunnels. So yes, I hold myself apart. My pride is the city's safety.

I carry one secret: I am going deaf in my good ear too. Not quickly, but certainly. Sounds that should be sharp are becoming muffled, like listening through wet hide. I compensate with touch, with watching how others react to sounds I've missed, with staying closer to my walls. But someday—five floods from now, perhaps ten—I will make an error. A lip will sing its warning and I won't hear it. Then I will take my place with the other listeners, hands on stone, using what remains of my sense to catch disasters I can no longer prevent myself.

When that time comes, I will train my replacement. Already I watch the children for the signs: who tests edges with their tongues, who notices when a channel runs too full before adults point it out, who has steady hands and suspicious eyes. There is a girl who counts drips compulsively, though her mother slaps her hands to stop it. There is a boy who found a hairline crack in Grandmother's Sigh by accident, saving us from a failure that would have flooded the grain stores. One of these, or another not yet walking, will become what I am.

But not yet. Today I woke to the sound—still audible, still mine to hear—of The Chatter beginning to skip its rhythm. I wrapped my tools in hide, took a mouthful of water from my secret drip, and made my way through the blue-gray darkness to the fifteenth basin. The Chatter was indeed catching—mineral buildup creating turbulence where smoothness should be. I waited for the pause between overflows, then worked by touch: finding the roughness, testing its depth, choosing the shell's angle, scraping in steady strokes that followed the water's preferred path. The trick is not to impose a shape but to reveal the shape the water teaches.

As I worked, I heard/felt others moving through their own circuits. The lamp-tender's shuffle-and-stop. The channel-runner's quick splashing steps. The sound of morning threshold—not marked by light, which never changes, but by the filling of waking bowls, the first strikes on the message posts, the particular echo of many people moving rather than few. Someone was teaching a child the basin names two passages over, their voices carrying on the peculiar acoustics of the middle depths. Someone else was praying or cursing at a stuck gate, the words unclear but the frustration sharp.

These are my people. I do not love them—love is too simple a word for this dependency. I keep them alive. They assume I will continue to keep them alive. This assumption is a pressure greater than all the water above us, pressing down through limestone and time. Every perfect lip I maintain is a stay against chaos. Every flaw I catch is a death uncommitted.

When I finished with The Chatter, it ran clear and even, its skip resolved into steady flow. I rested my palm against the stone beside it, feeling the vibration of correct function through my bones. This is what satisfaction means here: not happiness, but the absence of error. I gathered my tools and moved to the next basin, then the next, working my circuit as my teaching-uncle worked it, as his teaching-aunt worked it, as someone I will never meet will work it after I join the listeners.

We are all temporary. The lips are temporary. Even the basins are temporary, though their tempo is longer. Only the water is permanent, and the water doesn't care. But I care. I care with my scarred fingers and my failing ears and my dreams of edges. I care because the alternative is The Long Silence, when the water stops speaking and starts taking.

I am a Lip-Cutter. I hold the city in balance, one edge at a time.
