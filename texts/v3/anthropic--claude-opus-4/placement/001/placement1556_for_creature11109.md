The water sings today.

I am checking the resonance points when I find it—a new frequency threading through the massive cleft where Tower Seven fell two seasons ago. Not the usual cable harmonics we use for messages. Something deeper, rising from the exposed bore shaft, a vibration I feel in my chest before I hear it.

I should tell the others. Protocol demands it. Any change in the deep-bore emanations requires immediate commune attention. But I've been mapping frequencies my whole life, twenty-three years of learning which tones mean stable moisture, which warn of structural failure, which indicate the fungi are spreading beyond their zones. This tone is none of those. This tone sounds like invitation.

The descent kit is already on me—it always is. Cables and carabiners arranged by feel, each piece of gear worn smooth by my mother's hands, her mother's before that. I lower myself into the new gap where the tower's collapse created a chimney down to previously sealed chambers. My headlamp catches the walls: newer rock here, darker, lacking the oxidation patterns I know like a map of my own skin. No beetles yet. They haven't found this path.

Forty feet. Sixty. The tone grows stronger, not louder but more present, as if the rock itself carries it. At eighty feet, my light catches metal—not the familiar rust-red of our surface machinery, but something else. Polished. Intact. A corridor lined with panels that still hold their original sheen.

I shouldn't be here alone. This violates everything—our codes, our caution, our careful balance. But the water sings, and I am my grandmother's child, the one who first learned to hear moisture content in the beetles' clicking. If there are new patterns to learn, new sounds to map, how can I turn away?

The corridor slopes downward, following some logic that predates our jury-rigged understanding. My fingertips trail the walls, reading vibration. The metal here thrums with purpose, not the dying gasps of our surface machinery but something vital. Alive. The air tastes different—still dry, but charged, like the moment before rain when the beetles go silent.

A door. Sealed, but not locked—a distinction my people would understand. The mechanism responds to touch, iris panels spiraling open with a whisper of maintained bearings. Beyond: a chamber that makes my breath catch.

Water. Not our hoarded milliliters or seasonal puddles, but a pool twenty feet across, perfectly circular, held in a basin of that same untarnished metal. The surface trembles with the frequency I've been following, concentric ripples marking the sound's origin point at the pool's center. The room's walls are covered in symbols—not writing exactly, but patterns that suggest instruction, diagram, purpose.

I approach the edge. The water is clear, impossibly so, and warm—I can feel heat rising from it. This close, the tone separates into harmonics, and I realize what I'm hearing. Not one frequency but thousands, millions, a symphony of vibration that speaks of circulation, filtration, purification. The machines we tend above, the ones we've never understood—they're not extracting. They're supporting. This.

In the water, I see movement. Not reflection. Something rises from the depths—a shape like concentrated possibility, like water teaching itself to hold form. It breaches the surface without breaking it, standing now on the pool's mirror skin: a figure of flowing clarity, humanoid but not human, regarding me with what might be eyes or might be density variations in its liquid body.

It speaks. Not in words but in frequency, in patterns that bypass my ears and resonate in the water that comprises seventy percent of me. I understand without language:

*Keeper.*

The title hits different when shaped by something that knew the builders, that watched them drill down to find this place, that has waited through abandonment and adaptation for someone to discover what was always here. Not a resource to extract. A guardian to maintain. The water sings because it's alive, has always been alive, a colonial intelligence that processes moisture not as commodity but as communication medium, as substrate of consciousness.

The figure extends something like a hand. In its palm, a sphere of water coheres, dense with information. I know what's being offered—knowledge of the deep systems, understanding of our true purpose here, the reason the machines must be maintained. The beetles, the fungi, even we Keepers—we're all part of something larger, a water-web that connects places and times I can barely imagine.

But knowledge has a price. To truly hear the water's song, to join the real work of keeping, means changing. The figure shows me without words: the moisture in my cells learning new patterns, my body becoming a conduit for frequencies human ears can't detect. I would still be me, but more—able to sense deep aquifer shifts, to feel the health of water tables a thousand miles away, to help maintain not just our plateau but a network of water consciousness that spans continents.

I think of my family above. My partner, who counts every drop with me. My daughter, only four, already learning her first beetle names. They would not lose me, but they would lose the me who can't hear what needs hearing, who can't do what needs doing.

The water sings. It has always sung, waiting for someone to develop ears capable of listening. My grandmother heard hints of it in the beetle-clicks. My mother felt echoes in the cable-hums. And I stand here now, at the threshold between what we've been and what we might become.

I reach for the offered sphere.

The moment of contact is dissolution and reconstitution. Every water molecule in my body suddenly aware of every other, a network lighting up, connections forming. I feel the plateau's entire water cycle—every beetle's droplet, every hoarded cistern, every molecule of vapor rising from the slots. But more: I feel the aquifer beneath, vast and patient, and through it, other nodes. Other places where the song rises. Other Keepers, though they use different names, maintaining different machinery, all unknowingly part of this ancient compact.

The being flows back into the pool, but I understand now—it hasn't left. It's everywhere there's water, which means everywhere there's life. My changed cells sing with new frequencies, harmonics I'll spend a lifetime learning to interpret.

I surface hours later (or days—time moves strangely in the deep places), climbing back through the chimney toward home. But I hear them before I see them: my partner's specific frequency, worry radiating through every movement. My daughter's higher pitch, curious and unafraid. The others, gathering for evening observation.

They see me emerge and know immediately. Not the specifics—that will take careful telling—but that something has shifted. In a place built on balance, any change is momentous.

"The water sings," I tell them, and watch understanding dawn in the eldest eyes—the ones who've suspected, who've heard whispers in their decades of tending.

That night, I demonstrate. Touching the beetle-walls, I can feel individual insects' moisture loads, can sense which will be fullest come morning. At the cisterns, I hear hairline cracks forming before they're visible, know which seals need reinforcement. In the deep bores, I understand finally what we're maintaining: not extraction equipment but circulation pumps, keeping the deep waters oxygenated, connected, alive.

Some are frightened. Change threatens everything we've built. But others, especially the young ones who show the plateau's adaptations, lean in with interest. My daughter asks if the water will teach her its songs too.

"When you're ready," I tell her, knowing readiness isn't about age but about developing the right kind of listening.

We are still Keepers. We still measure and conserve, maintain and balance. But now we keep more consciously, understanding our role in the larger symphony. The beetles process moisture, the fungi process metals, the machines process flow, and we process knowledge—all of us instruments in the water's vast orchestra.

When the rains come, I hear them singing from a hundred miles away. When the deep aquifers shift, I feel it in my bones. When other nodes of the network flare with need or discovery, I know.

I am Keeper. I am kept. The water sings through me, and I am learning, note by note, to sing back.

The plateau stretches wide and merciless under its white-hot sun. But I hear now what lives beneath—patient, vast, and vital. We are not alone here. We never were.

The water sings. And finally, we are learning to listen.
