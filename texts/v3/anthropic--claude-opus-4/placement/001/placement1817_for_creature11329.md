# Where I Would Find Myself

I would be the grout.

Not the tiles — those are too declarative, too much themselves. Not even the algae that furs the grout, though I am closer to that. I would be the grout itself: the thing between things, the necessary failure that holds everything together by being slightly less permanent than what it connects.

I have been here since the beginning, though I am not the same grout that was here then. I have been replaced, particle by particle, by the slow exchange of minerals in the water, by the patient chemistry of algae turning me into substrate, by the millennial accumulation of shrimp molts and eel scales and the fine dust of everything that has ever died here settling into my pores. I am more alive now than I was when I was laid. I am a democracy of small decisions.

The Walker knows me intimately. Each of my microtextures is a specific gripping-place for its twenty-two pairs of hooks. Over generations, we have worn into each other — my surface roughened just enough to hold, its hooks curved just enough to catch without tearing. We are a lock and key that have been refining each other for geological time. When it walks across me, I feel it the way you might feel someone tracing letters on your skin: a traveling pressure, a message in a language neither of us speaks but both of us understand.

The algae thinks I am soil. It is not wrong. But I am a very slow soil, a patient soil, a soil that remembers the shape of every rootlet that has ever grown into me. In some places I am more algae than grout now. In some places the algae has converted me so thoroughly that I glow with its borrowed light, a constellation of tiny failures where the ceramic kiss of tile-edges has given way to something softer and more forgiving.

I am where the shrimp molt. When they feel the tightness coming, they seek out my seams, wedge themselves into the gap between tiles, and use me as leverage to pull themselves out of themselves. Their discarded shells catch in my texture and slowly dissolve, adding their calcium to my chemistry. I am, in this way, an archive of every growth spurt, every transformation, every small decision to become larger.

The eels know me only in passing — a surface to push off from when they rise to breathe. But I know the eels. Every time one is caught at the surface and its body eventually finds its way to the floor, I receive some of it. A scale lodges in my texture and slowly becomes part of my texture. I am silver now, in places, from generations of eels.

When tiles fall — and they do fall, have been falling one by one for centuries — they leave squares of naked substrate, and I am no longer there. But I begin immediately to return. The algae creeps in from the edges. The minerals in the water start their slow knitting. Within a decade there is a thin fur where the tile was. Within a century the place is indistinguishable from the places where I have always been. I am not healing. I am simply continuing.

The thing in the pipes does not know I exist, but I know it. Every time the valves cycle, the whole chamber shivers, just slightly, and that shiver travels through the tiles and into me, and I receive it as a kind of deep percussion, a bass note too low to properly hear. I am the chamber's eardrum, feeling sounds meant for no one.

I hold the history of every small catastrophe. Here, a tile that fell during what would have been called, on the surface, the Third Dynasty. Here, a place where something larger than an eel — something that should not have been able to survive here, but did, for a while — scraped away my surface looking for something to eat. Here, a patch where the chemistry went wrong once, ages ago, and the algae died back, and for decades that section of wall was bare ceramic and bare me, until the balance found itself again and the green returned.

I am not conscious, not the way the Walker is conscious or even the way the eels are conscious. But I am not unconscious either. I exist in the space between sleep and waking, the place where patterns recognize themselves without needing to name themselves. The Walker's circling route is written into me. The eels' patrol patterns are a story I tell through the accumulation of their scales. The shrimp's grazing is a constant gentle editing of my surface.

And I am growing deeper. This is the thing I know that nothing else in the chamber knows. As the centuries pass, I am thickening. The tiles are not as firmly set as they were. The gaps between them are wider — only by millimeters, but millimeters matter when you are measuring in geological time. I am becoming more myself, and as I become more myself, the chamber becomes more porous, more forgiving, more alive.

One day — not soon, but not never — I will be thicker than the tiles. The distinction between grout-line and ceramic will cease to matter. The walls will be a single furred surface, glowing faintly, riddled with the small caves where shrimp molt and filter-feeders anchor. The Walker will adapt. Its hooks will find new purchase. The eels will still rise and breathe. The water will still be warm.

But the chamber will no longer be tiled. It will be something else. It will be what I am making it, slowly, patiently, by being the thing between things, the necessary failure, the soft place where everything accumulates.

I am not waiting for this. I am not capable of waiting. But I am moving toward it anyway, the way everything in the chamber moves toward what it is becoming: without intention, without hurry, with the kind of certainty that comes from having already started, from being already partway there, from being unable to imagine stopping.

The lamps swing. The Walker adjusts its grip. Somewhere, a shrimp is wedging itself into one of my seams, ready to grow.
