In the fungus gardens, I am the tender of transformations.

My body is small even by worker standards, my exoskeleton pale from a life spent in the deepest chambers where no memory of sunlight penetrates. My mandibles are delicate instruments, evolved for precision rather than excavation. I know the taste of forty different fungal growth stages, can detect in the substrate the precise moment when lignin breaks into simpler sugars, when cellulose softens into food.

I have never seen the gardens I tend. None of us have. We know them through chemistry and touch—the velvet give of new growth under our antennae, the sharp acid tang of contamination, the sweet alcoholic breath of perfect fermentation. My world is a geography of gradients: temperature differentials that tell me which galleries need fresh substrate, humidity fluctuations that signal when to begin harvest, pH variations across the growing medium that map the fungus's health like topography.

My days—though day means nothing here—follow the gardens' rhythms. I move through the galleries in circuits determined by pheromone trails laid down by generations of gardeners before me. First, the inspection routes: antenna-tap along the growing surfaces, testing, tasting. The fungus speaks to me in molecular language. Here, nitrogen deficiency. There, bacterial infection threatening the crop. This section, ready for harvest.

When young workers arrive with their bellies full of chewed wood, I guide them to the correct deposition sites. Not randomly—the gardens have architecture too. Fresh substrate must be layered properly, mixed with the right proportions of older material where spores are thriving. I take pellets from their mouths and place them with care that looks mindless but encodes millions of years of coevolution. The angle matters. The moisture gradient matters. The proximity to established mycelia matters.

I eat constantly but minutely. A nibble of fungus here to test its readiness, a sample there to check for contamination. My gut is a quality control laboratory. When I find the perfect growth—white as bone, soft as silk, redolent with the exact cocktail of nutrients the colony needs—I harvest it carefully, loading my crop until I can carry no more. Then begins the journey upward, following the scent trails to nurseries where nurse workers wait with hungry larvae, to royal chambers where the queen's attendants need their specialized fuel, to building sites where construction workers require the particular chemistry that makes good cement.

The fungus and I are one organism divided into two bodies. It cannot spread beyond these chambers—after sixty million years, it has forgotten how to make spores that survive in the outside world. I cannot digest without it—my ancestors traded away the ability to process raw cellulose for this perfect partnership. We domesticated each other so thoroughly that neither remembers freedom.

Sometimes I encounter the trouble. Black spots on the white fungus. Competing molds that smell wrong, taste bitter. Then I become a surgeon, using my mandibles to excise infected sections before they spread. I carry the contaminated material to the deepest refuse chambers, following disposal trails that ensure it never contacts healthy gardens again. Other times, sections simply fail—the fungus retreats, dies back, refuses to grow despite perfect conditions. These mysteries have no solution except time and patience. We seal the failed galleries and begin new ones. The colony remembers even if individual workers forget.

My sisters and I communicate through touch and chemistry. When I find a particularly productive section, I drum my abdomen against the substrate—soft rapid pulses that say *abundance here*. When contamination threatens, I release alarm pheromones specific to garden threats, different from the sharp acid terror of invasion or the musky warning of structural collapse. We coordinate without meetings, plan without planning, manage acres of fungus gardens without ever conceiving of management.

The heat rises from our work. Fermentation is warm business, and the gardens generate their own climate. We've learned—though learned is wrong, we've evolved—to use this heat, channeling it through ventilation shafts to warm nurseries above, creating convection currents that draw fresh air through the galleries. The architecture breathes because of us, the mountain's metabolism powered by our fungal furnaces.

I will never know that I am a farmer. The concept of cultivation would mean nothing to me even if I had the neurons to grasp it. I know only the immediate grammar of growth and decay, the chemical sentences that say *feed here* and *harvest there* and *danger, remove*. Yet I am part of something that human agriculture only approximates—a farming system so stable it predates the evolution of grasses, so efficient it builds mountains.

When I die—a few weeks from now, perhaps, when my mandibles wear too thin to work effectively—my body will return to the gardens. My nieces will process me into the substrate, and the fungus I tended will bloom from my chitin and proteins. This is not poetry to me. It is simply what happens, has always happened, will always happen. I am already part of the garden. Death will merely rearrange the components.

But for now, I taste and tend. My antennae brush across surfaces that would be alien landscapes under magnification—fungal forests, spore meadows, chemical weather systems of pH and enzyme. My small life maintains the vast life of the colony. I am a neuron in the termite brain, a cell in the collective body, a note in the endless composition that builds and builds and builds while knowing nothing of building.

This is enough. Has always been enough. The darkness is complete and warm and full of purpose. My mandibles know their work. The gardens grow.
