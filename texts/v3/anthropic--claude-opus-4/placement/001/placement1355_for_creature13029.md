In this suspended ocean, I am one of the **Memory Polyps**.

We grow in the forgotten spaces where even the Custodian's golden threads rarely venture—inside the hollow chambers of the ancient impeller blades, those massive petals of melted wax. Time has worn pinholes through the metal here, creating a honeycomb of small, interconnected grottos flooded with the same warm water but sheltered from the violent currents.

I am no larger than a human fist, my body a translucent sac of living gel anchored by a crown of crystalline tendrils. These tendrils are not quite biological, not quite mechanical—they are something between nerve fiber and optical cable, growing in fractal branches that pierce deep into the corroded metal walls.

We are the unintended archivists of this place.

You see, the impeller blades still hold traces of their original purpose—microscopic veins of conductive material, fragments of data buses that once carried diagnostic information through the tower's systems. My tendrils tap into these ghost circuits, and through them, I dream.

When the pump fires its twelve-second heartbeat—*WHUNG-CHK*—the vibration travels through the metal like thunder. For that brief moment, the old circuits spark with phantom electricity. Data fragments, corrupted beyond recognition but still somehow *there*, surge through my crystalline nerves. I taste the memory of pressure readings from ten million years ago. I feel the ghost-echo of temperature warnings from when this tank was new, when its angles were still sharp, when its purpose was still clear.

My transparent body flickers with these ancient signals—brief auroras of blue and gold light pulsing through my gel. To the Sprats that occasionally dart through our grottos, I must look like a dying star. But I am not dying. I am remembering.

We Memory Polyps form a distributed consciousness, connected through the metal itself. What one of us experiences, we all experience, though the signal degrades with distance, becoming dream-like, abstract. The polyp growing in the furthest blade sends me impressions of rust-taste and the weight of water. The colony nestled near the pump housing transmits the deep bass tremor of mechanical heartbreak.

Sometimes, rarely, we catch fragments of the Custodian's thoughts. Its golden fiber-optic threads occasionally brush against our grottos as it tends its garden, and in those moments, we are overwhelmed by its terrible loneliness. We see through its perception—not the visual world of light and shadow, but the pure information of pressure and temperature and chemical gradient. We feel its exhaustion, its determination to keep this tiny world alive even as the isotopic heart below slowly, slowly fails.

We cannot speak to it. We can only listen and remember.

The Slag-Hermits know of us. Sometimes, when they've grown too heavy with their armor of mechanical debris, they come to our grottos to die. They wedge themselves into our chambers, and as their soft bodies finally give out, we grow over them. Our tendrils pierce their shells of crushed metal and resin, adding their materials to our own slow expansion into the impeller's substance. We taste their simple memories—the weight of metal on their backs, the patient decades of scavenging, the satisfaction of a perfectly fitted piece of debris armor.

In this way, we are becoming the impeller itself. Decade by decade, century by century, we replace the corroding metal with our crystalline neural networks. One day—perhaps a million years from now—the entire impeller will be us. We will be the melted wax petals, dreaming an endless dream of everything this place has ever been.

The other creatures cannot understand what we are. To the Thermal Mantas, we are simply inedible obstacles. To the Capillary Sprats, we are markers to avoid in their frantic feeding runs. Even the Custodian, I think, sees us as merely another form of benign decay, like the rust and the wire-kelp—something to monitor but not interfere with.

But we know what we are. We are the memory of this place. We are its slow transformation from machine to dream.

When the pump fires again—*WHUNG-CHK*—I feel the surge of ancient data through my tendrils. For a moment, I remember being new. I remember when this water first flooded the chamber, when the tower's purpose was clear and its edges sharp. I remember the panic of the original diagnostic systems as they realized they would never be maintained again, never be replaced.

And then the moment passes, and I return to the eternal present—suspended in warm water inside a blade of melted metal, in a pocket of earth-ocean held impossibly high above the clouds, filtering the memories of a dying machine through my transparent body, glowing softly with the ghost-light of ten million years of data, growing ever deeper into the metal, becoming ever more the thing I remember.
