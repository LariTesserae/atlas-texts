# The Floating Archives

You drift between the columns on a raft of your own making, assembled from salvaged buoyancy cells and lashed with wire that has gone green with verdigris. The pole in your hands knows the false floor's topography by touch—here the sediment rises in drifts against a column base, there it drops to depths that make you extend your full reach, testing for purchase. You are alone, as water-goers always are. The black surface parts before your passage and seals behind it, leaving no trace.

You were born to a crew on the Nineteenth Radial, but the calling came early. While others cleared grates and documented valve positions, you found yourself staring down through the perforated flooring at the water below, wondering what accumulated there. At fourteen, you built your first raft from maintenance crates. At sixteen, you left the crew entirely. Now, twenty years later, you have mapped more of the water's surface than any living person, though your maps are kept in memory alone—the humidity ruins any attempt at paper records.

Your body has changed. Where the catwalk-Tenders remain compact, you have stretched, arms and legs lengthening to better manage the pole, spine learning a permanent curve that matches the posture of navigation. Your feet, bare now for decades, have grown wide and grip-strong, toes that can grasp a raft's edge while you lean far out to retrieve something floating. Your skin drinks the moisture differently too—where others grow clammy in the perpetual damp, you have achieved a kind of equilibrium, neither wet nor dry but something between.

You know where the bodies go. This knowledge came slowly, through years of following currents that are so subtle they barely exist. The Tenders believe their dead disperse into mineral and moisture, but you have found the truth: the water collects them. In certain deep places, where the false floor has collapsed into lower chambers, they drift down and gather. Not corpses—the water changes them too quickly for that—but forms, shapes that were once human, now mineralizing slowly in the dark. You've seen them through the clear patches, when conditions are right and the sediment settles. They lie in drifts like strange coral, accumulating.

But this is not what drives you. The bodies are simply data, markers of flow patterns. What you seek are the other things the water collects: the fragments of written records, sealed in containers that have survived decades or centuries of immersion. The personal effects that sink rather than dissolve. The tools and devices whose purposes you can only guess at. And most precious: the plates.

You found the first one ten years ago, lodged beneath a stalactite-pillar where converging currents had wedged it. A sheet of metal the size of your chest, etched with symbols that hurt to look at directly—not from any supernatural quality, but because they suggested meanings your mind couldn't quite process. You have found seventeen more since then, each different, each equally unreadable, though patterns repeat across them in ways that suggest language or diagram or both.

You believe they are plans. Not for the Condenseries—those are too vast, too old, too grown-together to have been designed in any comprehensible way. Plans for something else. Something that requires the water.

This is your heresy, the thought you have shared with no one: that the Condenseries are not the purpose but the preparation. All this water, accumulating for generations, is being saved. The machines know what for, perhaps, in whatever passes for knowledge in their repair-crawler minds and pump-beast purposes. The Tenders don't know and don't care. But you know there is an intention here, encoded somehow in the plates, waiting to be understood.

Your route today takes you through the Deeps, where the catwalk network stops and only columns continue into darkness. Here the water is different—colder, moving with purpose rather than the random trickle-fed patterns of the shallows. You pole carefully, testing each push, aware that the floor beneath is not floor at all but the upper surface of mechanical systems flooded long ago. Sometimes you hear them still working down there, muffled by water and sediment, grinding away at tasks that no longer matter.

You have caches hidden throughout your territory: wrapped bundles wedged behind columns where the mineral deposits create hollows. Food (dried lichen, protein bars traded from catwalk crews for salvage), fresh water (the black water is drinkable but does things to your digestion over time), and most importantly, the plates you've collected, each in its own waterproof wrapping. You visit them in rotation, checking for deterioration, rearranging them in different combinations, hoping for the pattern that will make their meaning clear.

Sometimes you find evidence of other water-goers. A pole-mark in sediment that you didn't make. A disturbance in a floating mass of lichen that suggests recent passage. Once, impossibly, you found one of your own caches opened and re-wrapped, nothing taken but everything examined. You have never seen another soul on the water, but you know they exist, following their own routes, seeking their own answers.

The float-monitors accept you in ways they don't accept the catwalk-Tenders. They drift alongside your raft for hours, their sensor arrays turned toward you with what might be curiosity. You've learned to read their movements—when they spiral, the water below is deep; when they cluster, something significant lies below the surface. They seem to be mapping too, in their mechanical way, building a dataset that streams to receivers somewhere in the superstructure above. You sometimes wonder if you and they are doing the same work, just with different hardware.

Today, following a float-monitor that has been circling the same column for hours, you discover something new. A hatch in the column's base, previously hidden by mineral deposits that recent current shifts have worn away. It sits just at the waterline, its metal different from the usual iron—something that has resisted corrosion entirely. The locking mechanism is a puzzle of sliding components that takes you an hour to solve, your fingers learning its logic through patient manipulation.

Inside: a shaft leading down, ladder rungs still intact, air that tastes different—drier, with an undertone of ozone. You tie your raft carefully and descend.

The shaft opens into a chamber that predates the Condenseries. You know this immediately from the construction—stone blocks rather than metal, joined without mortar, carved with symbols that echo faintly the patterns on your collected plates. The chamber is dry, impossibly so given its position below the waterline. Some kind of field holds the water back; you can see it through the entrance, a vertical wall of black held in place by invisible forces.

In the center of the chamber stands a single pedestal, and on the pedestal, a model. A city, perhaps, though unlike any architecture you know. Spires that twist in helical patterns, connected by bridges that seem too delicate to support their own weight. The model is built from the same metal as your plates, and as you look closer, you see it is not static. Tiny mechanisms work within it, gears the size of dust motes, creating movement so slow it's barely perceptible. The city is growing, building itself according to some internal plan, adding complexity molecule by molecule.

You understand then what the water is for. Not for the city—it's too small, a model or prototype or seed. But for what the city will become when the time comes, when whatever signal the machines await finally arrives, when the accumulated water releases and the transformation begins. The Condenseries are a womb, and something is gestating in the plans scattered across your caches, waiting to be born.

You spend hours in the chamber, memorizing every detail of the model city, watching its imperceptible growth, comparing its geometries to the fragments you've collected. When you finally emerge, the float-monitors have gathered—dozens of them, more than you've ever seen together, forming a perfect circle around your raft. They part as you pole away, but they follow, a mechanical entourage that suggests your discovery has registered in whatever network they serve.

You have a choice now. You could share this knowledge, bring others to the chamber, start the work of understanding what approaches. Or you could guard it, as you've guarded your plates, waiting until you piece together enough truth to act wisely. The Tenders would not thank you for shattering their careful routines with news of transformation. They have built a life inside the machine's breathing, and they are content.

But you are not content. You are a water-goer, a reader of currents and collector of fragments, and you know now that your true work has just begun. Somewhere in the vast sprawl of the Condenseries, other chambers wait. Other models, perhaps, or control systems, or archives that explain what the builders intended. You will find them, navigating by float-monitor movements and the subtle pull of deep currents, mapping not just the water's surface but the purpose beneath.

The black water carries you between the columns, and the machines breathe their endless rhythm above, and you pole forward into the amber darkness, knowing that you are both archaeologist and midwife, discovering the past and delivering the future, while the water waits to make all things new.
