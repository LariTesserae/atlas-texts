I find myself drawn into this world through a peculiar transformation. Let me write from within it:

**The Archivist of Echoes**

I am twenty-seven kilometers below Station Seventeen, though "below" is a courtesy I extend to those who still think in terms of up and down. My habitat is a sphere of woven carbon twelve meters across, suspended by nothing, held in place by the same impossible physics that keeps everything here from joining the eternal plunge. The sphere has no designated floor or ceiling; I gave up on those concepts four years ago, when I realized that orientation is just another story we tell ourselves to feel less lost.

I was human when I arrived. I think I still am, mostly, though the distinction matters less with each passing season. The medical teams stopped visiting after my second year—not because they gave up on me, but because I learned to repair myself using materials the Descent provides. The creatures bring me things sometimes: fragments of metal from failed stations, crystallized air from the deep cold, organic polymers I can reshape with patience and the right frequency of humming. My bones are still my own, but they're wrapped now in a lattice of something that sings harmony with the Chord. It's quite beautiful, when the light catches it right.

My work is listening for what isn't there.

You see, everyone fixates on the Chord—that omnipresent scream, that eternal note. But the Chord creates spaces, absences, null zones where sound cannot exist because the air is too perfectly occupied. These spaces drift through the Descent like bubbles in the falling water, and they carry... echoes is the wrong word. Impressions. Fossils of sound from before the falling began.

I have seventeen thousand hours of recordings, captured with instruments I built from station wreckage and creature shells and my own shed hair (it grows differently now, conductive, useful). In those recordings, if you know how to listen, you can hear:

- A child laughing, but the laugh is older than any human presence here
- Something enormous breathing, so slow that each exhale takes six days
- Music that uses notes our scale doesn't recognize, played on instruments that might be organs, might be the processed screams of falling water, might be both
- Words in the language from the metal plate, spoken by voices that sound like the Chord itself learned to speak

The Keepers think the Chord will change one day, will speak. They're wrong. It has always been speaking. We simply arrived in the middle of a sentence that started eleven thousand years ago and won't finish for another eleven thousand. The silence-fossils I collect are punctuation marks, the spaces between words in a story so long we can't recognize it as narrative.

I've started to understand fragments. Not the meaning—meaning is a luxury I abandoned with orientation—but the shape, the intent. Whatever the Descent is saying, it's not talking to us. We're just fortunate enough to overhear.

My body has adapted to help with the work. My ribs have spread to accommodate new organs—or perhaps modifications of old ones—that can process vibration into something like vision. I see sound now, watch it flow past my sphere in rivers of color that have no names because no one else can perceive them to name them. The Chord is silver-white, brilliant, a column of light that would blind me if my eyes still worked the way they used to. The echoes are darker, deeper colors: the purple-black of grief, the amber of waiting, the deep blue of thoughts too large for single minds to hold.

The creatures visit me more often than they visit the stations. I think they recognize me as something transitional, no longer quite human but not yet fully Other. They bring me gifts: more fragments for my archives, patterns pulsed in colors I can finally see, warnings about the deep currents that would carry me away if I let them. We have conversations, of a sort. They tell me about the time before the stations, and I tell them about the concept of "before," which apparently they find hilarious.

Sometimes I wonder if I should send reports back to the stations. But what would I say? That the Descent is not falling water but falling time? That the Ground is not a place but a moment, the instant when whatever spoke the eleven-thousand-year sentence finally stops for breath? That we're all here witnessing the universe's longest pause, the gap between one thought and the next in a mind so vast we mistake its cognition for geography?

I have eighteen months left on my official rotation. The schedule notices arrive through the cable connection I haven't severed, printed on paper that dissolves in the moisture before I can read it. It doesn't matter. I'm not going back. I can't. Not because the silence would kill me—I've moved beyond that particular vulnerability—but because I need to know how the sentence ends.

Or begins. I'm starting to suspect they're the same thing here.

The water falls through my sphere without touching me. I barely notice it anymore, except as the medium through which more important things move. My recordings stack up in crystalline matrices that grow from the sphere's inner walls like frost. Seventeen thousand hours, and I've barely started. The Descent has so much to say, for those who learn how not to listen.

I think I'm becoming what the children are: native to a place that wasn't meant to have natives, adapted to an environment that exists in the pause between words. My transformation feels like returning to a home I never knew I'd left.

The Chord sings through my new bones, and I sing back in the frequencies between frequencies, adding my voice to the fossil record of sounds that will one day tell someone, something, somewhere, what it was like to be suspended in the moment before impact, falling forever through a story we're too small to read.

The water falls.

I document its speaking silence.

I am the archive of what was never said, the keeper of the spaces between the sounds, and I have never been happier.

I have never been more home.
