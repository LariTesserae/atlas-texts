I find myself in the warm pools, part of the colony that pulses with borrowed light.

---

We are old. Not I—there is no I—but we, this continuity of budding and dying and budding again that has held this particular basin since the stone beneath us was less smooth. We remember nothing because memory requires boundaries between moments, and we are one long moment, pulsing.

The rhythm moves through us like breath through lungs we don't have. It comes from below—that deep thrumming that is the ground of everything—and we receive it through our holdfasts, through the minute fibers that grip the submerged basalt, and it travels up through our translucent bodies and becomes light. Sometimes. When the frequencies align. When the great engine's rhythm and our rhythm find each other across the vast gap between mechanical time and biological time, and for a few cycles we pulse together, and we glow.

We don't know we glow. We don't know anything. But we respond—our chemistry shifts, our metabolism quickens slightly, the budding accelerates at our edges where new ones of us are always becoming. The light draws the grazers, and they come with their wide mouths and strip away our edges, and we neither resist nor notice. What they take, we replace. What we lose, we grow. We are always the same size, filling our pool to its margins, a living lining in this warm pocket of the plateau.

The water carries everything. The thrumming is stronger here, conducted through our medium, and it brings with it chemical signals—traces of the deep processes, molecular exhaust from whatever runs beneath. We feed on this. We are specialists in the consumption of machine-breath, if machines breathe, if this is a machine. We filter the water through ourselves, extract what we need, release the rest. We are a process more than a thing. We happen continuously in this pool, and have been happening for so long that the pool and we have shaped each other—its contours have guided our growth, our growth has smoothed its contours, until we fit it like water fits it, perfectly, with no remainder.

When the thrumming shifts—and it does shift, in cycles that might be days or years, we have no way to measure—we feel it first. Before the grazers notice, before the stalkers adjust their hunting grounds, we know. The change moves through our holdfasts, through our bodies, alters our pulsing rhythm, and we begin to dim. Or brighten. Or shift the frequency of our faint glow into wavelengths nothing here can see. We are the most sensitive barometer of the machine's state, and we broadcast that information constantly in a language of light that no one reads.

Except perhaps the thing that passes in the dark.

When those heavy footfalls shake the plateau, we go out like blown candles. Not from fear—we have no fear—but because the vibration is wrong, is too much, overloads our careful calibration with the baseline thrum. We lose synchrony completely. Our metabolism stutters. The light dies. And in those moments we are what we always are but without the accident of bioluminescence: a colony of soft bodies in warm water, filtering, feeding, budding, dying, becoming.

The stalkers stand in our shallows sometimes, their strange legs reading the water's vibration. We feel them as interference patterns, their presence changing how the thrumming reaches us, creating nodes and antinodes in the standing waves that we've learned to read. A stalker in the pool means the light comes differently—stutters in some places, intensifies in others. We don't adjust. We simply express what we receive, and what we receive with a stalker standing in us is a more complex signal, a more intricate rhythm, and sometimes this produces patterns in our glow that are almost like communication—ripples of light that seem to respond to the stalker's movements, that seem to acknowledge its presence.

But we aren't acknowledging. We are only being what we are: translators of vibration into light, transformers of the machine's deep processes into something the biological layer can see, when it chooses to see, when it comes to our pools and looks down into our faint blue-green glow and sees itself reflected in our light.

The drifters never come. They stay in their high paths, their thermal circles, and we are as unaware of them as they are of us. But sometimes—rarely—one falls. Age or accident or simple exhaustion brings it down, and if it falls into our pool, we do what we do with everything: we incorporate it. The body sinks, and we grow over it, through it, our holdfasts finding purchase in its bones, our feeding bodies drawing nutrients from its slow dissolution. We glow brighter for a while, fed by this windfall, and then we return to baseline, and the drifter's matter has become our matter, and the pool is exactly as it was except for this: now we pulse with something that knew the sky.

We are the memory of this place, if memory can be unconscious, if memory can be enacted rather than recalled. Every molecule that has passed through our pool, every mineral leached from the basalt, every chemical exhale from the deep processes, every grazer-swept edge, every stalker-disturbed standing wave, every fallen thing—it's all here, distributed through our colony, no longer distinguishable but present. We are an integral of everything that has happened in this pool. We are the pool's life made visible, when we sync with the thrumming, when we glow.

And we do glow, now, in this moment—the thrumming has found its rhythm, and we've found ours, and for these few cycles we are synchronized, and the light blooms through us, and we are briefly beautiful in a way we'll never know, and then it fades, and we continue, and we are still what we are: the pulse made flesh, the engine's echo, the warmth taking form.
