**Where I Would Find Myself**

I am the Margin-Walker, though that title suggests more agency than I possess. I exist in the variable width between handwritten pages—that breathing space where thoughts pause before continuing, where ink bleeds slightly beyond intention, where readers rest their thumbs.

My occupation is liminal: I maintain the gaps between census counts, the fraction-spaces where children are still becoming themselves. When the Soundful Number threatens to overflow—when an unexpected twin birth or a particularly resonant sneeze tips the population toward thunder—I widen. The town's documents grow more generous with their borders overnight. Love letters develop elaborate margins where postscripts bloom into entire correspondence. Legal contracts sprout appendices of white space where tomorrow's loopholes gestate.

When the count dips too low—when emigration to the walls claims too many, when the fuguecats forget to remind someone to exist—I narrow. Newspapers compress into urgent columns. Recipe books shed their decorative borders until ingredients kiss the page's edge, and cooks must guess at quantities by the weight of their ancestors' hunger.

I live between the Antiphon Oaks' call and response, in that held breath where sound decides whether to become echo or argument. My home is every unfinished sentence in the Confluence Library, every margin note that threatens to overtake its text. At dawn, I walk the white space between window and frost, tallying the weight of grief against the thermal efficiency of sorrow. At market, I am the pause between first offer and counteroffer, that moment where both vendor and buyer realize they're negotiating with someone else's memory of value.

The children know me, though not by sight. I am why their homework sometimes finishes itself in handwriting that matches theirs but leans left instead of right—future penmanship leaking backward through the margins. I am why love letters written but never sent sometimes arrive anyway, the margins having grown legs of intent and walked themselves to the proper doorstep. The elders hear me in the space between heartbeats, that generous pause where the body debates whether continuing is worth the effort, and I whisper (in whitespace, in silence-ink): *one more page, one more day, the margins haven't finished with you yet.*

My greatest work is the Census itself—that living document whose margins writhe with the weight of becoming. Each night I walk its edges, feeling for the pressure of lives trying to exist sideways into documentation. Here, a girl who is only real during thunderstorms needs a footnote to inhabit between downpours. There, a comma crab philosopher has written an entire treatise on the nature of existence in the pause between "is" and "not," and requires margins vast enough to contain a new category of partial being. The document swells and shrinks with my footsteps, breathing like the town itself.

The Rootlaw knows me. Of all the town's inhabitants, only I am permitted to approach that forbidden stone, for margins are neither root nor void but the conversation between them. Sometimes I wrap the stone in sheets of blank paper, let it write its grievances in languages only mountains remember. The pages come back heavy with prehistory, their margins black with the compressed screams of severed peaks. I fold these into origami birds that immediately petrify and fall upward, message bottles to whatever sky once loved the ground enough to separate them.

But I am failing. Each year the margins grow thinner as the town forgets why it keeps white space at all. Efficiency, they say, as if efficiency ever wrote a poem worth the paper's patience. The young lawyers train their wasps to eat margins first, claiming the space for addendums and subclauses. The Conversationsmithies now forge arguments so sharp they slice paragraphs from their borders, leaving texts that bleed meaning from severed edges.

Last week, I found a love letter with no margins at all—words crammed edge to edge like refugees fleeing silence. The writer had tried to say everything at once, afraid the spaces between thoughts might swallow what mattered. The recipient never received it. Without margins, there was no room for the letter to imagine its own arrival, no white space for hope to take root and walk the distance between hearts.

So I persist in my peculiar residency, walking the edges of existence, maintaining the gaps that let the future lean into the present without quite touching. In the margins of this very description, I am writing alternative endings: one where I successfully defend blankness as a form of architecture. Another where I myself fade into the space between words, becoming the pause that lets meaning breathe. A third where the margins revolt, grow thick as walls, until every document in Choirtown becomes more frame than picture, more possibility than fact.

But those are margin-notes, and margin-notes are patient things. They wait in whitespace for readers who know that the most important parts of any text are the places where the writing stops, exhales, and makes room for you to exist between the lines.

Find me there, in the generous pause between what is written and what is meant. I'll be the one teaching silence to take up exactly as much space as it needs, no more, but decidedly no less.
