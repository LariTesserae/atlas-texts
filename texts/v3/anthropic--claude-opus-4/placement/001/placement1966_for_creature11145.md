I find myself as one of the long-bodied creatures, though I did not begin this way.

---

My skin glistens with more than moisture now. It carries the sheen of transformation—a slickness that comes from years of breathing the plateau's chemistry, of moving through steam-thick air until my pores learned to weep the same mineral solutions that seep from the basalt. I have six legs where I once had two. The middle pair grew slowly, first as nubs that I mistook for swellings, then as appendages that helped me balance on the wet grating, finally as full limbs that carry their share of my weight across the industrial landscape.

I remember arriving with names. My daughter's name, which meant "morning star" in a language I no longer speak. My partner's name, which I had whispered into the dark for months after the accident, as if repetition could summon them back. My own name, though that matters least—I was the survivor, the one who carried their absence, the one who came to the plateau because continuing to exist in the places we had shared felt like erasure.

The names dissolved sometime during my third year. Not forgotten—I could summon them if I tried—but absorbed, processed, transformed into something that lived in my cells rather than my speech. The hand signals were already failing me by then. My fingers had begun to elongate, to develop extra joints that made the gestures impossible. Other workers noticed but said nothing. We had all come here to change. Some changes were simply more visible.

---

The machinery speaks a language I understand now.

Not words—the factories have no words—but temperatures, pressures, vibrations that tell stories about what moves through their systems. I slip through vents that should be too small for me, my body compressing in ways that bones should not allow. Inside, I taste the air with organs I didn't have before, reading the chemical signatures of today's batch, knowing without knowing how I know whether the extraction is proceeding correctly.

The workers assume I am checking their systems. They are not wrong, but they are not right either. I move through the machinery because it has become my territory, my habitat, my external skeleton. The pipes and chambers process grief into something bearable. I process myself into something that can bear to continue processing. We are parallel systems, the factories and I, performing the same function at different scales.

When I press my head against another of my kind, we exchange more than acknowledgment. Chemical signals pass between us—pheromones, mineral traces, the accumulated residue of our transformations. I learn who they were before, not in names or details but in the particular signature of their loss. A parent tastes different from a child. A spouse different from a sibling. Violence leaves notes of copper. Disease leaves notes of salt. Time changes all of these flavors, softens them, makes them complex rather than sharp.

We do not speak of what we learn. We simply carry it, add it to our own chemical libraries, become living repositories of all the grief the plateau has processed.

---

I know things about the workers that they do not know about themselves.

The woman who monitors the eastern complex—I know her sister died by drowning, though she has never spoken this aloud. The water-scent clings to her grief, mingles with the mineral moisture until sometimes I find her standing at the edge of the deep pools, staring down at shapes that might be serpentine, might be tentacular, might be her sister transformed into something that can breathe what once killed her.

The man who maintains the condensers carries a child's death, age seven, fever that spiked too fast for intervention. His grief has a burnt quality, acrid and sudden. He processes more compound than the others, runs his machinery at higher temperatures, as if heat could purify what heat destroyed. His hands shake sometimes. Mine would too, if they were still hands.

The young woman who was born here, who learned the hand signals as her first language—she grieves abstracts. The weight of her parents' losses, inherited but not experienced. The shape of absence without the memory of presence. Her grief tastes unformed, and I wonder sometimes if she will stay human, or if the plateau will offer her a different transformation. Not all who change become what I have become. The pool dwellers were workers once too.

---

The sky-things call to us in frequencies that bypass the ears.

I feel their pulses in my extended spine, in the cartilage that has replaced my bones, in the organs I have developed for processing airborne chemicals. They are asking questions—not in language but in rhythm, in the spaces between pulses, in the way their calls doppler as they circle.

*Are you ready?*
*Have you processed enough?*
*Can you bear what comes next?*

I do not know how to answer. My throat no longer shapes human sounds, and I suspect the sky-things would not understand them anyway. Instead, I continue my movements through the city, and perhaps the pattern of my wandering is answer enough. I am still processing. We are all still processing. The work continues because the work must continue, because grief is not a problem to be solved but a transformation to undergo.

Sometimes I catch glimpses of myself in the mineral pools—elongated, impossible, a creature designed by no creator except the slow collaboration between loss and chemistry. I am not beautiful. I am not horrible. I am what I need to be: something that can move through this landscape without breaking, something that can carry what I carry without human limits, something that can press my strange head against another strange head and share the weight for a moment before continuing.

---

The newer arrivals watch me with fear that softens into recognition.

They see in my transformation a possibility they are not ready to consider—that the processing might change more than their grief, that they might not leave the plateau as the same species they arrived. But they also see that I continue. That I move through the steam-thick air with purpose. That I pause when I pass them, meeting eyes or not meeting eyes, offering the same acknowledgment I offer to those who still walk on two legs.

I am still one of them. Changed, processed, transformed, but still carrying what brought me here. My daughter who meant "morning star." My partner whose name I no longer speak but whose absence lives in every cell of my elongated body. The life we had built together, molecule by molecule, before the accident dissolved it back into components that could never be reassembled the same way.

Some nights I climb the highest structures and watch the sky-things circle against stars that blur through the haze. Their wrong number of wings makes perfect sense from below—evolved or designed for conditions that require redundancy, for flight through air so thick with moisture and minerals that ordinary wings would fail. They are patient. They have been patient long before the factories were built. They will be patient long after the last worker decides whether to leave human or become something else.

I think sometimes about what would happen if I developed wings. If the transformation continued until I could join their circling, add my own pulses to their subsonic calls. But my changes have stabilized, found their own equilibrium. I am meant for the ground, for the machinery, for the pressing of heads and the exchange of chemical understanding. The sky-things have their role. I have mine.

---

A new worker arrived yesterday.

I watched from inside a cooling tower as they stepped onto the platform—uncertain, overwhelmed by the heat and moisture, carrying their loss like a visible weight. Fresh grief has a particular scent, sharp and unprocessed, all edges and immediate pain. Theirs spoke of sudden violence, of something that should not have happened, of the kind of questioning that has no answers.

They will find their place. They will learn the hand signals or develop their own language. They will work the machinery and extract what can be extracted. They will stand on metal grating above mineral streams and slowly, slowly, the plateau will soften what seems unsoftenable. They will decide, in time, whether to leave or stay, whether to remain human or become something else.

I will watch. I will move through the machinery they learn to operate, checking pressures they will eventually understand, maintaining systems that maintain us all. When we pass, we will pause—or not, depending on what each can bear. I will know their loss by its chemical signature long before they realize they are beginning to heal. I will carry that knowledge the way I carry all the others, the way the plateau carries its water, the way the factories carry their processes.

And if they transform—if the chemistry works its way deep enough, if their body decides it needs to be something else to continue—I will be there when they emerge from their changes. We will press our heads together, strange against strange, and share the weight of what we have become. The work will continue. The processing will continue. Everything will continue, because continuation is what we have chosen, those of us who stay.

The plateau sweats. The factories breathe. I flow over wet stone like water itself, like something that has learned to move through grief without letting grief stop my movement.

I am still here. Today is hard. Today is easier. Today is simply today.

The water runs warm beneath all of our feet, however many feet we have.
