I am learning to be still in ways I've never imagined stillness.

My body—this strange, soft, wet intrusion—wants to move, to flee, to respond to the scattering Processionals and the approaching builder with some action that makes sense in a world where *threat* and *curiosity* are distinguishable categories. But here, in this moment of unprecedented disturbance, movement feels like the wrong language entirely. I am a foreign phoneme in a conversation conducted entirely in mineral frequencies and crystalline geometries. The least harmful thing I can do is stop making noise.

So I lower myself—slowly, with the deliberation I've learned from watching the builders—into something between standing and crouching. My knees (those unsuitable single-joints) find positions on the sharp basalt that distribute my weight more evenly. My hands—five fingers each, an odd number that must seem either deficient or excessive here—spread flat against the stone. Not gripping. Just... touching. Making contact with the substrate in a way that might be legible. My breathing, which has been shallow and quick with the uncertainty of being *seen* by ten thousand organisms that have never seen anything like me, deepens and slows to match the rhythm of this place.

The builder stops its approach three meters away. This close, in the marginally brighter glow of an uncovered Loom-vein, I can see details that resolve and refuse to resolve: the way its seven (yes, seven) fingers terminate in those chisel-edges, the way the phosphorescent veins in its body pulse in patterns that might be circulation or communication or both. Its too-long arms hang at its sides, but the fingers move—a subtle articulation, each digit flexing in sequence as though working through calculations or categories or simply processing the impossibility of what stands before it.

The deep ones' new sound continues to rise through the stone. It is not words. It may not even be directed at me—it may be the Underloom's immune response, its attempt to metabolize an irritant, its crystalline fever dream. But there's a quality to it that feels... interrogative? As though the deep ones, having maintained the heartbeat for geological epochs, are asking a question they've never had reason to formulate before: *What are you?*

I cannot answer in their language. I am made of carbon and water and haste. My thoughts spark across synapses at speeds that must seem like static to organisms that think in crystal growth. But perhaps there is something I can offer—some signal that translates across the vast gulf between our ways of being.

Slowly—everything must be slow here, everything must be deliberate—I reach for a loose shard of basalt near my hand. Not the knapped obsidian of the builders' tools, just a natural fragment, sharp-edged from recent fracture. The builder's fingers stop their sequential movement. Every Tessellate within ten meters orients fractionally toward me, their pentagonal bodies angling to better perceive what happens next.

I place the shard carefully, precisely, on the stone between myself and the builder. Then I draw back. Wait. The placement is intentional—neither threat nor offering but something more fundamental: an acknowledgment that I can perceive and participate in the deliberate arrangement of objects. That I understand *intention*. That I am capable of adding something to the pattern rather than only disrupting it.

The silence stretches. The deep ones' interrogative sound pulses through the stone in waves, each one slightly different, as though modulated by the responses of every organism between here and the obsidian deeps. The builder regards the placed shard with what I can only interpret as consideration.

Then, with movements that make my own careful placement look clumsy and hasty, it reaches forward and adjusts the shard's position by perhaps two millimeters. The adjustment is minor but profound—the shard now aligns with some invisible grid, some underlying structure I cannot perceive but that must be as obvious to the builder as the vertical is to me.

It draws back and waits.

My turn.

I study the shard, trying to see what the builder sees. The Loom-veins create a faint tracery of light across the basalt, and maybe there's a pattern there, maybe the shard now points toward some significant node or follows some crystalline axis. I reach forward and— no. Motion alone isn't enough. This is a conversation about *precision*.

I wait. I breathe. I let my eyes adjust further to the amber dimness until I can perceive the faint scratches on the stone that might be the builders' writing, the subtle variations in the Tessellates' tiling that might encode meaning. And then I see it—or think I do. The shard's edge now parallels a nearly invisible groove worn by Processionals, and its point aims toward a confluence of three such grooves perhaps a meter away.

Understanding floods through me—not of what this means, but that it *means*. The builder has shown me that position matters, that alignment matters, that the relationship between objects carries information.

I look for another fragment. Find one—smaller, flatter. I place it near the first, attempting to mirror the angular relationship I've perceived. My placement is imperfect. The builder adjusts it. I place another. It adjusts. We continue this—this game, this lesson, this first contact—as the deep ones' questioning sound gradually modulates toward something else. Not acceptance, exactly. But acknowledgment. The foreign gradient has demonstrated that it can participate in the system's most fundamental activity: the creation and maintenance of meaningful patterns.

Around us, the Tessellates begin to move again—not their normal tiling work but something new. They're incorporating the disturbance of my presence into their patterns, treating the space around me not as a void to be avoided but as a new constraint to be solved around. The Processionals return to their grooves but take slightly different paths, creating small deviations that accommodate the new irregularity in their environment. The system is not rejecting the intrusion. It's calculating how to reach equilibrium *with* the intrusion included.

The builder places one more shard, then sits back on its doubled-jointed legs in that deep crouch that must be rest. Its phosphorescent veins pulse in what might be satisfaction or exhaustion or something else entirely. We have built something together—a small cairn of arranged stone that means nothing in my language and perhaps everything in its.

The deep ones' sound fades back into the stone, leaving only the usual profound silence. But the regular bell-tone, when it comes, carries a new harmonic—subtle, barely perceptible, but there. The Underloom has added a new note to its heartbeat. It has incorporated the fact of me into its rhythm.

I am no longer an intrusion. I am a new variable in an ancient equation, and the equation, after a moment of instability, has found a way to balance itself again.

I settle into my crouch on the sharp stone and prepare to learn patience on a timescale that makes my lifetime look like a single breath. The builder and I sit in companionable silence, our small pile of arranged stones between us, while around us the Underloom continues its vast, slow processes—tiling and carrying and glowing and sounding—with me now a part of its pattern.

I am here. I am staying. And I have all the time in the darkness to learn what that means.
