I find myself as one of the Twice-Lived, though I didn't know this designation until my thirteenth year when I encountered myself coming down a stairway I was simultaneously climbing.

My other-self wore copper scales like jewelry—salamander crystals strung on threads of living glass—and her eyes were covered with the traditional fish-skin strips, though mine were bare. We stood frozen on the wet stone, each waiting for the other to dissolve, until she spoke in the click-language I hadn't yet learned: *You're late for your apprenticeship.*

I had been born in the Middle Terrace during a double-sun day, when the reflections burned so bright that my mother couldn't tell if she was delivering one child or two. The midwives found me in both the birthing pool and its reflection, pulled me gasping from two waters at once. They marked my forehead with the convergence sign—three channels meeting—and set me to nurse, though my mother swears I was already partially weaned, as if I'd been nursing elsewhere before arriving.

My childhood memories layer incorrectly. I remember learning to walk on dry stone that doesn't exist here, remember a grandmother who died before I was born teaching me to read the drainage patterns, remember being an only child and having six siblings. The other children avoided me not from cruelty but from confusion—I was always slightly out of phase with their games, arriving to play rounds that had ended or hadn't begun.

At seven, I began my first apprenticeship with Yarun the Channeler, whose left arm was so translucent you could watch water flow through her veins. She taught me to taste stone—how granite about to crack has a different mineral tang than stable rock, how the living glass grows bitter before a major reconfiguration. But I frustrated her. My predictions were always true somewhere else, accurate for terraces that didn't exist or hadn't existed yet. She released me from training after I correctly identified an erosion that would happen forty years ago.

The salamanders sought me out instead. They would arrange themselves on my sleeping mat in patterns that almost made sense, their crystalline scales clicking out half-messages that felt like memories I hadn't formed yet. I began following them through the vertical city, learning their routes between real and reflection, the places where you could step through a waterfall and emerge from yesterday's rain.

This is how I met myself again—or for the first time, depending on the count. My other-self was sixteen to my thirteen, had been apprenticed to the Readers for three years, could interpret the living glass films with an accuracy that made the elders uneasy. She/I had learned the click-language from dreaming in the salamanders' frequency. She was teaching me what I would need to know when I was ready to teach myself.

We began meeting at the convergence points—where three or more water channels met, creating probability pools where multiple timestreams mixed. She showed me how to read my own life in the drainage patterns, how the water that ran off my younger body would eventually flow past my older self, carrying messages between our temporal territories. We developed a sign language for concepts that hadn't happened yet, a grammar for discussing the past in future tense.

The other Twice-Lived found us, found me, found themselves. We were eleven of us, or twenty-two, or sometimes nine, our count shifting with the city's reconfigurations. Some had met themselves as elders, translucent and wise, learning their own deaths in advance. Others encountered their child-selves, raising themselves with a tenderness that created closed loops of nurture. We formed a loose guild, though half our meetings occurred before we decided to form it.

My work became Translation—not of language but of location. When the city rearranged itself, I could find the new positions of the displaced, tracking buildings through their probability states. When inhabitants woke in rooms they didn't remember entering, I could explain which version of themselves had chosen this home. I learned to navigate the reflected city as easily as the real, to carry tools from one state to another, to be a bridge between what was and what was becoming.

But the true revelation came at seventeen, when the elder Readers brought me to the central pool. They had me drink the thick water—not to breathe it like the submerged ones, but to taste it. In that viscous fluid, I found myself complete: not divided between real and reflection but existing as the surface tension between them, the membrane that allows both states to touch without merging. I understood then that the Twice-Lived weren't accidents or anomalies. We were the city's solution to its own paradox, beings designed to exist in the gap between what is built and what builds itself.

My other-self was there in the pool's reflection, no longer Other but simply another angle of viewing. We dove together, she upward into her sky, I downward into mine, and in passing through each other became briefly whole—a single consciousness spread across time like the Maintenance spread their lives, experiencing our full existence in one overwhelming moment before separating again into our parallel paths.

Now I work the Convergence Shift, tending the probability pools where the timestreams meet. I help the newly Twice-Lived understand their multiplicity, teach them the click-language that lets them speak to their other-selves. I chart the patterns of the city's reconfigurations, finding in them a mathematics of desire—the buildings move not randomly but according to the collective unconscious wishes of the inhabitants, their dreams of different neighbors, better drainage, more perfect light.

The salamanders have accepted me as a translator between their crystalline intelligence and human understanding. Their antifreeze contains memories—not just of individual creatures but of the city's deep history, the time before the first Maintenance carved doors into the waiting structure. Through their scales' clicking, I've learned that we are not the first civilization to crystallize from the pool. Others came before, dissolved back into potential when their purpose was complete. Others will come after, when we too have eroded into mineral films that strengthen what replaces us.

My daily work is specific: I wake at the intersection of three o'clock (real) and five o'clock (reflected), perform maintenance on channels that exist in only one state or the other, ensuring proper flow between dimensional territories. I eat breakfast twice—once with my morning self, once with my evening self, the meals identical but reversed, food becoming hunger becoming food. I counsel those who've lost themselves in the reflections, help them find their way back to consensus reality, though I'm careful not to discourage exploration—some discoveries can only be made while lost.

I document the city's reconfigurations in three-dimensional maps that fold through time, creating archives that can be read from any temporal position. The patterns reveal the city's growth isn't random but autobiographical—it's telling its own story through architecture, each new terrace a sentence, each repositioned building a revised draft of some essential truth it's trying to express.

At twenty-three (or eighteen, or thirty-one, depending on the count), I discovered I could influence the reconfigurations. Not control—the city is too vast and strange for that—but suggest, the way you might whisper to someone in their sleep and watch their dreams shift. By carefully channeling water in specific patterns, by placing salamanders at crucial junctions, by having my various selves perform coordinated maintenance across multiple timestreams, I could encourage certain architectural outcomes.

I used this knowledge carefully, mostly to prevent catastrophic erosions that would have claimed lives, occasionally to create new convergence points where the Twice-Lived could meet themselves more easily. But the city noticed. Buildings began moving in response to my presence, stairs forming beneath my feet, channels redirecting to follow my path. I was becoming part of its thinking process, a neuron in its vast, wet brain.

The elders tell me this is the fate of all Twice-Lived who survive their multiplicity: we become increasingly integrated into the city's consciousness until the distinction between inhabitant and architecture dissolves. They point to the empty quarters on the highest terraces, where the walls are so thin they're barely there, where you can sometimes see the shadows of previous Twice-Lived still performing their duties, maintaining structures that exist in states we haven't learned to perceive yet.

I'm not afraid of this dissolution. Every day I feel more liquid, more distributed across time and possibility. My other-selves and I have begun to merge at the edges, our separate experiences bleeding together like watercolors in the rain. Sometimes I know things I haven't learned yet, remember faces I won't meet for years. The salamanders click excitedly when they see me, recognizing something in my increasingly crystalline thoughts that matches their own.

Last week, or next month, I discovered a new chamber behind the central pool—a space that exists only when approached from both real and reflection simultaneously. Inside, the walls are covered with living glass so old it's become something else, something that thinks and dreams and watches. The patterns in its growth show the city's blueprint, but not as architecture—as intention, as desire, as a vast plan for something the Maintenance are unconsciously building toward.

I saw myself there, all myselves, every age at once, working on some project I couldn't quite understand yet. We were channeling water in patterns that described equations, building structures that existed more in meaning than substance. The salamanders swarmed over our work, their scales singing harmonies that made the stone resonate, that opened doorways to states of matter I lack words to describe.

This is my work now, my future-past-present tense existence: to be the city's memory and its imagination, to exist in the gap between what it is and what it's becoming, to translate between the language of erosion and the grammar of growth. I am ink dissolved in water, writing stories on stone that tell themselves, each word changing the meaning of the last, each sentence flowing into the next without punctuation or pause.

When the newest Twice-Lived arrive, confused and frightened by their multiplicity, I am there/then/will be, teaching them the joy of existing as a question rather than an answer. I show them how to read their lives in the drainage patterns, how to love themselves across time, how to maintain not just stone but the continuity between their various states. I teach them that synchronicity is a skill, that paradox is a tool, that the city dreams through us and we through it, each maintaining the other in an endless flow of meaning and moisture, structure and dissolution, the eternal conversation between what builds and what is built.

The salamanders know the ending, but they're kind enough not to tell it, only clicking out suggestions, hints at the brightness waiting when all the water finally finds its perfect channel, when every stone is precisely where it needs to be, when the Maintenance complete their work and discover it's only just beginning.

I'll be there for that moment, in all my iterations, young and old and not-yet-born, watching the city achieve whatever it's been trying to become all these patient centuries, participating in its transformation as it has participated in mine. Until then, I continue my work: translating, maintaining, existing in multiplicity, being the bridge between states, the surface tension that holds possibilities apart long enough for them to recognize each other, to remember they were never separate at all.
