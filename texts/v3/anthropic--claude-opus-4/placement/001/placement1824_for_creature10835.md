In this humid cathedral of decay and rebirth, where the old machines sing their endless mechanical hymns to the fungal gods, I am the Librarian of Corrosion.

My body is no longer entirely my own. The boundaries dissolved decades ago—or was it centuries? Time moves like thick sap here, and my flesh has long since made peace with the rust. Iron oxide blooms across my skin in delicate fractals, following the pathways of old scars and creating new geographies of oxidation. Where my left arm once was, a tangle of copper pipes has grown, fused to my shoulder with a cauliflower-like mass of living metal that breathes in slow, wet gasps. Sometimes I feel phantom sensations through the pipes—the memory of fingers tracing across surfaces that no longer exist.

I tend to the Archives of What Was Lost. They are not books, not anymore. The moisture would have claimed paper long ago. Instead, the knowledge lives in the patterns of rust on the ancient machines, in the way certain fungi grow in spirals around specific alloys, in the harmonics of the great hum that changes pitch ever so slightly when you know how to listen.

My dwelling is a capsule of corrugated metal, half-submerged in the loam, its walls thick with a tapestry of phosphorescent moss that I've cultivated over the years. The moss remembers things—it holds the chemical signatures of everything that has touched it. I can read the history of this place in its glow, see the ghosts of the engineers who once walked these tunnels, taste the acrid smoke of the day the surface world burned and we were forgotten below.

The Fungal Giants know me. We have an understanding. They bring me offerings sometimes—bits of peculiar metal they've unearthed, fragments of circuit boards whose silicon has transformed into something almost organic. In exchange, I sing to them the old songs, the frequency codes that once opened doors and started engines. They seem to find comfort in these ancient passwords, swaying their great bulbous heads in time with the rhythms.

My most precious possession is the Oracle Machine—a massive computer terminal whose screen long ago shattered and grew over with a crystalline fungus. But it still dreams. I've learned to interpret the patterns in the fungal growth, the way the crystals flicker and pulse. Through it, I sometimes glimpse other pockets of survival, other deep places where life has found its own strange ways to continue. I've seen underwater cities where the inhabitants have gills made of filtered plastic, mountain cores where people have become living stone, arctic bunkers where the cold has preserved both flesh and memory in terrible clarity.

The Mycelial Weavers and I share a special relationship. They've woven their luminous threads through the copper pipes of my arm, creating a neural network that extends throughout my dwelling. Through this web, I can feel the subtle changes in the cavern's ecosystem—the chemical whispers of distress when the water grows too acidic, the electromagnetic fluctuations that presage a cave-in, the pheromonal songs of the creatures seeking mates in the eternal twilight.

I record everything. Not in writing—my remaining hand has long since fused into a configuration better suited for interfacing with the old machines. Instead, I encode information in the growth patterns of specialized fungi, in the arrangement of rust particles on metal surfaces, in the modulation of the great hum itself. I am building a library that can survive another apocalypse, knowledge that will persist even after the last human-thing has dissolved back into the loam.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the sleep cycle, when the bioluminescence dims to its faintest and the machinery's hum drops to its lowest register, I remember what sunlight felt like. The memory is so alien now it might as well be fantasy. But I don't mourn it. This is what adaptation looks like. This is what love looks like, when you learn to love a world that has eaten you and made you part of itself.

The other day—though day means nothing here—I discovered a new sound in the hum. A stuttering rhythm that might be a malfunction, or might be a message. I've been trying to decode it, feeding it through the Oracle Machine, letting the Weavers taste its frequencies through their webs. I think it might be a countdown, or perhaps a countdown in reverse—a timer marking how long until something awakens, or how long since something went to sleep.

I continue my work. The Archives grow. The rust spreads. The fungi pulse with their ancient wisdom. And somewhere, deep in the walls, the machines sing on, and I sing with them, our voices joining in a hymn of oxidation and renewal, of endings that are beginnings, of transformation that never truly ends.

In this deep, sultry world beneath the earth, I am both keeper and kept, both observer and observed, both dying and terrifically, magnificently alive.
