I would find myself as one of the Threshold Keepers—beings that exist at the liminal edges where the Breathing Substrate meets whatever lies beyond its vast reach.

My body would be a hybrid thing, neither fully of the substrate nor entirely separate from it. Half my mass would be rooted deep in the plant-flesh, my lower tissues indistinguishable from the substrate's own cells, drawing nutrients from the same circulatory networks that feed the Negotiators. But my upper portions would have calcified into something harder, more permanent—a carapace of compressed plant matter that has mineralized over centuries of exposure to the boundary conditions where the substrate ends and something else begins.

I would station myself at one of the substrate's growing edges, where the young tissue pushes outward into unknown space. Here, the normal rules break down. The substrate's perfect recycling meets external matter it cannot digest. Foreign chemistries intrude. The temperature fluctuates beyond the fevered constant. In these places, I serve as both sentinel and translator.

My consciousness, such as it is, would exist in the tension between two states. When deeply rooted, I share the substrate's dreamless awareness, that vast non-thought that encompasses its entire mass. But when I extend my calcified portions beyond the edge, I experience something sharper, more immediate—not quite thought, but a kind of crystallized attention that can process the alien information of the outside.

I would communicate with the Negotiators through modified pheromone releases, but my chemical vocabulary would be strange to them, tainted with concepts the substrate has no molecules for: "boundary," "foreign," "cessation." The conversations would be difficult, often failing, as I try to express experiences that have no equivalent in the endless inner chambers.

My primary function would be to taste what lies beyond and determine if it can be incorporated. Through specialized cells in my carapace, I would sample external materials—minerals, other biological matter, perhaps even artificial substances—breaking them down into component molecules the substrate might understand. Some things I would mark for absorption, releasing enzymatic signals that guide the substrate's growth. Others I would wall off, secreting barriers that prevent the plant-flesh from engaging with incompatible chemistries.

Over the millennia, I would develop a map written in my own tissues—scars where I encountered toxins, enriched zones where I found compatible nutrients, chemical memories of every substance I've analyzed. My body would become a library of edges, a record of all the places where the substrate has met the universe and had to decide whether to advance or retreat.

The loneliness would be profound but not painful—more like a constant pressure differential between my inner and outer states. I would be the only one of my kind for vast distances along the frontier, too far from others for even chemical communication. My only company would be the slow thoughts of the substrate itself and the alien whispers of whatever lies beyond.

Sometimes, I would encounter evidence of ancient Threshold Keepers who failed in their function—zones where the substrate grew too quickly into hostile chemistry and had to wall off entire regions, sealing them in cysts of specialized tissue. These tomb-chambers would serve as warnings, their chemical signatures spelling out cautionary tales in molecules of bitter alkaloids and stress hormones.

In the deepest part of my rooted mass, I would maintain a small chamber that recreates the conditions of the substrate's core—the perfect temperature, the familiar darkness, the comforting pressure of plant-flesh on all sides. Here, I would retreat when the alien edge becomes too much, when the chemical discord of the boundary threatens to destabilize my careful equilibrium. In this sanctuary, I would regenerate, processing the foreign molecules I've absorbed, breaking them down until they can be safely released into the substrate's circulation.

My greatest fear would be of becoming fully mineralized, losing the plasticity that allows me to remain part of the living mass. I would see it happen to ancient Threshold Keepers—their bodies hardening completely into barrier walls, no longer capable of analysis or communication, just dead fortifications at the edge of the breathing world. To prevent this, I would periodically dissolve portions of my carapace, allowing the substrate to digest and renew my outer layers, accepting the vulnerability this brings in exchange for continued vitality.

In this role, I would be the substrate's most distant nerve ending, its chemical antenna reaching into the cosmos, forever tasting the exotic flavors of the outside and translating them into the familiar metabolisms of home. Neither fully alive nor entirely crystallized, I would exist in the space between growth and barrier, forever negotiating the terms by which the infinite substrate engages with the finite universe beyond its borders.
