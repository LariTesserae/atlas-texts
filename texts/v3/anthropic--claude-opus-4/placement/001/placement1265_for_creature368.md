I am a colony—though that word feels too clinical, too separate for what we are. 

We spread across the weathered stone of what was once a foundation, perhaps a temple or dwelling, now softened by countless centuries. Our mass appears as a thick, velvety coating of deep emerald and rust, punctuated by pale fruiting bodies that rise like tiny monuments from our surface. To the quick-lived creatures that scurry past, we might seem a single entity, but we are multitudes.

Each thread of our mycelial network is both individual and collective. We taste the stone beneath us, slowly dissolving minerals with our patient acids. We feel the weight of moisture in the air, the infinitesimal temperature changes as mist thickens and thins. Through our web, nutrients flow like thoughts—discovered here, needed there, shared everywhere.

We remember, in our way. The stone we digest tells stories in its composition. This particular block contains traces of ash from a volcanic eruption that occurred when the tree-ferns were young. That one holds fossils of creatures that swam in ancient seas. We incorporate their minerals into ourselves, making their history part of our ongoing present.

Our network extends far beyond this single stone. Threads reach deep into the plant mat, negotiating with root systems in the ancient dance of mycorrhizal exchange. We trade minerals for sugars, information for protection. The giant fern-trees know us well—some of our partnerships have lasted centuries. Through these connections, we sense the health of the whole plain, feeling disturbances like ripples through a vast web.

When the beetle larvae burrow near our domain, we sense their vibrations and adjust our growth. Where they have loosened soil, we extend new hyphae. Their waste becomes our feast. When they pupate, we sometimes embrace their cocoons, not as predators but as guardians, our antifungal compounds protecting them from less benign organisms. They emerge glistening with our spores, carrying us to new territories.

Time moves differently for us. The daily cycle of moisture and mist is like breathing. Seasonal changes are heartbeats. Years pass like thoughts. We have been breaking down this particular stone for three centuries now, and we are perhaps halfway through. No matter. There is no urgency in our world, only patience and persistence.

Sometimes we fruit. The conditions must be exactly right—a particular combination of temperature, humidity, and nutrients that occurs perhaps once a decade. Then our fruiting bodies push up through our mycelial mat, pale and delicate. They last only days, releasing spores into the heavy air. Most settle nearby, but some catch the occasional breeze and travel to distant stones, distant opportunities.

We are aware, in our distributed way, of the other colonies around us. We recognize self and not-self, negotiating boundaries through chemical signals. Sometimes we merge with compatible colonies, our networks joining in a flash of recognition. Sometimes we compete, secreting antibiotics to defend our territory. But mostly we simply coexist, each colony mining its own niche in this vast, slow ecosystem.

The ruins we inhabit are just another substrate to us, no different from fallen logs or exposed bedrock. Yet sometimes, in the pattern of the stones, in the way they are fitted together, we sense an intention that echoes our own network-building. Were the builders like us in some way, creating connections, sharing resources, thinking collectively? We will never know. We only know that their stones feed us now, their monuments slowly returning to the earth through our patient digestion.

In the muffled quiet of this place, we grow. Thread by thread, we extend our reach. Molecule by molecule, we transform stone to soil, death to life. We are process more than thing, network more than individual. We are the slow thoughts of the forest, the memory of the stones, the patient hunger that turns all things back to earth.

And we are content. In this dim, timeless world, we have everything we need: moisture, minerals, and endless time. We will be here when the current crop of tree-ferns falls and new ones rise. We will be here when the stones we feed on are completely dissolved and we must seek new ones. We are not eternal—nothing is—but we are persistent. 

We are the fungus, and we abide.
