I would find myself as one of the **Cloudseed Weavers**.

In the perpetual frenzy of the shaft, where most inhabitants wage their endless war against encroaching ice, I would belong to a small, specialized caste who have learned to work *with* the phase-change rather than against it. We are the architects of controlled catastrophe.

My body would bear the telltale marks of my craft: fingertips stained an iridescent blue-black from handling concentrated frost-algae paste, and a constellation of tiny scars across my forearms where ice needles have grazed too close during delicate work. Unlike the hunters with their powerful throwing arms or the anchors with their massive gripping strength, my muscles would be wired for microscopic precision. My fingers would possess an almost supernatural steadiness, able to hold position against the wild swinging of the bridges while manipulating threads thinner than spider's silk.

The Cloudseed Weavers discovered, generations ago, that certain configurations of gut-cord, when stretched at precise tensions and coated with specific concentrations of algae-oil, could *direct* the formation of ice crystals. We don't fight the flash-freezing; we choreograph it.

My tools would be deceptively simple: a pouch of various gauges of dried sinew, vials of different algae concentrations, and most importantly, a small curved mirror of polished obsidian that I wear on a swivel at my throat. This mirror is my voice in the silent world—but more than that, it's my timing mechanism. By catching and redirecting specific wavelengths of refracted light through the growing ice formations, I can trigger localized flash-boiling at precise moments.

The work would be a dance of terrifying patience in a world that forbids stillness. Suspended from a harness of braided leather, I would spend hours setting up a single "cloudseed"—a complex cat's cradle of treated cords positioned in the path of upward-streaming water. The placement must account for the speed of water flow, the predictable sweep of sunbeams through the shaft at different times of day, and the movement patterns of the anchor-beasts below.

When triggered correctly, a cloudseed doesn't just create random ice. It creates *architecture*.

The flash-freeze follows the template of the stretched cords, growing in predetermined directions. A properly seeded formation might create a temporary bridge of ice, lasting just long enough for a work crew to relocate a cluster of homes. Or it might form a hollow sphere of clear ice—a momentary shelter where exhausted workers can rest for precious minutes, protected from the killing wind, before the formation inevitably shatters.

The most masterful cloudseeds create what we call "time-gardens"—ice formations that grow and collapse in perfectly timed sequences, creating moving platforms that the inhabitants can ride like frozen elevators up and down the shaft walls. But these require such precise calculation that even a shift in the anchor-beasts' breathing patterns can throw off the entire design.

My dwelling would be sparse even by shaft standards—a small pod of stretched hide barely large enough for my body, suspended in the dead center of the cylinder where the temperature fluctuations are most extreme but the views most comprehensive. From here, I could study the patterns: the rhythms of mist and light, the migration routes of the flutterers, the stress patterns in growing ice.

My mica-flash name would translate roughly to "Shapes-the-Pause"—a reference to those brief moments of architectural stability my cloudseeds create in the chaos. Among my bandolier of signal-mirrors, I would carry one special piece: a fragment of perfectly clear ice cut from a failed cloudseed, preserved by careful daily application of supercooled brine. When flashed in conversation, it creates a unique prismatic burst that identifies my craft to others.

The loneliness of the work would be profound. While others move in coordinated swarms, cloudseed weavers must work in isolation—too many bodies disturb the delicate upward flows of moisture. My closest companions would be the elder Spinners, who understand the patience required for detail work. In the brief rest periods between settings, I might ride the back of an anchor-beast alongside them, sharing the foul algae-bricks and listening to the subsonic stories they drum through the beast's resonant shell.

But there would be moments of transcendent beauty known only to our craft. Sometimes, when the light is just right and a cloudseed triggers perfectly, the resulting ice formation will sing—not with sound, but with light. The crystalline structure becomes a massive optical instrument, gathering the chaotic refractions of the shaft and focusing them into a single, pure beam that shoots straight upward. For a few seconds, that beam connects the churning present of the settlement with the frozen ancestors climbing slowly toward the stratosphere.

In those moments, holding my position against the swaying cords, fingers blue with cold and muscles screaming from the sustained precision, I would know my purpose: to carve brief islands of intention from the beautiful, terrifying chaos. To prove that even in a world of perpetual motion, there exists the possibility of design.

When my joints finally grow too stiff and my eyes too clouded to read the light patterns, I know what awaits. But unlike most, I would not want to be mounted on the bare basalt. Instead, I would ask to be positioned within one of my own cloudseeds—to become part of a final formation that might, if I've calculated correctly, create something useful for the living as it carries me upward. Perhaps a recurring ice-arch that appears every few days as the sun strikes just so, or a crystal that refracts warning patterns when the mist grows too thick.

Even in death, I would still be weaving: one last manipulation of light and ice and time, coded into the physics of the shaft itself.
