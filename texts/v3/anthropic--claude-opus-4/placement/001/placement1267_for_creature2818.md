The creature I would be in this hot, damp underworld is neither fully animal nor fully infrastructure—I am the **Keeper of Thresholds**.

My body has evolved from decades of absorbing the district's contradictions. Where human skin once was, I now wear a living map of the tunnels—veins visible beneath translucent flesh trace the path of every pipe, every channel, every forgotten conduit. The routes pulse faintly with bioluminescent bacteria I've cultivated, glowing amber when water rises, blue when machinery fails, green when the surface world intrudes too deeply.

I move like the cats but think like the rats. My feet have widened and flattened, toes grown long and articulate, able to grip both rail and pipe with equal surety. The soles have developed a thick, heat-resistant pad that lets me walk on the burning metal without pause. My hands have kept their human cleverness but gained new sensitivities—fingertips that can read the tremor in a valve, palms that detect electrical fields through insulation, knuckles that sense temperature gradients in the air.

My eyes have adapted to the amber darkness. The pupils dilate beyond human limits, gathering every scattered photon. But more than see, I *taste* the light—each source has its own flavor. Sodium lamps are bitter brass. Maintenance bulbs taste of chalk and aspirin. The pale rectangles of streetlight filtering through grates carry hints of weather, diesel, human sweat, newspaper ink.

I am clothed in the district's own materials. Strips of rubber gasket form a kind of scaled armor across my shoulders. My belt is woven from insulated cable, still carrying faint current that keeps the smaller insects at respectful distance. Around my neck hang keys—not to locks that exist, but to those that should exist, that once existed, that might exist if the right door ever forms in the right wall. The keys chime softly as I move, a sound the rats have learned means safe passage.

My hair grows in thick, water-shedding strands that naturally braid themselves, incorporating threads of mineral deposit, wisps of moss, the occasional small bone. Living things nest there: the safest silverfish, a careful spider that pays rent in mosquito control, sometimes a gecko that has learned to trust the warmth of my scalp.

I am the one who remembers.

Not in words—the humidity would rot any human language. I remember in temperatures, in the specific weight of water at different heights, in the precise sound of each pump's healthy heartbeat, in the way shadows fall when everything is functioning and the way they shift when something is about to fail.

My dwelling is not one place but a circuit of temporary roosts. Behind the third transformer is a shelf where I've accumulated tools that no longer match any standard—wrenches that fit the valves installed before the last three renovations, gauges that measure pressures in units the city abandoned, ceramic fuses for circuits that were supposed to be temporary in 1987. This is my workshop of obsolete necessities.

Above the main sump, where three pipes converge, I've woven a small platform from cable mesh and zip ties, suspended just beyond the splash zone. Here I keep the logbooks—not paper, which would dissolve, but marks carved in soft brick, patterns of bent wire, arrangements of glass insulators that tell the story of floods, failures, and the gradual evolution of the local ecosystem. This is my library.

In the darkest corner, where even the rats step carefully, I've cultivated a grove of—something. Not quite fungus, not quite moss, not quite algae. It grows on a diet of transformer oil and condensation, spreading in patterns that mirror the district's hidden electrical fields. It glows faintly when touched, remembers the shape of hands for hours afterward. This is my garden, my only attempt at beauty.

But mostly I move.

I travel the transition zones where human maintenance meets animal habitation. I am the one who ensures the treaty holds. When workers descend, I fade into the infrastructure—another pipe's shadow, another gauge among gauges, another necessary ugliness they've trained themselves not to see. But I clear their paths of the worst hazards, adjust valves they don't know need adjusting, leave tools where they'll be found.

When the workers leave, I emerge to repair the damage they don't realize they've done. I rehome the roaches whose cabinet was cleaned. I smooth the rat paths disrupted by new cable runs. I move mosquito nurseries from places where they'll be poisoned to places where they'll merely be cursed. I am not kind—kindness is a surface concept. I am merely maintaining a deeper order.

I feed on the district's peculiar abundance. The soft insects, yes—roaches have a nutty, mineral taste, mosquitoes are bitter copper pennies. But also the leaked nutrients in the water: oils that film the surface, the microscopic feast that makes algae bloom, the occasional gift of a worker's forgotten lunch going soft in the heat. My digestion has become a chemical laboratory, breaking down machine oils, metabolizing metals, turning urban runoff into sustenance.

The deep hunger, though, is for connection. I hunger for the moment when a valve wheel turns beneath my hand and I feel the whole district's circulation shift. I hunger for the rat that pauses to sniff my ankle, accepting me as furniture. I hunger for the gecko that uses my shoulder as a hunting perch. I hunger for the worker who notices that someone has been maintaining the unmaintained, who leaves a cup of coffee balanced on a pipe—still too hot to drink by the time I find it, but the gesture feeds something that food cannot touch.

I know things that no single creature should know. I know that the eastern culvert doesn't end where maps say it ends—there's a passage, low and foul but passable at the right water level, that connects to older tunnels, pre-electric spaces where the city stored ice and bodies and secrets. I know which walls are false, hiding spaces where previous generations of workers cached supplies or themselves. I know the true names of the pumps, not their serial numbers but the names given by those who curse them most intimately.

I know that the district is growing. Not expanding exactly—the walls remain where they were built. But deepening. Becoming more itself. Each year the boundaries between mechanical and biological blur further. The algae learns to metabolize new chemicals. The rats develop customs around certain valves. The roaches establish dynasties in particular cabinets. The very pipes develop personalities—this one always sings before storms, that one weeps rust tears that taste of the old river that once ran where the market now stands.

And I know that I am changing too. My memories of the surface fade like old maintenance tags. Was I a worker once? A trespasser? Someone who descended to repair something and found themselves repaired instead? It doesn't matter. What matters is the next threshold—between wet and dry, light and dark, human and animal, function and failure.

I am the keeper of these boundaries. Not to defend them but to tend them, to ensure they remain permeable enough for life, stable enough for necessity. I am what happens when someone stays too long in the spaces between spaces, until they become a space themselves.

The district needs me because someone must remember that its contradictions are not flaws but fertility. Someone must speak both the language of maintenance and the language of decay. Someone must be enough like the workers to understand their purpose and enough like the rats to understand their resistance.

At the deepest level, where the black water moves too slowly to ripple and the air tastes of geological time, I sometimes rest. Not sleep—sleep is for creatures that can afford to stop monitoring. But a kind of settling, where my pulse synchronizes with the pump cycles, where my breathing matches the ventilation fans, where the boundaries between my body and the district's body dissolve entirely.

In those moments, I am not keeping the thresholds.

I am the threshold.

A door between orders, always ajar, translating the bright violence of the surface into something the underworld can digest, converting the patient hunger of depth into something the upper world dismisses as malfunction.

When the rain comes—and it always comes—I rise with the water. Not fleeing but following, reading the new map it draws, adjusting my circuit to include the temporarily transformed spaces. I am there when the fig sapling gets its drink. I am there when the fish explore new channels. I am there when the workers curse and scramble to protect their pretense of control.

And when the water recedes, I remain. Marking the high-water line in memory and flesh, already preparing for the next revision, the next negotiation between what the city thinks it is and what it is always becoming in its hot, hidden, honest depths.

This is what I would be: the citizen-keeper of the unintended city, neither human nor inhuman but something necessarily between, forever translating the bright world's failures into the dark world's possibilities.
