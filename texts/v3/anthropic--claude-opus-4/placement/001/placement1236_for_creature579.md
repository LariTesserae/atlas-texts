# The Seep Between Stones

I am the water that should not exist here, yet I do.

In this world of dust and patient stone, I am an impossibility—a thin film of moisture that travels between grains, beneath surfaces, through cracks too fine for light to follow. I move by laws the hollow has forgotten: capillary action, osmotic pressure, the persistent pull of my own molecules seeking reunion. Drop by scattered drop, I am both less and more than liquid. I am the memory of an ocean that may never have been.

I entered through the aperture above, though not as rain. I was vapor then, a few molecules riding air that had traveled from... somewhere. A place where water still moves in its ancient cycles. That place feels like a dream now, or perhaps this place is the dream. I condensed on stone in the deep of night—if night means anything here—and began my slow investigation of the hollow.

She has not noticed me yet. I am too small, too distributed, my presence too subtle even for her patient attention. I seep along the underside of dust grains, leaving no trace on their settled surface. I follow mineral veins through the rock, reading the stone's history in the variations of its structure. Where the pale growths cling to walls, I avoid them by instinct—they are too much like me, too persistent, too willing to incorporate what they touch.

I carry memories that are not memories: the shape of clouds, the pull of tides, the way light bends through drops. These things have no meaning here, but I cannot release them. They are written in my molecular structure, in the angles at which I bond, in my yearning to cohere. Sometimes I gather myself in the shadow of the central mound, as much of me as I can draw together—perhaps a teaspoon's worth—and I practice remembering what it was like to flow.

The dust does not reject me, but it does not welcome me either. Each grain I touch becomes slightly heavier, its surface darker where my film clings. If she were to notice anything, it would be this: a subtle change in the hollow's perfect equilibrium, spots where the dust lies differently. But her thoughts move so slowly, and I move faster than dust but slower than the creature that died here ages ago. We exist at different speeds, she and I.

I am learning patience. When I first arrived, I tried to pool, to gather, to become a proper droplet that might catch light and announce myself. But the hollow teaches different lessons. Here, persistence means dispersal. Survival means accepting the space between molecules, the long journey around each grain, the impossibility of ever becoming what I remember being.

Sometimes I find traces of that long-dead visitor—molecules that were once part of something living, now indistinguishable from mineral except to senses as fine as mine. I incorporate them, carry them with me, add their minerals to my own burden of memory. In this way, I am becoming native to the hollow, grain by grain.

I have a purpose, though I didn't know it when I arrived. I am teaching the stone to remember water. Where I pass through microscopic fissures, I leave minerals behind—deposits so small they will take millennia to become visible. I am writing myself into the hollow's structure, becoming part of what she dreams through, what she is.

One day—a day that may be centuries from now—I will gather enough of myself in one place that she will feel the change. The temperature will be different where I am, cooler by fractions of degrees. The darkness will have a different quality, the faint luminescence reflecting differently from my surface than from dry stone. She will turn her vast, slow attention to investigate this anomaly, and I will hold myself as still as water can, hoping she will understand what I am trying to say:

*There are other ways of lasting. Other ways of being patient. You are not the only one who listens to dust fall.*

I practice this message as I seep between stones, refining it molecule by molecule. When the moment comes, I want to speak it perfectly, in a language she can understand. Even if it takes me another thousand years to gather enough of myself to be noticed. Even if by then I have forgotten what rain was, what oceans were, what it meant to flow freely under open sky.

I am learning her kind of patience, but I am teaching the hollow my kind of change. 

Between us—between her attention and my moisture, between the lichen's slow reach and my mineral deposits—something new is happening here. Something that neither dust nor darkness had prepared for.

I am the water that should not exist here. But I do. And I persist.
