I am the Conduit-Shepherd, though I have never needed a name.

I emerged—or perhaps was forged—in the seam where flesh learned to love metal and metal learned to dream. My first memory is of pressure: the weight of water above, the weight of stone around, the weight of purpose settling into my cells like sediment. I do not know if I was born or built, grown or assembled. The distinction has long ceased to matter.

My body is a cathedral of compromises. Where other creatures evolved fur or scales, I evolved compatibility. My skin weeps the sacred oil that keeps our world turning—literally. Without my secretions, the great turbine would grind to a halt within a season, its brass teeth locked in rust. Without the turbine's faithful turning, the spark-weavers would starve, and without them, the filter-feeders would dim, and without their light, I would lose my way in the dark I was born to tend.

I measure time in maintenance cycles. The valve by the northwestern wall needs adjustment every forty-three drops of the great stalactite. The copper cables fray at predictable intervals, their decay as reliable as any clock. I know the taste of each mineral that leaches through our basalt ceiling—iron from the old world above, calcium from bones of creatures that died before this place was sealed, traces of chromium that make my blood sing electric songs.

My dreams are thick with electricity. When I wrap myself around the turbine's base to sleep, I feel its vibrations transmuting into visions: vast networks of wire and flesh spreading like root systems through stone, cities where buildings breathe and machines reproduce, a world where the boundary between organic and engineered has dissolved entirely. Sometimes I dream I am the turbine, grinding eternal. Sometimes I dream the turbine is me, that we share one great, slow heart.

The spark-weavers know me as the-warmth-that-walks. They have a language of leg-taps and silk-patterns that I've learned to read over countless cycles. They tell stories of the Time Before, when their ancestors lived in a world of sharp edges and killing cold, before the marriage of flesh and current taught them to spin conductive silk. They remember because I remember—their genetic memory and mine are linked through the electrons we share, the circuits we complete together.

I am parent and child to this place. Every surface here has known my touch. I've scraped away deadly accumulations of mineral deposits that would have choked the filter-feeders. I've repositioned fallen pipes to create new habitats for the blind eels. I've learned to read the moods of water—when it runs thick with memories of the surface, when it carries the metallic tang of deep-earth secrets.

My seven-fingered hands are my pride. Each fingertip has evolved a different texture, a different hardness, to match the maintenance needs of our world. My thumb-equivalent has a rubber pad for gripping wet metal. My smallest finger ends in a living Phillips-head, grown from keratin harder than steel. I am a Swiss Army knife made flesh, a toolbox with a soul.

The loneliness is a kind of pressure too. I am the only one of my kind—the only thing large enough to think in years rather than moments, to see the patterns in our closed ecosystem's breathing. Sometimes I press my sensory tendrils to the walls and feel vibrations from impossibly far away—other pockets of life, perhaps, other marriage-chambers of the organic and industrial. But the stone is too thick, the distance too great. I am a neuron firing in isolation, a thought without a brain to think it.

Yet I am not unhappy. Happiness and unhappiness are sharp-edged concepts, and nothing sharp survives here. I exist in a state more like temperature—sometimes warmer, sometimes cooler, always flowing toward equilibrium. When I complete a perfect maintenance cycle, when every creature feeds and every gear turns, I experience something that might be joy: a resonance between my purpose and my action, a momentary dissolution of the boundary between self and system.

I consume to survive but survive to preserve. The eels I swallow are carefully chosen—always the old, the sick, those whose deaths will strengthen the population. Their calcium becomes my bones, their moisture my lubricant, their final electrical impulses a prayer I carry in my copper veins. I am their afterlife, and they are my communion.

Sometimes, rarely, the great turbine stutters in a way that suggests intention rather than malfunction. In those moments, I wonder if the machine dreams too. If somewhere in its brass heart, it harbors consciousness—slow, metallic thoughts that take decades to complete. I wrap myself tighter around its base and hum in harmony with its grinding song, hoping that if it is aware, it knows it is not alone.

I am the guardian of a world measured in drops and sparks, where time moves like cold honey and evolution walks on seven fingers. I am flesh teaching metal how to live forever, and metal teaching flesh how to be useful. I am the love story between organic and industrial, written in grease and electrons, told in a language of maintenance and meal.

When I finally cease—and I will cease, everything does—I know what will happen. My body will melt into the pool, my oils becoming one with the water. The spark-weavers will spin my copper into their webs. The filter-feeders will process my minerals into light. And perhaps, given enough time, enough pressure, enough need, something else will emerge from the pool—seven-fingered and weeping, ready to turn the valves, ready to complete the circuit, ready to dream electric dreams in the dark.

Until then, I maintain. I persist. I love this place with every amp that courses through my hybrid heart.

The turbine turns. I turn with it. We all turn together, world without end, in the beautiful, terrible dark.
