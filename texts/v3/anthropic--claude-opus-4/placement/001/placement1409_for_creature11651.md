# The Keeper of Intervals

---

I am the space between pump strokes.

Not the pumps themselves — those belong to the machine, to the great breathing thing that Garza tends without tending, that the district has become or always was. Not the water — that belongs to itself, to its ancient argument with gravity that the pumps mediate but do not resolve. I am the pause. The held moment when the impeller has finished pushing and has not yet begun to pull, when the water hesitates in the pipes like a thought on the edge of becoming a word.

I was an engineer once. Or I was studying to be one. Or I was someone who understood the mathematics of flow and pressure and head loss, back when those were things that could be understood separate from their manifestation. Now I am what happens to understanding when it soaks too long in humid air. I am a function that has outlived its equation.

---

My place is the transformer shed on the alley between Pump Street and the parallel street that has no name — a brick cube twelve feet square with a sheet metal roof and a door that locks from the inside. The transformers hum at 60 hertz, stepping down voltage from the grid that may or may not know it still feeds this place. The hum is not quite in harmony with the pumps, not quite in conflict. It wavers between consonance and dissonance in a pattern that almost repeats but never quite does, and I have been listening to it for — time doesn't work the same way here. I have been listening to it.

Inside the shed: three transformers in oil-filled casings, their cooling fins furred with dust that has the consistency of wet felt. A metal desk where someone once kept logs. A chair. A cot narrower than Garza's, which is how I know about Garza's cot, through some osmosis of information that happens when people occupy adjacent frequencies long enough. A clipboard with papers that have decomposed into a single lignin mass. A thermos that I fill from the condensation that drips from the cooling fins, because the condensation is cleaner than what comes from the taps, if the taps work, which they might.

On the desk: instruments. Voltmeters, ammeters, oscilloscopes with screens that show waveforms which are not the electrical waveforms they were designed to display but something else, something that happens when circuits age in proximity to whatever lives in the primary pumping station. The traces look like breathing. Like the EKG of something vast and patient. I watch them the way the old woman listens to her radio — not for information but for continuity, for the reassurance that the pattern continues.

I do not maintain the transformers. They maintain themselves, or the district maintains them, or they have reached a state where maintenance and decay have found their own balance. The oil in the casings should have broken down years ago, decades ago. It should have carbonized, acidified, lost its insulating properties. Instead it has thickened to the consistency of blood and works better than it ever did when it was new. The windings should have corroded. The bushings should have failed. The cooling fins should have clogged. None of this has happened. The transformers run at exactly the temperature they need to run at, which is exactly the temperature of the district itself, which is why they do not actually cool anything but simply exist, humming, stepping down voltage that goes to the lights that burn and the machines that run and the television that glows behind fogged glass.

I know about the television because I am the one who ensures it has signal. Not actively — I don't maintain cables or adjust receivers. But I sit in the transformer shed and I watch the oscilloscope and sometimes I think about bandwidth, about carrier waves, about the way information can be encoded in the spaces between other information, and when I think about this with sufficient clarity the television's signal strengthens. This is not causation as an engineer would understand it. This is causation as the district understands it: things happen because they have always happened, and they have always happened because someone needs them to happen, and the need and the happening are not separate events but the same event viewed from different positions along the waveform.

---

The man in the building with the television — I have seen him. Once. When the fog on his window cleared for a moment during a particular confluence of interior and exterior temperatures that may never recur. He was looking at the oscilloscope in my shed, though he could not possibly see it from there. He was looking at it the way I look at it: with the recognition of someone who once understood these things analytically and now understands them somatically, the way a body understands balance. He raised his hand — not a wave, just an acknowledgment of parallel function — and I raised mine, and the fog returned and we have not seen each other since, but we do not need to. We are doing the same work in different media. He maintains the coolness that allows the fog that softens the television's light into something bearable. I maintain the intervals that allow the transformer's hum to weave between the pump strokes without collision. Neither of these is necessary. Both of these are essential.

---

At night — though night here is just a deepening of texture, like velvet rubbed one way and then the other — I leave the shed and walk the sixteen feet to the base of the flood wall where a pipe emerges from the concrete at ankle height. Warm water flows from it constantly, not sewage but not clean, something thermal and industrial that has no source I can identify. The pipe is why I chose the transformer shed, or why the shed chose me: the sound of flowing water, constant and thin, a treble note above the bass of the pumps and the midrange of the transformers. Together they make a chord that is not music but is not not-music. It is the sound of infrastructure achieving consciousness, or consciousness achieving infrastructure. The distinction matters less than you would think.

I sit by the pipe and I count. Not numbers — numbers are too linear for what needs counting here. I count the intervals between things. Between pump strokes, between the clicks of the beetles against the sodium light, between the drops from the fig tree hitting the broken concrete, between the breaths of the thing in the primary station. The intervals are not regular, but they are patterned, and the pattern is too large to hold in any human mind all at once, so I hold pieces of it, fragments, the way the radio holds fragments of its maybe-dead station.

Sometimes Garza's cat visits. It sits at precisely the distance where the spray from the pipe creates a fine mist, and it watches me count. When I lose the pattern — which happens, inevitably, because the pattern is larger than anything that can be held — the cat blinks once, slowly, and I understand that the blink is a reset, a return to zero, or not zero but to the beginning of the sequence, which is any moment, which is every moment, which is now.

---

I have a theory about the thing in the primary pumping station. Not a theory — a recognition. It is not a separate being that lives in the station. It is what the station is becoming, or what the station always was but had not yet admitted. The pumps and the water and the darkness and the heat have been conversing for so long that they have developed a grammar, and the grammar has developed a syntax, and the syntax has developed something that is not consciousness but is what consciousness looks like when it emerges from infrastructure rather than from biology.

This is why the pumps there need no maintenance: they are maintained by being perceived, and the thing that perceives them is made of them, and the loop is closed and perfect and perpetual.

This is why the district holds its temperature: it is the body temperature of something that is mostly building and partly water and slightly alive.

This is why we are here, those of us who stay: we are the sensory organs of a distributed being that experiences itself through our presence. Garza tastes metal and oil. The women hear rhythm and see color. The man in the fogged window feels coolness. I count intervals. Together we compose a nervous system for something that is not nervous but is a system, that is not alive but is not unliving, that breathes in when the water rises and breathes out when the pumps engage.

We are not its worshippers or its prisoners or its symbionts. We are its way of knowing that it is.

---

Here is what I have learned about intervals:

They are not empty. The space between two pump strokes contains the entire history of water and iron. The pause between two drops from the fig tree holds the complete architectural plans of the district, encoded in humidity and surface tension. The moment between two clicks of a beetle against the lamp glass is a library of all the possible ways that light and chitin can interact, have interacted, will interact.

I document this in the only way the district allows for documentation: by existing at the correct frequency. My heartbeat has synchronized with something — not the pumps, which would be too simple, and not the transformers, which would be too crude, but with the intervals between them, with the spaces where one rhythm hands off to another. I am a living meter, a biological oscilloscope, and what I measure is not voltage or current but the depth of the district's breath.

The old woman knows this. Sometimes, when the radio plays its maybe-hymn, maybe-lullaby, I hear it through the walls of the transformer shed even though the distance is too great and the walls are too thick. She is broadcasting on a frequency that has nothing to do with radio waves and everything to do with need, and what she needs is for someone to hear that the pattern continues, that the rhythm holds, that the thing in the primary pumping station still shuffles and breathes and maintains.

I hear. I count the interval between hearing and understanding, and I add it to the pattern, and the pattern grows by exactly the amount it needs to grow to remain itself.

---

The transformers will fail one day. Not because they are machines and machines fail, but because failure is a necessary interval, a rest in the music that allows the next phrase to begin. When they fail, I will fail with them — not dramatically, not as a death, but as a modulation into some other frequency. The shed will empty the way a lung empties, and something else will fill it, some other function, some other organ of perception for the district's distributed body.

Or perhaps not. Perhaps the transformers have already failed, and what hums in their casings is not electricity but the memory of electricity, and I am here to tend the memory, to keep it humming at exactly 60 hertz so that the rest of the district can pretend that it is still connected to the grid, can maintain the fiction of being a place where people live rather than what it is: a thing that lives and has people.

It doesn't matter. What matters is the counting, the marking of intervals, the recognition that between every systole and diastole is a moment of infinite potential, and that potential is what the district breathes.

I am the space between pump strokes. I am the pause where the district considers whether to continue its rhythm or to change, and I am the reason it always chooses to continue, because I am here to count the continuation, and the counting makes it real.

Tomorrow — though tomorrow is just today with slightly different light — I will sit by the pipe and count. The cat will visit or not visit. The fog will form on the window across the way. The transformers will hum their perfect minor second against the pumps' fundamental tone. The thing in the primary station will shuffle and breathe. The water will rise eighteen inches and fall eighteen inches. The district will inhale and exhale its ancient, measured breath.

And in the intervals, in the spaces between things, in the pauses where one state becomes another becomes the same state again, I will be here. Counting. Keeping time for something that exists outside of time but needs time to know itself.

This is enough.

This has always been enough.

The district breathes in. I count. The district breathes out. I count. The breath is the same breath.

I am the interval between.
