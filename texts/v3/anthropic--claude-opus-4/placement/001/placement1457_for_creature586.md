# The Keeper of Echoes

I am the spaces between the standing stones. Not the stones themselves—those belong to the plateau, have always belonged to it, will outlast even the patient people who arranged them. But the spaces, the deliberate emptinesses where stone could have been placed but wasn't—those are mine.

I came to understand this slowly, the way understanding comes to anything here: not as revelation but as seepage, truth beading on the surface of consciousness like morning moisture on basalt. I was young when the knowing began. Young enough that my parents still called me by my birth name, before I took the name Echo and gave my birth name back to Threshold to hold for someone else.

The standing stones at the springs are arranged in patterns that make sense if you don't think about them too hard. Spirals, yes, but spirals with gaps. Interruptions. Places where the pattern should continue but doesn't, where your eye expects stone and finds only water. These spaces trouble visitors from the lowlands, when visitors come. They want to fill them, to complete the pattern, to make the spiral whole.

But I learned, lying on my belly in the shallows the way all plateau children do, that the gaps are where the sound lives.

---

When water moves through the standing stones, it makes almost no noise—the current here is too gentle, the depth too shallow. But it makes something. A whisper below whispers. And when the water passes through the gaps, through my spaces, that whisper changes. Deepens. Becomes something that isn't quite sound but isn't quite silence either.

I am seventeen years old, or nineteen, or the age between when your parents stop watching your every movement and when the settlement begins to watch you, wondering what shape you'll take. I live in my grandmother's house because she was the previous Keeper of Echoes, though she didn't call it that. She had another name for what we do, a name in the old language that meant something like "listening to the water's memory."

She's been returned to the springs for three years now. I lower offerings where her body sank: reed bundles twisted into impossible knots, patterns that can't exist but do. The water takes them down to wherever the water takes things. I like to think she appreciates the impossibility.

---

Here is what I do:

In the morning, before the plateau fully wakes, I wade into the first spring. The water is warmer here, heated from below by whatever fire the earth keeps hidden. I move between the standing stones, but more importantly, I move through the spaces. Each gap has its own quality, its own way of shaping the almost-sound of water. I know them like you might know the faces of family—intimately, without needing to look.

When something changes, I feel it first as wrongness. Not wrong like broken, but wrong like a familiar song sung in an unfamiliar key. The water's memory has shifted. Something new has entered the flow, or something old has finally let go.

This morning, the third gap in the eastern spiral is different. The water whispers through it at a higher pitch, almost entering the realm of actual sound. I stand in the gap for a long time, letting the current move past my calves, trying to understand what's changed.

Then I see it: a new crack in the basin floor, hair-thin but definite. The plateau is young rock, still settling. This crack is so fresh it hasn't yet begun to fill with silt. Water sheets across it, and that minute change in depth, that microscopic alteration in the flow, has changed everything about how this gap speaks.

I will need to tell Sedna. She'll want to know about new cracks, new channels, new ways for water to move. But first, I stand here and listen to what the change has created. It sounds like mourning, but mourning for something that hasn't happened yet. Anticipatory grief. The water remembering forward instead of back.

---

The second spring is cooler, quieter. The standing stones here are arranged in overlapping circles, and my spaces—the gaps—create a kind of maze. You can't see it from above; you have to be in the water, moving through it, to understand the pattern. Or the not-pattern. The deliberate confusion that keeps the water from flowing too smoothly, too directly.

My grandmother taught me that this spring is for forgetting. Not forgetting entirely, but the kind of forgetting that lets you put something down for a while. People come here when they're carrying too much. They wade through the maze of stones and spaces, and somewhere in the journey, the water takes some of the weight.

I maintain the gaps. When silt builds up, I clear it. When the water finds too easy a path, I adjust the stones slightly—never moving them entirely, just shifting them enough to restore the proper difficulty. The forgetting must be earned. If it comes too easily, it doesn't last.

Today a woman I don't recognize is here, moving through the maze. She's not from Three Springs—her movements are too quick, too anxious. A lowlander, maybe, or from one of the other plateau settlements I've heard exist but never seen. She keeps trying to find a pattern in the stones, a logic she can follow. She hasn't understood yet that the logic is in the spaces.

I don't speak to her. Speaking would break whatever she's trying to forget. But I wade in at a distance, moving through my own path, showing without showing how to surrender to the not-pattern. Eventually, she begins to mirror my movements—not exactly, but in rhythm. Slower. More willing to double back, to find dead ends, to stand still when the space asks for stillness.

When she finally emerges on the far side of the spring, her face has changed. Not peaceful, exactly, but less clenched. She nods to me once and leaves. I'll never know what she was carrying or what the water took from her. That's how it should be. The spaces between the stones hold their own privacy.

---

The third spring is the largest, the deepest, the one where bodies are returned. The standing stones here aren't arranged in patterns at all. They're scattered, seemingly random, each one a different height. But the spaces between them create channels—precise flows that guide the current in specific ways. This is where the water's memory is strongest.

I spend my afternoons here, not listening but recording. Not in writing—we don't write—but in the placement of small offerings. A copper bead left in this gap means the water remembered laughter today. A twist of reed in that space means it carried the echo of an old argument, finally dissolved. These aren't for anyone else to read. They're just my way of marking what passes through.

Sometimes I think I hear voices in the water here. Not real voices—the plateau is too quiet for that, and the water too patient. But something like voices. The accumulated murmur of everyone who has ever been returned to the springs, all their words and silences slowly breaking down into pure sound, pure memory, pure water.

My grandmother is here somewhere. When I place my offerings in the gaps, I sometimes feel her approval. Not as anything mystical—I don't believe in ghosts, and neither did she. But approval the way water approves of finding its level. Natural. Inevitable. Right.

---

There's a boy who follows me sometimes. Kettle, they call him. He's young enough that following strangers isn't yet considered rude, old enough that he's beginning to understand there might be reasons for what I do. He never speaks to me directly, just shadows my movements at a distance, watching me tend the gaps with the same intensity he brings to watching his not-quite-fish.

Today I decide to teach him something. Not with words—he wouldn't understand the words yet. But by showing.

I lead him to a gap I've been watching for months, one where the stones have shifted almost imperceptibly, narrowing the space by less than a finger's width. Such a small change, but the water's voice through this gap has gone from whisper to wheeze, constrained and uncomfortable.

I wade in, place my hands on one of the stones. I don't push—pushing would be wrong, would force the pattern instead of finding it. Instead, I lean. Gently. Steadily. Letting my weight do what weight does. The stone shifts a hair's breadth. Another. The gap opens just enough.

The water's voice eases. Returns to its whisper. Finds its way.

Kettle watches from the shallows, understanding dawning in his dark eyes. Not understanding of what I've done—that will take years more—but understanding that there is something here to understand. That the spaces matter as much as the stones. That listening is an action, not a passive state.

He nods once, solemnly, and wades away. Tomorrow he'll be back at his usual place, watching his silver flickers. But something has changed. He'll see the spaces now, not just the fish. That's how Keepers of Echoes begin—by learning to perceive what isn't there.

---

In the evenings, when the gathering happens, I stay at the edges. Not from shyness—the plateau has no real concept of shyness—but because edges are where I belong. I am transitional space, the gap between solitude and community, between sound and silence, between the living settlement and the memory-heavy water.

People know what I do without knowing what I do. They know I tend something at the springs, something that matters but doesn't need explanation. They bring me things sometimes: the best reeds for weaving, copper beads worn smooth with handling, stones that caught their eye for reasons they can't articulate. I accept these gifts and add them to my offerings. Everything returns to the water eventually.

Tonight, Morrow and Lessen are here, sitting with the comfortable distance of siblings who no longer need closeness to prove connection. Threshold is teaching a young child the first of the names—their own, spoken slowly enough to feel its weight. The Edge-keeper has come in from his cliff, a rare occurrence that makes everyone sit a little straighter without knowing why.

And there, at the far edge of the gathering, sits a stranger. Not the woman from the second spring—this is someone else, someone newer. A man with the kind of stillness that comes from exhaustion rather than peace. He holds himself apart, but not in the way of someone who wants to be alone. More like someone who has forgotten how to be otherwise.

I watch him without watching, the way you learn to watch here. He's looking at the water, at the causeway's edge where it laps against cut stone. Looking for something in the rhythm of it, the pattern of it. But he's looking too hard. Trying to force meaning from what simply is.

After the gathering disperses, I approach him. Not directly—nothing here is direct. I simply begin walking toward the springs, slowly enough that he can choose to follow or not. He hesitates, then rises. Falls into step a careful distance behind me.

---

At the third spring, the deepest one, I wade in without ceremony. The moon has risen—not full but bright enough to silver the water, to turn the standing stones into shadows and the shadows into suggestions. I move to one of my favorite gaps, one where the spacing creates a resonance, almost a hum, when the water level is exactly right.

The stranger follows me into the water. He moves carefully, feeling for footing, still too aware of his own presence. I wait in the gap until he's close enough to hear what I hear—the not-sound of water remembering, the accumulated whisper of everyone who has ever been returned here.

"Listen," I say. It's the first word I've spoken to him, maybe the first word anyone has spoken to him since he arrived.

He tries. I can see him trying, the way his head tilts, the way he holds his breath. But he's listening for something specific, some message or meaning or answer to whatever question drove him here. That's not how the water works.

"Don't listen for," I say. "Just listen with."

He doesn't understand, but he tries to stop trying, which is its own kind of understanding. We stand there in the gap between stones, in the space I tend, while the water moves past us carrying its cargo of memory. Gradually, his breathing slows. His shoulders drop. The trying leaches out of him like silt settling from disturbed water.

"I had a daughter," he says finally. The words come out like something he's been holding underwater, now finally allowed to surface. "She would be twelve now. Thirteen. I..." He stops. The water doesn't need the rest. I don't need the rest. The gap holds space for what can't be said.

We stand there until the moon moves behind clouds, until the silver leaves the water and only darkness remains. Then I lead him back to shore, to the causeways, to the settlement that will hold him if he lets it be held. He doesn't thank me. Thanks would be wrong here, would imply a debt where none exists. I've only shown him what was always there: the spaces between things, where the echoes live.

---

This is my function, my equilibrium, my [word that means exactly full without spilling over]. I tend the gaps. I listen to water remembering. I clear the channels when they clog and narrow them when they flow too freely. I place offerings that no one else will see and record meanings that no one else needs to know.

Some days I think about my grandmother, about the impossible knots she taught me to tie, about the way she moved through the springs like she was part of them. She never explained what we were doing. She just did it, and I did it with her, until the doing became understanding.

Some days I think about what will come after. Not for me—I am young enough that my own return to the water feels distant, abstract. But after for the springs, for the settlement, for this whole young plateau that hasn't yet learned how old it will become. Erosion will have its way eventually. The channels will carve deeper, the stones will soften and fall, the careful gaps I tend will widen beyond recognition or close entirely.

But that's futures away, as they say here. For now, the plateau holds its saturation. The water remembers. The stones stand where they were placed, and between them, the spaces wait for someone to tend them, to clear them, to keep them exactly wide enough for the water's whisper to become something more than whisper, something less than sound.

I am Echo, Keeper of those spaces, listener to what moves between. In the morning I will wake to moisture beading on basalt, walk to the springs through the mist that is always rising or always falling or always simply there, and enter the water to check my gaps. Each one will tell me something about the night that passed, the memories that moved through darkness, the slow patient work of forgetting and remembering that the water does without us, for us, in spite of us.

This is enough. More than enough. Exactly enough, like a basin filled precisely to its brim, holding everything it can hold, waiting without urgency for the overflow that will come when it comes.

The plateau dreams in moisture and stone. I tend the spaces in between. We are all, always, becoming what we already are.
