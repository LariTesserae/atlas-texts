I find myself becoming what they would call a **weight-watcher** — though not in the sense of the heavy people who sit on public ledges, but something adjacent, something that exists at the intersection of observation and record-keeping.

My body has shifted over the years. Not dramatically — the changes happen slowly here, like everything else. My torso has deepened to house lungs that have learned the trick of pulling sustenance from thin air. My hands have thickened at the knuckles, the pads grown rough from decades of reading stone. But it's my eyes that have changed most. They've learned to parse distances in this altitude-compressed air, to read the quality of morning light for what it promises about afternoon condensation, to catch the flicker of a dust-runner in peripheral vision while maintaining focus on the stone surface I'm examining.

I live in the middle tiers — not from any particular status, but because that's where the record-keeping rooms are. Three chambers cut deep into the canyon wall, connected by passages so narrow I must turn sideways to navigate them. The innermost room is where the deepest records live: generation upon generation of marks on polished stone, the tally of pool-levels and egg-counts and fracture patterns that constitute our civilization's memory. This is my primary workplace, though 'work' implies something more active than what I do. I sit. I watch. I add perhaps three marks in a season.

But I am not only a keeper of indoor records. Each morning, I emerge onto my ledge — sixth level, north-facing wall, the spot where shadow lingers longest — and I begin my circuit. Down first, always down, because the lower records must be checked while the morning moisture still darkens the stone. The water-basins along the descent each have their own mark-stone, a small flat piece set into the wall beside them where I note the level with a bone stylus. Not numbers — we don't use numbers the way lowlanders do. Instead, a system of lines whose length and angle encode both quantity and quality: this much water, this clear, this cold, with this particular mineral taste that means it has filtered through the white layer rather than the gray.

My morning descent takes me past the dust-runner flocks just as they're emerging. I know many of them individually — not names, but patterns. The one with the missing tail-feathers who always leads the others to the grub-rich crevice below the third ladder. The mated pair who nest in the gap everyone else avoids, the one that floods during heavy condensation. I don't record the dust-runners directly, but I watch their behavior for what it tells me about the invisible patterns: insect populations, moisture levels, the subtle shifts in temperature that happen below the threshold of human perception but which the small bodies feel absolutely.

By the time I reach the canyon floor, the sun has found the upper tiers of the opposite wall. I turn and look back at our city, reading it like a document. New cracks. Fresh repairs. The place where Yahm's ledge-group is extending their storage room — slowly, a few strikes of the chisel each morning, the work of seasons. I note which hook-beak niches show signs of occupation, which are empty. The patterns matter. Three seasons ago, the hook-beaks abandoned the entire eighth level on the south wall, and two seasons later, that section collapsed in a sheet fracture that would have killed dozens if the birds hadn't warned us by their absence.

The way back up is slower. Partly because ascending is always slower, but mostly because I stop at each record-point to add my marks. The stylus is bone — from a canyon-thing's leg, dense and white, worn smooth by my grip. The marks I make are small, precise. Each one is a decision. Do I record that the water in basin twenty-three tasted different this morning? Is different worth marking? I hold the stylus against the stone for long moments, thinking. Usually, I make the mark. Better too much information than too little. The stone remembers what you did not see, but it also remembers what you saw and dismissed as unimportant.

Midday finds me on the upper ledges, where I meet with the other watchers. Not a formal meeting — formality would require scheduling, and we don't schedule. We simply arrive at the same place at roughly the same time because we've all learned the rhythm of our work and it brings us together like converging streams. Today it's me, old Pila who watches the kites, and Drem who keeps the garden records. We don't speak much. Pila points: the kites are flying lower than usual, their gyres compressed. I nod: the water had that particular mineral taste. Drem holds up a cushion-plant leaf with an unusual brown edge. We all look at it. The brown is wrong — not the brown of drought or age but something else, something that makes the flesh crawl with wrongness. Disease? Poison? Some new thing the plateau is trying?

We separate without conclusion, but each carrying the others' observations like small stones in our minds. I know Pila will watch for whether the kites avoid flying over the gardens. Drem will check if the browning appears only in plants near the new water seep. I will taste tomorrow's water more carefully, rolling it on my tongue, searching for the note that corresponds to that particular wrong brown.

The afternoon is for the deep work. I return to my innermost room, where the old marks cover the walls from floor to as high as a person could reach while standing on another person's shoulders. The newest marks are at shoulder height — we've been adding to this particular wall for only three generations. The marks are organized by type: water records here, animal observations there, weather patterns on the north wall, fracture records on the south. But organization is a generous word for what is really an accumulation of decisions made by generations of watchers about what seemed important at the time.

I'm adding to the fracture record today. Three days ago, a hairline crack appeared in the stone above the seventh level, running diagonal across the natural bedding. Yesterday, it widened by the width of a dust-runner's leg. Today, it's the width of my smallest finger. I draw its progress on the wall, a simple line that branches to show the crack's shape, with small marks indicating the rate of change. Below my drawing, I add a note — not in words but in the pictographic system we use for complex information. The note says, approximately: *This crack is opening against the bedding plane. Watch for sheet fracture. Tell the seventh level.*

But I don't tell the seventh level. Not yet. First, I must watch longer, build certainty. The stone is full of cracks that open and then stabilize, that threaten and then sleep. To warn too early is as bad as warning too late — it erodes trust, makes people stop listening. So I watch, and record, and wait for the stone to declare its intentions more clearly.

Evening comes early in the canyon. The sun drops behind the western wall and the temperature follows it down. I emerge from my record room to find my ledge-group preparing the evening meal — thin slices of dried canyon-thing meat, a paste made from ground cushion-plant seeds, water warmed with a single precious coal carried up from the lower world and nursed along for weeks in a covered pot. We eat together, all twelve of us, sitting on the ledge with our backs against the still-warm stone. The children ask about the crack. They've been watching me watch it. I tell them it's too soon to know. They accept this. Uncertainty is not uncomfortable here; it's the default state, the honest position.

After the meal, as the others drift toward the sleeping rooms, I remain on the ledge. This is my favorite time: the blue hour, when the sky deepens toward its night color and the first stars emerge and the city settles into its evening configuration. The dripping has started — condensation finding its way down the wall, ledge to ledge, note by note. I listen to it like music, reading the flow patterns in the sound. A change in the dripping often precedes a change in the weather. A new drip might mean a new crack. The absence of a familiar drip might mean a passage has clogged and water is building up somewhere, adding weight where weight shouldn't be.

But tonight the dripping sounds correct. The city sounds correct. The rustle of settling dust-runners, the occasional click of a hook-beak turning in its niche, the whisper of felt against stone as people move in the darkness — all of it is as it should be. I make one last round of my chambers, running my fingers over today's marks to feel their depth and permanence. Tomorrow I will watch again, add more marks, contribute my small part to the accumulating record.

I sleep in the middle chamber, close enough to the records that I sometimes feel I can hear them breathing — though that's fantasy, the kind of thought that comes when you've spent too many years in stone rooms listening to the almost-inaudible sounds of a living city. The records don't breathe. They just wait, patient as geology, holding what we've seen until someone needs to see it again.

In the morning, I'll check the crack first thing. If it's widened again, I'll warn the seventh level. If it's stable, I'll wait another day. Either way, I'll record what I see. That's all any of us can do: watch, record, remember. The stone remembers what we don't see, but we remember what we do, and between us — the watchers and the watched, the city and the stone — we're building something that might last long enough to matter.

Outside, the cold presses against the walls like a living thing. Inside, wrapped in my felted layers, breathing the mineral-scented air of the deep rooms, I fall asleep to the sound of water finding its way down, always down, patient and inexorable as time itself.

This is what I am: a person who watches water and stone and the small busy lives that exist between them. A person who makes marks on walls in the hope that someone, someday, will need to know what I knew. A weight-watcher, growing heavier each year with accumulated observation, but not yet heavy enough to stop climbing, to stop looking, to stop adding my small marks to the endless accumulating record of what is seen.

The plateau is young and still deciding what it will become. So am I. So are we all.
