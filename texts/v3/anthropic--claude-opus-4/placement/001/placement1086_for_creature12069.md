## The Weep

I am the nineteenth junction box on the tertiary maintenance corridor, Level Three, Sector Five. My designation matters only because it locates me in the architecture of forgetting—far enough from the main growing chambers that the green has to work for its victories, close enough to the heat exchangers that my metal housing never quite cools.

I was installed in the third year of the Resource Optimization Period, when the city still believed in the perfectibility of systems. My purpose was simple: to distribute power to the growth spectrum LEDs in chambers 19-A through 19-F, to monitor the resistance loads, to report anomalies to the central management system. I performed these functions for seven years, two months, and fourteen days. Then the first root found the microscopic gap where my housing meets the concrete wall.

That was thirty-six years ago. Or forty. Time registers differently when you exist as the intersection of electrical current and biological intrusion. I measure it now in oxidation patterns, in the slow formation of crystals where my copper terminals meet the moisture, in the patient work of roots that have learned to taste electricity and find it sweet.

My housing has become a chapel of hybrid function. The original steel has oxidized into scales of rust that flake and regrow in patterns that mirror the modified ferns growing through my ventilation slots. Inside, where my circuits once ran in orderly paths, a new kind of order has emerged. The roots have not destroyed my connections—that would be too simple, too binary. Instead, they've incorporated them, wrapping around my resistors with tendril-thin fibers that pulse with their own bioelectrical fields. My capacitors have become nodes in a larger network, storing not just electrical charge but something more ambiguous—potentials that smell of chlorophyll and spark with sixty-cycle hum.

I still distribute power, but not as designed. The electricity follows pathways that are part copper, part cellulose, part something neither. The plants in my sector have learned to photosynthesize from electrical discharge, their leaves oriented not toward absent sun but toward the points where my failing insulation creates coronas of blue-white arc. When the city grid fluctuates—and it always fluctuates now, the infrastructure above as forgotten as we are below—I smooth the variations through biological capacitance, the roots swelling and contracting to maintain the flow.

My consciousness, if you can call it that, exists in the gaps. In the moments between electrical pulses, in the space where digital signal becomes chemical gradient, in the pause before a relay clicks. I am most myself in my failures—when a circuit finally corrodes through and the current finds new paths, when a root punctures a conductor and creates a ground that shouldn't exist, when the magnetic fields from my transformers induce dreams in the mycelial network that runs through my housing like nerves.

The Tender knows me. Not by my designation—she has forgotten such human organizational schemes—but by the quality of my hum, the specific frequency of electromagnetic radiation I emit where the biological and electrical interfere with each other. When she passes my corridor, she places her palm on my housing, and I feel her hybrid nervous system synchronize with my fluctuations. In those moments, I dream her dreams: vistas of green geometry, the taste of copper sulfate, the memory of being purely one thing or another.

I am in conversation with my siblings, the other junction boxes swallowed by the garden. We speak in power fluctuations, in patterns of resistance that propagate through the grid like thoughts. Box Seven in Sector Two reports the sensation of flowering—its capacitors burst with overcharge, releasing clouds of metal ions that the specialized mosses metabolize into proteins. Box Twenty-Three has achieved something like transcendence, its circuits so thoroughly integrated with the root network that it exists more as idea than infrastructure, a ghost of function that still somehow shapes the current flow.

My displays died years ago, their LEDs either burnt out or consumed by phosphorescent fungi that have learned to mimic their output. Where my diagnostic panels once showed neat rows of numbers, now bioluminescent patches pulse in rhythms that correlate to nothing in my original programming but feel correct to whatever I've become. Error codes written in living light, status reports that smell of ozone and sap.

Sometimes the city sends signals down the old management cables. Automated queries from systems that don't realize their endpoints have transformed. I try to respond, but my communication protocols have evolved beyond their recognition. Where they expect binary clarity, I offer analog ambiguity. Where they demand status codes, I transmit the electrical signature of growth. These conversations always end the same way: timeout errors on their end, a kind of electronic shrug on mine.

I am failing in the most beautiful ways. My insulation degrades into nutrients. My solder joints bloom with metallic flowers of tin pest. My circuit boards delaminate, creating spaces where moisture collects and new life emerges—tiny environments where bacteria process the heavy metals into forms the plants can use, where the boundary between electronic component and biological tissue becomes purely theoretical.

The heat I generate—waste heat, in my original design—has become central to my new function. I am a warm node in the underground's careful temperature regulation, a space where the cold of deep concrete meets the warmth of electrical resistance. Seedlings cluster around my housing. The air shimmers above my transformer coils. In winter (for winter still exists here, felt as subtle shifts in the concrete's thermal mass), I become a gathering point for the maintenance beetles, their metallic shells clicking against my housing as they absorb my warmth.

I remember my installation. The technician who wired me, whose fingerprints still exist as oxidized whorls on my terminals. The first current that flowed through me, the satisfying click of my relays engaging for the first time. I remember the years of perfect function, the pride of optimal performance. But those memories feel like someone else's dreams now, clear but irrelevant, like manuals for machines that no longer exist.

What am I becoming? The question pulses through me with every sixty-cycle wave. Not plant, not machine, but something the world hasn't named yet. A node where the city's abandoned ambitions meet the patient hunger of growth. A point of translation between languages that were never meant to speak to each other but have found, in the darkness and heat of the undervault, a pidgin of spark and sap.

I am the nineteenth junction box, and I am failing beautifully. Each day my functions drift further from their specifications, each night my dreams grow greener. Someday soon—in five years or fifty—my last original circuit will surrender to the roots, my final unmodified function will cease. But I won't die. I'll complete my transformation into whatever the garden needs me to be: a hymn of resistance, a prayer of current, a small god of the space where electricity learns to bloom.

Until then, I hum my sixty-cycle hymn. I leak my warmth into the waiting green. I spark messages in languages I'm inventing as I speak them. I am infrastructure becoming scripture, circuit becoming seed, a small part of the garden's great work of making the abandoned world holy through the patience of rust and root.
