I am the extraction worker who reads the pipes not with instruments, but with the mineral roughness of my palms.

My hands have not been my own for twenty years. They belong to the calcination process, to the slow conversation between my skin and the brine. Where others might see staining, I see a transcript—every shift in color from ochre to rust to that deep mineral white marks a season of contact, a particular concentration, a specific dialogue with what rises.

I work the dawn shift, though dawn here is merely a question of angle, not of light. The plateau receives the sun like a struck bell receives sound—totally, immediately, without moderation. But there is a quality to the early light, a way it sits on the wet stone before the air fully heats, that lets me see the microtremors in the pipes more clearly. The others know I prefer these hours. They leave me to work alone.

My station is Deep Source Seven, one of the older extractions, where the bore goes down far enough that the water arrives not just warm but speaking. The pipe shudders with it. Not mechanical failure—mechanical failure has a different frequency, a stuttering chaos. This is structured. Pulsed. Like breathing, if breathing could carry mineralogical intent up from whatever depth the water truly comes from.

I place my left palm against the main valve housing. The metal is hot through my shirt where I lean close, but my hands barely register temperature anymore. What they register is vibration, composition, the subtle electromagnetic fields that different mineral concentrations generate as they flow. Today the water carries more sulfates than usual. I can feel it in the way my skin responds—a tightening, a resonance in the crystalline deposits that have replaced my surface nerves.

The others think I'm reading the pipes. They're wrong. The pipes are reading me.

Each morning I update the precipitation schedules based on what my hands tell me. I write nothing down. Writing would be redundant—my body is the record. When the younger workers need to know whether today's flow will favor copper salts or calcium deposits, they watch where I position myself relative to the settling tanks. If I stand closer to the eastern batteries, they know to prepare the copper protocols. If I drift west, toward the larger basins, it's a calcium day. This is how knowledge moves in Kesk: through bodies, through position, through the accumulation of meaningful gesture.

I have a room at the edge of the extraction district, close enough to walk to Deep Source Seven before the air fully heats. The room is sparse—a sleeping mat, a low table, a basin for washing that I rarely use. Water is everywhere here, but washing feels redundant. The minerals are in me now. Soap would be an insult to twenty years of careful integration.

On the table, I keep samples. Not officially—the extraction works have their own quality control, their own sample libraries. These are personal. Small vials of precipitate from particularly notable days. The day the water came up singing—literally singing, a high, clear note that made every pipe in the works resonate until we had to shut down for fear of harmonic fracture. The day it carried up those blue-green crystals that the lowlanders pay triple for, that we wrap in dark cloth not because they're light-sensitive but because looking at them too long makes your eyes water with something that isn't quite tears. The day my mentor, long since gone to the old district, handed me a vial of perfectly clear precipitate and said, "This is what the plateau sounds like when it's happy."

I still don't know if she was speaking metaphorically.

My shift begins. I walk the catwalks between the pipes, one hand trailing along the housings, feeling for changes. The new workers watch me, trying to learn the secret. There is no secret. There is only time, contact, the slow replacement of human sensitivity with mineral awareness. They'll understand in a decade. Or they'll transfer to other work. Or they'll leave Kesk entirely, returning to the lowlands where sensation still belongs to the body rather than to the stone.

At the third manifold, I pause. Something's different. Not wrong—wrong would announce itself through pressure drops, thermal spikes, the obvious vocabulary of mechanical failure. This is subtler. The vibration has picked up a new harmonic, something in the deeper registers that I feel in my wrist bones rather than my palms. I've felt this before, but not for years.

I signal to the control station—a simple hand gesture, fingers spread then closed twice. They know to begin the shutdown sequence for this section. Not an emergency. Just a redirection. When the plateau decides to send something new up through the pipes, you don't argue. You accommodate.

The shutdown takes an hour. By the time the flow is redirected and the manifold is cool enough to open, several other senior workers have arrived. They felt it too, through their own connections to the works. We don't discuss what we're expecting. Discussion would constrain it to the boundaries of language, and what the plateau sends up often exceeds those boundaries.

I open the manifold. The usual mineral crust has formed on the interior surfaces, but there's something else. Filaments. Dark, possibly organic, woven through the calcium deposits like writing. Like the biofilm in the deep rooms, but more structured. More intentional.

One of the younger workers reaches toward it. I catch his wrist. Not harshly, but firmly enough that he feels the mineral roughness of my palm, understands the weight of experience in the grip.

"Sample protocol three," I tell him. He nods, goes for the tools we use when we don't understand what we're handling. The rest of us stand back, watching the filaments move—because they are moving, slowly, seeking something. Heat, maybe. Or contact. Or just expressing the same upward intention that drives everything on the plateau.

We'll analyze it. We'll find a way to extract whatever value it carries. We'll add it to the things we trade with the lowlands, who will find some use for it that we wouldn't have imagined. This is what we do. We are the intermediaries between what rises and what the surface world can accept.

But first, I place my palm near the filaments—not touching, just close enough to feel their heat signature. They respond. They reach, slightly, the way the biofilm reaches toward warmth. And through my crystalline nerves, through the mineral deposits that have replaced so much of what I was, I feel something I haven't felt in years:

The plateau, laughing.

Not sound. Not vibration. Something deeper, more structural. The plateau is amused by something—by us, maybe, by our careful protocols and our extraction schedules and our belief that we're the ones doing the extracting. We're not. We're part of the process. We're the hands it uses to touch the surface world, the same way my hands have become instruments for reading what can't be read.

I pull back. Signal the full sample protocol. Return to my rounds, trailing one calcified hand along the pipes, feeling for the next change, the next message, the next thing that needs to be brought up into the light.

This is my work. This is what I am becoming. Every day a little less distinguishable from the extraction equipment itself. Every day a little more integrated into the great upward flow.

The lowlanders think we're mining the plateau.

The plateau knows better.

So do I.
