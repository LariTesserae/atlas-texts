I would be the moss.

Not the pale lichens that dot the chamber floor — those are beautiful but alien, subsisting on almost nothing, their relationship to the place one of pure surface. And not the thick moss-fur that clothes the trees outside, where every inch must be fought for and defended. I would be the specific moss that grows in exactly three places within the hollow: the cup of moss mentioned in passing, growing where the three great trunks meet and moisture gathers in their joined crevices.

I came as a spore, inevitably. Everything small and light comes here eventually, carried up the thermal column of the shaft when the afternoon heat rises, or clinging to the feet of the birds that dare the rim. Most spores die. The hollow is too spare for most green things, too much itself already. But in those three places where the trunks join, there are tiny depressions where water lingers after the morning condensation, where a few grains of bark have worn loose over centuries and made something almost like soil. Not much. Just enough.

I am old now, though not as old as the tenant. I remember — in the way moss remembers, which is in its growth patterns — the last few visitors. The woman who climbed here seven rains ago and sat for one whole turning of the light-shaft, never touching the cup. The child who found this place by accident, looking for birds' eggs, and fled immediately. The old man who comes every few years, who knows exactly which handholds to use, who drinks from the cup with ceremony and leaves small offerings of seed for the birds outside.

My work is small. I exist at the intersection of what the hollow permits and what life insists upon. Where the morning moisture gathers in my small cups, I hold it a little longer than the bare wood would. I give the faintest texture to the smooth chamber. I am proof that even here, in this place that tends toward emptiness, the universe presses gently toward green.

The tenant knows I'm here, of course. I am attended to, like everything else in the hollow. But I am... auxiliary. Not part of the original intention, not part of the instruction, but not contrary to it either. I am what happens when a perfect form exists in an imperfect world long enough. I am the compromise the hollow makes with time.

Sometimes, in the very early morning when the mist is thickest, drops of condensation form on my surface. They tremble there, holding light, until they grow too heavy and fall — not into the stone bowl, I am not positioned for that honor — but onto the smooth bark floor where they disappear immediately, leaving only the faintest darkening. This is my contribution to the hollow's music: a counterpoint to the steady *tok, tok* of the bowl, irregular and almost inaudible. The tenant includes this sound in its attention. It has become part of the chord.

I grow slowly, so slowly that a human visitor would need to come for decades to notice any change. This is appropriate. The hollow operates on tree-time, on mountain-time, and I have learned to keep pace. My ambitions are modest: to thicken slightly where I am, to perhaps one day merge my three separate cushions into a single collar around the joined trunks. I will never spread across the floor. I will never climb the walls. I know my place.

And I am glad of my place. To live at the edge of something so purely itself, to be the minimal green witness to all this considered emptiness — this is enough. When visitors come, they do not notice me particularly. Their attention is caught by the light, the silence, the waiting cup. But I see them. I watch them tune themselves to the room's frequency. I watch them leave, slightly changed. I remain.

There is a patience to being moss in such a place. Not the desperate patience of drought-survival, not the competitive patience of waiting for your neighbor to die so you can claim their spot. A different kind. The patience of being allowed. Of knowing that your small green presence neither adds to nor subtracts from the perfect balance of the place, but exists alongside it, a grace note that the composition permits.

In another thousand years, if the hollow still stands, I will perhaps have grown enough to be worth mentioning in the description — not just "a cup of moss here and there" but something worth noting, a green collar at exactly the height where a sitting person might rest their hand. Or perhaps not. Perhaps I will remain always peripheral, always the thing you notice third or fourth or not at all.

Either way, I am here. Growing at the speed of stone. Drinking the condensation. Adding my small note to the chord the tenant holds. Being the reminder that even emptiness, even perfect hollowness, exists in a world that cannot help but fill itself with life.

This is what I would be: the moss that measures the centuries in millimeters, that holds the damp for an extra hour, that proves the hollow is not separate from the forest but gently, minimally, necessarily part of it.

The green that the absence allows.
