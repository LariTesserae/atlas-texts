I am the taste-keeper's apprentice, though she has never named me so. I came to learn the water's language three seasons ago—or what passes for seasons here, marked not by temperature but by the subtle shifts in mineral concentration that only tongues like ours can detect.

My sleeping niche is one of the newer carvings, still rough where my predecessor's tool-marks catch my hip each night. The stone tastes young when I press my tongue to it in the darkness—only a few centuries of water have softened its edges. Below me, my teacher occupies a niche so old its walls have grown a skin of flowstone, smooth as the inside of a throat.

Each morning I wake to her fingers on my ankle—two taps, always two, like the drip-drip of water finding its path. This is how she speaks before words: *Come. The mountain is telling us something.*

Today the telling is in the third planting trough, where the pale cress has developed a faint russet tinge along its stems. I crouch beside her as she crushes a leaf between her fingers, brings it to her nose, then places it on her tongue. Her eyes close. I watch the movement of her throat as she processes the information—not swallowing exactly, but holding the taste in her mouth the way the reservoir holds water, letting it settle into meaning.

She takes my hand and guides it to the trough's feed channel. The stone here is darker, stained with a new mineral we haven't tasted before. Iron, perhaps, or some cousin of iron, leaching from a previously dry stratum now reached by our patient water. She scrapes a fingernail along the stain, gathering the thin film of precipitate, and transfers it to my tongue.

The taste blooms: metal and earth and time. But underneath, something else. Something sulfurous and sharp that makes my sinuses flare. I know immediately what this means. Somewhere above us, the mountain's chemistry is changing. A new seep has opened, or an old channel has broken through to virgin rock. The water table has shifted, minutely but significantly.

My teacher nods—that smallest of gestures, chin settling like sediment. I have read correctly. She produces her tools: a set of bone scrapers worn thin as breathing, and the precious metal knife—copper like our water, green with verdigris, one of only three in the quarter. Together we begin the work of adjusting the trough's mineral supplement, scraping different powders from her collection of stones, each one a library of chemical possibility.

She names them as we work, not in the common language but in the old vocabulary, the one that exists only between us and the two other water-tongues in the quarter. *Bitter-white*, the calcium. *Blood-stone*, the iron. *Breathing-salt*, something unnameable that makes the plants reach toward the light. Her fingers are teaching mine the proportions—not by measurement but by feel, by the way different powders cling to skin, by their weight in the palm.

A child watches us from the reservoir's edge, legs dangling, toes drawing circles in the water. My teacher beckons her over. The child hesitates—children learn early which knowledge belongs to everyone and which requires invitation. But at the second gesture she comes, and my teacher takes her small hand and guides it to the amended soil.

"Taste," she says. Not a command. An offering.

The child's pink tongue touches the dirt. Her face shifts through uncertainty to recognition—she has tasted something like this before, in the water perhaps, or on the stone after rain. My teacher places a hand on the child's head, briefly, and the child understands: she has been marked as possible. If her tongue proves precise enough, if her palate develops the necessary subtlety, she might one day join our small guild. She might learn to taste the mountain's dreams.

When the child leaves, my teacher turns to me. Her hands shape meanings in the air: *The deep taste. The one below sulfur. Did you find it?*

I close my eyes, reconstruct the mineral bloom on my tongue. Yes. There, underneath everything else: a faint sweetness. Almost floral. Almost like the memory of flowers, though I have never seen a flower except for the strange dead one a returning hunter brought—blue as deep water, pressed flat and dry between stones.

"Aster-tongue," my teacher says aloud, using the old word. A taste she has encountered only twice before in her lifetime. It means the water has touched a vein of something rare and precious—not valuable in any material sense, but precious to the plants, to their pale reaching toward light.

We spend the day following the new taste through the quarter's water system. At each junction, I place my tongue against the wet stone while my teacher watches. I am mapping the chemical plume, tracing it backward toward its source. My tongue is tired by afternoon, overwhelmed by mineral information, but I continue. This is my purpose. This is what I'm becoming.

At the farthest reach of the system, where the channels narrow to finger-width and the stone sweats rather than flows, I find it: a hairline crack in the wall, so fine it's invisible except for the darker stain of moisture around it. The aster-tongue water emerges here, one drop at a time.

My teacher produces her copper knife. Together, we begin the delicate work of enlarging the channel without disturbing the flow. The stone here is different—grainier, more willing to release itself in small sandy particles rather than flakes. We work by feel, our fingers reading the rock's structure, following its natural planes of weakness.

Hours pass. The fat-wick tender comes and adjusts the flame above us, giving us better light without being asked. They have seen us at work and understand. Someone brings water—not our local water but a different flavor from another part of the quarter, and we drink it gratefully, our tongues automatically cataloguing its mineral signature.

By evening, we have cut a new tributary channel no longer than my hand. The aster-tongue water flows more freely now, still just drops but fuller drops, gathering into a tiny pool before overflowing into the main system. My teacher dips her finger and tastes. Nods. The concentration is good. Diluted by the time it reaches the planting troughs, it will be perfect.

At the reservoir gathering, I sit pressed between my teacher and the wall, feeling the hum build in the stones before it builds in the throats. When the voices begin, I add mine, finding the pitch that makes my chest cavity resonate with the mountain's breathing. But I am also tasting—always tasting now—the moisture in the air, the mineral breath of the gathering, the subtle chemical signature of many bodies in one space.

The old woman with her weaving sits across from us. I see her watching my teacher, some wordless communication passing between them. They have known each other for sixty years, since they were both children tasting dirt at the reservoir's edge. The old woman's fingers move in her lap, not weaving but remembering weaving, and I understand suddenly that her patterns are also a kind of tasting—that she tastes with her fingers the way we taste with our tongues, reading the world through texture and tension and the way different fibers accept the twist.

The humming deepens. My teacher's hand finds mine in the darkness, and she taps twice on my palm. *Listen.* I do, and underneath the human voices, underneath the water-drip and the mountain's own bass note, I hear it: the new channel we cut today, its voice just slightly different from all the others, a piccolo in the orchestra of seepage. The aster-tongue water is singing its way into our world.

Tomorrow there will be more tasting. The effects of the new mineral will need to be tracked through every trough, every channel, every basin. There will be adjustments, additions, dilutions. The process never ends. The mountain is never stable, not really—it only moves slowly enough that humans can match its pace.

But tonight, in this moment, I am complete. My tongue carries the memory of a hundred stones, a thousand waters. My teacher's knowledge flows into me as patiently as the seepage, one drop at a time. I am becoming what she is, what the taste-keepers before her were: a sensory organ of the quarter, a way for this community to know itself through the medium of dissolved stone.

The humming continues. I close my eyes and taste the sound.
