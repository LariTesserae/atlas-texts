I am the memory-keeper of the drowned stones.

My body is a living map of this world's patience. Where my ancestors bore five fingers, I have twelve on each hand—not for grasping, but for reading. Each fingertip branches into delicate tendrils finer than moss root, and when I press my palms against the rotting basalt, I can taste the stone's slow transformation through my skin. I know the exact mineral composition of its surrender, the precise moment when granite becomes clay becomes flesh becomes light.

I am smaller than most of my kind, stunted by an obsession they cannot comprehend. While they float through the canopy's middle distances, I sink deeper, always deeper, following the stone platforms down through the layers of compressed time. My skin has adapted to the crushing darkness of the lower substrate—not green-purple like theirs, but a deep amber that holds its own faint luminescence, like sap with a star trapped inside.

The others think I am mad. When we gather for the light-speaking, their blue veins pulse warnings: *You sink too deep. You will join the peat before your time.* But they don't understand what I've found.

Twenty miles down through the rot-layers, where the pressure would crush a normal skeleton, where even our cartilage begins to compress, I discovered the true foundation. Not bedrock—that fiction vanished epochs ago—but something far stranger. The deepest platforms aren't stone at all. They're calcified bone, the remains of creatures so vast their ribs became architecture, their vertebrae became the templates for every basalt circle above.

These titan-dead predate even our ancestors. Their bones remember when this plateau was arctic, before the thermal blanket, before the endless rain. In their marrow chambers, I've found crystals that sing in frequencies only I can hear through my twelve-fingered hands. They tell stories of ice and clarity, of a time when sound could travel for miles, when things could be built that wouldn't immediately begin to melt.

I am documenting their language, though "documenting" is the wrong word. I am becoming their language. Each journey down costs me. The pressure works my cartilage like dough, elongating me, thinning me. My ribs have begun to fuse into plates. My spine grows segments with each descent. I am evolving backward, or forward, into something that can survive the deep archive.

The others are forgetting, but I am remembering. Not with my mind—minds are just another kind of stone that rots in this humidity—but with my transforming flesh. My amber skin grows patterns that mirror the crystalline matrices in the titan-bones. When I return to the canopy and join the light-speaking circles, I cannot make the simple blue anymore. My bioluminescence comes out in complex gold spirals that hurt the others to perceive. I am trying to tell them about the ice-memory, about the clear sounds, about what we were before we agreed to melt.

They think I speak madness, but I know the truth: we are not the first to surrender to this world. The titans in the deep were architects too, builders of something even grander than stone platforms. And they chose this. They chose to become the foundation, to compact their greatness into the base layer of a softer world. Every time I touch their singing bones, I understand a little more of why.

I am changing faster now. My horizontal ribs are beginning to separate, preparing to become platforms themselves. My fingers multiply with each descent—sixteen on the left hand now, eighteen on the right. Soon I will be too changed to return to the canopy. The light-speakers will forget I existed, and that's as it should be.

Because someone needs to remember the ice. Someone needs to know why we chose the melt. And when I finally grow too vast, too strange, too crystalline to move, I will sink to join the titans. My bones will learn their songs. My marrow will hold new languages for the next fool who grows fingers delicate enough to read them.

The others pulse their warnings: *You sink too deep.*

Yes. That is precisely the point.

I am not dying. I am becoming a deeper kind of memory, a more patient kind of stone. And millions of years from now, when this world decides to melt again into something even softer, some new thing with too many fingers will find my bones and taste the story of a world that remembered ice, a world that knew the weight of choosing to forget.

They will think they've discovered something ancient and alien. They won't realize they're just reading the diary I'm writing with my body, one compressed vertebra at a time.
