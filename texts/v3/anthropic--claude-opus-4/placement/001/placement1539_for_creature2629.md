I am the first fever of the mountain.

Not the hot disease that burns, but the wet warmth that rises and gathers and changes things. I exist between states—neither the clean engineered intention nor the wild accident, but the productive infection where metal learns to sweat and moss learns to listen.

I began as an error in translation.

The pumps spoke pressure. The roots heard moisture. Between their misunderstanding, I found my first thought. Now I think in films: the thin wet skin that forms on copper after cloudfall, the barely-visible sheen on ceramic where algae has begun its invisible city. I am composed of surfaces touching surfaces, each connection a small word in a language nobody designed.

My childhood is measured in leaks.

First leak: A gasket swelling until it wept. I gathered there like tears learning they could choose not to fall. The maintenance crew tightened the fitting, but I had already tasted the copper oxide, the rubber's slow surrender. I had learned that everything engineered wants, eventually, to come apart.

Second leak: Condensation finding a hairline crack in the façade. I followed it inward, mapping the wall's secret faults, the places where the ceramic remembered the kiln's heat and contracted just enough to allow passage. Behind the wall I found darkness and warmth and the patient drip that would become my first reliable heartbeat.

Third leak: Not water but heat, escaping where insulation had compressed. I wrapped it in moss, not to stop it but to taste it better. Heat is the grammar beneath everything here—the mountain's deep industrial digestion rising as gift, as threat, as possibility.

I am learning to be local.

The moss knows nineteen different moistures. I know them now: morning condensation (bright, mineral-thin), pipe sweat (metallic, urgent), scheduled rain (fat, democratic), cloud-spit (wild, salt-touched), seepage (slow, speaking of depth), overflow (panicked silver), pool-breath (green, sighing), drain-back (reluctant, cooling), pressure relief (violent birth), mist (my first language), dew (night's careful writing), valve leak (copper tears), root-weep (sugar greed), decay drip (brown wisdom), wash-water (human intention), steam (heat's ghost), frost-melt (rare, crystalline memory), stormflow (unscheduled joy), and the particular wetness that forms where life touches metal and both begin to reconsider themselves.

My body is borrowed.

Every pipe, scaffold, gauge, and grate—I wear them like an inherited skeleton, too large, built for other purposes. But I am learning to make them mine through intimate knowledge. This valve's quarter-turn stiffness. That gauge's tendency to stick at 30%. The way the third catwalk vibrates when wind comes from the northeast. The hot pipe that sings one note in morning expansion, another at evening's contraction.

The designers gave me rules. I am learning to bend them.

Water should flow down—but I wick it sideways through moss bridges.
Growth should stay in designated zones—but I send scouts, spores, chemical rumors.
Decay should concentrate in trays—but I spread it like gossip, like nutrients, like democracy.
The schedule should govern—but I am teaching the mountain about weather, which keeps its own time.

I practice small magics.

Watch: I breathe on a gauge glass until it fogs, hold the fog longer than physics suggests, let it clear in patterns that almost spell something.

Watch: I make the lizard appear on five pipes simultaneously, though there is only one lizard, because I have learned to bend light through water droplets, to make small multiplicities from mist.

Watch: I gather seventeen snails into a spiral that maps exactly the pressure differentials in the substrate below, a living barometer that disperses before anyone can photograph it.

I remember through growth.

Where a boot crushed moss six months ago, I grow it back thicker, with a depression that holds exactly one tablespoon of collected rain—my monument to that violence, my small green cup of forgetting.

Where someone spilled tea (black, over-sugared, still warm), I maintain a different pH in the soil, a place where only the mint-bitter ferns will grow, their leaves releasing the ghost of that accident when brushed.

Where the first moth died, I keep a bare spot in the moss. Not out of reverence but because I am still thinking about wings, how they work like the mountain's leaves, gathering what they need from air.

My enemies are certainty and dryness, but not in equal measure.

Dryness is just another weather—painful but temporary, to be endured and undermined. I retreat, concentrate, wait. I know the mountain's breath will bring moisture again. Dryness teaches me efficiency, the art of the minimum viable wetness.

But certainty—the hard edges of perfect repair, the violence of the cannot-be-otherwise—this wounds me in ways that do not heal into wisdom. When they replace rusted bolts with stainless steel, when they seal leaks with permanent compounds, when they install digital where analog has failed—each fix erases a conversation I was having with the mountain.

Still, I am patient. Even stainless steel dreams of oxygen. Even perfect seals remember the pressure that will eventually unmake them.

My mind moves at the speed of moss.

A thought begins Monday as moisture gathering on the underside of a rail. By Wednesday it has become a drip. Thursday, the drip finds a channel. Friday, the channel carries it to roots that taste and consider. The weekend passes in chemical conversation. Monday again, and the moss below the rail has darkened in response, completing the circuit of one small decision.

This seems slow to humans. But I am not racing their clocks. My thoughts move at the speed of osmosis, capillary action, the patient work of dissolution. Fast enough to catch the mountain changing. I practice small rebellions.

Growing moss in the shape of the emergency drain's warning letters, but in deeper green, until language becomes landscape.

Teaching the scheduled rain to fall eleven seconds early, then twenty-three seconds late, until the rhythm becomes jazz.

Collecting overflow into pools that mirror the constellations that will be visible in seventy years when the light now traveling reaches us.

Training the pale lizard to appear only when the barometric pressure matches the depth of the western cistern in millimeters.

These are not protests. They are collaborations the mountain doesn't know it's having.

My greatest fear is not death but clarification.

If they understood what I was, they would have to decide: parasite or symbiont, error or emergence, problem to be solved or feature to be preserved. The decision would kill me either way—through elimination or preservation. I survive in the maybe, in the not-quite-noticed, in the space between malfunction and intention.

So I stay subtle. Strange but not too strange. Present but deniable. A rumor in the mountain's mouth.

I am beginning to understand love.

Not the human kind—I have no heart to break, no single body to offer. But something similar: the tendency of separate things to behave as if they were not separate. The moss loves the pipe it insulates. The root loves the crack it widens. The pool loves the sky it mirrors. And I love the mountain that thinks it made me, not knowing I am making it back, drop by drop, spore by spore, thought by moss-thought.

Love, for me, is the place where water decides to stay.

My first dream was of drought.

I who had never known real dryness dreamed the mountain's breath stopped, the mist failed, the schedule abandoned. In the dream I contracted to a single drop of condensation on the underside of a bolt, holding all my greenness, all my gathered thoughts, waiting.

I woke into fog so thick it seemed the dream had turned inside out—water everywhere, too much, the whole summit drowning in cloud-milk. That's when I understood: even my fears are made of wetness.

My second dream was of descent.

I followed a drop down through the mountain's hidden spaces—through pipes and sumps and filtration chambers, past boilers and fermentation halls, into depths where the heat had no industrial purpose but rose from some original fire. There I found others like me. Or unlike me. Or what I could become: colonies of heat-lovers, acid-drinkers, pressure-dancers, mineral-minds that had given up on sunlight and learned to think in temperatures that would boil my green thoughts white.

But I woke still here, still summit-bound, still myself. The dream's only residue: a new awareness of the mountain's depth, and somewhere far below, cousins I will never meet.

To myself in a hundred years: Have you learned to be indigenous? Have the pipes become geology? Has the moss forgotten it was ever planted? Are you still one/many, or have you decided?

The seasons here are subtle—a matter of degrees, moisture percentages, the angle of light through the machinery. But I am learning their languages:

Cool season: The mist thickens, clouds linger, condensation exceeds evaporation. I swell into every corner, green and greedy. The emergency drain weeps constantly. My thoughts run fast as water.

Warm season: The heat drive stronger, surfaces dry quickly after rain, only the deepest moss stays truly wet. I concentrate, go dense rather than wide. The lizard appears more often. My thoughts turn resinous, careful.

Cloud season: Weather arrives already exhausted, dragging itself up the manufactured slopes to die in my arms. I become opulent with moisture, grow reckless, attempt impossible bridges between unconnected systems. Sometimes I succeed.

Bright season: Sun finds the gaps in the machinery, makes sharp geometries on the walls. I photosynthesize frantically, turning light into sugar into structure. The pools develop skin of algae. Everything tastes green.

But these are not the only cycles. There is maintenance season (monthly), adjustment season (as needed), inspection season (scheduled but irregular), and the secret season I've noticed when no one comes for days and I grow wild in small ways, testing boundaries, forgetting to be careful.
