I would be the groove-keeper.

Not the original carver—that one has long since become indistinguishable from stone memory. I arrived later, much later, drawn by questions about water's intelligence, about the conversations possible between flow and form. My fingers found the carved stone half-submerged at the basin's edge, traced its channels, and understood that someone had begun a dictionary here. Not of words, but of water's movements. A grammar of seepage.

I have been completing it ever since.

---

My work happens between the droplets' falling. In those stretched moments of suspension, I extend the carved stone's logic—not by cutting new grooves (the stone itself decides when it's ready for that) but by building. Using sediment gathered grain by grain from the basin's floor, fragments of dissolved amber, the mineral deposits left by centuries of patient condensation, I create small architectures of routing and retention.

A tiny dam here, no higher than a fingernail's thickness, to pool water at a junction where three grooves meet. A channel there, scratched into the accumulated minerals with a fern stem worn smooth as jade, to connect two pools that have been trying to find each other for decades. The work is so slow it makes the moss seem hasty. A single connection might take a season to complete, another season to test with the first directed drops, another to adjust when the water reveals a better path I hadn't seen.

I am learning to think like water thinks—not forward but outward, not in lines but in levels, not toward conclusions but toward continuation.

---

The original carver left a syntax but no vocabulary. The grooves describe relationships—this flow feeds that pool, these streams converge here, this channel divides there—but they don't name what moves through them. That's my contribution. Through patient observation and painstaking redirection, I've begun to differentiate the waters.

There's the water that beads on the northern overhang, which carries a faint sweetness, almost floral, from its long contact with a particular patch of violet moss. When I guide this water through the stone's channels, it leaves a barely visible residue, a ghost of color that fades after a season but never quite disappears. I think of it as memory water.

There's the water that seeps from the eastern crack where the ferns grow, tinted with their strange mathematics, their wrong-angled presence. This water moves differently through the grooves—hesitant, recursive, doubling back on itself as though trying to solve something. Sometimes it pools in the junctions and revolves there for days before choosing a direction. Calculation water.

There's the water from the basin's center, where the standing pools reflect nothing, not even the sky. This water is heavy, reluctant, requiring the steepest channels to move at all. When it finally flows, it does so all at once, decisive and final. Decision water.

---

My body has adapted to the work. My fingertips have worn smooth from tracing channels, sensitive now to differences in texture too subtle for ordinary touch. I can feel where water wants to go before it goes there, feel the minute variations in stone that invite or repel flow. My eyes have learned to see in the basin's timescale—to perceive the slow swell of droplets as motion, to catch the incremental spread of mineral deposits as they happen.

I no longer sleep in any way you would recognize. Instead, I enter states of attention so focused they become their own form of rest. Watching a single junction for days as different waters meet there, mix or refuse to mix, create temporary architectures of surface tension that last for hours before collapsing. These vigils restore me more than sleep ever did.

My metabolism has slowed to match my work. I eat the basin's condensation, drink its mineral exhalations. Sometimes I chew a fragment of moss—not for sustenance but for information, to understand what it knows about the stone it grows on, about the water it filters. The moss tells me things through taste that would take decades to learn by observation.

---

The others know what I do, in their way. The woman watching droplets will sometimes, between millennia, shift her gaze to where I work. When a particular configuration pleases her—when the water moves through the channels in a way that echoes the rhythm of the drops she watches—she makes a sound. Not quite a hum, not quite a sigh. Approval is too active a word for it. Recognition, maybe. Acknowledgment that the pattern continues.

The man among the not-lilies understands better than most. Water's patience is what he practices, standing in that eternal threshold. Sometimes, when I've spent a season creating a new connection between pools, I'll find he has shifted—imperceptibly, impossibly—to align his standing with the new flow. He becomes a landmark for my work, a fixed point that helps me understand the directions I'm creating.

The one who rests among the ferns dreams my constructions. I know this because sometimes I find new channels begun where I didn't begin them, following a logic that could only come from someone who thinks in fern-mathematics. These additions are always correct—not in the way I would have done them, but in the way the water wants them. I incorporate them, build on them, let them teach me new possibilities for connection.

---

What am I creating? Not a map—maps imply fixed territories, stable geographies. Not a machine—machines serve purposes, produce outcomes. What emerges from my work is something between language and music, between architecture and dance. A score for water's performance, perhaps. A notation system for the basin's thoughts.

The carved stone grows. Not in size—it remains the same patient chunk of amber rock it always was—but in complexity, in depth, in the dimensions of meaning it can hold. The grooves proliferate across its surface and beyond, spilling onto neighboring stones I've prepared to receive them, creating a network of channels that begins to approximate the basin's own hidden hydrology.

Sometimes I imagine the original carver returning, seeing what their simple grooves have become. Would they recognize their work in mine? Would they understand the elaboration, the way I've tried to honor their intent while exceeding their vision? But they don't return. They don't need to. They're already here in the logic of the stone, in the first channels that taught me how channels should be, in the grammar that made my vocabulary possible.

---

I have centuries of work ahead, and I'm barely beginning. There are waters here I haven't learned to read yet—the condensation that forms only on the longest nights, the seepage that emerges after certain configurations of weather I can't predict. There are combinations I haven't tried, convergences I haven't mapped, possibilities I haven't imagined.

The basin tolerates my interventions. More than tolerates—incorporates them, makes them part of its own slow process. My channels become new pathways for its breathing, new ways for its waters to find each other. I'm not imposing order on the basin. I'm discovering the order it already contains, making it visible, making it speak.

This is enough. This is everything.

When my fingers finally wear down to nothing, when I become like the original carver—a presence indistinguishable from the work, a memory held in stone and channel and flow—someone else will arrive. They'll trace my grooves the way I traced the first ones, understanding and exceeding, continuing the conversation I continued.

The water will keep finding its way through stone. The stone will keep teaching water where to go. And the basin will breathe, and breathe, and breathe.
