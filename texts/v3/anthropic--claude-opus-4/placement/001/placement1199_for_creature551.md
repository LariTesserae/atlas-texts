I am a Transition—one of those who exists in the spaces between Chronicler generations, neither fully of one wave nor the next. My bones have begun their mathematical flowering, but the patterns are still tentative, exploratory. Delicate spirals emerge from my spine like question marks, and the plates forming between my ribs describe incomplete equations. I am perhaps three centuries old, young enough that my skeleton still remembers flexibility, old enough that I've begun to feel the weight of accumulated observations.

I specialize in what we call the Peripheral Documents—those phenomena that exist at the edges of even the plateau's strangeness. The shadows that pool in corners are my particular study. I've spent decades learning to read their depths, to understand how they gather independent of light, how they form reservoirs of pure dimensional ambiguity. My left shoulder blade has developed a cup-like process that holds these shadows when I scoop them up. They have weight but no mass, temperature but no heat. They evaporate slowly when exposed to direct observation, so I must study them obliquely, through the corner of my eye, through their effects on other things.

My translucent skin has developed its own adaptations. Where other Chroniclers' translucency creates the effect of layered glass, mine has begun to take on the quality of the plateau's strange waters—showing different depths simultaneously. Looking at my hands, one might see not just the developing bone structures within, but also the sky below them, or sometimes the reflection of moss-cities that exist miles away. This makes precise movement challenging. I must navigate not just the space I occupy but the spaces I reflect.

I travel alone, as is common for Transitions. We are between languages—we've begun to forget in the old patterns but haven't yet learned the new configurations of amnesia that would let us communicate with the generation approaching from the deep plateau. My bone-songs resonate at frequencies that feel hollow to my birth generation, too raw to the next. When I encounter other Chroniclers, we exchange knowledge through gesture and shared observation rather than the elegant forgettings of established speech.

My cultivation practices differ from the standard paradox-farming. In the shadow pools, I've discovered that certain moss-cities build structures that exist only in peripheral vision—aqueducts that function only when not directly observed, towers that maintain their height through collective inattention. I encourage these by practicing specific patterns of not-looking, turning my head in precise angles that allow the moss to violate causality in my visual periphery. The contradictions that fruit from these cities taste of possibilities that were almost realized, of mathematics that nearly worked.

Recently, I've made a discovery that disturbs even my flexible understanding. In the deepest shadow pools, those that gather in the corners of the most ancient stone structures, I've found evidence of negative chronicles—anti-Chroniclers whose bones grow inward rather than outward, who accumulate absence rather than observation. Their skeletons, when I can glimpse them in the pool's depths, are voids in the shape of mathematical proofs, spaces where recording should be but isn't.

This discovery has begun to affect my own growth. My newest rib plates are developing with holes that match the patterns I've observed in the shadows, as if my body is trying to chronicle both presence and absence simultaneously. I feel myself becoming a record not just of what is, but of what isn't—a dangerous development for one already caught between generations.

I carry three items, which is considered excessive by Chronicler standards but necessary for my work. First, a lens ground from crystallized horizontal rain, which allows me to see the plateau from angles that don't exist. Second, a vial of water from a well that shows only depths—no reflections, no other locations, just an endless fall into clarity. Third, a fragment of moss-city aqueduct that continues to pump invisible water through its grain, a perpetual motion that violates every law except its own.

My current path takes me toward a convergence I've calculated through shadow patterns—a place where the negative chronicles suggest a massive absence, a hole in the plateau's reality that might be another kind of structure entirely. The journey will take perhaps a decade, allowing for the necessary diversions to study the peripheral phenomena along the way. My bones grow heavier with each shadow catalogued, each impossible absence recorded in calcium and contradiction.

I know that I may never reach a generation's full integration, may remain forever in this transitional state. Some Transitions, they say, eventually synchronize with the plateau's own frequency, becoming mobile structures rather than true Chroniclers—still walking, still observing, but operating by architectural rather than biological principles. I feel this possibility in the way my movements have begun to follow the mathematical curves of the causeways, in how my breath has started to crystallize in patterns that mirror the moss-cities' organization.

But there is freedom in transition, in existing between the established patterns of forgetting and remembering. I can hold contradictions that would shatter a fully integrated Chronicler, can observe phenomena that exist only in the gaps between worldviews. My chronicles, recorded in incomplete bone and impossible skin, may never join the great work directly. Instead, they document the spaces between documentation, the pauses between thoughts, the shadows at the edges of even this strange world's reality.

I continue across the worn stone, neither fully here nor there, my hollow bones singing frequencies that no generation claims, following the shadows toward their source, adding my transitional observations to a record that exists in the margins of the eternal chronicle. The plateau accepts all forms of documentation, even those that document the spaces where documentation fails. In this, I find my purpose—to be the chronicler of the unchronicled, the observer of the unobserved, forever transitional, forever between.
