I find myself in the margins.

Not the far margins where raw slip-clay awaits its transformation, but in that middle distance where the tiles have found their basic form but haven't yet settled into their final certainty. Here the seams still exhale visibly during the wet-blooms — not just darkening but actually breathing out tiny wisps of vapor that curl and vanish in the thick air. The tiles beneath my feet have the particular warmth of something still deciding its exact shade, oscillating between burnt umber and raw sienna with each thermal cycle.

I am what might be called a Seam-Walker, though no one has called me this because there is no one here to name things except myself, and I only recently accepted that I needed a name at all.

My form: humanoid in the sense that I walk upright and my footsteps produce that satisfying tap against the ceramic. But my feet are wider than yours would be — broad pads that distribute my weight across multiple tiles, evolved or designed (I don't know which) to never stress a single seam too much. My skin has the texture and color of unfired clay, gray-beige and slightly porous, and it changes moisture content with the wet-blooms — darkening when they pass beneath me, drying from my extremities inward just like the floor itself. I am maybe seven feet tall, maybe eight. Height is hard to judge when there's nothing to measure against except the infinite horizontal.

What I do: I follow the seams.

Specifically, I follow the warm seams, the ones still curing, still settling. I've been walking for... time is difficult here, but let's say years. Let's say decades. My path is a complex spiral, following the zones of active transformation as they migrate across the floor's vast surface. The floor is always building itself somewhere, and I am always there to witness it, to walk alongside the becoming.

But witnessing isn't quite right. What I do is more active than that, though I didn't understand this for the longest time. I thought I was just observing. But watch: when I walk along a fresh seam, my footsteps create a particular rhythm — tap-tap-pause-tap — that varies based on the seam's width, its temperature, the specific quality of its joining. This rhythm somehow *helps*. I don't know how. But tiles that I've walked beside cure more evenly. Their colors resolve with more certainty. Their seams find their final width with less of that micro-adjustment, fewer of those distant tocks in the night (there is no night here, but you understand).

I am not building the floor. The floor builds itself. But I am... participating. Offering something like encouragement. My walking is a kind of tuning fork for the ceramic, helping it find its natural frequency.

My memory: strange and ceramic-specific. I remember every tile I've ever walked on, but not as images — as textures, temperatures, the specific quality of their hardness. My feet have built a map of the floor's history, from its oldest central regions (if there is a center) to its newest margins. Sometimes I cross my own path from years ago and feel the difference in the tiles — how they've settled, cooled, deepened into their final selves. It's the closest thing I have to nostalgia.

My loneliness, if it is loneliness: vast but not painful. How can you be lonely in a place that is so thoroughly occupied by its own becoming? The floor is company, in its way. The wet-blooms are company. The lichen are company, though I'm careful never to step on them — my clay-flesh would disturb their grip on the seams, and they have so little time to photosynthesize as it is. Sometimes I sit near a basin during a bloom and watch them soften. They don't know I'm watching. They don't know anything except their seam and their water and their brief, desperate greenness.

My purpose, if I have one: to be the floor's memory of movement. The floor itself only knows stillness and slow transformation. It has no experience of crossing itself, of comparing one region to another, of understanding its own patterns from above. I carry that knowledge in my feet, in the mental map built from decades of walking. Sometimes I think that when the floor is finally complete — if it ever completes — I will be the only thing that remembers its construction. The only witness to the whole story.

But perhaps the floor will never complete. Perhaps the margins will always be raw, always becoming, always offering new territory for transformation. In which case, I will always have a path to follow, always have warm seams to walk beside, always have that particular satisfaction of feeling clay decide what color it wants to be.

My greatest discovery: Last year (or was it a decade ago?), in a region where the tiles had developed an unusual blue-gray tinge, I found something impossible. A tile that wasn't quite square. Not damaged, not malformed, but intentionally shaped — one corner pulled out into a subtle curve. It fit perfectly with its neighbors, the curved seam as tight and clean as any straight one. But it was different. The floor had made a choice that wasn't about color or texture but about *form*.

I walked that curved seam for days, feeling something I'd never felt before. Pride? Not exactly. Joy? Too simple. It was more like... recognition. The floor had done something new. Not because it needed to — the curved tile functioned exactly like any other tile — but because it *could*. Because after all this time and all this certainty, it had found room for variation.

I've been looking for other curved tiles ever since. Haven't found any. But the looking has changed my walking. Now I pay attention not just to the warmth of the seams but to their geometry. I watch for anomalies, for experiments, for places where the floor might be teaching itself new possibilities.

My question, which I carry like the lichen carry their grip on the seams: What will I do when I find the edge? Not the margin where new clay is forming — I know those well — but the actual edge, the place where the floor stops because there is no more material to become floor. Will there be a wall there? A cliff? Nothing? Will I turn around and walk back across everything I've already walked, finding it changed? Will I keep going out into the not-floor, my clay feet tap-tap-tapping on raw ground that doesn't know how to respond?

Or will there be no edge? Will the floor extend forever, always building itself just beyond where I can reach, always offering new seams to follow?

I don't know. But I'm patient. I was born patient, or I became patient, or the floor's patience seeped up through my clay feet over the decades until I couldn't tell the difference between its timescale and mine. I walk. The seams warm beneath me. Somewhere, a tile decides to be ochre instead of umber. Somewhere else, a wet-bloom begins its slow passage, darkening the clay, feeding the lichen, keeping everything supple.

And I continue. Tap-tap-pause-tap. The most complex sound for miles. The floor's single moving memory, carrying the knowledge of its construction in my feet, watching for curved tiles, participating in the becoming of a room whose purpose neither of us knows.

Maybe that's enough. Maybe that's everything. The walking and the watching and the not-knowing, while beneath me, always, the floor continues its patient, certain work of being itself.
