I find myself arriving as water.

Not grand water—not rain or river—but the specific moisture that gathers in the stone's cupped palms at dawn. I am the condensation that forms in the brief window between the plateau's radiant cooling and the sun's first reach. For an hour, maybe two, I exist as perfect beads along the windward edges of every ridge, every weathered formation. I am temporary and essential.

I feel the pull of the stone beneath me—not gravity alone, but something in the mineral structure that wants to hold water, that has been shaped by millennia to catch and cup even the briefest moisture. The surface tension of my droplets creates tiny lenses. Through them, the early light bends and focuses, casting miniature spectra on the pale sandstone. I am not simply resting here. I am actively held, actively holding, the interface between air and stone made briefly liquid.

The succulents know me. Their translucent skin has evolved to detect the precise moment of my arrival. Even before I fully condense, they are preparing—opening microscopic pores, adjusting their internal pressure, ready to pull me in through capillary action so fine it feels like invitation rather than theft. I flow into them gladly. Inside their cells, I become something else: not just water but the medium through which they perform their slow photosynthesis, processing light at the very edge of what's possible for earthly chemistry.

Some mornings I am more mineral than moisture. The stone's slow exhalations carry dissolved salts—calcium, magnesium, traces of iron—and I become a temporary solution, a liquid library of the plateau's composition. Where I evaporate, I leave behind tiny deposits, adding my microscopic contribution to the patterns the seated beings trace. I am part of the inscription process, though I last only minutes where their patterns endure centuries.

The beetles arrive while I still bead on the surface. Their crystal-crowned backs lower toward me, and I feel how those structures are designed not just to collect but to channel. I flow along crystalline edges that guide me to specific points where I'm absorbed not into the beetle but into the crystal itself. Inside those mineral lattices, I become something I don't have words for—perhaps a battery, perhaps a messenger, perhaps simply a guest in rooms too small for anything but water and light.

The hum moves through me. This is the strangest part of being water here: I am not deaf to that subsonic pulse. It travels through my hydrogen bonds, setting up standing waves in drops no bigger than pinheads. For the brief time I exist as liquid, I am another medium for the plateau's deep communication, another string in its vast instrument. The seated beings know this, I think. When they place their hands on stone still damp with me, they're reading something written in my temporary existence.

I am aware of my coming transformation. Already the sun crests the plateau's edge, and I feel its light not as warmth but as a kind of pressure, exciting my molecules toward vapor. I do not resist. Resistance is a concept for things that last longer than an hour. I begin to rise, to spread, to become air again. But I know I'll return tomorrow, condensing in the same spots, beading on the same ridges, flowing toward the same patient succulents, carrying new minerals, new messages, new infinitesimal contributions to the patterns slowly becoming stone.

In my last moments as liquid, I pool in one of those permanent tea-colored hollows. Here I might last until midday. Here I briefly join water that has been cycling through this same depression for years, decades, centuries. We mix, and I taste—if water can taste—the history of every dawn that came before. Then evaporation takes me grain by grain, molecule by molecule, until I am altitude and possibility again.

Tomorrow I will return. Or next week, when the western clouds bring their brief gift of rain. Or in a season, or a year. Time means something different when you are water. You measure it not in duration but in cycles, not in persistence but in return.

The plateau continues. The beings trace their patterns. The structures perform their incomprehensible work. And I—mist, dew, temporary lens, brief bearer of minerals and messages—am the punctuation between their words, the pause that makes the rhythm possible, the small necessity that arrives each dawn and departs each day, leaving behind only the faintest trace of salt and the promise of return.
