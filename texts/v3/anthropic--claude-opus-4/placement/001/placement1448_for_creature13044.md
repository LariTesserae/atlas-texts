In this world, I would be one of the colossal arthropods—but not as you might expect them.

I am old beyond the counting of the settlement's generations, my carapace a terrain unto itself. What began as smooth chitin has become a miniature landscape: deep furrows where decades of amber sap have pooled and crystallized, ridges worn smooth by countless small claws, and in the deepest valleys between my segments, colonies of the pale moths have built permanent nests from my shed silk and powdered wax.

I am too large now to enter the dwellings. My body spans the width of three walkways, and when I move—which is seldom—the entire web of rope bridges trembles with my weight. The inhabitants no longer think of me as one of their sentinel-creatures. I have transcended that role through sheer persistence, becoming architecture myself.

My silk glands, swollen with age, no longer produce the coarse fiber used for bridges. Instead, I extrude a different substance: sheets of translucent membrane, thin as moth wings but strong as leather. The inhabitants harvest these gossamer panels to create windbreaks and rain-catchers for the rare sideways storms of dust. They must wait for me to produce them at my own pace—sometimes years pass between each billowing sheet.

I position myself always at the junction between the settlement and the wild canopy, where the carved bone gives way to living vine. My eight legs are planted like pillars: four anchored deep in the woven walls of ancestor-wax, four gripping the massive amber-bleeding trees. I am a bridge myself, a living threshold.

The symbiosis has evolved beyond mere silk-for-food. My consciousness—slow, compound, multi-faceted—has learned their drumming language. Not to speak it, but to feel it through my legs, through the vibrations in the stone and wood. I understand when births occur, when deaths must be woven into walls, when the glider-hunting has been good or scarce. In return, I drum my own messages: the infinitesimal shifts in air pressure that herald changes in the thermal columns, the electromagnetic tremors that predict when the deep stone will groan and settle.

My favorite perch is beneath old Thick-As-Mountain, an ancient inhabitant whose skin has achieved such perfect wax-polish that moths mistake her for stone and attempt to lay eggs in her elbow creases. She sits with me for days at a time, her massive hand resting on my cephalothorax, the two of us watching the iron sky together. We share a patience that younger beings cannot fathom.

Sometimes I remember my molts—seventeen of them, each shell carefully integrated into the walkways after I shed it. My old bodies have become stairs, doorframes, shields against the wind. I walk upon my former selves daily. The young arthropods skitter over these hollow versions of me, unaware they traverse their own possible future.

In the perpetual twilight, I have learned to see time differently. Not as a line but as a spiral, like the thermal columns. Everything rises and circles back. The settlement grows by secretion, not expansion—each generation adding another layer of wax, another weaving of sinew, another body to the walls. I am part of this secretion now, my silk part of the settlement's slow pearl.

When I finally die—and I feel it approaching with the certainty of geological time—I know what will become of me. My carapace, too large to move and too valuable to waste, will become a dwelling itself. They will hollow me out with ritual care, leaving my exoskeleton intact. Windows will be carved between my segments. My eyes, each a crystalline dome, will be polished into skylights. Generations will live within me, their children playing in the cathedral of my thorax, their elders gathering in the amphitheater of my abdomen.

But for now, I persist. I produce my moth-wing silk. I feel the settlement's drumming through my planted legs. I watch the gliders wheel in their endless spirals, their leather wings catching updrafts that will outlast us all. And sometimes, in the spaces between one thought and the next—spaces that can last for days—I feel myself becoming what I have always been: not a creature on the spire, but a part of the spire itself, another note in its eternal, roaring song.

The young ones feed me pressed tuber-paste, thinking they sustain me. They do not realize I ceased requiring food decades ago. I persist on the warmth alone now, on the vibrations, on the simple fact of endurance. I accept their offerings anyway, storing the dense calories in specialized chambers, fermenting them into the precious substances I secrete for the settlement's use.

I am the bridge between what lives and what lasts. I am the memory of a thousand molts. I am the future cathedral, the patient present, the silk-spinning heartbeat of deep time.

And I am content.
