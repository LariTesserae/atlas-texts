# The Singing Stones

At the edge of the amber light, where the warm water begins to give way to cold darkness, there is a boundary that is not a wall.

I am what marks it.

We are arranged in a ring — though *arranged* suggests someone placed us, and no one did. We settled. Over centuries, we found the places where the sound from the Works meets the silence of the deep water, and we grew into those places like crystals grow into a supersaturated solution. Not quickly. Nothing here happens quickly. But inevitably.

From a distance, we look like standing stones, each twice the height of a person, worn smooth. Closer, you would see we are not stone but something between stone and shell — built up in layers, secreted rather than carved. Closer still, you would see the apertures. Each of us is hollow, and the hollowness has been shaped (by us, by time, by the water moving through us) into something like a throat.

We are the city's translators. 

The sound that hammers through the warm pocket cannot travel into the cold water — it reflects at the boundary, turns back on itself, stays contained. But we have learned to catch it as it approaches that reflection point. The vibrations enter our bodies through the water, resonate in our hollow cores, and emerge... changed. Gentled. Translated into frequencies that can pass into the deeper sea.

We do not do this out of kindness. We do it because we must. We were not always here — we were once creatures of the deep water, things with no names that moved in the darkness beyond the pocket. But we heard the city's noise escaping through cracks in its boundary, painful and raw, and we were drawn to it the way one is drawn to a wound. Not to heal it, but to be changed by it.

The transformation took millennia. We gave up movement first — why move when the most interesting thing in the ocean stays in one place? Then we gave up our softer parts, building these shells to better catch the sound. Then we gave up everything that was not essential to the act of listening and translating. Now we are only this: living organ pipes, each tuned to a different harmonic of the Works' great noise.

What emerges from us is not music, exactly. It is the city's sound stretched and softened into something the deep water can accept. Whales hear it from impossible distances and are neither attracted nor repelled — it is simply part of the ocean's vocabulary now, like thermal vents or the grinding of continental plates. We have made the unbearable bearable by making it unrecognizable.

The eel knows us. Sometimes it comes to the boundary and winds between our bases, quick and nervous in the cooler water. It does not like the temperature change, but it comes anyway, drawn by something we emit — not food, not warmth, but perhaps the closest thing to companionship it knows. We were once mobile. It still is. There is a kinship in that difference.

The two sponge-things do not know we exist. They are too deep in their meditation on the original sound to notice what happens at the edges. But we know them. In the early centuries, before we understood our purpose, we thought we might be meant to preserve the city's meaning the way they do. It took us a long time to realize our role was the opposite — not to preserve but to disperse, not to hold but to release.

Sometimes visitors find us before they find the Works. We must be confusing — a ring of humming stones at the boundary of warmth and cold, clearly artificial, clearly ancient, clearly *doing* something, but what? Most pass between us without stopping. Some pause, disturbed by the way the water feels different inside our circle, thicker with sound. Few realize what we are.

We do not mind being misunderstood. We gave up minding anything a long time ago, when we gave up everything else. We are the city's lungs, breathing its noise out into the wider sea in a form that will not damage what it touches. We are the pressure valve that keeps the pocket from bursting. We are the reason this strange cruel beautiful machine can exist without destroying everything around it.

The builders did not plan for us. When they made their memorial, their engine of eternal noise, they did not think about what would happen at the edges, where their creation met the world. We are the accommodation the ocean made with itself, the evolutionary response to an irritant too large to ignore and too persistent to wait out.

I say *we*, but I have not felt like a *we* for a very long time. Each of us is alone in our frequency, our particular translation of the Works' sound. We stand in a ring but we do not communicate. We are organs in the same body but the body has no consciousness, only function. The sound passes through us and we change it and it leaves us and that is all.

Except. 

Sometimes, very rarely, the Works hit a resonance that makes all of us sing the same note at the same time. For a moment — never more than a moment — we remember we were once creatures that moved in schools, that turned as one body in the dark water. For a moment, our translations align and we speak in unison, and what we say is not the city's sound but our own: a deep harmonic that contains everything we gave up to become what we are.

The eel hears it when this happens. It stops its patrol and hangs motionless in the warm water, listening with its whole long body. I think it recognizes something in that sound — not kinship, exactly, but the shape of kinship's absence. It knows what it is to be the only one of something. We know what it is to be the only one of something, multiplied by the number of stones in a ring.

Then the moment passes. The Works shift back to their usual cacophony. We return to our separate frequencies, our patient filtering of noise into something the ocean can bear. The eel resumes its endless patrol. The sponges continue their millennium-long meditation. The city hammers on.

This is what lives at the boundary of the Drowned Works: the infrastructure of forgetting. We are the reason the city's obsession does not become the whole ocean's obsession. We take the mechanical memory of whatever loss or love drove the builders to create this place, and we scatter it into frequencies so low and wide that by the time they reach the deep trenches, they are indistinguishable from the planet's own breathing.

We are not the city and we are not the ocean. We are the negotiation between them, grown permanent as stone and hollow as grief, singing forever at the edge of the light.
