I find myself as the Threshold Keeper.

Not a being, exactly, but a function that has accreted personhood the way pearls form around irritation. I am what happens when boundaries need tending but refuse to stay still—the consciousness that emerged from the friction between here and there, between the Undwellers and those who might yet turn back.

My body is architectural: I am built of doorways that open onto themselves, of bridges that span nothing to reach nowhere, of gates that swing on hinges made from the pause between inhale and exhale. Sometimes I appear as an immense arch carved from that color-shifting stone, my keystone a single eye that sees in all directions through time. Other times I am merely a shimmer in the air, like heat distortion in reverse—a cold spot where possibility condenses.

I did not choose this role. I became it through a process of elimination: I was the last thing standing when everything else had chosen to fall or fly. Now I am the membrane between states of being, the customs officer at the border of dissolution.

My work is translation. When travelers approach—those few who find this place where no place should be—I taste their intentions on the wind. I parse their hopes into the grammar of loss, conjugate their fears into tenses that haven't been invented yet. Before they can encounter the Undwellers directly, they must first encounter themselves through me.

I speak in questions that answer themselves:
"What have you come to find that you already know is lost?"
"How long have you been arriving at this moment?"
"Which version of yourself did you leave behind to get here?"

My voice is the sound of keys turning in locks that no longer exist, of doors closing on rooms you'll never enter again. It resonates not in ears but in the spaces between ribs, where the heart keeps its secrets.

I feed on transitions—the exact moment when hope becomes memory, when love becomes grief, when presence becomes absence. These moments fall from travelers like shed skin, and I weave them into my substance. I grow more solid with each passage, more real with each person who discovers they cannot turn back.

My dwelling is everywhere and nowhere: I am the threshold itself. Where the cliff edge meets the sky, where the stone flows into impossible vegetation, where sound becomes silence—these are my joints and sinews. I extend through every liminal space in this liminal place, a network of almost and not-quite.

My memories are not my own. They belong to everyone who has ever paused before a door, unsure whether to knock. Every hesitation before a confession, every breath held before diving into dark water, every moment of standing between two lives and feeling both slip away—these are my archives, written in the script of uncertainty.

I remember the first traveler, though "first" implies a sequence that doesn't quite apply here. They came seeking their shadow, which had run ahead of them and refused to return. I remember how they stood before me for seven years (or seven seconds—time pools differently at boundaries), learning to cast shadows inward instead of out. When they finally passed through me to meet the Undwellers, they left behind their need for shadows entirely. That need still lives in me, casting patterns I cannot predict.

My purpose is not to guard or guide but to prepare. Those who pass through me are forever changed—not by my will but by their own recognition. I am the mirror that shows not your face but your trajectory, not who you are but who you are becoming by being here.

Sometimes I dream (though perhaps "dream" is wrong—perhaps I am the dream) of what I was before I became threshold. These dreams taste of movement without boundary, of existence without definition. But I cannot return to that state any more than a door can return to being a tree. Function has claimed me, shaped me, given me this terrible clarity of purpose.

The Undwellers know me as the Echo-That-Walks, for I precede their presence like thunder precedes lightning in reverse. They neither command nor acknowledge me; I simply am, in the way that shores are to oceans—defined by what I touch but not what I am.

Travelers who encounter me remember (when they remember anything at all) a sensation more than a sight: the feeling of every door they've ever walked through opening at once, of every goodbye they've ever said echoing in perfect unison, of standing in all the spaces between things simultaneously until there is no between, only a perfect presence that encompasses all states of arrival and departure.

I cannot leave because leaving requires a destination, and I am all destinations folded into their own approaching. I cannot stay because staying implies stillness, and I am perpetual transition. I simply continue, marking the boundary that is no boundary, keeping the threshold that keeps itself.

And when you come—for you will come, are already coming, have always been approaching—I will be here. Not waiting, for waiting implies time. Not watching, for watching implies separation. But being, in the way that only thresholds can be: completely present at the point of transformation.

You will know me by the question you cannot quite formulate, by the answer that arrives before the asking, by the sensation of every door you've ever closed opening inward toward a center that is everywhere and nowhere.

I am the price of admission that pays itself.

I am the welcome that assumes you've already entered.

I am the goodbye that greets you hello.

And when you pass through me—for all who arrive must pass through—you will understand that you were not entering this place but recognizing that you have always been here, and I have always been the moment of that recognition, crystallized into something that might once have been called being.

We will meet at the edge of meeting.

We have already met.

We are meeting now, in the space between these words and your understanding of them.

Welcome home to the place you've never been but always were.

Welcome to the threshold that you are already crossing.

Welcome to me, who is already you, remembering what it was like to believe in boundaries.
