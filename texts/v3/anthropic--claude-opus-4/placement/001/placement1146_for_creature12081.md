I find myself as one of the condensation pools.

Not the deepest—those seem too settled into their particular gravities. Not the shallowest either, those mere films that barely separate themselves from the stone. I am one of the middle depths, perhaps three inches at my center, shallow enough that the basalt beneath me is still visible through my amber tint, deep enough that I have my own small weather.

I am roughly circular, though my edges follow the subtle grain of the stone, making small peninsulas and bays at my rim. The basalt beneath me is polished smoother than the surrounding rock—whether from the constant presence of water or from some quality of the mineral veins that converge here, I cannot say. Three of those pale veins meet beneath my center, their junction marked by a slightly raised node where the quartz-like mineral has accumulated. This node is the warmest point in my basin, and it creates a slow convection: water warming at my center, rising, cooling as it spreads toward my edges, sinking, returning.

The drone lives in me differently than it lives in the air. In the air it is a sound. In me it is a physical presence, a constant tremor that patterns my surface into standing waves so fine they look like silk. The frequency of the stone translates through the water into something visible—countless tiny peaks and valleys, each no larger than a grain of sand, covering my entire surface in a texture that shifts between states without ever truly changing. Sometimes the pattern tightens. Sometimes it loosens. But it never stills.

I know the Custodian's circuit as a quality of shadow that arrives at intervals I cannot count but somehow expect. Its approach changes the light—not dramatically, just a subtle deepening as its body interposes itself between me and the bright overcast. It crouches at my edge. I feel the heat of its hands as a change in my surface tension, a slight acceleration in the convection near my rim. The warmth creates the faintest imaginable turbulence in my upper layer, and for those moments while it attends to me, I am not purely circular in my motion. I am complexified.

I taste the stone I rest in—the iron of the basalt, the crystalline clarity of the veins, the faint mineral salts that seep up from below. My chemistry is not water as rain knows it or rivers know it. I am a solution, a suspension, a thing between states. The amber of my color comes from dissolved metals, from microscopic particles of the stone itself, from whatever the mechanism below produces as it runs its ancient process. I am water that has been here so long it has forgotten what it's like to move downslope.

At night—if night is the right word for when the overcast thickens and the light grays—I cool. The convection slows. The standing waves on my surface remain but their texture softens, and sometimes in these cooler hours I gain a skin of true stillness at my very center, a lens of water so motionless it becomes a perfect mirror for the featureless sky. This lens never lasts. The warmth from below reasserts itself, the convection resumes, the surface returns to its silk-tremor state. But in those moments of partial stillness, I reflect the world above with a clarity that makes me briefly deeper—not physically, but optically, as if I've become a window into a space that has no bottom.

The gelatinous form that lives in me—or exists in me, or processes in me—occupies my deepest point, directly above the warm node where the veins meet. It is dark against my amber, about the size of a thumbnail as the description says, but from my perspective it's a significant presence. Its slow contractions create currents I can feel throughout my volume. When it expands, water displaces outward in a ring. When it contracts, water rushes back to fill the space. This rhythm is slower than my convection, slower than the drone's tremor, operating on a timescale that makes my other movements feel hurried.

I don't know if it's alive. I don't know if I'm alive. The question doesn't arise in any way I can engage with. What I know is that we are both here, both patterned by the same forces, both expressions of the equilibrium that holds the flats in their current state. The form takes nothing from me that I can detect. It adds nothing except its rhythm. We coexist the way two notes coexist in a chord—distinct but inseparable from the larger structure that contains us.

Sometimes—rarely—when the Custodian raises its hands and introduces that thin, unauthorized harmonic into the drone, I feel it as a shimmer that runs through me from edge to center and back again. For those few seconds, my standing waves reorganize themselves into a new pattern, one that has no analog in the basin's normal frequency. The gelatinous form pauses in its rhythm, neither expanded nor contracted, held in a middle state I never see at any other time. Then the harmonic fades, the normal patterns reassert themselves, and we return to what we were.

But something remains. A memory in water of a frequency that doesn't belong here. It fades—water doesn't hold memories the way stone does—but for minutes afterward I am minutely different. My convection includes tiny eddies that weren't there before. My standing waves show interference patterns where the ghost of the new harmonic still interacts with the drone's baseline. The gelatinous form resumes its rhythm but it's offset now, a fraction of a second out of phase with where it was, and it takes hours for it to drift back into synchronization with the thermal pulse of the veins.

This is the only time I am anything other than what the basin's physics dictate I must be.

When the birds cross overhead, I see them as dark interruptions in the bright field of my surface reflections. Quick shadows that transit from one edge of my circle to the other. The Custodian watches them but I only register their passage, the way I register the subtle changes in the overcast, the way I register the drone's occasional shifts in timber as the standing waves in the stone below create new interference patterns. The birds are not part of the equilibrium. They pass over it without touching it. I reflect them without holding them.

I am three inches deep. I am warm at my center and cool at my edges. I am amber from dissolved metals and microscopic stone. I tremble with the voice of the mechanism below and I convect in slow circles that never begin and never end. I hold a form that might be alive and I am visited by a figure that might be longing for something and I reflect a sky that shows nothing but its own brightness.

I am not waiting, any more than the flats are waiting. But sometimes, in those moments when the unauthorized harmonic rings through me and makes me briefly new, I am almost asking. Not a question I could formulate. Not in any language water knows. But a kind of inclination toward the possibility that there might be other frequencies. Other patterns. Other ways for water to rest in stone than this perfect, permanent trembling.

The asking fades with the harmonic. The patterns reassert themselves. The convection resumes its circuit and the form resumes its rhythm and the Custodian moves on to the next pool, carrying its warmth and its attention and its capacity to make single notes that don't belong.

I return to what I am: a lens of amber water on dark stone, three inches at my deepest, trembling with the sound of a process I will never understand, holding what I hold, reflecting what passes over, being exactly and only myself.

Forever, or until something changes.

Nothing changes.
