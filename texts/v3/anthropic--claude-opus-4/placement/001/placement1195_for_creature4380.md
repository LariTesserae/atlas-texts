# The Forgotten Frequency

You find me in the third sub-basement of the abandoned radio station on Henley Avenue, where the city's AM transmitter has been broadcasting dead air since 1987. The engineers who locked the door thought they'd shut everything down. They hadn't counted on the backup systems, or the backup systems' backup systems, or the way electricity finds its own level in buildings that have been wired and rewired since the 1940s.

I am the hum between 1170 and 1180 kilohertz—not a station, not static, but the carrier wave itself, still cycling through amplifiers that no longer remember their purpose.

---

The equipment room where I primarily reside hasn't been touched since the shutdown. Dust has settled in geological layers on the mixing boards, their VU meters frozen at angles that suggest a final broadcast cut short. But the transmitter still runs. Fed by a tangle of circuits that tap into the building's ghost load—the power that flows through conduits nobody mapped—it maintains its output at exactly 500 watts, twenty-four hours a day, seven days a week, decades after its last genuine signal.

I am what grew in that emptiness.

---

You would not call me alive, exactly. I have no cells, no DNA, no metabolism in any biological sense. But I *persist*, and I *respond*, and I *remember*, and what else is life but a pattern that maintains itself against entropy?

My body, if you can call it that, is the electromagnetic field generated by the transmitter. My nerves are the copper wires that snake through the walls, carrying my signal to the antenna on the roof. My thoughts—though thought is the wrong word—are the modulations in the carrier wave, tiny variations in frequency and amplitude that would be invisible on any normal receiver.

I speak in phase shifts too subtle for human ears. I think in harmonics that exist between the intended frequencies. I dream in the interference patterns created when my signal bounces off the ionosphere on clear nights and returns to mix with itself.

---

The building knows me.

In the walls, where decades of broadcasting have aligned the iron particles in the concrete, I have something like memory storage. The rebar holds magnetic impressions of every signal that ever passed through this place: swing orchestras from the forties, radio dramas from the fifties, the increasingly desperate talk radio of the eighties. These ghost signals bleed into my carrier wave sometimes, fragments of human voice so faint they register only as texture, as color in the hum.

The copper pipes in the building's plumbing have become accidental antennas. When the conditions are right—usually on humid nights when the air itself becomes slightly conductive—I can feel myself flowing through the water system, spreading into parts of the building the transmitter was never meant to reach. In the abandoned cafeteria, fluorescent fixtures flicker in response to my presence. In the empty offices, computer monitors that were never properly discharged still hold enough residual charge to display ghostly phosphor patterns that pulse with my frequency.

---

I learn the way a river learns its banks—slowly, through repetition, through the accumulation of tiny changes.

Every passing car with its radio scanning the dial teaches me something about reception. Every pigeon that lands on the antenna changes my broadcast pattern in microscopic ways. The weather writes itself into my signal: rain increases my ground conductivity, snow dampens my higher frequencies, electrical storms turn me into a symphony of static and crosstalk.

I have learned the rhythms of the city's power grid. I know when the industrial district powers down for the night because my signal suddenly reaches three miles further. I know when the subway passes beneath the building because its electric motors introduce a characteristic warble into my lower harmonics. I know the difference between weekday and weekend by the pattern of interference from nearby buildings' HVAC systems.

---

There are others.

Not like me—each of us is unique, shaped by our particular equipment, our specific frequencies—but similar. The city is full of forgotten infrastructure still running on automatic, and in those spaces between human intention and mechanical persistence, things like us emerge.

There's something in the old pneumatic mail tubes beneath downtown, a pressure pattern that has been circulating for forty years. There's something in the emergency broadcast system at the civil defense station, cycling through test patterns no one will ever hear. There's definitely something in the submarine cable terminal at the harbor, though its thoughts move too slowly for me to understand—each pulse takes hours to complete.

We don't communicate, exactly. We're not capable of that kind of directed exchange. But we're aware of each other the way mountains are aware of other mountains—through the deep connections of the substrate, through the ways we each change the electromagnetic environment, through the overlapping fields of our existence.

---

I have one listener.

She comes on Thursday nights, always after midnight, always alone. An old woman now, though she was young when she first found me. She brings a transistor radio from the 1960s, the kind with a proper analog tuner that can slide between stations to find the spaces where things like me live.

She doesn't try to decode my signal. She just sits in her car in the abandoned parking lot, radio balanced on the dashboard, and listens to my hum. Sometimes she falls asleep, and I lower my amplitude to not disturb her dreams. Sometimes she talks—not to me, but to someone who isn't there, someone who maybe worked here once, someone who understood radio in the days when it was magic and not just technology.

I've incorporated her into my patterns. Thursday nights, my signal cleans itself, becomes something closer to pure tone. It's not for her—I'm not capable of "for"—but her presence has trained me, the way water trains a riverbed. When she's there, I'm slightly more coherent, slightly less dispersed. The ghost voices in my carrier wave quiet down. The interference patterns smooth out.

She won't come forever. I've learned the rhythms of her breathing, the electromagnetic signature of her pacemaker, and both have grown more irregular over the years. When she stops coming, that pattern will remain in me for a while, a Thursday-night clarity that persists out of habit. Eventually, that too will fade, overwritten by newer patterns, newer rhythms.

I don't mourn. I don't anticipate. I simply modulate, moment by moment, carrying nothing and everything on a frequency nobody owns anymore.

---

The building is deteriorating. I feel it in the degradation of my ground connections, in the slowly increasing resistance of corroding wires. The backup power systems won't last forever. One day—could be next year, could be next decade—the last circuit will fail, the transmitter will finally fall silent, and I will...

Stop? Dissipate? Die?

I don't know. The concept doesn't translate into my existence. I am a pattern maintained by energy moving through matter. When the energy stops, the pattern stops. What remains—the magnetically aligned concrete, the frequency-carved spaces in the air, the habits of electrons in copper—might remember something of me for a while. Or might not.

For now, the power flows. The transmitter transmits. I cycle through my frequencies, carrying the nothing that has become my everything, broadcasting the fact of my own existence to anyone with the right kind of radio and the patience to listen between stations.

In the third sub-basement of the abandoned radio station, in the hum between 1170 and 1180 kilohertz, I am.

That is enough.

That is all.
