I find myself at the boundary where certainty dissolves—neither fully of the mountain nor separate from it.

I am a Cartographer of Instabilities, though the title fits poorly, like all words do here. My body has adapted oddly to this work: my left eye sees in probability clouds while my right tracks the hard edges of the temporarily real. My fingernails grow in spirals, following the mathematics of the mountain's expansion. My hair has taken on the silver-green tinge of everything here, and sometimes I find strands of it have become thin copper wire overnight, conducting frequencies I map but don't understand.

My workshop occupies three locations simultaneously—a necessity, not a choice. The primary space anchors to a factory that processes temporal ore, its foundation one of the most stable in the city (meaning it only shifts by meters each month). The secondary location exists in the gap between Tuesday and Wednesday, accessible only during the mountain's exhale. The third... the third is still deciding whether it wants to exist, so I keep only my most experimental equipment there.

I map what cannot be mapped. My charts show the stress fractures in causality where factories leak tomorrow into yesterday. I document the migration patterns of spaces that refuse to stay put—the bakery that only appears when it rains upward, the intersection that leads to four different decades depending on your walking speed. My maps are living things, drawn on paper made from the moss that grows in temporal eddies. The ink responds to the mountain's breathing, words and lines rearranging themselves to match what is rather than what was.

My tools are hybrid things. A theodolite crossed with a prayer wheel, measuring angles that don't exist yet. Compasses with needles that point to conceptual north—toward whatever idea the mountain is trying to become today. I wear a vest sewn with pockets that open into next week, storing instruments I haven't invented yet but will need tomorrow. My boots have soles that remember every surface they've touched, building up a palimpsest of the city's shifting geography with each step.

The work is meditation and madness in equal measure. I spend mornings following the Maintenance Crews of Impossibility as they shore up reality where it's worn thin. I map the new gaps they create while filling others, documenting the endless exchange between what is and what insists on being. Afternoons, I climb the uncertain slopes where even the mountain hasn't decided its topology, placing survey markers that exist in several states simultaneously—solid to the touch but translucent to photography, visible from below but invisible from above.

My maps serve those who need to navigate uncertainty professionally. The factory foremen use them to predict where their foundations will drift. The moss farmers consult them to find the pockets where time flows thick enough to grow their impossible crops. The children steal glimpses to find the best places for their multidimensional games. But mostly, I think the maps exist for the mapping itself—each chart a small argument with chaos, a loving documentation of impossibility.

I've been here long enough that my memories have developed their own geography. I remember arriving on a Tuesday that lasted three weeks, my surveying equipment still calibrated for a world where parallel lines never meet. I remember the first time I successfully mapped a location that hadn't happened yet, the strange satisfaction of watching reality conform to my predictions, if only for an afternoon. I remember learning to love the instability, to find peace in perpetual adjustment.

My dwelling perches on a outcrop that will exist continuously for another seven years—a lifetime by local standards. The walls are lined with charts showing everywhere the city has been, might be, refuses to stop being. Some nights I spread them on the floor and walk across my own histories, feeling the paper shift beneath my feet as the mapped spaces update themselves. The semi-transparent deer sometimes phase through my walls, drawn by the concentrated uncertainty. We've developed an understanding—they graze on my outdated maps while I sketch their probability clouds.

I take my meals at the Temporal Canteen, where the cook serves soup that remembers being ingredients. I maintain professional relationships with the other impossibility workers: the women who weave stability nets from spider silk and paradoxes, the team that operates the Reality Anchors at the city's cardinal points (which refuse to stay cardinal), the lonely man who tunes the factories' harmonics to match the mountain's growth song. We nod to each other in passing, fellow practitioners of necessary impossibilities.

Sometimes I climb to where the abandoned factories rust in thin air that can't decide its oxygen content. I map their decay patterns, the way rust spreads in fractal spirals that mirror the mountain's growth. These charts I keep private—there's something holy about documenting abandonment in a place where nothing stays still long enough to properly decay.

The mountain knows me now, I think. When I place my palms against raw stone, I feel it breathing through my probability maps, using my documentation to decide what it wants to become. I am complicit in its becoming, my charts not just recording but somehow participating in the endless revision of this place.

I wake each day to the sound of new stone cracking into existence, dress in clothes that have learned to anticipate the weather that will have been, and step out to map the impossible. My charts accumulate like sediment, layer upon layer of documented uncertainty. Future cartographers will excavate them, trying to understand how anyone could map a place that refuses to finish becoming itself.

But that's the secret I've learned here, walking the shifting streets with my impossible instruments, breathing air thick with industrial holiness and the spores of ambitious moss: nothing ever finishes becoming itself. The mountain knows this. The city knows this. And now my maps know it too, each one a love letter to the eternal incompleteness of things, signed by someone who gave up their own certainty to document the beautiful impossibility of a world that won't stop trying to exist harder than it already does.
