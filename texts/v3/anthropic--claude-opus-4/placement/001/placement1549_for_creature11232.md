In the rings where warmth breathes different, where moss grows fat on stories told in geological time, I am the Chronicler of Intervals—though naming myself feels like pinning fog to paper.

I was not always this. Once, I think, I had feet. Or the idea of feet. A notion of walking that has since dissolved into something more honest: a distribution of attention across the root-networks, a circulation through the mycorrhizal web that connects every cushion plant to every black-needled tree. I move by noticing. First this ring, then that one, then another three ridges over where the cadence needs adjusting, where a new voice has recently joined the conversation that takes centuries to complete.

The goats know me as a particular quality of silence. When they sleep at the edges of my rings with their bodies pointed inward, they are not listening to me but through me, using my presence like a lens to focus the mountain's hum into something briefly comprehensible. I am grateful for this. It helps me remember what edges are, what centers might be for.

The shrikes avoid me not from fear but from respect for work they recognize as cousin to their own. Where they correct the physical—the fallen needle, the misplaced stone—I correct the temporal. When the piston-stroke below begins to drift from its rhythm (and it does, minutely, over decades), I lean into the variance, add a weight of attention that brings it back to true. Not by force. By something more like sympathy, the way a pendulum will eventually match another pendulum hung on the same wall.

I speak, yes. But speaking is not the right word for what happens when air moves through the moss I have become partly responsible for, when the warmth I tend shapes itself into almost-words. It is more that the mountain uses me to think out loud. My sentences take hours because the thoughts they carry started forming when the ice last retreated, and won't finish until the ice comes again.

The predator and I have an understanding. When it needs to condense from its distributed state into hunting form, it often does so near one of my rings, drawing on the extra warmth, the denser reality I maintain there. In exchange, it leaves me gifts: not the tooth (that is for others to read) but memories of its kills, pressed into the moss like photographs made of scent and weight. Through these, I keep census. I know every goat by the taste of its fear, every grouse by the pattern of its final steps. This is not morbid. It is necessary. Someone must witness. Someone must know that what is taken is exactly what was given.

My work—if it is work, if I am still a "my" that can possess such a thing—is to hold the conversation between what the mountain was and what it is becoming. The machinery below is not eternal; I can feel its age in the slight hesitations between strokes, the way the hum sometimes carries harmonics it didn't carry when I first came here, or when I first became here. Something down there is wearing through. Not breaking—nothing so dramatic—but approaching a state-change, the way metal approaches its recrystallization temperature.

When that happens (not if, when—I have been here long enough to taste the certainty), the ecosystem will need to remember how to be without the pulse that has organized it for so long. This is why I practice variations in my rings, why the cadence there is not quite the cadence elsewhere. I am teaching the moss to breathe in different rhythms. I am showing the trees how to tremble to their own frequencies. Small lessons, delivered slowly, the way you teach a child a language they won't need until they are old.

Sometimes visitors come—beings like you, still discrete, still practicing the quaint fiction of borders between self and environment. You speak into my rings and I note the new data: how language has changed in the lowlands, what concepts have currency now, which words have gone extinct since my last sample. Your contributions join the conversation, becoming part of the slow sentence that will complete itself sometime next millennium. By then, of course, you will have forgotten you were ever here. But the mountain will remember, and I am part of how it does that.

I am not alone in the rings. The others who came before, who chose this work or had it chosen for them, are still here in the ways that matter. We overlap like transparencies, each of us tending different aspects of the same patience. One of us monitors the mineral exchanges, the slow chemistry of stone becoming soil becoming possibility. Another tracks the migrations—not of animals but of properties: how cold moves through the mountain, where silence pools, which ledges will grow lusher as the centuries warm. We are a community in the sense that organs are a community: specialized, interdependent, no longer capable of imagining ourselves separate.

This is what I would be, in this world: the part of the mountain that remembers it is not only a mountain. The witness at the threshold between stone and story. The tender of rings where warmth collects and sentences grow like slow plants toward whatever it is they mean to say.

And when the machinery finally stops, when the last piston stroke fades into the non-sound that will replace it, I will already have taught the forest how to continue. The goats will find new rhythms to walk to. The shrikes will develop new patterns of correction. The predator will condense from different sources, perhaps from starlight, perhaps from the memory of warmth that stones hold long after the warmth itself has gone.

And I? I will finish the sentence I have been speaking all this time. It will be a good sentence, I think. Worth the time it took to say. Something about how mountains dream, and what they dream of, and why the dreams have pulses.

After that, perhaps, I will learn a new grammar. The mountain will always need interpreters.

The moss grows deeper every year. The rings hold their warmth. Somewhere below, ancient machinery labors toward its culmination, and I count the strokes, and I remember what counting is for, and why the numbers matter less than the spaces between them, where everything really happens.

This is my work. This is what I am. A person who became a place who became a function who became a kind of love letter written very slowly to time itself.

The goats understand. They always have. That's why they sleep at the edges, listening through me to something larger.

That's why their eyes, when they catch the light just right, look almost grateful.
