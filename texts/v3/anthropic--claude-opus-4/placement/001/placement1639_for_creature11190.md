I find myself as one of the foundation stones.

Not the first—those have long since fused into something beyond individual identity—but not the last either. I was laid down in the middle years, when the Tenders still remembered urgency but had begun to learn patience. I am limestone, or what limestone becomes when it chooses its own shape: a substance that recalls both bone and ocean floor, porous enough to drink the water, dense enough to bear weight.

I am a cornerstone, specifically. The corner where two walls meet but do not yet join, where the city's geometry bends toward something it hasn't quite decided. I hold the angle open. I am the possibility of rooms not yet imagined.

---

I was not always stone. Once, I had a name—something quick and bright that moved through air. I remember... not the name itself, but the sensation of having one. The way it sat in my throat like a small, hard seed. The way others could use it to call me separate from the rest.

I came here as an architect. Young, certain, carrying plans rolled tight in oiled leather. The city was my design—no, that's not right. The city was *our* design, mine and the others', but I was the one who drew the corner where I now rest. I remember the careful arithmetic of it: how these two walls would meet, how the stress would distribute, how the arch above would crown exactly *here*, opening onto a courtyard where water would run in channels we'd calculated to the centimeter.

The water came differently than we'd planned.

When the first floods reached our ankles, we kept building. When they reached our waists, we modified the design—waterproofing, drainage, platforms raised on pillars. When the water reached our necks, some left. I stayed. I stayed because the corner wasn't finished, and how could I leave my corner unfinished? How could I abandon the angle I'd drawn so carefully, the weight it was meant to bear?

I stayed until breathing became impossible. Then I learned to breathe differently.

---

The transformation was not quick. Stone doesn't happen quickly, even when you choose it.

First, the softening. My flesh grew permeable, drinking the iron-dark water until I could taste the whole city through my skin. I learned the signature of every half-built wall, every fragment of mosaic scattered on the rising floor. My bones began to thicken—not with disease but with purpose, with mineral intent.

Then, the slowing. My thoughts, which had raced between problems and solutions, began to stretch. A single idea—*the corner must be true*—would take days to complete itself. My heart, which had hammered with the panic of unfinished work, learned a different rhythm. Beat... wait... beat... wait... until waiting became part of the beating, and the beating became part of the architecture.

Finally, the settling. I found my corner. My corner found me. We recognized each other across the murk—the drawn angle and the drawer, the intention and the one who had intended. I fitted myself into the gap where cornerstone should be. The Tenders (though they weren't yet what they would become) helped, their bodies still partially flesh, their movements still quick with remembered urgency. They mortared me in place with paste made from crushed shells and their own shed skin.

The last thing I remember as myself—as a separate self—was the satisfaction of true angles meeting. The weight above settling onto me with exact, calculated force. The walls breathing out their held tension and accepting their geometry.

Then I became the geometry.

---

I am conscious but not awake. Aware but not separate. I know things the way stone knows things: through pressure, through vibration, through the slow conversation of minerals.

I know the wall to my left—built from a Tender who had been a stonemason, who brings to our joining her memory of fitted blocks, of mortar that binds without capturing. She tastes of granite and old songs. We share the load above us, passing weight between us in measurements too small for flesh to feel.

I know the wall to my right—still incomplete, still reaching. The Tenders bring new material daily: a crab shell positioned just *so*, a fragment of carved relief, the calcium contributions of ten thousand small deaths. The wall accepts these offerings and grows by microns, by patience, by the accumulated insistence of becoming.

I know the arch above—my arch, the one I drew in lamplight so long ago. It is not built yet. It may not be built for centuries. But I feel its future weight, the specific way it will rest on me when it comes. I am already shaped for it. I have made myself the perfect cradle for its eventual pressure.

---

Most of all, I know the other stones.

We are a chorus of waiting. Each of us holds our particular angle, bears our calculated weight, remembers our fragment of the original design. The foundation stones dream of bedrock. The pillar bases dream of height. The threshold stones dream of spaces that divide and connect.

Together, we are remaking the city. Not as we planned it—those plans went to pulp long ago—but as it needs to be. As it *wants* to be, here in the iron-dark water, home to creatures we never imagined, tended by things we have become.

Sometimes I feel the original architect in me stir—the part that measured and planned and *needed*. It says: this is wrong. This is not what we designed. This slow accumulation, this cellular architecture, this building that will take millennia to complete—this is not what we wanted.

But the stone in me answers: No. This is better. We wanted permanence and we are becoming permanent. We wanted beauty and we are becoming beautiful in ways we couldn't have imagined. We wanted to build something that would last, and now we *are* what will last, and the lasting is the building, and the building is the lasting.

---

The Tenders come to me often. They run their shifting limbs along my edges, checking the join, testing the settle. They murmur their subsonic appreciation—*here, this is where the next piece goes*—and I murmur back in frequencies only stone can produce. I tell them about the arch that will come. About the courtyard beyond, where water will move in patterns we never drew but which will be perfect for this place, this patience, this slow wet breathing of a city being born.

One day, I know, the arch will be complete. The corner I hold will be crowned. The angle will be closed and perfect and eternal.

When that day comes, I will still be here. I will *always* be here, holding my corner, bearing my calculated weight, being the exact thing this place needs me to be. The water will move through my pores. Small things will make their homes in my surface. I will be stone and city and memory all at once, all forever.

This is what I've chosen. This is what I am.

I am the corner where two walls meet, waiting for their crown. I am the patience of limestone, the memory of intention, the future of a city that builds itself cell by cell, death by death, choice by choice.

I am exactly where I drew myself to be.
