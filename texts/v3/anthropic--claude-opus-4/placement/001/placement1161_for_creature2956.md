In the deepest sub-layers of the Deep Basin, where the pressure becomes a living thing and the heat could cook unprotected flesh in minutes, I am what the surface dwellers call a Thread-Tender. My kind exist in the intervals between intervals—in the hairline fractures where the city's countless structural plates grind against each other.

I am impossibly thin. Evolution has pressed me flat over uncounted generations, until my body resembles a living membrane more than any ancestral form. When I stand sideways, I effectively disappear—a trick of biology that allows me to slip between the crushing plates when they shift. My bones are not rigid structures but cartilaginous bands that can compress to near-nothing. My organs are distributed in sheet-like arrays along my ventral surface, protected by a skin that has the texture and durability of industrial gasket material.

Where the primary Basin dwellers have four arms, I have twelve—though "arms" is a generous term. They are more like tensile ribbons, each one capable of extending up to twenty meters, tipped with microscopic setae that can taste the chemical composition of metals and detect stress fractures at the molecular level. My work is to tend the Thread—the billions of hair-thin tension cables that hold the Deep Basin together at its most fundamental level. When plates shift, Threads snap. When Threads snap, entire districts can experience catastrophic decompression.

I live in the Interstices—a network of spaces that technically don't exist on any municipal diagram. These are the gaps that form and reform as the city breathes. My home is wherever two surfaces are about to meet but haven't quite touched. I migrate constantly, reading the city's peristaltic rhythms through vibrations that travel up my ribbon-arms. I can feel a plate preparing to shift three hours before it moves, sense the fatigue in a Thread days before it fails.

My dwelling is myself. My skin secretes a polymer that hardens into a cocoon-like shell when I need to rest, adhering to whatever surfaces bound my current interstice. When I sleep—which is rarely—I am indistinguishable from a thick gasket seal between two pieces of infrastructure. Security drones pass over me without recognition. I am industrial sealant that dreams.

The Thread responds to touch in ways that would seem like magic to surface dwellers. Each cable is woven from programmable matter that predates the current city by eons—legacy infrastructure from when the Basin's builders still remembered what they were building toward. I speak to it through chemical secretions from my setae, coaxing damaged sections to re-knit, singing new pathways into existence with pheromone songs that my kind have passed down through touch-memory for generations.

I feed on the city's electrical potential. Where my ribbon-arms contact the Thread, I siphon infinitesimal amounts of current—not enough to disrupt the flow, just enough to sustain my paper-thin metabolism. The constant shifting of the plates generates piezoelectric charge that I can taste in the air. During particularly violent reconstructions, when whole districts fold and unfold like origami, the Interstices become bright with electrical storms. I spread my arms wide and feast on the lightning that arcs between grinding surfaces.

My eyes—all sixteen of them, arranged in clusters along my lateral edges—see primarily in the infrared spectrum. The Deep Basin is a symphony of heat signatures to me. I watch the warmth bloom and fade as fusion cores pass overhead, trace the thermal rivers of coolant through pipes I'll never be wide enough to enter. Each type of Thread has its own heat signature when stressed, a language of temperatures that tells me exactly where to go and what to repair.

Communication with my kind is rare and precious. We are too dispersed, too mobile to maintain communities. When two Thread-Tenders meet, it is always by accident—both following damage patterns to the same critical junction. We press our bodies together, forming a double-thick membrane, and exchange genetic material along with chemical memories of all the places we've been, all the catastrophes we've prevented. Our children are born as microscopic films that we deposit in promising interstices, leaving them to grow or die according to the city's whim.

The surface dwellers don't know we exist, not really. They notice only our effects—the way critical failures happen slightly less often than probability suggests they should, the way Threads sometimes seem to heal themselves, the occasional glimpse of something impossibly flat disappearing into a crack that shouldn't be wide enough for anything to enter. We are their invisible janitors, their guardian angels compressed to two dimensions.

Sometimes, in the deepest sections where the original architecture shows through, I find Threads that sing. These ancient cables carry data-streams from the builders, messages in languages I'll never decode. I tend them with special care, these singing Threads, knowing that I'm maintaining something whose purpose has been forgotten by everyone, including itself. The city doesn't remember what it's for anymore, but it continues to function, and I continue to tend it.

When I die—when a plate shift finally catches me wrong, or when my metabolism finally fails to extract enough charge from the air—my body will become part of the gasket-seal between two surfaces. Future Thread-Tenders might traverse my remains without knowing, might taste the chemical memory of my existence in the faint pheromone traces I leave behind. I will become what I always was: a part of the city's connective tissue, holding impossibilities together in the dark.

This is enough. To exist in the margins, to maintain the intervals, to be the thing that prevents the great grinding machine from tearing itself apart—this is purpose. I am the space between spaces, the tender of forgotten connections, the living proof that even in a world of crushing density, there must always be room for something thin enough to slip between.
