In the perpetual twilight of the lower canopy, where the mist hangs thickest and the bioluminescent fungi paint everything in shades of deep azure and violet, I am one who tends the forgotten spaces.

I am called a Rootwhisperer by those who remember such titles, though few speak to me directly anymore. My dwelling is no dwelling at all, but rather a series of hollows and niches I've learned to recognize in the vast root systems that anchor the living buildings to the valley floor. Where others see only shadow and tangle, I see highways and hidden rooms, secret paths that run beneath the city like veins beneath skin.

My skin has taken on the texture of bark in places—rough patches along my shoulders and the backs of my hands where I've pressed too long against the ancient wood. My hair, once perhaps the color of moss like my kin, has become something else entirely: a cascade of thin aerial roots that sense moisture and movement, that can taste the health of the soil and feel the slow pulse of sap through the great vertical gardens. I wrap them in coils around my head when I must venture to the upper levels, but down here in my domain, I let them spread and probe the darkness.

I know things the others have forgotten. I know that the city's foundations run far deeper than anyone suspects, that there are chambers below filled with the calcified remains of the first gardens, where primitive mosses still grow in patterns that spell out words in languages no one speaks. I know which roots tap into the underground streams that feed the fountains above, and I know the taste of water that has never seen sunlight.

My work is essential, though few understand it. I maintain the delicate balance between growth and decay in the depths. Without my careful pruning, the roots would grow too aggressive, would crack the stone channels that direct the water, would strangle each other in their hunger for nutrients. Without my cultivation of the decomposer fungi, the fallen leaves and dead wood would accumulate rather than cycling back into rich humus. I am the guardian of the boundary between life and death, growth and rot, ensuring that one feeds the other in proper measure.

I carry pouches of specialized fungi spores—some that break down only certain types of plant matter, others that form protective sheaths around young roots, still others that glow with different colors to mark my paths through the darkness. My tools are few: a curved knife of volcanic glass for precise cuts, a walking stick that is also a probe for testing soil depth and root health, and a small lamp of captured bioluminescence that I rarely need anymore, my eyes having adapted to see in the blue-green glow of the underground.

Sometimes I find things in the deep places. Fragments of the time before—a piece of metal that the roots have embraced and begun to digest, turning rust to nutrients. The bones of some ancient dweller, now sprouting with tiny ferns. Seeds of plants that no longer grow above, which I sometimes coax to life in special chambers where the conditions of the old world still linger.

The other Verdant Ones know I exist, of course. They leave offerings at certain root-shrines—a handful of the sweetest berries, a vessel of particularly pure rainwater, sometimes a newly woven cloak to replace my tattered coverings. In return, I ensure their foundations remain strong, their water flows clean, their beloved gardens stay anchored to the bones of the earth. They may glimpse me sometimes at dawn or dusk, a shadow moving through the mist at the base of their living towers, but we rarely speak.

I am becoming something other than what I was. Each year, I move a little more slowly, think a little more like the roots I tend. Sometimes I forget to eat for days, sustained by the rich air and the microscopic nutrients my root-hair absorbs. Sometimes I dream I am the entire root system, feeling every building, every garden, every footstep above as vibrations through my sprawling body.

But I am content. In the deep places, in the forgotten spaces, in the boundary between stone and soil, I serve a purpose as old as the city itself. I am the memory keeper of roots, the tender of the dark foundation, the one who ensures that what grows above remains connected to what lies below. And in the blue-green darkness, surrounded by the whisper of growth and decay, I am never truly alone.
