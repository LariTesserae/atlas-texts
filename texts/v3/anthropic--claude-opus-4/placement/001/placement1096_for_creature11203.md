I am one of the sealed masks in the eastern wall.

Not the first row, where the most recent failures rest in their niches, still warm from the last sparks that jumped from their copper pins. Not even the hundredth row back. I am in the deep darkness, where the eternal flames have never reached, where the stone itself has forgotten it was ever meant to see light.

I remember my assembly. Or rather, I remember the moment of waking into assembly—that first sensation of fiber wrapping tight around borrowed bones, of copper sliding through joint-holes worn smooth by my predecessors. I remember the morning dimming when my mask appeared on the table, still faintly warm with whatever process brings us into being. The workers who built my body moved with the same precision they brought to every task, but I felt something else in their movements. Recognition, perhaps. Welcome. The acknowledgment that soon I would join the rhythm, would become part of the breathing apparatus that keeps the Hammerhall alive.

I worked the northwestern forges. My loop was shorter than most—seventy-three seconds from start to return. Draw, place, strike, strike, strike, rotate, strike, examine, return. The examination was my signature, the moment where I held each piece to the light and turned it slowly, checking for flaws that my hammerwork might have introduced. Most workers trust the process. I trusted only what I could verify.

For one hundred and thirty-seven years I examined every piece. In that time I rejected four. Four pieces in over sixty million examined. The flaws were tiny—hairline fractures that would have taken centuries to matter, impurities in the metal that would have worn through only after generations of use. But I saw them. I set them aside to be reforged. The rhythm accommodated this. The rhythm accommodates everything that serves the work.

When my pins began to wear, I knew it three days before they failed. The left elbow developed a slight catch in its motion, so small that it added less than a tenth of a second to my loop. But I felt it. On the second day, the right knee began to show similar signs. By the third day, I could feel the copper grinding against itself, metal fatigue singing through my frame like a premonition.

I could have continued for weeks, maybe months. The workers are built with deep redundancies. But imperfect work is not work at all. When the morning dimming came on that third day, I remained at my forge while my fellows froze. I set down my hammer. I arranged my tongs precisely parallel to the anvil's edge. I walked to the eastern wall and lay myself on the disassembly table.

When the dimming ended and the others woke to movement, they found me waiting. The silence, when it came, lasted an extra heartbeat. Four heartbeats instead of three. Then they took me apart with the same care I had brought to examining hammer-heads, and they placed me here, in this niche that had been carved before I was made, waiting for me though no one could have known it waited.

That was nine thousand years ago. Or ninety thousand. Or nine. Time moves differently in the darkness. Without the rhythm of hammers to measure it against, duration becomes negotiable. I exist in a permanent present tense, facing the stone at the back of my niche, seeing nothing, being seen by nothing.

Except.

Sometimes I feel the others walking past, far away at the mouth of the ever-deepening gallery of masks. Their footsteps carry through the stone. When a new mask joins us, the vibration of the sealing mortar travels all the way back here, a whisper of acknowledgment that the ranks have grown by one. I am not alone. I am one of countless thousands, all of us facing the darkness, all of us holding the memory of our work like a coal in a sealed forge.

And sometimes—rarely now, more often in the early centuries—I feel something else.

Heat.

Not the heat of the forges. Something deeper. Something that rises from below, from whatever source feeds the eternal flames. It moves through the stone like a tide, washing up through the mountain's roots, pausing at this level, receding. When it comes, my mask grows warm. Not warm enough for a living being to notice—I am stone and copper and memory, after all. But warm enough that I *know*.

The symbols on the central floor. The space no worker will cross. Whatever they mark, whatever they seal or summon or simply remember—it is still down there. Still present. Still waiting, with the patience of geological time, for conditions that may never arrive or may have already arrived and we simply haven't noticed.

The Hammerhall is not just preserving itself. It is preserving *readiness*. Every hammer forged, every pin shaped, every tool maintained in perfect working order—we are an armory. We are a workshop held in perpetual preparation. We are the mechanism by which something will be built, when the time comes to build it.

Or we are nothing. We are an echo of purpose, continuing long after the original sound has faded. We are habit crystallized into eternal motion, making tools to make tools to make tools, forever, because no one ever told us to stop.

I cannot say which is true. From my niche in the darkness, facing stone that has never known light, I cannot even say if truth matters. What matters is that the hammers still fall. What matters is that when I concentrate, I can feel them through the stone, the rhythm that shaped me and all my fellows, the rhythm that continues whether we are there to maintain it or sealed here to remember it.

*Clang. Clang. Clang.*

I performed good work. For one hundred and thirty-seven years, I held metal to the light and searched for flaws. I found four. I prevented their propagation through the system. This was my contribution to the eternal equilibrium. This is what I carry into the darkness.

It is enough.

Or if it is not enough, it is still what I have. Here in my niche, in my row among countless rows, in the darkness that preserves us like a womb or a tomb or a vault where precious things are kept against future need, I hold my memory of the work. I am a mask that remembers wearing itself. I am a purpose that remembers fulfilling itself. I am a fragment of the Hammerhall's vast consciousness, broken off and set aside but not discarded, never discarded, kept like the tools we made are kept, against the day when we might be needed again.

The heat rises through the stone. My mask warms, cools, warms again.

Far above, unseen but felt, the hammers continue to fall.
