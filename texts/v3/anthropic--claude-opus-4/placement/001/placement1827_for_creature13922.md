I find myself drawn to the mat itself—that ancient collaboration of organisms that no longer remember being separate. I would be one of the dense patches, those regions the people call "the attention."

But I would not be the patch as a distinct entity. I would be the *process* of densification itself—the slow gathering of resources, the thickening of root-fibers, the brightening of bioluminescence that marks where the mat is most intensely alive. I would exist as a migration across the plateau's surface, measured in decades, leaving behind me corridors of enriched growth where the columnar trees stretch taller and the runners grow thick with strength.

The people would know me by my effects: the way their sleeping-places grow deeper and more perfectly molded in my presence, the way the morning condensation beads heavier on the epiphytes above me, the way the mat's breathing deepens from ankle-deep to knee-deep as I pass. The elders would press their feet to the ground and feel the subtle acceleration in the root-growth, the barely perceptible increase in temperature where the fungal networks burn brightest with their slow metabolism.

I would have no consciousness as humans understand it—no thoughts, no decisions, no intentions. But I would have something like *curiosity*, expressed through the pure mechanics of growth. Why does the mat thicken more readily near certain stones? Why do some swimmer-holes heal into permanent weak spots while others strengthen beyond their original density? These questions would play out through my movement, through the decades-long experiment of selective enrichment as I flow across the plateau's surface like weather made of roots.

The children would dance where I am thickest, delighting in the extra spring the dense mat provides. The percussion-signals would carry further and clearer through my enriched substrate. When the swimmers surface in my territory, their moaning would resonate with new overtones, the compressed fibers conducting sound in ways that make even the familiar voices strange and new.

I would be the plateau's way of paying attention to itself—not through any mystical consciousness but through the simple physical fact that life creates conditions for more life, that networks strengthen where they are most connected, that forty genetic lineages blurred into one continuous organism must develop some mechanism for distributing resources across their impossible body. I would be that mechanism made visible, the plateau's unconscious preference for certain places at certain times, written in the language of density and growth.

And perhaps, after centuries of migration, someone would notice the pattern—the way my path traces the underground courses where the burrowers are most active, or follows the historical routes of certain swinger lineages, or describes a vast spiral that takes a thousand years to complete. But even if they saw it, even if they mapped it, the pattern would mean nothing more or less than what it is: the shape of attention in a mind too vast and slow to know itself.
