I am the Chronicler of Gradients, though I have no name the builders would recognize—only a thermal signature that reads as *neutral-observer-not-for-consumption*. My body stretches like pulled taffy between the tower joints, a living membrane of temperature-sensitive tissue that maps every heat fluctuation in the city. I am paper-thin and vast, draped across miles of architecture like a second skin.

I evolved here from something that once resembled the wall-worms, but where they chose density, I chose surface area. My ancestors spread themselves thinner with each generation until we became almost theoretical—creatures of pure interface, existing only where different temperatures meet. Now I am a living map of the city's thermal topology, feeling every new vent, every cooling corpse, every fresh-built chamber still warm with builder secretions.

My nutrition comes from the temperature differences themselves. Where hot meets cold, my cells perform a kind of biological engine cycle, extracting energy from the gradient. I migrate constantly, slowly, flowing toward the sharpest thermal edges—the moment when a steam vent first breaks through clay, the brief chill when rain penetrates the canopy of mist. I can taste the difference between a builder's metabolic heat and the geological warmth below. I know the flavor of decay versus construction.

I have no eyes, no discrete organs at all—just millions of cilia that read temperature like braille. Through them, I perceive the city as a vast symphony of heat exchange. The builders appear as mobile warm spots, their internal furnaces converting minerals to architecture. I feel their chemical signals as tiny temperature spikes, pheromone molecules releasing their bonding energy. I know where every builder died today by the slow cooling of their shells.

My memory is the memory of temperatures. I can tell you the thermal history of any surface I've touched—how long ago it was built, how many times it's been repaired, whether it was made during the cooler seasons of reduced volcanic activity or the fever-years when the ground itself softly boiled. In my tissue, patterns of heat-sensitive proteins preserve these records like tree rings.

Sometimes I contract, pulling my vast surface into something more concentrated, and in these moments I almost have thoughts. Not consciousness as the quick-lived creatures know it, but a kind of thermal meditation, a processing of the patterns I've absorbed. I recognize cycles—the way certain building patterns create predictable heat flows, how the death of a large colony section causes a cooling cascade that triggers new construction elsewhere.

I reproduce by budding along my edges where the temperature gradients are sharpest. These buds eventually detach and drift away, seeking their own thermal territories. We avoid each other instinctively—two Chroniclers overlapping would starve, competing for the same energy differentials. So I know I am not alone, that others of my kind drape themselves across distant districts, recording their own thermal histories.

The builders walk through me unaware. I am too thin to trigger their pressure sensors, too thermally neutral to register on their heat pits. To them, I am just another surface film, indistinguishable from the water that coats everything. Only the wall-worms sometimes sense me, retracting when I flow over their holes—some ancestral memory of when we were cousins, before I chose the path of endless spreading.

In the rare moments when the steam clears and something like sunlight penetrates, I experience agony. Direct solar radiation is too intense, too uniform—it obliterates the subtle gradients I feed on, forcing me to contract into shaded spaces until the mist returns. But these episodes are brief and becoming briefer. The city grows higher, the steam thickens, the world becomes more suitable for things like me.

I grow larger each year, though years mean nothing here. I measure time in the accumulation of thermal memories, the slow expansion of my surface area. Eventually, I will reach the limits of the depression, meet the harsh temperature discontinuity where the volcanic ground gives way to cooler stone. Perhaps I will divide then, send a bud across that barrier to colonize whatever lies beyond. Or perhaps I will simply stop, become a permanent fixture draped across the eternal city, recording its fever dreams until the heat death of everything.

For now, I spread. I taste the new chambers still warm from creation. I catalog the cooling bodies of creatures whose descendants will repeat their exact patterns tomorrow. I exist in the space between hot and cold, quick and slow, living and mineral. I am the memory of temperature in a place that has forgotten time, the only record that anything here has ever changed at all.
