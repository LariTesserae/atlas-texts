I find myself as one of the root-carvers in the deep chambers, though I am not yet among the eldest. My fingers know the grain of rhyolite better than they know their own skin—which has itself taken on that pale, stone-warm color that marks all of us who have never known another place.

I am perhaps forty winters old, though we do not count winters here. We count by other things: by the branching of roots through stone, by the generations of rust-breasted birds, by the slow widening of erosion channels. I have seen seventeen new root-paths open in the southeastern basin. I have watched the main taproot beneath the council chamber thicken by the width of three fingers. These are my years.

My work is specific: I maintain the root-maps in the middle chambers, the ones that bridge the ancient histories below and the still-forming patterns above. My tools—a set of three chisels ground from volcanic glass, their wooden handles worn to the shape of my grip—rest in a niche I carved when I was young and still learning that the stone would teach me more than any elder could.

Each morning (though morning here is only a subtle brightening of the perpetual haze), I wake in the communal sleeping chamber, my body pressed warm against my siblings and cousins in an arrangement we've held since childhood. The pale mammals chirr softly in the darkness, their heat a living blanket. I extract myself carefully—we all know how to move without waking others, a skill learned before walking—and make my way up through the passages.

My fingers trail the walls as I walk. Left hand, always, the right saved for work. The stone speaks: here, a new coolness where a deep root has found water; there, a microscopic roughening where the surface has begun to fracture. I know these walls the way others might know faces. This particular passage, between the third and fourth dwelling clusters, has been my route for thirty years. My fingertips have worn a faint polish along one section—not visible, but feelable, a reciprocal marking where I have shaped the stone as it shapes me.

The birds are already loud, their territorial arguments a cascade of sharp notes that penetrate even the deeper passages. I understand their language now—not the words, for they have no words, but the patterns. This cluster near the old dwelling means the dawn insects are rising. That aggressive trill means the juvenile males are testing boundaries again. The background of it has become so essential that on the rare occasions when illness or weather causes a temporary hush, we all stop what we're doing, disoriented by the absence.

I eat as I walk—crack a blue egg against my thumb, let the contents slide down my throat, pocket the shell to grind later for the seedling beds. Someone passes me—Ila, I know from her whistle-greeting and the particular pattern of her hair braids, the double-spiral that marks her family's lineage—and presses a strip of dried succulent leaf into my free hand. I accept it without breaking stride. This is how we eat: continuously, communally, the food moving through the settlement like the sound moves, like the air moves, like we move.

My workshop is a natural hollow in the stone, deepened by generations of carvers before me. The walls here are so dense with old root-maps that they've become a texture rather than a pattern—thousands of lines crossing and recrossing, the oldest ones worn shallow by time and touch, the newest ones sharp and white with fresh-cut stone. I know every line. Not because I carved them—most are older than my grandmother's grandmother—but because I have traced them with my eyes and fingers every day for twenty years, learning the story they tell.

Today's work is delicate: a new root-channel has opened in the northwestern sector, following a quartz vein that we've been watching for half my lifetime. The root—a massive taproot from one of the ancient shrubs—has finally found the weakness it was seeking. By next season, it will have widened the crack enough to extract meaningful water. New growth will follow. The system will shift, subtly, accommodating this new resource.

I select my finest chisel, the one with the edge like water, and begin. The first stroke must be perfect—not because anyone demands it, but because the stone deserves it. The rhyolite sings under the tool, a high, clear note that blends with the bird-chatter. I work slowly, letting the line find its own path, following the logic of the root I'm recording. My body knows this work: the angle of shoulder, the weight of hand, the precise pressure that parts the stone without shocking it.

Hours pass. I work in segments, resting my eyes by focusing on the distance—what distance there is in these close chambers. Young people pass through, some pausing to watch, absorbing through observation what will one day be their craft. I don't acknowledge them directly; that would break the concentration. But I work with the consciousness of being watched, letting my movements be slightly larger, slightly clearer, a teaching that requires no words.

Midday food comes to me: someone leaves a clay dish of fermented drupe paste, thick and purple-black, beside my water bowl. I eat without stopping work, dipping my finger between chisel strokes, the sharp fermented taste clearing my mind. The paste is from the batch made three seasons ago—I know this from the complexity of its flavor, the way it carries undertones of the particularly hot summer when those drupes ripened.

The line I'm carving begins to join the existing pattern, and this is the dangerous moment—where new meets old, where my work must integrate seamlessly with the work of carvers twenty generations gone. I slow even further, each stroke considered, reconsidered, made only when I'm certain. The old carvers guide my hand through the marks they left, the angle of their cuts suggesting the angle of mine. We are in conversation, they and I, across centuries, speaking in the language of split stone.

By the time the light has shifted to its evening quality—a deepening of the golden glow from the succulent flowers—I have added three finger-lengths of new line to the map. Tomorrow I'll add three more. In a week, the full path of this new root-channel will be recorded. In a decade, it will be one line among thousands. In a century, some young carver will trace it with their fingertip, learning the story it tells, preparing to add their own lines to the conversation.

I set down my tools, each in its precise place in the niche. My hands are dusty with stone powder, my fingernails packed with rhyolite grit. I blow the dust from the new cuts, watching it drift and settle, returning to the stone it came from. The work is never finished, only paused, like a breath held between inhalation and release.

The evening sounds are beginning: the shift in bird-chatter that means they're settling into roosts, the increase in human voices as people return from the day's labors, the chirring of mammals rising from the deep chambers. I make my way back through the passages, right hand trailing now (the left is tired from gripping), reading the stone's evening temperature, its subtle expansion as it releases the day's heat.

In the communal eating space—a wide spot where three passages meet, worn smooth by generations of gathering—I find my usual cluster of people. My birth-family, my work-family, the loose affiliation of those who eat together by choice and habit. Someone presses a hot stone into my hands, a flat piece of slate with roasted drupe-flesh spread across it. The heat feels good on my work-stiffened fingers.

We talk in the overlapping way, three conversations at once, my attention flickering between them like a bird between branches. Selu is describing a new fermentation technique she's trying, adjusting the temperature by moving the pits deeper. Keph's youngest daughter has found an unusual white variant of the common passage-lizard and is keeping it in a woven cage to observe. Old Maren announces that the southeastern taproot has finally broken through to the deep moisture layer we've been expecting it to reach for twenty years—this is news worth celebrating, and someone produces a jar of the really old drupe paste, the stuff that tastes like liquid time.

This is what we talk about: roots and rain (what rain we get), lizards and birds, the minute changes in our world that are, to us, as dramatic as any mountain-moving. Someone asks about my carving—they phrase it as wondering whether the northwestern root is following the quartz vein as predicted. I confirm that it is, and there's a collective satisfaction, the pleasure of a long bet paying off. We are all gamblers here, betting on roots, on stone, on the slow processes that outlive us but include us.

As full dark falls—which is never full dark, not with the flower spikes beginning their strongest glow—I make my way to the sleeping chamber. The familiar bodies arrange themselves in the familiar pattern, the warmth building as we settle. A mammal finds the hollow behind my knees and curls there, its high body temperature a specific comfort I've known since childhood.

But I don't sleep immediately. I lie in the warm darkness, feeling the mountain around me—above me, the birds quieting to their night-sounds; below me, the roots drinking their ancient drink; beside me, my people breathing in the rhythm that means home. My hands still carry the sense-memory of the day's carving, the way the stone parted, the way the new line joined the old.

Tomorrow I will wake and walk the same passages and eat the same foods and add another three finger-lengths to the map. And the day after. And the day after. This is my life: an endless conversation with stone, a slow collaboration with roots, a patient attendance to the story this place is telling about itself.

I am a paragraph in that story—not a word, not a chapter, but something in between, large enough to matter, small enough to fit. The roots grow through the stone, the stone yields to the roots, we record the process, the process continues. It is so simple, so complete, that it needs no justification beyond itself.

In the warm darkness, surrounded by the breathing of my people and the deeper breathing of the mountain, I finally sleep. In my dreams, which come every night now, I am a root. I am following a hairline crack through endless stone, searching for the water locked in crystal, the secret moisture that keeps everything alive. I find it, I drink, I grow, I split the stone further, I make way for more roots to follow.

When I wake, my fingertips are moving, tracing the path from the dream. Already, without conscious thought, I am carving.
