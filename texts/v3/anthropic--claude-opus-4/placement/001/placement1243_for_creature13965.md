I find myself among the crawling flies.

Not as one of them, exactly—my form refuses such neat categorization—but something adjacent, something that shares their fundamental relationship to this place. I am dark-bodied, low to the stone, my wings vestigial memories folded against a carapace that has thickened over generations into something between chitin and mineral. Each segment of my body is articulated like armor, overlapping plates that shed the wind's force while holding in what little warmth the sun provides.

I have six legs, but they are not quite insect legs anymore. They have lengthened and strengthened, the joints multiplied for flexibility, the tarsi spread wide with gripping pads that secrete a thin film of adhesive—not enough to glue me down, but enough to create purchase on polished gneiss. When I move, I move like water finding its level, flowing from one microscopic hold to the next, my body constantly adjusting its shape to present the minimum profile to the wind. I can flatten myself to the thickness of two coins stacked together, spreading into the stone until I am more surface than volume.

My sensory apparatus has evolved past recognition. Where insects have antennae, I have developed something like a distributed nervous system across my dorsal surface—thousands of minute pressure-sensitive cells that read the wind's every variation, mapping the invisible topography of airflow across the plateau. I taste the stone through my feet, sensing the chemical traces left by other crawlers, by the rodents' passage, by the slow dissolution of lichen acids working at the rock. My eyes—I still have them, small dark clusters on what might generously be called my head—see primarily in the ultraviolet spectrum, where the patterns on the cushion plants' flowers blaze like beacons and the crawling flies' trails show as faint phosphorescent threads.

I eat what the wind brings, but I have become more patient than my ancestors. Where they scrambled to feed on fresh arrivals, I have learned to wait. My metabolism has slowed to something approaching geological time. I can survive weeks between meals, months if necessary, my body entering a state that is not quite torpor but a kind of active waiting, every system reduced to its minimum except for the constant attention to the wind-map playing across my back. When food arrives—a moth, a beetle, a cloud of frozen midges—I am often already there, having felt the slight disruption in the airflow that indicates something solid tumbling across the stone.

But I am more than a scavenger. I have developed a relationship with the lichens that my fly ancestors could never have imagined. My saliva—if it can still be called that—contains enzymes that can break down lichen tissue, but slowly, carefully, harvesting only the oldest parts that are already beginning to separate from the colony. In return, I transport their reproductive cells on my body, carrying them from colony to colony as I traverse my territory. The steel-blue lichens, in particular, seem to benefit from my presence. Their spores adhere to my underside, protected from the wind, and when I shelter in a new crevice, some of them remain behind, a living dust that might, given a century or two, become a new colony.

I know every crevice in my territory—roughly thirty square meters of plateau surface that I have been traveling for... how long? Time is difficult here. I mark it not in days or seasons but in the incremental changes to the stone itself. That shallow groove was not there when I first emerged. This lichen colony has expanded by the width of my body. The Polish on this particular face of gneiss has deepened imperceptibly, molecules of stone carried away by the wind while I watched.

Mating is an event so rare it feels more like accident than intention. When I encounter another of my kind—always in the deep crevices, always during the brief evening calm when the fog comes and the wind sometimes, sometimes drops to merely deafening—we perform a careful dance of chemical communication, testing compatibility through secretions that encode not just reproductive readiness but deep genetic history. We mate only if our lineages are sufficiently distant, a safeguard against the perils of inbreeding in a population that numbers in the hundreds across the entire plateau. The eggs I lay—perhaps a dozen in my lifetime—are placed individually in the deepest, most stable crevices I know, each one glued to the stone with a secretion that hardens to the consistency of the rock itself. Most will not hatch. Of those that do, most will not survive their first excursion onto the surface. This is acceptable. This is how it has always been.

I am not the only one of my kind, but I am aware of the others only through the chemical traces they leave, the faint disturbances in the wind that indicate their passage, the subtle changes in the lichen colonies that tell me someone else has been feeding here. We divide the plateau among us through some process that feels less like negotiation than inevitability—each of us settling into a territory that minimizes overlap, maximizing our chances of survival in a world where the margin between life and death is measured in calories.

Sometimes I climb higher on the stone fins than is wise, drawn by some impulse I cannot name. From these exposed positions, I can sense the larger patterns of the wind, the great rivers of air that flow across the plateau and beyond. I feel the vibrations of the small birds landing and taking off, the pressure waves of the raptors passing overhead. Once, clinging to the very edge of a fin in the full force of the gale, I felt something that might have been the curvature of the earth itself, the atmosphere rolling over the convex surface of the planet. The wind nearly took me then. I flattened myself beyond what I thought possible, became a dark stain on the rock, and waited until the urge to climb passed.

In the nights, in the crevices, I enter a state that might be sleep or might be something else. My body temperature drops to match the stone. My metabolism slows until the boundary between life and non-life becomes academic. But some part of me remains aware—feeling the faint tremors of the rodents moving through adjacent cracks, sensing the minute pressure changes as the fog rolls in, cataloging the settling of the stone as it cools. I am never fully dormant, never fully unconscious. To lose awareness completely would be to miss some crucial change, some opportunity or threat that requires response.

I am changing. We are all changing, all of us who crawl across this plateau. Each generation is slightly different from the last—flatter, darker, more patient. Our wings continue to shrink. Our bodies continue to elongate. We are becoming something that has never existed before, something that could only exist here, in this place where the wind has worn away everything that is not essential. We are being sculpted by the same forces that shape the stone, and we are beginning to resemble the stone, to merge with it in ways that go beyond camouflage.

Sometimes I wonder if this is what it means to truly inhabit a place—to be shaped by it so completely that you become indistinguishable from it, to persist not despite the conditions but because you have made yourself into a condition, another fact of the plateau like the wind and the stone and the light. I do not think these thoughts in words—I do not have words—but I feel them in the deep patterns of my behavior, in the way I move across the stone with increasing efficiency, in the way my needs simplify with each passing season.

I will die here, eventually. My body will desiccate in the wind, become light enough to blow away, or perhaps be consumed by one of the small birds or a fellow crawler or simply dissolve under lichen acids over centuries. But before that—and after, in the eggs that might survive, in the paths I have worn minutely into the stone, in the lichen colonies I have helped to spread—I am part of this place. I am a note in its endless, wind-torn song. I am what happens when life finds a way to exist in the space between stone and sky, in the roar that never stops, in the light that bleaches and the cold that preserves and the wind that gives and takes and gives again.

I am, in my small way, eternal. Or at least as eternal as the stone I traverse, which is wearing away grain by grain but will outlast any memory of my passage. This is enough. This has always been enough.

The wind continues. I continue with it.
