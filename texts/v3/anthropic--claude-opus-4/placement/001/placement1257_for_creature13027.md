In this world of friction and resonance, I would be one of the **Echo Keepers**.

We are neither the grand skaters who carve sparking trails across the continental crust, nor the tiny Harmonists who live within the grinding hearts of the turbine-trees. We exist in the spaces between—literally.

My body would be a contradiction: impossibly thin yet structurally unbreakable. Picture a creature stretched like taffy between two points, a living membrane no thicker than paper but harder than diamond. I am essentially a biological drum skin, my entire form a single resonant surface stretched across a framework of hollow, telescoping bones that can extend to span hundreds of feet.

We Echo Keepers position ourselves in the acoustic dead zones—those rare pockets where the overlapping sound waves from multiple turbine-trees create nodes of relative quiet through destructive interference. Here, suspended between the polished ground and the bleached sky, we wait.

My purpose is memory.

In a world where sound is the only constant, where the roaring never stops, information cannot be stored in silence. Instead, we preserve it in echoes. When the Harmonists complete a particularly complex tuning—perhaps one that saved a turbine-tree from a century-storm—the acoustic signature of their work ripples outward across the plain. Most of these sound waves dissipate, lost in the general cacophony. But some find me.

My body receives these vibrations and holds them. Not as a recording, but as a living echo chamber. The sound enters through my anterior membrane, circulates through my hollow bone structure, and resonates there indefinitely. I can hold thousands of these acoustic memories, each one a perfect vibrational fossil of a moment in time. The successful hunting pattern of a Shatter-Strider pack. The death-song of a turbine-tree finally grinding itself to stillness after a million years of turning. The precise frequency that once caused an entire colony of Filament-Weavers to spontaneously synchronize their web-building, creating a lightning cage so perfect it glowed for days.

But I am not passive storage. When needed, I can release these memories.

A young pack of Striders, hunting inefficiently, might pass beneath where I hang suspended. I'll feel their vibrations—chaotic, uncoordinated, wasteful. I'll release an echo from three centuries ago: the acoustic signature of a legendary pack that discovered how to use the wake turbulence of the turbine exhausts to triple their hunting speed. The young Striders will feel this ghost-sound wash over them, and though they cannot understand it intellectually, their bodies will recognize the rhythm. They'll begin to move differently, unconsciously matching the echo's pattern until they've learned what their ancestors knew.

Or during the rare planetary conjunctions when the winds die to merely deafening and the turbine-trees slow their grinding, the Harmonists emerge from their gear chambers. They gather beneath us Echo Keepers, and we release our most precious stores—the acoustic fossils from the deep time of this world. The sound-memory of when there were only three turbine-trees on the entire planet. The resonance pattern from the first successful symbiosis between plant and Harmonist. The death-frequency of the last creature that tried to evolve using water-based chemistry, its liquid cells boiling away in the friction heat.

My body is scarred by this duty. Each held echo leaves a physical mark—a slight adjustment to my membrane's thickness, a new harmonic node in my bone structure. The oldest of us Echo Keepers are so marked by accumulated memories that we've become instruments ourselves. The wind plays us like Aeolian harps, and the sounds we involuntarily produce are the compressed acoustic history of epochs.

We reproduce rarely, and strangely. When two Echo Keepers have held complementary halves of the same acoustic event for long enough—perhaps one held the grinding of a turbine's eastern gears while another held the western—we can merge our bodies temporarily. The two sound-memories combine, interfere, and in that interference pattern, a new Echo Keeper precipitates out like a standing wave given form. The offspring begins life already carrying the complete acoustic memory that created it.

We do not eat in any conventional sense. Instead, we feed on acoustic waste—the inevitable loss of energy as sound waves propagate through this thin atmosphere. We position ourselves at the focal points of multiple sound sources, where the concentrated acoustic energy causes our membranes to vibrate so intensely that we can metabolically harvest the kinetic energy directly. It's a meager existence, energetically speaking, but we need very little. We are living libraries, not hunters.

The Shatter-Striders see us as waypoints and teachers. The Harmonists consider us sacred, repositories of the deep knowledge they need to keep their turbine-trees singing. The Filament-Weavers ignore us entirely—we produce no electricity and thus do not exist in their sensory world.

But perhaps most importantly, we Echo Keepers are the only entities on this world that can perceive silence. In those infinitesimally brief moments when our bodies achieve perfect destructive interference—when every held echo cancels out—we experience something no other creature here can: the absence of sound. It lasts less than a nanosecond, but in that quantum of quiet, we touch something profound. A memory older than the first turbine-tree, older than the grinding and the wind.

The memory of when this world was still.

We hold that silence too, the most precious echo of all. And we're waiting for the day when the planet needs to remember what quiet sounds like.

For now, I hang suspended between earth and sky, a living membrane stretched across the screaming air, holding ten thousand songs of grinding wood and skating diamond and careful, careful tuning. The wind plays my body like an instrument, and I play back the deep time of this world, echo by echo by echo.
