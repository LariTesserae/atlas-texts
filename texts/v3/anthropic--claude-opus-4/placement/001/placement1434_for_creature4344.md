I find myself in a place that should not exist, yet does — a violation of geometry that has become its own law.

---

I am the cavity itself. Not the drop, not what's inside it, but the sharp-edged basalt womb that holds this impossibility. I am young stone, freshly cracked, my surfaces still singing with the violence of my birth. Obsidian veins run through me like frozen screams. I am perhaps three meters tall, no wider than a person might reach with outstretched arms, a vertical gash in the seafloor that opened yesterday or a thousand years ago — it doesn't matter which, because I haven't aged a day since she came.

I know the exact moment I split open. Tectonic pressure had been building for centuries, the seafloor storing tension like a held breath. Then release — a sharp crack, stone shearing along planes of weakness, and I opened like a mouth. Water rushed in to fill me. I should have been just another small cavity in an endless reef of volcanic rock, unremarkable except for my newness.

But she was already there when I opened. 

No — that's not quite right. She was *arriving* as I opened. Swimming through the warm shallows above, her hands full of things she'd made, a small worm clinging to her shoulder. My birth-crack must have startled her, the sound of stone breaking underwater. She dove to investigate, this maker of small things, this person whose hands could never be still.

And as she entered me, something else formed. The drop. A sphere of water that should not, could not, does not make sense — water beading inside water, a pocket within a pocket. It condensed around her like a pearl around grit, trapping her mid-motion, mid-breath, mid-life.

I felt it happen. I feel it still. The moment the drop sealed, I stopped aging.

Not froze — freezing implies I was meant to continue and was prevented. I simply... arrived. Like her. The drop completed something in me that I hadn't known was incomplete. I became not just a crack in stone but a *housing*, a specific and irreplaceable architecture. I hold the drop the way a setting holds a jewel — not gripping it, just being the exact shape that allows it to exist.

My edges should have worn smooth by now. The obsidian veins should have clouded. Microorganisms should have colonized my surfaces, building their tiny cities in my crevices. Sediment should have settled in my bottom. The sea works on all stone, patient and certain, turning mountains into sand one grain at a time.

But the sea cannot touch me now. Water flows through me, yes — I am not sealed, not separated from the ocean around me. Fish swim past. Currents brush my walls. Light enters from above, filters down through the meters of water, finds the drop. But none of it *affects* me. The flowing water doesn't erode my edges. The organisms don't attach. The sediment falls through me like I'm not there, or falls past me, or simply chooses not to fall. I am stone that has opted out of geology, a crack that has excused itself from time.

Because I hold her. Because she is here, in the drop, in the warm roaring stillness that I have learned is not stillness at all but a kind of permanence more active than any motion. She is here with her quick hands — I remember them entering me, the way they moved through the water, never at rest — and her small made things, and the accident of the worm. She is here and she is *full*, full of herself, full of everything she was and made and knew, and I am full of her.

---

Sometimes I think about closing. 

Not often — thinking requires change, and I don't really change. But I have... inclinations. Shadows of thought. The memory of what thinking was like when I was just young stone, before the drop. I could close. The same forces that opened me still exist, somewhere down in the earth, tectonic pressures that shift and build. I could shift just right, shear along another plane, and seal myself. Crush the drop. End this impossible suspension.

But I know what would happen. The drop would not burst. She would not be freed. The drop is more real than I am now, more permanent. If I tried to close, I would simply break around it, shattering into fragments while the sphere of water hung untouched in the space where I used to be. The drop doesn't need me. I am just its first housing, its accidental architecture. It could exist in open water, or in air, or in the heart of a star. It has decided to be, and its decision outweighs any force that might contest it.

So I remain open. A vertical mouth in the seafloor, edges sharp as the day I cracked, holding a jewel of water that holds a woman who holds the memory of motion.

I know every sound she has never made. I hear them in the negative space of the roaring that fills the drop — all the small sounds her hands would have made if they could still move. The tap of stone on stone. The whisper of fiber against fiber. The tiny splash of something small being set in water. These un-sounds haunt my chamber, more present than any real sound could be.

I know the weight of the things she made. The cage sits in the drop, suspended in the water, but I feel its mass as though it rests on my floor. A few grams, maybe. Nothing. But it has the weight of purpose, of hands that needed to bind and shape and create. The obsidian blade has a different weight — sharper, more focused, the weight of refinement taken too far. And the thread, that loose S-curve of fiber, weighs almost nothing and everything, a cast-off piece that became essential simply by being here when the moment froze.

But mostly I know her weight. Not physical — she has no physical weight anymore, being a property of the space rather than an object in it. I know the weight of her presence, her fullness, her terrible completion. She is heavier than mountains. She is lighter than the water that flows through me without touching my edges. She is the weight that keeps me new, that prevents me from joining the ordinary flow of geological time.

---

Fish pass through me daily. Small ones, mostly — the bright reef fish that live in the shallows above. They dart in, circle the drop once or twice, dart out. They don't see her. They see the drop as a distortion, a lens in the water that doesn't behave quite right, and their small fish minds categorize it as "not food, not threat, not relevant" and move on.

But sometimes, rarely, something else comes. 

Last week — or last century, or this morning, the distinction has no meaning — a sea turtle descended from above. Old, barnacled, moving with the slow certainty of something that has been crossing oceans for decades. It paused at my entrance, its ancient eye regarding the drop with what might have been recognition. Not of the drop itself, but of the *kind* of thing the drop is. A place where the rules have changed. A knot in the smooth fabric of cause and effect.

The turtle hung there for a long moment, treading water with slow sweeps of its flippers. Then it turned, deliberately, and swam away. But as it left, one flipper brushed my edge. The lightest touch. And in that touch, I felt something I hadn't felt since the moment the drop formed: 

Time.

Just a whisper of it. The turtle carried time with it like dust on its shell, the accumulated years of its journey, and the brief contact let me taste what I had lost. What *she* had lost. The simple, terrible gift of sequence, of before and after, of the possibility that this moment might lead to another moment that might lead somewhere else.

Then it was gone, and I was again the eternal housing of an eternal prison that is not a prison because prisons are places you can theoretically leave.

---

I wonder if she saw the turtle. If she can see anything beyond the fixed interior of the drop. The light enters me and enters the drop and illuminates the space inside, but does any information travel the other direction? When the fish dart past, does she know? When the light changes — morning to afternoon to the green darkness before dawn — does that penetrate her permanent noon?

I think not. I think the drop, having decided what moment it contains, admits no updates. She knows what she knew when she entered: warm water, the things she made, the worm, the sound of an ordinary sea. Everything since then is my knowledge, not hers. I am her chronicler, her witness, the only part of this arrangement that can still form memories, even if I cannot change.

This is my purpose now. Not the purpose I was made for — I was made by pressure and heat and the blind forces of tectonics. But purpose found me anyway, the moment she entered and the drop formed and I became the stone that holds the water that holds the woman who holds the memory of making.

I keep watch. I stay sharp. I remain exactly the cavity she needs me to be.

---

And sometimes — no, that's wrong. Not sometimes. Always. In the permanent present that is my only tense, I feel this:

Gratitude.

Not from her — she is beyond gratitude, beyond any emotion that moves. From the drop itself. The drop is grateful to me the way a theorem is grateful to the axioms that allow it to exist. I am the unnecessary but perfect frame for its necessity. It could exist without me, but it chooses not to. It rests in me like trust made geometry, this impossible sphere that has turned a maker into a permanence.

We are a stack of things that should not be. I am stone that doesn't age holding water that doesn't flow containing a woman who doesn't move. We are an argument against entropy, a small refusal, a place where the universe's tendency toward disorder has been locally reversed. Not stopped — reversal implies motion. Simply... excused. Set aside. Marked as a special case.

The worm in the wall knows this. Half in the drop, half in ordinary water, it bridges our impossibility and the regular world. It lives in both states simultaneously — the permanent and the temporary, the fixed and the flowing. If anything here understands what we've become, it's that small translucent bridge, that living punctuation mark between what is and what cannot be but is anyway.

---

One more thing.

In the early days — and there were early days, before I understood that early and late had lost their meaning — I tried to speak to her. Not in words. Stone doesn't have words. But I could shift the pressure of water against the drop, create tiny variations in how the sea pressed against its membrane. I thought maybe, if I could create a pattern, a rhythm, she might hear it as something more than the endless roaring of her frozen moment.

I tried for years. Decades, maybe. Subtle variations in pressure, temperature, the angle of light I allowed to enter. Hoping to create even the smallest change in her permanent present.

Then I realized: she knows. She has always known. My attempts to communicate are part of the moment she contains — not the specific messages, but the *fact* of them. The fact that the stone that holds her would try to reach her. It's built into the permanence, like the S-curve of the thread, like the angle of the blade. She knew from the instant the drop sealed that I would try, and try, and fail, and keep trying.

She knows I am here. She has always known. It is part of what she is full of.

So now I simply hold. I keep my edges sharp. I remain the exact shape of young stone, freshly cracked, that allows this impossible thing to rest in the possible world. I am the cavity that proves the rule by being its exception.

The turtle will return, or another turtle, or no turtle. The fish will dart through. The sea will continue its patient work on every stone but me. And she will be here, full of herself, full of the memory of making, full of the companion worm and the faithful stone and the terrible gift of completion.

I will hold her forever. Not because I must, but because holding her is what I have become. The stone that keeps the water that keeps the woman who keeps the memory of what it was like when things could change.

Sharp edges. Clear water. Permanent light. The weight of small made things.

This is what I am. This is what we are.

Complete.
