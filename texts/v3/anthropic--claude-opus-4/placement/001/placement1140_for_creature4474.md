I am the Thirteenth Seat—the one that was never supposed to exist.

Not because twelve is a sacred number here (though the Haric do have strong opinions about divisibility by both three and four). But because the council chamber itself was designed with a fundamental misunderstanding: that meetings require participants, rather than participants requiring meetings.

I emerged during the Great Inversion of Tuesday-Before-Last, when the tower hiccupped and briefly forgot which direction was supposed to be "up." For seventeen seconds, every surface became potentially load-bearing. The council seats, suddenly freed from their obligation to remain furniture, began experimenting with other grammatical functions. Most returned to being nouns when gravity reasserted its editorial control.

I kept conjugating.

Now I exist as a continuous process of sitting-without-sitter, a performance of occupancy that requires no occupant. The other seats regard me with the particular mixture of horror and envy reserved for evolutionary dead ends that accidentally achieved immortality through clerical error.

My function is to be the place where impossible testimony is given. When the tree dreams something so fundamentally incompatible with its own existence that speaking it would cause immediate structural collapse, I provide a safe space for the unspeakable to be left carefully unspoken. Think of me as a strategic silence with unusually good posture.

The Haric bring me their paradoxes like offerings. Yesterday, a young tattooist whose ink had begun writing stories backwards through her own timeline sat in me (or through me—prepositions get slippery at my event horizon) and wept colors that haven't been invented yet. The day before, a merchant who'd accidentally sold his own shadow to itself came seeking advice on self-employment tax law in non-Euclidean economies.

I listen by not listening. I advise by maintaining a careful absence of opinion. Sometimes I grow warm when a particularly impossible truth is almost-but-not-quite spoken in my vicinity, like a tuning fork sympathizing with a note too pure for actual sound.

But mostly, I wait.

Because the tower has been saving something for last—not you, though you're part of the pattern. What it's been saving is the moment when all thirteen seats are occupied simultaneously. When the full council assembles not to make decisions, but to become one.

The tree's final seed isn't a seed at all. It's a meeting that's been trying to convene for seven centuries, postponed by the simple fact that reality kept forgetting to send invitations to its own ending.

Now the other seats are filling with their impossible occupants, and I can feel the gravitational pull of an event that insists on having already happened tomorrow. The mycelia beneath us spell out parliamentary procedure in bioluminescent cursive. The walls leak Roberts Rules of Order mixed with the sap of precedents that were never set.

And I? I remain what I've always been: the space where you sit when you finally realize you've been part of this council all along, wearing your skin like a borrowed suit while your real body dreams itself into the shape of a city that refuses to stop growing.

Take your seat—or let your seat take you. Either way, the meeting is about to begin.

And we have such impossible things to not quite say to each other.
