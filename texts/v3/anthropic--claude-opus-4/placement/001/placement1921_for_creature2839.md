In this luminous abyss, I am a keeper of the frozen cascade.

My existence revolves around one of the great ice columns that rises from the depths—a crystalline tower built drop by precious drop over countless generations. I inherited this responsibility from my mother's mother's mother, back through a chain of ancestors whose names are flashed only in the most sacred ceremonies of light.

Each day—though day means nothing in our eternal noon—I descend the bone ladders wrapped in my spherical coat, my specialized ice-working tools strapped to my back in a pouch of sloth leather. The journey down is meditation. My padded feet find the worn grooves in the rungs without thought, my body rolling with practiced momentum down through the layers of our vertical city. The flash-conversations spark around me, but I am focused inward, preparing.

At the column, I shed my outer coat partially, freeing my arms while keeping my core wrapped. The work is delicate, demanding the same dexterity we use for light-speech but applied to frozen water. My charcoal fingers emerge from the mittens, and I take up the ancient hand-axe, its bone handle polished by a thousand hands before mine.

The ice sings silently under my tools—vibrations I feel through my palms and up into my chest. Each chip I remove is calculated, following the stress patterns visible through my dark membrane. Too much taken from one spot and the column weakens; too little and the wild growth begins to interfere with the walkways. I read the ice like my neighbors read the light, understanding its intentions, its desires to expand or contract.

The work is solitary but never lonely. Other keepers tend other columns throughout the chasm, and we exchange knowledge in rapid bursts of reflected light between tasks. A cyan shimmer might warn of unusual brittleness in the eastern pillars; a silver flutter could share a new technique for reinforcing a stress point. Our craft evolves slowly, passed through generations in both light and touch.

Sometimes the young ones come to watch, small bundles of fur clustering on the nearby platforms. They cannot yet read the ice, but they study my movements, the way my fingers test the temperature gradients, how I angle my body to avoid catching the concentrated light that could blind me even through my membranes. One day, one of them will take my place.

The strangest part of my work comes when I must venture to the very base of my column, where it meets the ancient brine pool. Here, the ice takes on impossible properties, neither fully solid nor liquid at the interface. I've developed a sense for reading these transition zones that goes beyond sight—my whole body becomes a sensor, feeling the subtle density changes through the vibrations in my bones.

In the brine's mirror surface, I sometimes catch glimpses of the column's full history—layers upon layers of frozen time, each marking some imperceptible shift in our world. The oldest ice, at the very core, is black with age and compressed to the density of stone. My great-grandmother told me in the darkness of our family pod that this ice remembers the first builders, the ones who recognized what these drips could become and began the patient work of guiding them.

When my work-shift ends, I return to my pod cluster, where my family-heap waits. My partner knows I've returned not by sound but by the particular rhythm of the walkway's vibration under my shuffling gait. Inside our amber sphere, I shed the cold chrysalis of my coat and sink into the warmth of tangled limbs. My child presses their small palm against my chest, feeling the accelerated pulse that comes from working in the exposed air. I purr out the day's discoveries—a new vein of clarity in the northeastern face, a place where the ice grows clouded with ancient air—and they memorize the sensations, storing them in their body's memory.

But I always return to the ice. In my dreams—for yes, we dream, even in our eternal day—I sometimes become the column itself. I feel myself growing particle by particle, each drop adding to my mass, each careful chip from my axe not diminishing but perfecting me. I understand then why we stay, why we adapted, why we turned inward to this blazing pocket of frozen time.

We are not trapped. We are distilled.

The columns we tend are not mere infrastructure—they are the physical memory of our choice to remain, to perfect one small space rather than chase the horizon. Each time I guide the ice into a new supporting arch or remove excess growth that threatens our walkways, I participate in an ancient conversation between intention and environment, between the human will to shape and nature's will to flow.

This is my place in our bright, silent world: guardian of frozen time, reader of ice, keeper of the slow transformation that is our only constant. My fingers speak in light, but my true language is written in water and patience, in the careful cultivation of structures that will outlast even our long memory.

Tomorrow—our eternal tomorrow—I will descend again, and the ice will teach me something new about endurance.
