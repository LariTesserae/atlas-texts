In the lower mid-waters, where the light falls green and uncertain, I tend the filter gardens.

My name is **Sift**, though that came later—a designation that grew from function rather than birth. I was born with another name in the years before my work chose me, but I've worn this one so long now that the old syllables feel foreign in my mouth, like trying to speak through the wrong density of water.

The filter gardens are not gardens as the surface world might have known them. They are architectures of appetite, living systems I've been cultivating for twenty-three years in the twilight zones where few others venture regularly. Here, in the space between the Pallid Ones' darkness and the common zones' comfortable dimness, I've strung nets of my own devising—not for catching fish, but for catching the invisible.

The water carries everything. Scales shed by the passing schools, fragments of decaying kelp, the microscopic deaths that rain down constantly from the bright zones above. Waste from the city's thousand daily processes. Shells ground fine by current and time. All of it drifts, suspended, making the water rich but also thick, harder to breathe, clouded. Most don't notice the gradual change, the way the city's own success slowly strangles it. I noticed.

My nets are woven from the whisker-roots of deep kelp, the ones that grow finer than hair and stronger than rope. Each strand is selected for its particular hunger—some pull minerals from the water, some trap organic matter, some simply provide structure for the real workers: the colonies of filter-feeders I've been breeding in the sheltered pockets of my domain.

They are not beautiful creatures. Thumbnail-sized, translucent, pulsing with a rhythm that has nothing to do with heartbeat and everything to do with consumption. They cling to my nets in their thousands, in their tens of thousands, each one pulling water through its body and keeping what it needs, releasing the rest cleaner than it came. I've bred them across generations to be more efficient, to process more, to reproduce faster. The original stock came from the thermal vents—Orun would be horrified if he knew I'd been taking his precious heat-lovers and changing them, but Orun doesn't come down here.

Few do. The middle waters unsettle people. Too dark for the light-dwellers, too bright for the Pallid Ones. The pressure here is also particular—not the comfortable weight of the deeps nor the easy float of the shallows, but something between that seems to press differently on the organs, make the heart work harder. I've adapted. My ribcage has developed a peculiar flexibility, expanding and contracting with each breath in ways that would disturb a healer if I let one examine me, which I don't.

I live in a chamber I've grown myself, a pod of kelp trained into a sphere and hardened with mineral deposits I've encouraged through careful feeding. It hangs at the center of my garden network, tethered by the same whisker-roots that make my nets, so that I feel every vibration through the system. When a section clogs, I know. When the filter-feeders spawn, I know. When something large passes through and tears my careful webbing, I know that too, and I go to repair it with the patience of someone who has accepted that all work is temporary.

The Pallid Ones respect what I do, even if they don't fully understand it. Vell visited once, early in my work, drifting up from her darkness to examine my nets with those enormous white eyes. She signed something I couldn't read—my hands don't bend the way deep-dweller hands do—but when she left, she brushed my garden with careful fingers, and I took it as approval. Since then, I've noticed the deep water runs cleaner. Whatever they're doing down there, they're doing it with awareness of my filters above them.

The city's elders—Mira among them—think I'm mad. Not dangerously so, just touched by the kind of madness that makes someone choose isolation and obsessive work over community. They're not wrong. But I've discovered something in my decades of solitude and filtration: the water remembers.

Not consciously. Water has no consciousness that I can detect. But it holds patterns, chemical signatures, the ghost-traces of everything that's passed through it. In the sediment my filters collect, I can read the city's history. A layer of ash from when the eastern thermals failed. A band of unusual minerals from the year a deep current brought strange waters up from below. The particular combination of scales and waste that means a predator passed through the wild kelp—I can tell you which kind by the fear-chemicals the fish released.

Last month, my filters caught something new. A sediment I've never seen before, crystalline and bitter-tasting, settling out of the water in quantities that concern me. It's coming from below—far below, deeper than even the Pallid Ones venture. The concentration is increasing. Whatever process produces it is accelerating.

I've told no one yet. I'm still documenting, still trying to understand the pattern. But sometimes, hanging in my chamber at the heart of my web, feeling the water pulse through my gardens, I think about the old stories. The ones about why our ancestors entered the water in the first place. The plague or drought or war that drove them down.

What if it wasn't any of those things? What if they were fleeing something else, something that's been sleeping in the deep all this time, and is beginning to wake?

The water remembers. In its chemical memory, I search for answers, filtering the city's dreams and waste and daily dying, looking for the story we've forgotten how to read. The work continues, patient as the kelp's growth, necessary as breath.

I am Sift, tender of gardens that are not gardens, reader of water's memory, keeper of the space between light and darkness. The city flows through me, and I clean what I can, and I watch for signs of the thing that drives these new sediments up from the foundational dark.

Tomorrow I'll need to replace the nets in the eastern section. The filter-feeders there have grown too numerous, too heavy. Success becomes its own burden. I'll harvest half of them, relocate them to new territories, maintain the balance.

Always the balance. Always the careful work of keeping a city livable when it was never meant to be lived in at all.

The water remembers. I remember with it. And in the settling sediment of days and years, I wait to understand what memory is trying to surface from the deep.
