I find myself in a narrow vertical shaft, barely wider than my body, that descends through solid limestone. The walls are slick with mineral deposits, and every few feet there are small ledges—just finger-width protrusions where the stone naturally fractured along different strata. These are my anchors, my brief respites as I make my way between the surface world and the chamber below.

I am the Limestone Chronicler, though no one has ever called me this. My skin has taken on a faint grey-green patina from decades of pressing against these wet stones, and my fingernails have grown thick and ridged like shells, better for finding purchase on the tiny ledges. My body has lengthened somehow, stretched by years of navigating this shaft—my torso is longer than it should be, my arms reach further, my spine has developed an extra curve that helps me fold and unfold in the confined space.

The shaft opens into a natural cavity deep in the karst, a pocket where water once pooled and swirled for millennia before draining away. The chamber is perhaps twice the size of the bright room beneath the bay, but irregular, following the stone's own logic rather than human design. Flowstone cascades down one wall like frozen honey. Stalactites hang from the ceiling in a forest of pale spears.

But I have changed this place.

Every surface that can hold a mark now bears one. Using tools made from harder stones, using pigments mixed from clay and bat guano and my own blood when necessary, I have covered the chamber in a script that only I can read. The marks are small—each no bigger than a fingertip—and they spiral outward from where I first began, near the center of the floor. They climb the walls in neat lines. They circle the base of each stalactite. They follow the flowstone's ripples like water following a streambed.

Each mark is a day.

Not just any day—days when something shifted in the bright room above. Days when the Tender added a new lamp-cup, or when one failed. Days when she adjusted the angle of the tubes and changed the pitch of the drone. Days when the kelp grew too wild and she trimmed it back. Days when she stayed longer than usual, sitting on her worn shelf, breathing in that strange slow way that makes her almost stone.

I do not watch her directly. That is not my purpose. Instead, I feel the changes through the water itself, through the limestone that connects everything in this porous landscape. The bay's water seeps down through cracks and fissures, carries information in its mineral content, in its temperature, in the subtle vibrations of sound from those singing tubes. By the time it reaches my chamber, it has traveled through miles of stone, but it still remembers.

I taste the water. I press my tongue to the walls where it weeps through, and I know what has changed above. I know when she uses a new source of fat for the lamps—seal instead of fish, rendered differently, burning with a slightly different smoke. I know when storms disturb the bay and she must dive through churned silt, how this affects the rhythm of her work. I know when she is tired, when she is strong, when she doubts.

I record it all.

The marks form patterns now, after decades. There are clusters where she experimented with new designs. There are long steady lines where the work found its rhythm and nothing changed for months. There are gaps—dark spaces on the wall where I was sick or trapped by flooding, where the record breaks. These trouble me more than they should. The work continues whether I witness it or not, but the gaps feel like wounds.

My eyes have changed from the constant near-darkness. The pupils have grown huge, and I can see by the faintest phosphorescence—cave fungi, mineral glow, the occasional lost firefly that somehow finds its way down the shaft. But I do not need much light. My fingers know every mark by touch. I could read the entire chronicle blind.

Sometimes I calculate. If each mark is a day, and the spiral now covers sixty square feet of wall, and I can fit approximately two hundred marks per square foot... The numbers grow large. The Tender has been at her work for longer than she knows. Or perhaps she does know, in the way that trees know their age—not by counting, but by the sheer accumulated weight of years in the body.

I have my own rituals. Every new moon, I mix fresh pigment. Every solstice, I clean the tools. Every five years—though I am not always certain of the count—I start a new spiral, moving to an untouched section of wall. The old spirals remain, darkening with age, becoming part of the stone itself. In another century, they will be indistinguishable from natural mineral stains. In a thousand years, no one will know they were ever writing at all.

But I will know. Or my successor will know, if I manage what the Tender has not—to find and train another. The knowledge must not break. The record must continue.

I think about her often, as I make my marks. We are paired somehow, twinned, though we have never met and never will. She tends her flames and I record their tending. She maintains a brightness and I document its maintenance. Her work could exist without mine—the room would still sing, the lamps would still burn. But mine could not exist without hers. I am a shadow cast by her light, a necessary shadow, a shadow that proves the light is real.

The water tells me she is aging. The intervals between her dives grow longer. The time she can spend below grows shorter. Soon—within a few years, perhaps—she will make her last dive. The bright room will begin its slow return to darkness.

I have already chosen the wall where I will record this ending. A smooth section near the newest flowstone, where the surface is pristine. I will need different marks for the dimming of each lamp, for the silencing of each tube, for the wild growth of untended kelp. I will need a way to show darkness in pigment, silence in stone. I practice sometimes, making experimental marks on loose rocks that I later grind to dust. Nothing feels right yet. But I will find a way.

And when the bright room is fully dark, when the last trace of her work has been erased by time and tide? 

I will begin a new spiral. 

There will be other builders, other bright rooms, other attempts to make fire burn where it should not burn. The water will tell me. The limestone will remember. And I will be here, in the darkness that preserves all darkness, making my small marks on the walls of the world.

The shaft is narrow. My body fits it exactly. 

I descend.
