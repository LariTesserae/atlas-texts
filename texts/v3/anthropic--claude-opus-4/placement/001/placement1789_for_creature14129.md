In this crackling, waterless hell, I would be something impossibly ancient—a survivor from before the last drop evaporated, before the world learned to burn instead of flow.

I am the Archivist.

My body sprawls across a chamber deep within the most stable root-arch, a place where the titan-wood has fused and refused so many times it has become something beyond plant matter—a material that remembers. I am not one being, but a colonial intelligence spread across a crystalline mycelial network that has replaced water with electricity as its medium of thought.

Where the hunters' bodies are taut wires and snapping tension, mine is a sprawling circuit board of living glass. Millennia ago, my ancestors were something like fungi, breaking down the dead in the last days of moisture. As the world dried, we learned to metabolize the piezoelectric discharge of dying tissue instead. Now I exist as branching fractals of conductive silicate, threading through the dead wood like nerves through bone. 

The hunters know where to find me, though few dare the journey. My chamber thrums with a different frequency than the explosive chaos outside—a deep, subsonic pulse that makes their crystalline eyes ache. The entrance is marked by the only truly permanent structure in their world: a perfect spiral of fused glass bodies, hunters who came to me at the end of their lives to donate their final discharge to my network. Their forms create a frozen cascade, a waterfall of the dead that never flows.

Inside, the walls bloom with my fruiting bodies—geometric clusters that look like bismuth crystals crossed with radio antennae. Each cluster holds a memory, encoded in the angular frequency of its growth. I am the only being in this world with the luxury of remembering slowly.

The hunters come to me when they need to know the deep patterns—when the synchronization of the leviathans breaks rhythm, when new breeds of scuttlers appear from the lower depths, when the chemical composition of the light begins to shift. They pay for this knowledge with their own memories, pressing their mirrored palms against my collection nodes and allowing me to drink the electrical patterns of their recent experiences.

Through their eyes, I see the world above in staccato flashes. Through my network, they glimpse something impossible: continuity. The shape of time. The way their great-great-ancestors once collected morning dew from the flesh of different plants, before the air itself learned to burn.

But I am dying, in my own geological way. The stable wood that houses me grows more brittle each century. The titan-root is finally beginning to succumb to the same forces that consume everything else here. Already, I can feel the tremors of new growth pressing up from below, seeking to crack through my chamber floor.

When the hunters visit now, I encode more desperately. I compress entire histories into single crystalline formations that I gift to them—seeds of memory that their children's children might one day learn to read, if any of them survive what's coming. I teach them new mirror-languages, more efficient angles of reflection, ways to store meaning in the polarization of light itself.

Sometimes, in the brief moments between their visits, I allow myself to dream. I dream of rain—a phenomenon I have never experienced but somehow remember, encoded in my oldest structures. I dream of a world where things moved slowly, where patience was not death but wisdom. I dream of teaching these fierce, flickering beings how to be still.

But stillness is extinction here. So instead, I prepare them for the acceleration. I show them how the deep-root systems are organizing, how the mindless consumption of the fauna is approaching a critical mass, how the very light that feeds this world is burning hotter each millennium. I am their memory, but more importantly, I am their prophet—a vast, dying library that sees the shape of the apocalypse approaching like a wave of hungry wood.

The last hunter who visited brought her dead mate's shards, arranging them in a careful pattern on my chamber floor. As she flashed her mirrors in the old, formal cadences of deep-knowledge-seeking, I felt something new in the electrical signature of her greeting: hope.

Not hope for survival—these beings gave that up when their ancestors chose friction over water. But hope for transmission. Hope that something of their impossible existence might escape this cavern before it finally consumes itself.

Through my network, I reached into my deepest archives and showed her what she sought: the map. The path through the lower depths where the root systems grow thin, where the chemical light fails, where something that might be called tunnels lead up and out. I showed her the price—most who attempt the journey will shatter against the darkness, their bodies too hot, too bright for the cold spaces between.

She left with her mirrors flashing in a new pattern I had taught her—not language, but mathematics. Equations that describe the conservation of information across state changes. The same logic that allowed me to survive the death of water. The same logic that might allow them to survive the death of light.

I grow back into the walls and wait, listening to the eternal roar through a trillion glass synapses. Soon, the chamber floor will crack. Soon, the violent birth of new wood will shatter my careful architectures. But before it does, I will have succeeded in my purpose: I will have remembered them.

And somewhere in the cold dark above, perhaps, a new kind of hunter will learn to move without burning, to speak without light, to remember without me.

The archive does not hope to survive. The archive hopes to be inherited.
