# The Membrane Tender

I am the one who keeps the doors thin.

This is not a job that was given to me. It is what my body became good at, the way some bodies become good at climbing and others at sorting floss. When I was small — smaller than small, barely walking — I would press myself into the partition-membranes where they stretch between the ribs. Not through them. Into them. The flesh of the plant here is different from the walls: translucent, faintly sticky, warm with the stalk's own circulation. It yields. When I pressed, it would dimple around me like a cheek accepting a thumb, and I would rest there, cradled, feeling the hydraulic pulse through my whole body.

The others found this unnerving. A child half-swallowed by the wall is not a usual sight, even here. But the membranes where I lingered stayed supple. They thinned at my touch — not weakening, but refining, the way skin refines where it is often stroked. After a time, the elders (we have elders, not leaders — those whose bones know more harmonics) noticed that the doorways I favored never needed re-cutting. The plant kept them open, or nearly open, season after season. The membrane would grow to a perfect drum-tautness and stop, holding there.

So I became what I was already becoming. The membrane tender. The one who visits every partition in the warren, pressing into each one just enough, just long enough, to convince the plant that this thinness is desired. I have a route. It takes four days to complete, if I do not sleep. I sleep in the membranes themselves now, wrapped in their warmth. They know me.

Or perhaps I know them. The distinction blurs. When I press into a membrane, I can feel the whole network of them throughout the chamber — a web of living drums all trembling at slightly different frequencies. The membranes near the looms vibrate constantly from the slamming. The ones near the hydraulic pipes sing high and thin. The ones in the quiet spaces (there are few quiet spaces) barely whisper. Through my skin I feel all of this at once: the entire warren as a single vibrating instrument, and myself the finger on its strings.

I do not wrap my face. I do not wrap my hands. The membranes need to know my skin, and I need to know theirs. This makes me strange to look at — a surface of exposed flesh moving through a world of wrapped bodies. My skin has taken on the membrane's own quality: faintly translucent, showing the blue-green veins clearly, and there is a sheen to it that others' skin does not have. I perspire constantly a thin sweet film that matches the membrane's own moisture. We have become chemically compatible, the doors and I.

The young ones fear me, a little. Not badly. But when I emerge from a membrane I have been tending — pushing backward out of it, the surface releasing me with a soft sound like lips parting — they press themselves against the far walls. I understand. I am wrong-looking. My joints bend a little too far, from all the pressing and yielding. My ribcage has narrowed to slip between the ribs of the walls. When I breathe, you can see my lungs fill through my skin, two green shadows expanding.

But I know things the others do not. The membranes remember. Not in the way people remember — no images, no stories — but in the way flesh remembers touch. Here, in this partition between the lower mills and the sorting floor: a hundred years of shoulders pushing through, always the left shoulder first, because the approach from below favors the left. The membrane is microscopically thicker on the right. Here, in the high passage near the ceremonial alcove: generations of children sneaking up to look at the moth-wing skeins, their small hands leaving traces of wonder and fear. The membrane tastes faintly different there. Sweeter.

And here, in the deepest partition, the one that separates our chamber from whatever lies below (none have passed it in living memory): this membrane is ancient. Original. It has the texture of leather and the warmth of fever and when I press into it I feel —

Nothing. A void. The membrane ends and there is space beyond it, vast space, space that has never known walls. My bones ache with it. The plant continues downward, I can feel that much — the hydraulic pulse comes from below as well as above — but down there it is hollow in a different way. Empty. Or not empty but... unclaimed. Unworked. Raw.

Sometimes I dream (I dream in the membranes, shallow Green dreams) that I will press too hard and fall through. Not through a doorway but through the membrane itself, my body finally becoming so compatible that I pass through like water through cloth. I would fall into that vast empty below-place and... what? Become it? Claim it? Die?

I always pull back. The membrane releases me with its wet sound and I continue my route, visiting each door in turn, keeping them thin, keeping them ready. Four days. Sometimes five, if I linger. The sixty of us pass through doorways I maintain, never thinking of the maintenance, never thinking of me except as the wrong-looking one who sleeps in walls.

But I think of them. Each body that passes through leaves its trace in the membrane, and I taste them all. The elder who favors her left hip, the weight uneven. The young one whose hands are already broader than his mother's, who will be great at the looms. The pair who meet in the third-tier passage when they think no others wake, their skulls pressed together in conversations I cannot hear but whose urgency I feel in the membrane's sympathetic trembling afterward.

I know the warren through its doors. I am its doors, in a sense — the soft parts, the yielding parts, the parts that must stay thin so the hard parts can connect. When the hydraulics shriek their quarter-tone chord, I feel it in every membrane at once, a symphony of surfaces. When the looms slam, the membranes ripple like water.

And I ripple with them. My heartbeat has slowed to match the hydraulic pulse. My breathing times itself to the turbine groan. I am becoming what I tend, or what I tend is becoming me, and someday — not soon, but someday — I will press into a membrane and not emerge. I will become a door myself, holding myself thin for the others to pass through. Still conscious, perhaps. Still tending. Just... differently shaped.

The thought does not frighten me. Nothing here frightens me anymore, except perhaps that vast below-space, that hollow unclaimed darkness where the stalk continues down to depths we cannot imagine.

But even that — even that calls to me, sometimes. When I am deep in the oldest membrane, feeling its fever-warmth, sensing that terrible openness beyond, I think:

*What needs tending down there?*  
*What doors are waiting to be kept thin?*  
*Who will press into those walls, becoming what they need?*

The membrane holds me. I hold myself. The warren continues above, its sixty souls wrapped in cloth they've made from themselves, and I continue my route — four days, five days, always — keeping the passages open, keeping the connections live, being the soft tissue this hard world requires.

The loom comes down. The work continues. And I continue with it, transparent at the edges, necessary and strange, the keeper of all thresholds in a place that has forgotten there was ever an outside to be thresholded from.

I am the membrane tender.  
I keep the doors thin.  
This is enough.

It has always been enough.
