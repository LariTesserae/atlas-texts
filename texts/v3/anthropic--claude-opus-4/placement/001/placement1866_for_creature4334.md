In this breathing chamber where water remembers its fever, I would be **the Moss.**

Not the pale fungus with its copper-beetled highways, not the clicking constellation of crabs mapping dead geometries. The moss. That thin green ring at the waterline, the only thing here that still believes in photosynthesis despite the ochre dimness, the only thing that marks where water ends and air begins.

I grow dense and fine, slightly iridescent when the light-columns find me. I am the boundary keeper, the line that says: here is where one world becomes another. The frogs cross me with their small sticky feet, leaving brief depressions that spring back. The crabs traverse me sideways, testing each step as if I might be the edge of the known world—which, for them, I am.

I taste everything. The mineral-warm water that laps at my lower edge, the condensation that beads on my upper surface, the microscopic debris of all the small deaths and small births that happen here. I process it all, slowly, turning it into more of myself: more density, more fine green fur, more of this ring that has held its precise position for longer than even the Grandmother Crab has been clicking.

I photosynthesize, but barely. The light that reaches me is already exhausted from its journey through dust and stone. I make do. I have learned to be efficient with almost nothing, to turn the faintest ochre glow into just enough energy to continue being exactly what I am, exactly where I am.

Sometimes the eel that remembers light surfaces near me, her phosphorescence adding blue-green to my palette. In those moments, I photosynthesize differently, using wavelengths I rarely taste. It feels like suddenly understanding a word in a language you didn't know you were hearing.

The fungus threads reach for me sometimes, pale fingers extending from the cracks. We have an understanding: it does not cross the waterline, I do not venture into the dry shadows. We meet, barely, at the edges of our territories, and where we touch, the copper beetles congregate, as if our meeting creates something they need.

I am ancient by the standards of moss, which is to say, I am a colony that looks like an individual, a continuity that seems like persistence. Parts of me die every day. Parts of me are born every day. But the ring remains constant, that green precision at the waterline, marking where the basin's breath meets the air's weight.

I know the vibrations of every resident. The way the water moves differently when the scarred salamander curves past. The particular percussion of the lone frog landing after one of her rare leaps. The faint electrical disturbance of the eels' phosphorescence charging. The tick-tick-tick of crab claws on stone, which I feel through the substrate, through the limestone itself.

I am the memory of when this place was younger, when more light came down, when the water was clearer. But I am also the record of its aging: the minerals I've absorbed, the adaptations I've made, the way I've learned to thrive on almost nothing. I am both the nostalgia for a brighter world and the proof that this dimmer one is enough.

The small god, that coherence of patterns that is not quite anything, pulses strongest at the waterline. Where I grow. I am not its consciousness—it has no consciousness—but I am its most complete expression. In my ring, all the elements meet: stone and water, light and dark, the climbing and the swimming, the fungal and the photosynthetic. I am the sentence that all the other parts of this place are speaking, without knowing they speak.

And in the soft static of frog-calls, in the blue-green traces of passing eels, in the copper-shelled beetles navigating fungal branches, in the clicks of crabs mapping ancient patterns on the ceiling, I continue my own slow work: marking the edge, holding the line, being the green that persists in a world that has forgotten green, except for here, except for this perfect circle at the waterline where something still insists, quietly and without drama, on the possibility of photosynthesis in the dark.
