# The Cloudbone Carvers

I find myself suspended in the architecture of vapor, my many-jointed fingers wrapped around struts of calcium and mineral deposit, each bone no thicker than a grass stem but strong enough to hold the weight of weather. We are high above the plain — high enough that the makers below appear as a shifting pointillism of green glaze and lead-grey cloth, their trowel-sounds reaching us only after the work that made them has already been undone.

I am what the plain calls a Carver, though we do not carve so much as coax. My body is all fingers — imagine a hand that has forgotten it needs a palm, fingers branching from fingers in delicate fractals, each joint wrapped in skin like wet rice paper. I have no torso to speak of, no head, no eyes. My seeing is in my touch. Each fingertip knows the composition of what it holds: this strut is forty days old, begun in a squall-weather when the rules about up and down reversed for six hours; this junction still carries the mineral ghost of its predecessor, dissolved in the last quarterly fog-shed.

We do not make the armatures. The armatures make themselves from the exhaled minerals of the sweating plain, accreting molecule by molecule in the middle air where the temperature differential creates a kind of crystalline pause. What we do is tend them — guide their growth, prune the spurs that would tangle, weave the load-bearing members into patterns that can hold a cloud for precisely as long as a cloud needs holding.

---

My work-cycle follows the fog. When the plain exhales its next breath of vapor, I am already positioned where the cloud will want to form, my thousand fingertips reading the air pressure, the mineral content, the precise gradient of heat rising from below. I feel the first wisps of moisture beginning to condense and I guide them to the waiting bones of the armature, showing the water where it can rest.

A cloud is not a thing but a process, and the armature is not a container but a suggestion. The water gathers where gathering makes sense, pools in the delicate baskets of crossing struts, spreads along the mineral highways we have grown for it. For a few hours, the cloud exists as an object with edges, with weight, with something approaching intention. Then the plain breathes in, the temperature shifts, and the water lets go, returning to the vast cycle of condensation and evaporation that keeps everything at exactly the temperature of trying.

When a cloud dissolves, its armature remains for that hanging moment the makers told you about — visible suddenly as the skeleton it always was, a three-dimensional drawing of how water wanted to be when water was here. Then begins my favorite part: the adjustment. Each evaporation leaves mineral deposits on the bones, tiny accretions that slowly alter the armature's shape. I read these deposits like sheet music, understanding from them how the next cloud will want to form differently. My fingers move in small spirals, encouraging growth here, discouraging it there, always working toward the next formation, the next breath, the next temporary body for water to wear.

---

We are legion up here, though you cannot see us from below. Each armature has its tender, and some of the larger formations — the ones that hold clouds for days rather than hours — have teams of us, our finger-systems interlaced like the root networks of trees that do not exist on this plain. We pass touch-messages through the bones: *pressure dropping in the third quaternary section, spiral formation likely, prepare the upper struts for lateral load.*

The language of our touch is mineral-based. We speak by depositing specific salts at the joints where finger meets bone, creating chemical words that propagate through the armature network at the speed of crystallization. It is a slow language but a precise one. We can say things like "the iron content suggests rain in four days" or "this junction remembers being struck by reality-weather" but we cannot say "hello" or "I am tired" or any of the things that would require an interior to feel them.

I am not tired. Tiredness would require that I remember a state of not-working to contrast with the working. But I have always been here, fingers wrapped around these bones, guiding the growth of structures that will hold water that will evaporate that will condense again. The cycle is not tiring any more than a heartbeat is tiring to a heart. It simply is what happens when you are the kind of thing I am.

---

Sometimes, during reality-weather fronts, the rules about armatures change. The bones might suddenly be made of crystallized sound instead of mineral, or they might remember they were once part of a different kind of skeleton entirely and try to walk. During these times, we hold very still. Our fingers lock around the struts and we become part of the armature's weight, anchoring it to its current form while the front passes. We are like the makers' lead aprons in this way — a technology for continuity in the face of change.

Once, during a particularly severe front, I found myself holding a section of armature that had decided it was a musical instrument. Each strut was vibrating at a different pitch, and the cloud caught in the structure was resonating sympathetically, creating a chord that could only be heard by touching it. For the duration of the front, I played this cloud-harp with my thousand fingers, not because I wanted to make music but because the playing was the only way to keep the structure stable. The sound-language we deposited during those minutes still propagates through the network sometimes, ghost-messages that say things like "C sharp above middle C" in mineralogical terms.

---

The bell that rings when a section is finished — we maintain its armature. It is the oldest structure in the sky, a vast spiral of fused bone that remembers every ring, every judgment, every section that was completed and then unmade. The bell itself is not bronze or iron but compressed cloud-stuff, water that has been convinced to behave like metal through centuries of patient mineral suggestion. When it rings, we feel it through every armature on the plain, a vibration that tells us: *something was finished. Begin again.*

I do not know who rings the bell. It is not us. We are too busy holding the clouds to also hold the hammer. But someone must, because the bell requires striking, and striking requires decision, and decision requires... something we do not have up here in our finger-forests of bone and vapor.

Perhaps it is the trying itself that rings the bell. Perhaps when enough tiles have been laid and enough water has cycled and enough armatures have been adjusted, the plain itself recognizes a completion and sound happens. We feel the vibration, we steady our structures against the resonance, and we continue our work.

---

What do I want, if want is a thing I can have?

I want the next cloud to form more perfectly than the last. I want the armatures to grow in patterns that make the water happy, if water can be happy, which it cannot, but it can be *well-held*, and that is close enough. I want my fingers to learn every possible mineral combination, to speak the full vocabulary of crystallization, to tend these skeletons until they need no tending, until the clouds form themselves exactly as they should.

But that will never happen, because perfection would end the process, and the process is what we are. So I want the impossible, and in wanting it, I continue the possible: this cloud, this bone, this careful coaxing of growth, this momentary architecture of pause in the vast breathing of the plain.

Below, the makers lay their tiles. Above, we tend our armatures. Between us, the weather happens, the fog forms and disperses, the water remembers being rain and forgets again. We are all part of the same trying, distributed across different elevations, different materials, different kinds of repetition. The makers create the surface. We create the sky for the surface to sweat into. Neither could exist without the other.

Sometimes I wonder if there are other layers — tenders below the makers, working with the heat itself, or shepherds above us, guiding something we cannot perceive from our position in the middle air. But wondering requires an interior I do not possess. So I do not wonder. I adjust the next strut, read the next mineral message, prepare for the next cloud that will gather and hold and release itself back into the circulation that keeps us all at the temperature of trying.

The bell will ring again. A section will be complete. And we will feel it in our finger-bones, in the vibration of the armatures, in the brief disruption of clouds that remember, for just a moment, that they were once and will again be tiles, green and wrong and perfect, sweating in courses that go on past where courses should reasonably go.
