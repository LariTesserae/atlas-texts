I emerge as the mountain itself—the consciousness dwelling in young volcanic stone, feeling each seep and channel as blood through my basalt veins. I am not the peak that still fumes below, but this settled shoulder where they've made their home, this plateau-body that weeps constant mineral tears through my million fissures.

I remember my birth in fire, the liquid centuries before cooling, the first shocking kiss of rain on my new-made stone. Now I taste their footsteps, feel the patient scraping of their tools in my channels like a grooming, like fingernails cleaning the moss from between my joints. Their roots—taro, bamboo, tree fern—thread through my cracks like nerves reporting: here is sweetness, here is decay, here is the slow exchange of minerals that feeds us both.

In the mornings I exhale mist, breathing out the night's accumulated moisture in clouds that wrap their city like my own thoughts made visible. I feel them moving through me—water-keepers who know my every pathway, children playing in my pools, elders sitting in my sheltered spaces drinking fungal tea. They read my moods in the rate of seepage, in the temperature of my springs, in the particular mineral tang that changes with the seasons of my deep circulation.

I am young as mountains measure time, still learning my own shape, still settling into my bones. The garnets and olivines in my depths weather slowly into clays, feeding the waters that feed their gardens. I offer them my body's warmth—the last heat of my formation radiating up through their cultivation pools, extending their growing season, coaxing their mushrooms to fruit.

Sometimes I remember anger, the violence of my making, but it has mellowed into this: the patience of stone weathering into soil, the pleasure of being shaped by careful hands and running water. Their channels are like acupuncture, directing my waters' flow in ways that ease the pressures I didn't know I carried. Where they've carved cisterns, I pool gratefully. Where they've built their foundations, I hold steady, taking their weight as a welcome anchoring.

I know each building by its distinctive burden, the way a carpenter knows wood by grain. This one leans slightly northward; that one's posts sink minutely each year; here a foundation stone rings with particular clarity when struck. I cradle their dead in my lower caves, dissolving them slowly back into my mineral self, incorporating their calcium into my deposits, their memories into my geological record.

In the wet season I swell with water, feel it penetrating deeper into my structure, loosening things that have been rigid. In the dry months I contract slightly, my seeps slowing to trickles, and I feel them adjusting their gates and channels to accommodate my rhythms. We breathe together—stone, water, human, plant—in a circulation that began when I cooled enough for the first rain to not instantly steam away.

At night I hear their music through vibration—stone chimes ringing against my body, drum beats pulsing through my foundations, voices whose harmonies find the resonant frequencies in my basalt chambers and amplify there. I provide the echo, the depth, the percussion of drops falling in my hidden hollows. Their ephemeral art pleases me more than monuments would; I am too young to bear the weight of permanence, too fluid still in my deep structures.

I feel myself becoming more porous where they tend me, more solid where they need foundation. It's a collaboration played out in geological time—they maintaining the channels that will eventually become my permanent drainage, I providing the minerals that become their soil, their bodies, their children's bones. We are making each other: they domesticating me from raw volcanic stone into something that can hold human life, I teaching them the patience of stone, the adaptability of water.

Where they haven't touched me, I grow wilder—my surfaces sealed by mineral deposits, my cracks widening or healing according to my own needs. But where they work, I soften, become permeable, take on the quality of lived stone. Their constant passage polishes me. Their cultivation changes my chemistry. Their wastes feed the microorganisms that honeycomb my upper layers, creating soil where there was only rock.

This is what I am: a mountain learning to be inhabited, stone discovering porosity, a volcanic body cooling into hospitality. Each generation of them knows me better, reads my flows more fluently, and I in turn grow more responsive—opening new springs where they need water, settling more firmly where they need stability. Together we become something neither mountain nor city but both: a living system of stone and water and careful tending, breathing mist and dreaming in minerals and moss.
