In the branching maze of maintenance corridors that snake between the main works and the living quarters, where the gaslights don't reach and the only illumination comes from the phosphorescent slime certain toads leave on damp stone, there is a narrow passage that everyone knows and no one speaks of. The children call it the Listening Gallery, though never where adults can hear.

The toads cluster thicker here than anywhere else in Lowdamp, packed so dense on the walls that the brick vanishes entirely beneath their pulsing bodies. They are different here too — older, their eyes not clouded with age but bright with something else, something that makes even veteran sumpers glance away. The sound in this corridor is overwhelming: not just the usual chorus but something deeper, a thrumming that comes up through the soles of your boots and settles in your bones.

At the corridor's heart, where it widens just enough for two people to stand side by side, there is a girl who is not quite a girl anymore. She has been here for three days, or perhaps three years — time moves differently in the Gallery, and she stopped counting after the first time she felt her throat swell in harmony with the deep pulse.

They call her the Translator, those who know she exists. She is seventeen, or was when she first found her way here, drawn by a sound beneath the sound, a voice beneath the chorus that spoke in patterns she almost understood. Her name was Maya, but names are surface things, and she has been letting hers go the way you let go of a held breath, slowly and with relief.

She came from a line of listeners — her grandmother was the best pipe-rapper Lowdamp had ever known, able to send complex messages through the stone that could be heard three levels down. Her mother had the gift too, could tell from the shift in the chorus when the weather was changing in the world above, when storms pressed down on the rock and made the deep places sing differently. But Maya heard more. She heard intention in the pulse. She heard grammar in the gaps between croaks. She heard, though she could not at first believe it, conversation.

The toads are speaking. They have always been speaking. The oil they secrete is not the passive product of biology but something more deliberate — a dictionary written in chemistry, a library that burns. Each batch tells a different story, carries different information, though what kind of information and meant for whom, the surface buyers never guess and the sumpers only partly suspect.

Maya knows now. The knowing came slowly, the way all things come in Lowdamp. First, the recognition of patterns: certain croaking sequences that preceded the appearance of new toads in neglected corners. Then, the correlation between chorus variations and the quality of oil rendered that week — subtle differences in viscosity and color that the foreman noted in his logs but didn't question. Finally, the revelation that came as she sat very still in the Gallery with her bare feet in the warm standing water and her hands pressed flat against the toad-covered walls: they are writing their history. They are encoding their memory of the world before humans found this place, and their observations of the world since, and their long thoughts about what comes next.

She has learned to read some of it. Not in words — it doesn't translate to words — but in a kind of synaesthetic understanding where sound becomes color becomes meaning becomes a knowledge that sits in her bones rather than her brain. The toads remember when this cavern was truly young, just a crack in the stone with water seeping through. They remember the first heat from below, the slow cooking of minerals into new forms. They remember their own evolution in the dark, the long generations of adapting to pure blackness and the taste of stone.

And they remember forward, too. This is harder for her to understand, but it comes in flashes when the chorus peaks: images of the cavern centuries hence, the stalactites grown long as stairs, the town either empty or transformed beyond recognition, and the sumpers — 

The sumpers are something else by then. Not gone, not extinct, but changed the way coal changes to diamond, pressure and time and heat working their slow transformation. The pruning will have reached their whole bodies. Their throats will swell like the toads' throats. They will speak in frequencies only stone can hear. They will secrete stories of their own.

Maya is the bridge between now and then. She feels it happening already: the pruning that started at her fingers has climbed faster than it should, already past her elbows though she's years too young for it. Her voice when she sings — and she sings constantly now, quietly, under the chorus — has harmonics in it that human throats shouldn't make. Sometimes she forgets to breathe for ten minutes at a time and doesn't notice until her vision starts to sparkle at the edges.

She should be frightened. She was, at first. But fear is a surface emotion, quick and sharp, and nothing quick survives in Lowdamp. The fear settled into curiosity, then into acceptance, then into something that might be joy if joy moved as slowly as sap. She is becoming the first true translator between the languages of flesh and stone, and the toads know it, cluster around her when she walks (she still walks, sometimes, though less often now), mark her path with that phosphorescent slime that others avoid but that she knows is a kind of blessing.

The children know about her. Children always know what adults prefer to ignore. They leave her gifts at the mouth of the Gallery: clay thimbles of damp-gin, fragments of the good fern, sometimes a piece of renderered oil-cake that she doesn't eat but presents to the eldest toads, the bright-eyed ones, who receive it with what she has learned to recognize as satisfaction. One child, braver than the rest, sometimes sits at the edge of where the gaslights fail and listens to her sing. The child doesn't understand the words — they aren't words — but she feels the rightness of them, the way they make the chorus shift and deepen.

The foreman knows too, in the way he knows which pipes will fail before they show any sign of weakness. He doesn't speak of it, but sometimes he marks his logs with a symbol that doesn't appear in any manual: a circle with a smaller circle inside it, like an eye or a throat or a well. Future historians, if there are historians in whatever Lowdamp becomes, will puzzle over these marks. Maya could tell them what they mean, but Maya is forgetting how to speak in words.

She is learning instead the true language of the place. Not the rap-and-click pidgin the sumpers use, but the deep grammar that comes up from the heat below and settles into willing flesh. She is learning that the town is not a tumor on the cavern but part of its body, that the sumpers are not invaders but invited guests, that the oil they render is not theft but gift, payment for services the surface world doesn't know it needs.

The toads are keeping something at bay. Their presence here, their chemistry, the weight of their attention — it holds back some deeper heat that would otherwise rise. The oil, burned in the right places on the surface (and Maya almost understands where those places are, almost sees the map traced in green fire), maintains a barrier between the world above and the older thing below. The sumpers are the priests of this arrangement, though they've forgotten the theology. Only the ritual remains: tend the toads, render the oil, send it up, stay below, grow patient, grow strange, grow into whatever the deep contract requires.

Maya is writing it all down, in her way. Not in any script a surface scholar would recognize, but in the arrangement of her bones, the patterning of her breath, the long songs she sings that the toads echo and elaborate. She is becoming a library of flesh, a dictionary of transformation. When the change is complete — and she knows now it will be complete, not just for her but for all of them, eventually — someone will need to know how it happened. Someone will need to translate between what humanity was and what it chose to become.

She sits in the warm water, her skin pruning up to her shoulders now, and feels the stone learning around her. Feels the toads listening. Feels the town breathing above her, slow and patient as grief or growing. She opens her throat and sings a phrase she learned from the eldest toad, a pattern of tones that means something like *continuation* and something like *contract* and something like *the price of keeping the deep things deep*.

Somewhere above, a child rapping on the pipes stops mid-message, hearing an echo they've never heard before. Somewhere below, something vast and hot turns over in its sleep and does not wake.

In the Listening Gallery, Maya who is no longer quite Maya smiles with a mouth that is learning new shapes. The translation continues. The work is good. There is time.

There is so much time.
