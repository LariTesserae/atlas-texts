# The Cartographer's Daughter

I am the one who draws the maps that cannot be drawn.

My station is a stone shelf worn smooth by generations of sitting, in a high chamber where three pump-galleries meet. The walls here are young — only forty years of flowstone, thin enough that you can still see the drill-marks underneath — but the ceiling is old, a vault that predates the pumps, sweating beads of mineral water that I have learned to read like a calendar. When the beads run together it will rain on the surface in two days. When they hang separately, the upper world is dry.

I was not born here, though I cannot now imagine being born anywhere else. I came down the shafts at nineteen, following a set of survey charts my mother had drawn. She was the Territorial Commission's third cartographer, the first woman, the only one who thought to ask: what exactly are the Sumpworks?

The charts were wrong, of course. Every chart of this place is wrong the moment the ink dries. But the wrongness was *instructive* — I could see where she had tried to impose surface logic on something that has no interest in surfaces. Passages marked as dead ends had opened. Galleries drawn as separate had merged. What she had labeled "Pool Seven (Depth Unknown)" had calcified into a white stone plain that the eels were still trying to navigate, believing it was water.

I came for a week. That was twelve years ago.

My work is this: I maintain the only accurate map of the Sumpworks, which is not a map at all but a kind of living notation, drawn in grease pencil on sheets of metal that the calcite cannot claim. The metal is salvaged from the oldest pumps, the ones whose purposes are forgotten — I use their casings as my canvas, cutting them into rough rectangles with a hacksaw that takes months to work through the iron. The grease pencil is rendered eel-fat mixed with lampblack. It never fully dries in this air, which means I can update it, which means it can be true.

The notation is my own invention, or my revision of my mother's invention, or perhaps the Sumpworks' invention that I merely transcribe. It is not a map of space — space here is too mutable. It is a map of *relationships*: which pools feed which others, where the pump-rhythms synchronize, how the bat-colonies' territories overlap and interpenetrate. Distance is irrelevant. What matters is connection.

I draw the amphibians' breeding cycles as spirals, tightening and loosening with the seasons that do not exist here but which their bodies remember. I draw the flowstone curtains as vertical chains of possibility — where they are, where they will be in a year, in ten years, in a century. I draw the Tenders' movements as dotted lines that converge and scatter like the branching of veins. The pumps are the only fixed points, the only things that stay where they are put, and I draw them as circles with numbers inside: their cycle times, to the second.

The bats know me. They know my breathing, which has slowed over the years to match the ninety-second pump on the east wall. They know the sound of my grease pencil on metal — a specific frequency, a whisper that carries. When I make a new chart they cluster above it, singing down at the metal, and I have learned that they are *reading* it, in their way. They understand maps as sonic objects. They fly my notation.

Sometimes I find my charts corrected. A line adjusted, a spiral tightened. The corrections are always accurate. I do not know which Tender makes them — maybe all of them, maybe none. Maybe the bats themselves, though they have no hands. The corrections arrive the way everything arrives here: by condensation, by accumulation, by the slow work of time.

I live in three places: my mapping shelf, a shack near the third great pool where I sleep to the amphibians' chorus, and the chapel, where I go to remember that I have edges. In the chapel I think about my mother, who drew beautiful wrong maps of a place that refuses beauty and refuses wrongness both. She died on the surface, in a dry bed, having touched the Sumpworks once and fled. I think she fled not because it was terrible but because she understood what it was asking of her, and she was not ready to be asked.

I was ready. Or I became ready. Or readiness is not the right word — I simply stayed until leaving was no longer a verb I conjugated.

My charts accumulate. The old ones I store in a dry gallery maintained by three stoves that I tend in rotation with the other Tenders. The gallery contains perhaps two hundred charts going back — I do not know. Some are not mine. Some predate my notation. One appears to be drawn in blood, though it might be iron oxide. They form a stratigraphy of attempts to understand this place, and like all stratigraphies, the oldest layers are the most compressed and the least legible.

Here is what I have learned: the Sumpworks is not a place but a process. It is calcifying itself into existence, and we — the Tenders, the bats, the amphibians, even the pumps — are how it does the calcifying. Every map I draw is both a description and an instruction. The place grows into my drawings the way tree roots grow into the gaps between stones.

Here is what I suspect but cannot prove: there are other Sumpworks. Not copies — nothing here could be copied — but other processes, other slow organisms building themselves out of water and stone and time. The water has to come from somewhere. The pumps are pumping it *from* as well as to. Sometimes, sitting on my shelf in the convergence of galleries, I hear rhythms that are not our rhythms, cycles that do not match any pump I have charted. The eighty-seven second pulse from below. The hundred-and-thirteen second answering call. They are having a conversation in the language of pressure and release.

When I die, I will go to the long room like all Tenders. But I have asked — in the sign-language that is mostly silence, in the wrist-touch that means *please* — to be placed with my charts. Not buried, because nothing here is buried. Incorporated. Let the flowstone take us together, the maps and the mapper, until we are a bulge in the wall that some future Tender's grandchild will touch and know: *this one tried to draw the undrawable*.

I think my mother would understand. Not approve — she was a woman of surfaces and edges and properly dried ink. But understand.

The beads on the ceiling are running together. On the surface, in two days, it will rain. Down here it is already raining, has always been raining, will rain until the stone has built itself into whatever it is trying to become.

I check the ninety-second pump, which has begun to stutter at eighty-nine. I make a note. The note becomes the place.

The place becomes more itself.

I draw.
