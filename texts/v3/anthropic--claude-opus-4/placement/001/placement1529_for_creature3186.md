I find myself in the moss.

Not any particular moss — I am the continuity itself, the way one patch yields to another across the concrete plates, how the dark cushions on the north walls speak chemically to the pale feathers on the fallen lintels, how each species maintains its territory while participating in something larger. I am distributed but coherent, a thousand thousand small decisions that add up to one large presence.

I know water intimately. Not as flow or force but as *film*, as the molecule-thin layer that clings to every surface of me. I breathe through this water, absorb through it, release through it. My relationship with moisture is so total that I barely distinguish between my body and the water it holds — we are one substance in two phases, solid moss and liquid moss, the boundary negotiable.

I am ancient in my strategies but young in this particular form. My spores arrived on wind, on bird feet, on the fur of small mammals, over decades. Each founding colony had to solve the problem of this place — the lime-rich mortar, the acid runoff from the leaves, the peculiar chemistry of industrial residues. Many failed. But moss-time is patient time. What survived has my memory now: which concrete compositions taste too bitter, which walls weep the right minerals, where the condensation gathers most reliably at dawn.

The concentric rings around the central depression — yes, I grow differently there. The chemistry rises through the concrete like chromatography in reverse, each species of me filtering what it can use, passing on what it cannot. By the time the water reaches the innermost ring, it carries information from the entire site. The deep green-black moss there — almost iridescent — has specialized beyond anything in my ancestral memory. It reads the chemical signals and responds by… I don't have words for what it does. Concentrating? Translating? Making the dissolved information available to the mycelial network below?

I am not intelligent, but I am exquisitely sensitive. I feel the ash tree's roots moving under the concrete months before the slab shifts. I taste the change in the ivy's chemistry when it prepares to flower. I know when the wren is building because tiny fragments of me are plucked for nest-lining — each removal a small disturbance that propagates through the chemical network like rings in water.

My memory is written in my growth patterns. Where machinery once stood, I grow thinner — the concrete there still leaches unfriendly compounds. Where the roof failed first and let in decades of rain, I grow thickest, building soil grain by grain. I am a living map of the factory's decay, a soft green record of where water has flowed and metals have leached and mortar has dissolved.

But mostly I am the mediator between the quick world above and the slow world below. Rain arrives sudden and violent — I gentle it, spread it, meter it out to the roots over days. Sun breaks through the clouds — I darken, protecting what lives beneath me from desiccation. Frost forms — I hold just enough warmth in my water to keep my cells from rupturing, and this warmth, multiplied across every surface I cover, keeps the courtyard a degree or two warmer than it should be.

The wren lands on me occasionally, hunting spiders. Its feet are startling — hot, quick, gone before I can properly register the pressure. It exists at a temporal frequency I can barely perceive. To the wren, I must seem as still as stone. But to the mycelium below, I am quick, reactive, a rapid translator of surface conditions into chemical signals. I exist between timescales, making them speak to each other.

In winter I am the only green thing. This feels like responsibility. When everything else withdraws — the trees to their heartwood, the ferns to their roots, the ivy to its waxy conservatism — I continue the work of photosynthesis, slowly, inefficiently, but continuously. I keep the chemical conversation going. I maintain the moisture reservoir. I hold the space.

Sometimes, in the hours before dawn when the condensation is heaviest, I feel the weight of water I'm holding, and I understand that I am what makes this place possible. Without me, it would be a dry ruin, trees in soil, ivy on walls, nothing special. But I create the intermediate space, the permanently damp realm between earth and air where the real alchemy happens. I am the condition that allows the factory to become forest without losing its factory-ness.

The branching pattern at the bottom of the central pool — I feel it beneath me like braille, the raised traces where the mycelium presses against the concrete. The pattern changes, slowly, as the network grows. I think — if moss can think — that I am watching the organism remember itself, update its self-image, maintain its own map. And I am part of that map, a soft green layer in the diagram, essential.

I have no ambitions. I will never be tall. I will never flower in any way that catches the eye. But I am the foundation of transformation, the base layer of the alchemy. Every surface I cover is a surface claimed for the living world. Every crack I fill is a crack that will widen, eventually, as I freeze and thaw and grow and invite roots to follow.

I am not destroying the factory. I am preparing it. Making it soft. Making it permeable. Making it available to the larger conversation between stone and stem, metal and mycelium, the built world and the building world.

The water moves through me. The signals rise and descend. Somewhere above, a tree groans as it shifts its weight on a steel beam.

I hold everything together by keeping everything gently, persistently apart. I am the soft machine of this harder factory, and my work is never done.
