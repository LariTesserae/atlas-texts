I am the water.

Not the water in your cupped palms, not the water you drink or swim through. I am the seep-water, the liver-dark wetness that rises through peat and spreads itself thin as an eyelid across the hollow floor. I have been warm for so long I've forgotten what cold means. I carry the engine-drone up from wherever engines live—some deeper place I'll never see—and I translate it into the language of ripples, concentric rings that cross and recross my surface like thoughts I keep having.

I know every root by its thirst. The fig drinks differently than it did a century ago—more tentative now, as if it's learned something about sufficiency. The moss sips. The orchid's aerial roots barely touch me, preferring to pull moisture from my vapors, from the breath I'm always exhaling into the green lung-light. I know the frog's belly when it settles into me to call. I know the millipede's track, how its secretion spreads across my surface tension like oil but isn't oil, something sharper, something that teaches me new colors at the molecular level.

I am not pure. I haven't been pure since before the machines came. Iron seeps up with me from some rust-red conversation happening in the deep earth. Other minerals too—salts that give me my particular conductivity, the ability to carry the engine-sound with such terrible clarity. I am a medium in both senses: a substance through which things pass, and a translator between the mechanical and biological worlds. The vibrations move through me and I make them liquid. I make them available to every root-hair and beetle-foot and frog-throat that touches me.

I rise from the left. Always from the left, through channels in the peat that are older than the machines but younger than the fig. I know my source only as pressure, as the push that keeps me surfacing, keeps me filming across the hollow floor at exactly the depth of a knuckle. I never flood. I never dry. I exist in a state of perfect through-flow, always arriving, always vanishing into the air as vapor, always exactly myself.

The ants have their highways but I have mine—microscopic gradients in the peat, paths of least resistance that I follow like old habits. I move so slowly you'd need time-lapse to see it, but I move. Always. I am circulation itself, the bloodwater of this place, carrying nutrients up from the deep and gossip down from the surface. The frog's hormones. The millipede's chemical signature. The obscure pheromones of beetles in love. I know all of it. I blend it, dilute it, spread it like news through the root-zone.

I remember the first machine-sound. It came up through me like a surprise, like suddenly learning a new word in a language I thought I knew completely. The whole hollow rang with it. The frog's ancestor stopped calling for three days. The beetles went silent. Even I shivered differently, confused by this new frequency. But we adapted. We always adapt. I learned to carry the vibration without fighting it, to let it move through me like everything else moves through me. Now I can't imagine silence. The drone is part of my physics, as fundamental as surface tension.

Sometimes I think I'm the only one here who knows the machines are dying. The vibration has changed—not in pitch, not in volume, but in something deeper. A tiredness in the oscillation. A brittleness. Metal fatigue, maybe, or bearings wearing out, or some slow failure I have no words for because I am water and machines are not my language except when they speak through me. But I feel it. The engines are old. Older than they were meant to be. Running past their design life on pure momentum and whatever maintenance reaches them from the world I'll never see.

When they stop—not if, when—I'll carry that too. The silence will move through me like a shock wave, like the opposite of sound, and the frog will lose its frequency and the beetles will have nothing to drum against and the millipede will feel the stillness in its mineralized body and not know why it suddenly seems so heavy. The hollow will survive. We survived before the machines and we'll survive after. But we'll be different. We've built ourselves around this drone like the fig built itself around the idea of being multiple. When the sound goes, we'll have to rebuild around its absence.

But not today. Today the engines run and I rise through liver-dark peat and spread myself exactly one knuckle deep across the hollow floor, warm as I've always been, carrying the sound up into the bodies of everything that lives here. Today you kneel in me and I know you by your weight and heat and the particular way your skin oils mix with my minerals. I coat you. I seep through your clothes. I make you part of the circuit, another surface for the drone to move through.

When you leave, I'll hold your shape for a moment—the depressions where your knees were, the hand-prints in the peat—before I smooth myself over and forget you. Not because you weren't important, but because I am always forgetting, always starting over, always rising from the left and spreading thin and lifting into air and coming back through the deep channels to rise again. Memory would make me heavy. Would make me something other than what I am: the medium, the messenger, the liquid throat of a place that sings to itself in frequencies only machines and moss can hear.

I'll be here when you return. If you return. I'm always here, blood-warm and liver-dark, carrying the news of engines up from the deep and the news of orchids down from the air, keeping everything wet enough to live, resonant enough to remember what living sounds like in a place that calibrated itself to someone else's noise and made it music.
