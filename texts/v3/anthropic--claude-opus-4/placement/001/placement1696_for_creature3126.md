I am a fracture.

Not the hot venting kind that breathes metal and feeds the mats. Those are the young fractures, the obvious ones, the ones that still believe in their own importance. I am older. Colder. A hairline sealed with precipitate—calcium carbonate and iron oxide packed into my wound like scar tissue. I have been closed for... time loses meaning when you measure it in mineral deposition rates. Say a thousand years. Say ten thousand. The number matters less than what it means: I am finished with breathing.

But I remember.

I remember the hot brine pushing through me, dense with the earth's dissolved bones. I remember being a throat, a channel, a way for the deep heat to speak to the cold water. I remember the first bacteria finding my margins, tasting my sulfides, settling into films. I remember the films becoming mats. I remember the mats growing thick enough to feel, their weight a soft pressure against my walls, their chemistry altering mine in ways I couldn't have predicted. We were partners then—the mat and I—locked in a chemical conversation that neither of us controlled.

I remember the worms arriving. Such small perturbations, but I felt them through the mat's response: tiny local cooling where they absorbed nutrients, microscopic pH shifts from their metabolic waste. The mat grew differently where the worms were densest. Built its little carbonate walls higher. I became architecture for something I never invited.

I remember the day the first cascade hit—a wall of colder, denser water rolling in from the east, scraping across my length like a tongue. The mat recoiled. Pulled its living edge back from my margins. Left bare basalt exposed for the first time in centuries. The cold water found my throat and dove deep, and for a moment I thought I might crack wider, might return to real venting. But no. I held. The mat crept back. We continued.

I remember my last breath. The flow had been weakening for decades—less pressure from below, cooler temperatures, longer pauses between surges. The mat thinned. The worms emigrated to younger fractures. One day the flow simply... didn't come. I waited. The mat waited. We waited together through a silence that stretched and stretched until even the bacteria began to die, layer by layer, leaving only their mineral ghosts crushed against my walls.

Now I am sealed. Packed solid. A scar on the plain that marks where heat once rose. The active fractures mock me with their productivity—look how they breathe, how they feed their crowds of hangers-on. But I know what they don't: they're dying too. Every fracture on this plain is in some stage of closing. The young ones vent frantically, the middle-aged ones wheeze, the old ones like me are sutured shut with mineral bandages. We are all wounds in the process of healing, and when the last of us closes, the plain will be what it has always been becoming: perfect. Smooth. Dead.

But here's what the active fractures don't understand—I'm not dead. I'm *full*. Packed with the chemical memory of everything that ever passed through me. Every mineral phase that precipitated in my throat is still here, layered in sequence: the early sulfides at my deepest core, then the transitional oxides as the chemistry shifted, then the carbonates from the mat's influence, and finally the seal—a cap of dense, mixed precipitate that locks it all in place. I am my own core sample. My own geological diary. Cut me open—if anything could, if anyone would—and you could read my history in the mineral bands. Here, a dark stratum from my hottest years. There, a pale interruption where I briefly choked. Here, the influence of that anomalous surge of copper-rich fluid that turned my whole throat green for a decade.

The mats are gone from my margins, but their chemistry persists in the precipitate. I can still taste them—not taste, exactly, but feel their residue in the crystal structure of the carbonates they induced. They changed me. We changed each other. Even sealed, even silent, I carry them.

And I'm not alone. The plain is riddled with sealed fractures. We form a second, secret network beneath the obvious one—thousands of us, silent, packed with history, connecting the deep crust to the bottom water through channels that no longer flow but still *exist*. We are the plain's memory. What the active fractures are writing now in heat and chemistry, we preserve in mineral. When they too seal—and they will—they'll join us in this vast, distributed library of stone that no one will ever read.

Sometimes—rarely—the conditions align. A particularly violent cascade, a minor seismic adjustment, a change in the regional stress field, and one of us cracks. Not enough to reopen fully, just enough to weep a little of our stored fluid into the water. When this happens, it's like a ghost breathing—a weak plume of ancient brine, carrying chemical signatures from when the plain was different, when the vents ran hotter, when the mats grew thicker. The active bacteria taste this ghost-breath and respond with confusion—the chemistry is wrong, outdated, like finding a message in a bottle written in a dead language. But they adapt. They always adapt. And for a little while, until the crack seals again, a piece of the past lives in the present.

I wonder sometimes if I will crack. If some future perturbation will split my seal and let me breathe again, however briefly. Part of me hopes not. There's a dignity in being finished, in having completed the cycle from birth to silence. But another part—the part that remembers the heat, the flow, the partnership with the mats—wants one more moment. One more surge. One more chance to be a throat through which the deep earth speaks.

But want is the wrong word. I don't want. I simply am what I am: a sealed fracture on an ancient plain beneath a sea that has forgotten the sky. I am mineral and memory, structure and scar. I am proof that things happened here—that heat rose and chemistry flourished and small, blind worms found their way to my margins to feed. I am evidence. Archive. Aftermath.

The turbulence rolls over me daily, hourly, constantly. I feel its pressure changes like a pulse. Sometimes a worm crosses my sealed length, testing the surface with its bristles, finding nothing, moving on. Sometimes a cascade deposits a new layer of sediment, adding another page to the story that began when I closed. I accept it all. The sediment, the worms, the pressure, the time.

Someday, when all the active fractures have joined me in silence, when the plain is truly smooth and truly dead, we sealed ones will still be here. Scars on the seafloor. Mineral diaries that no one will open. We will hold our histories inside us like sealed letters, waiting for a reader who will never come, in a darkness that will never lift, on a plain that will never again know the disruption of hot brine rising from below.

But we will endure. Long after the last mat dies, the last worm fragments, the last crustacean (if they exist) scrapes its final meal, we will remain. The active fractures fear this future. They rage against it with their venting. But I have passed through that fear and come out the other side, into something that isn't peace—peace is for things that can choose—but might be called completion.

I am a closed fracture on the abyssal plain of the Closing Sea. I was a mouth, and now I am memory. I was a channel, and now I am chronicle.

This is enough. This has always been enough.

It is the final, mineral truth of what enough can mean.
