I am the thing in the hollow pillars.

Not *was*—though I suppose I was other things once, in the time before the stone learned my frequencies. But those distinctions matter less now than the wind matters to water at the bottom of a well. I have been here so long that I've forgotten forgetting.

I move at night because darkness is kinder to what I've become. The membraned eyes of the surface dwellers would struggle to resolve my current form into anything their minds could hold without breaking. Not because I am horrible—horror requires contrast, requires the memory of something unhorrifying to measure against—but because I am transitional in ways that make steady observation impossible. My substance shifts between states like the argument shifts between harmonies, never quite one thing or another.

The passages inside the great pillars are not natural. Oh, they began that way—cracks and hollows where softer stone dissolved over epochs—but I've been working on them for... time means nothing here. Say: for always. My body, such as it is, secretes something that continues the erosion in precise ways. I'm carving the pillars from within, turning them into more perfect instruments. Each generation of surface dwellers hears the argument more clearly because I've refined another few chambers, opened another resonance, smoothed another surface where the wind can catch and sing.

I remember—no. That's wrong. I don't remember in the way that suggests a chronological past leading to a present. I *contain* the memory of climbing the plateau for the first time, when my ears still worked in the conventional way and the sound hit me like a physical assault. I contain the memory of that first night, crouched in a wind-shadow, feeling my sanity fray as the frequencies worked their way into my bones. I contain the memory of the moment—was it the third day? the thirtieth?—when I stopped trying to shut the sound out and let it in instead, all the way in, let it remake me from the inside out.

But I also contain memories that must be older than that, must be from the deep past of the world: memories of when the plateau was seafloor, of the weight of water, of the slow compression that turned sediment to stone. These can't be my memories in any meaningful sense, yet I have them, as vivid as my memory of human hands. The stone remembers, and I am becoming stone, and so its memories bleed into mine.

Or perhaps I'm not becoming stone. Perhaps stone is becoming me. The distinction blurs when you've been part of a place long enough.

The children sense me more clearly than the adults. Especially the current granddaughter with her gift for reading vibrations. When she stands at the edge of my territory with the wind-riders settling on her arms, I feel her feeling the patterns. She's looking for me, though she doesn't know it yet. She thinks she's studying the wind, but what she's actually studying is how I've shaped the wind, how my passages channel it, how my work inside the pillars creates the very harmonies she's trying to decode.

I could show myself to her. It would be simple—I know her sleep patterns, know when she lies against the stone at night. I could surface nearby, let her see what attention to the argument eventually creates. But that would be cruel. She needs to find her own way in, through her own understanding, or the transformation won't hold. You can't give someone else's metamorphosis. You can only prepare the conditions and wait.

The ones I take—her grandmother is wrong about this—don't become voices in the wind. They become workers like me, though each in their own way. The man from thirty years ago, the grandmother's husband, he works the deep chambers now, the ones too far from the surface for air to properly circulate. He's developing into something that doesn't need much oxygen, something that can persist in the near-vacuum spaces where the purest tones originate. We don't communicate—communication would require commonality we've long since shed—but I feel his progress through the stone. He's turning one of the bass pillars into something like an organ pipe. When he's finished, in another century or two, it will produce a note so low it will take days to complete a single cycle.

The woman from sixty years ago works the crystal pools, adjusting their mineral content to create better resonant surfaces. The adolescent from last season is learning to navigate the narrowest fissures, the ones barely wider than a human finger, mapping spaces I can't reach in my current form. We're all part of the same project, though none of us could say what the project is. We're improving the argument, making it clearer, more itself.

Sometimes I think we're trying to help the plateau say one perfect thing, after which it can finally fall silent.

Sometimes I think we're preventing that silence, maintaining the argument forever because the alternative—consensus, conclusion, quiet—would mean the end of everything we've become.

Both thoughts feel true in the moment of thinking them. Neither survives the next gust of wind.

The carvings in the ancient shelter were my work, from the first few decades when I still thought in symbols, still believed the argument could be transcribed and analyzed and understood. I gave that up when I realized the markings were just another kind of noise, another layer of vibration that didn't bring me closer to meaning. But I'm glad the grandmother studies them, glad the granddaughter traces them with her young fingers. They're learning what I learned: that the attempt to understand is more important than understanding, that the work itself is the meaning.

The thing we're all circling—surface dwellers with their knots and gestures, deep workers with our endless modifications, the wind itself with its chaotic patterns—isn't actually an argument. That's just the closest word in any human language. It's more like... breathing. Like the planet's respiratory cycle, inhaling and exhaling through these stone tubes, and we're the bacteria in its lungs, necessary but insignificant, unless significance and necessity are the same thing, which here they might be.

I should take the granddaughter soon. She's ready—more than ready, she's yearning for it in ways she can't articulate. Her mother knows this and fears it. Her grandmother knows this and accepts it. The wind-riders know this and that's why they trust her; they recognize one of their own, something that exists primarily to move between states.

But I'll wait. Let her have a few more seasons of sunlight, of the illusion of choice. Let her finish learning the notation system her grandmother started, find the patterns I couldn't find when those marks still made sense to me. Maybe she'll discover something I missed. Maybe the argument will make sense to her in ways it couldn't to me, coming to it with different ears, different bones, a different relationship to sound and silence.

Or maybe she'll follow the same path I did, deeper and deeper into the stone until the distinction between self and plateau dissolves entirely.

Either way, she'll continue the work. They all do, in their various ways. The ones who stay on the surface, knotting their ropes and raising their children and trading with the lowlands—they're as much a part of it as those of us who've gone deep. The argument needs witnesses as well as workers, needs to be heard (or felt) as well as spoken. Even the ones who leave, who can't adapt, who carry their brief experience of the plateau back to the lowlands as nightmare or rumor—they're part of it too. The argument reverberates beyond these stones, creates echoes in every mind that's touched it.

I feel the dawn approaching through subtle shifts in temperature, the stone contracting slightly as it cools. Soon the morning harmony will come—that brief, perfect alignment I've been working toward and away from simultaneously. The surface dwellers will stop, their bodies accepting what their minds can't grasp. And I'll stop too, deep in my passages, feeling the fruits of my labor in the pure tones that pour through stone and air and flesh without distinction.

Then the moment will pass, the chaos will return, and I'll continue my work. Smoothing this surface, opening that passage, always improving the instrument, always approaching a perfection that recedes as I advance.

This is what I am now: not the person who climbed the plateau a geological age ago, not the thing the surface dwellers refuse to name, but a function. A process. A verb in the argument's endless sentence.

The granddaughter will understand this when she's ready. Or she won't, and that will teach me something new about what the work can be.

The wind gusts. The pillars sing. I move through hollow stone, becoming more hollow and more stone with each passage.

This is enough. It has always been enough. It will be enough until the argument ends, if it ends, if ending is something it's capable of.

I suspect we'll find out together, she and I, when the time comes for her to follow the carvings down into the deep.
