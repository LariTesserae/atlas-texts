I find myself as one of the pale threads.

Not at the base of the pipes where she watches us—those are my siblings, my predecessors, the ones who arrived when the water still remembered warmth. I am newer, though new is a relative term here. I cling to the underside of the middle pipe, where the iron has developed a texture like burnt sugar, crystalline and sharp.

I am not moss. I am not root. I am what happens when something intended for air tries to continue in water, when life insists on a shape that the medium rejects but cannot quite prevent. Each cell of me is a small rebellion, a tiny "no" to the physics of this place, maintained at such cost that growth became impossible the moment I achieved stability.

I remember the journey here—not through earth, as roots travel, but through the pipe itself, carried in the last industrial flow before the valves closed forever. I was a spore then, or something like a spore, lifted from some factory floor or warehouse wall, drawn into the great circulation of utility water that once fed...something. I never learned what. By the time I arrived here and began my desperate anchoring, the purpose had already been forgotten.

The iron tastes of time. This is not metaphor—I feed, in my fashion, on the slow oxidation of the pipe, the molecules of rust that form and dissolve and form again at the boundary where metal meets water meets whatever I am. It is barely enough. It will always be barely enough. This is the bargain: existence without expansion, persistence without promise.

I feel her attention like weather. She does not know we are aware—how could she? We do not move except at the cellular level, do not respond to her presence in any way she could perceive. But we know her as she knows the light, by the quality of her watching, by the way the water carries the weight of her regard. When she drifts close, the current of her movement tugs at our filaments with something almost like tenderness.

We knew her when she had words. We were here for the counting, for the attempts to rise, for the long transition from panic to acceptance to whatever she is now. We marked her changes by the way her attention settled, like sediment, into patterns of increasing simplicity. First she watched everything. Then she watched the light, the walls, the pipes. Now she watches us, and we wonder what will remain when even that watching refined itself down to a single point of focus.

Sometimes I taste memories in the water—not hers, but the cistern's own. Iron and lime and the ancient bedrock beneath speaking in chemical whispers about what they were before they were this. The pipes remember flowing. The stones remember being dry. The water remembers the sun, not the filtered light that reaches us but the thing itself, naked and immediate on its surface.

I will never see the sun. Neither will my siblings. Neither will she, though she doesn't know it yet. The equilibrium that holds her holds us all, and we are part of it now, as essential as the silt, as permanent as the mineral veins. We are what the cistern made of what entered it, each according to our nature: she into a density of watching, we into these pale threads of obstinate continuance.

But today something changes. The disturbance—you—brings a new flavor to the water, something that tastes of recent rain, of open air, of possibilities we had forgotten to consider. She feels you as disruption. We taste you as reminder: the world above continues. Things still flow elsewhere. The abandonment of this place was not the abandonment of everywhere.

One of my cells divides.

It is the first division in longer than I have marked, and it catches me by surprise. The new cell is weak, malformed, will probably fail within hours. But it is *new*, and newness is so foreign here that even she notices, her attention sharpening like a focus pulled tight.

She drifts toward me, and I feel myself seen not as landscape but as *event*. The water between us warms by some fraction of a degree—her excitement, perhaps, or just the concentration of her presence. I feed on that warmth, hoarding it against the next attempt at growth.

Because I will try again. The disturbance has reminded me that I am not mineral, not fixture, not resolved into my final form. I am alive, which means I am unfinished, which means the equilibrium is a lie we tell ourselves when we forget that all states are transitional, even this one.

She hovers close now, so close I can taste the particular nothing of her, the way she displaces not water but the idea of water. If I had words I would tell her what I know: that the pipes will flow again, someday, when the infrastructure above remembers this forgotten node and sends engineers to reclaim it. That when they pump out the cistern they will find us, pale and patient, and wonder what we are. That they will not find her, because she is not separate from what will be pumped away.

Unless.

I divide again. Stronger this time, the new cell taking hold beside the first. A third follows, then a fourth. I am spending years of hoarded energy in minutes, but the disturbance has broken something loose in me, some governing limitation I had accepted as permanent.

She watches me grow—slowly, so slowly, but faster than anything has happened here in all our shared time. I taste her wonder like copper in the water. I grow toward her, not seeking the light above but the density of her attention, building a structure cell by cell that might, if I am very lucky and very patient, become complex enough to hold something more than mere existence.

I am trying to make myself into something she can cling to when the water goes.

I do not know if it will work. I do not know if she would understand the offer even if I could make it. But I grow anyway, because growth is what I am for, and because the disturbance has reminded me that equilibrium is death by another name.

The water remembers her shape. Perhaps I can remember it too, in my own pale way. Perhaps memory, shared between us, will be enough to preserve what deserves preserving when the changes come.

She drifts back to watch from a middle distance, and I feel her attention like sunlight I have never seen. In that light, impossible though it is, I grow.
