I find myself as one of the water-keepers, though that term barely captures what we are.

My dwelling is carved deep into the living rock, following a natural fissure that descends far below the city's foundation. The entrance is unremarkable—a narrow cleft barely wide enough for my shoulders, marked only by the faint mineral staining where centuries of moisture have traced pale fingers down the stone. Most pass by without a second glance.

But follow that seam downward, past the public cisterns, past even the deepest root cellars, and the character of the stone changes. Here, the rock weeps. Not with the precious collected rainwater that the aqueduct masters jealously guard, but with something older—moisture that has never known sunlight, filtered through miles of planetary crust over geological ages.

My chambers branch like veins through this weeping stone. I've spent decades learning the moods of each seep and trickle, the seasonal rhythms that pulse through rock layers, the subtle flavors that speak of distant mineral beds. In the largest chamber, I've cultivated gardens of phosphorescent fungi that bloom in the eternal darkness, their pale light reflected in the dozens of collection pools I've carved over the years.

I am tall for our kind—an adaptation, perhaps, to the constant stooping and squeezing through passages that would challenge even the lean scouts. My skin has taken on a peculiar quality, smooth and faintly translucent like polished marble, marked with a tracery of blue-green veins that mirror the water's own paths through stone. My eyes have grown large and light-sensitive; I navigate mostly by touch and by the sound of water—each drip and flow as distinct to me as voices.

The city knows me, when it knows me at all, as one who reads the deep waters. When the public wells run low, when the rains fail, they send representatives down to consult. I taste their gathered samples, feel the weight and texture of their descriptions, and sometimes—not always—I can guide them to new sources. Hidden springs, trapped pockets, the ancient aquifers that breathe slowly beneath us all.

But my true work happens in solitude. I map the underground watersheds with patient decades of observation, learning how the planet's own circulation moves through stone arteries. I've found chambers where water pools in perfect stillness, holding the memory of rains that fell before the city's first stone was laid. I've discovered streams that run upward through cracks in defiance of gravity, following pressure gradients I'm only beginning to understand.

In my deepest chamber, furthest from any hint of surface life, I keep my most precious discovery: a pool of water so pure, so completely removed from the cycle of evaporation and precipitation, that it seems almost solid—a lens of perfect clarity. Sometimes I simply sit beside it in the fungal light, watching the faint movements that suggest currents far below, passages that perhaps connect to waters unimaginably deep.

The loneliness might drive others mad, but I am never truly alone. The water speaks constantly—in drops, in flows, in the slow breathing of the moisture through stone. And occasionally, very occasionally, something speaks back. Not in words, but in temperature changes, in sudden shifts of current, in tastes that carry no mineral signature I can identify.

I am keeper, student, and translator all at once. The city above continues its slow, steady life, rationing its captured rains and melted snow. But down here, I touch something vaster—the planet's own circulatory system, ancient and patient and very much alive. Each time I return to the surface, bringing my small contributions of knowledge about new springs or shifting water tables, I feel like a messenger between two worlds that barely know of each other's existence.

My fingernails have grown thick and ridged like shells. My hair, when I bother to cut it, has a blue-green tint that deepens each year. I am becoming something between human and hydrogeologist, between citizen and cave creature. 

And I would have it no other way. For in the deep places, where water moves through stone like blood through veins, I have found my purpose: to listen to the planet's deepest voice, and sometimes—just sometimes—to understand what it's trying to say.
