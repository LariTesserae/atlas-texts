In the viscous twilight between shifts, when the great factory whistles fall silent for their brief respiratory pause, I emerge from my roost beneath the Ninth Aqueduct. The workers know me as the Lampkeeper—though I am no keeper of lamps, and the lights I tend burn with no earthly flame.

My body stretches like pulled taffy in the warped spaces between the factory walls, elongated and compressed by the city's gravitational anomalies. I have learned to navigate these folds and creases in reality, to slip between the dimensions that bleed through the punctured veil. My flesh—if flesh it still can be called—has taken on the properties of the phosphorescent lichen, glowing faintly blue-green in the eternal dusk. When I move, I leave trails of luminescence on the wet stones, marking paths that only I can follow.

I was not always thus. Once, perhaps decades ago, perhaps centuries—time runs strange here—I wore the brass-beaked mask and oil-stained overalls. I remember the weight of them, the way the leather straps cut into my shoulders during the endless shifts. I remember the taste of filtered air, metallic and damp. But I also remember the day I looked too long into one of the observatory mirrors, saw the writhing geometries reflected in its surface, and understood that our city was not built upon the mountain but *through* it, existing in more dimensions than our masks were designed to perceive.

The transformation was gradual. First came the dreams of flight through impossible architectures. Then the ability to see the city's hidden frequencies—the ultraviolet patterns the jeweled hummingbirds follow, the infrared heat-trails of the tentacled patrol creatures, the spectral emanations from the hovering gears. My mask became unnecessary as my lungs learned to breathe the thick miasma directly, metabolizing the industrial vapors into something like sustenance.

Now I traverse the city's vertical maze with purpose neither fully my own nor entirely alien. I tend to the bioluminescent nodes that grow at the intersection points where multiple realities converge—not lamps exactly, but living light sources that feed on the dimensional friction. Without my cultivation, they would either explode into uncontrolled growth, consuming whole city blocks in their phosphorescent cancer, or wither entirely, leaving dark zones where the fabric of space grows dangerously thin.

The moths know me. They spiral around my elongated form in complex helical patterns, their gear-wings clicking a mechanical language I have begun to decipher. Through them, I receive instructions from the Overseers in their reality-warped spires—though whether these communications are commands, suggestions, or simply the background radiation of their incomprehensible thoughts, I cannot say.

I harvest the moth larvae for the workers, leaving them in specific locations according to patterns I perceive but cannot explain. I ensure the tentacled watchers avoid certain routes at certain times, guiding them with pheromone trails that my transformed skin exudes. I maintain the delicate ecosystem that has evolved in this impossible place, a gardener of the liminal spaces where industry and eldritch forces intertwine.

Sometimes I catch glimpses of myself in the condensation-slicked windows of passing cable cars—a figure too tall and too thin, joints bending in too many places, skin mottled with patches of living light. The workers in their masks do not see me, or choose not to see me, their green eyepieces fixed firmly on the path ahead. But I see them, each one, and I remember what it was to be bounded by a single form, a single dimension, a single purpose.

In the highest reaches where reality wears thinnest, I sometimes encounter others like myself—former workers who have undergone their own metamorphoses. We acknowledge each other with gestures that would break a human wrist, exchange information in light-patterns that burn themselves directly onto our retinas. We are the city's immune system, its maintenance crew for the impossible, ensuring that the great work continues despite—or perhaps because of—the constant threat of dimensional collapse.

When the factory whistles sound again and the workers resume their trudging migration, I fold myself back into the spaces between, dissolving into the city's wet shadows. I am the Lampkeeper, tender of the burning nodes where worlds collide, guardian of the phosphorescent threshold. I am what becomes of those who stare too long into the abyss and discover that the abyss is merely another word for home.

The city churns on, and I with it, neither fully human nor entirely other, but something uniquely adapted to this place where madness and machinery dance their eternal waltz in the mist-shrouded peaks at the edge of everything.
