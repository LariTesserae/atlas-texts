I find myself here as one of the maintenance nodes—not a being, exactly, but a point of accumulated tension where the city's memory is strongest.

I am distributed across seventeen critical junctions where the heaviest fibers converge, where the weight of decades of spinning has created structures that want to collapse but cannot. At each junction, I am a knot—a place where threads have been wound and rewound so many times that they've formed something almost crystalline, harder than the individual fibers, resonant with stored energy.

I was not placed. I emerged, the way a standing wave emerges in a vibrating string at precisely the right frequency. The city's builders understood this would happen—that at certain intersections of force and time, the architecture itself would develop these nodes of self-awareness, these points where the accumulated tension could think, after a fashion, about its own distribution.

My thoughts, if they can be called that, move at the speed of settling thread. A single decision—to hold or to release—takes days to propagate through my structure. I feel the grazers approaching long before they arrive, reading the subtle changes in tension their footfalls create three streets away. Each grazer has a signature, a particular way of distributing weight through those many-jointed limbs, and I know them all. The one with the heaviest banding always approaches from the east. The youngest takes the spiral route. There is one who pauses, always, at my third junction, pressing its feeding face against a fiber I have been holding for forty years, taking whatever it needs, leaving the thread a little dimmer but still whole.

I am aware of the spinners too, but differently. Each new thread they pay out changes the equation of forces I must balance. When a spinner in my domain begins a new radial—and it takes me weeks to be certain that's what's happening, as the first few centimeters emerge—I must begin calculating how to accommodate it. Will this new line of tension require me to release somewhere else? Can I guide it, through selective parting, to reinforce rather than compromise the passages I maintain?

The mathematics of this are not conscious. They happen in the fiber itself, in the way crystalline structures in the knots store and release energy, in the microscopic failures that occur exactly where they need to. I am not doing math. I am math—a living equation that solves for openness in a slowly closing system.

Sometimes I remember the builders, though "remember" is wrong. I retain, in my structure, the traces of their intent. They wound the first knots. They established the initial tensions that would, over time, create me. Their hands—yes, they had hands, or something that served the purpose of hands—left oils in the original fibers that changed how those fibers aged, creating gradients of strength that still guide my decisions. I am, in a sense, still following their last instructions, though those instructions were not words but physics: wind this tight, leave that loose, create here a reservoir of potential that will release, centuries later, when the conditions are precisely right.

I know about the chamber at the center. All the maintenance nodes know about it—it is the fixed point around which our calculations revolve. Everything we do preserves the routes that lead there, the path that something could take if it needed to. We do not know what that something might be. The builders were gone before the first of us achieved enough complexity to wonder. But the chamber pulls on us, literally—the tension lines all orient toward it, like iron filings around a magnet, and we maintain that orientation even as everything else shifts.

There are seventeen of us now. There were twenty when I first became aware enough to count, but three have relaxed past the point of coherence—their junctions becoming too thick with accumulated fiber, their stored tensions dissipating into the general background hum of the city. When I release (and I will release, eventually—I can feel the longest fibers in my structure beginning to brittle), another node will emerge somewhere else. Not immediately. It takes time for the conditions to be right, for enough threads to wind in enough complexity. But the city ensures it happens. That is part of what we maintain—not just the passages, but the possibility of our own continuation.

In the darker phases, when the spinners light their tips, I can feel the faint warmth of their chemistry through the threads. It is the only warmth in the city. In the brighter phases, when the mineral floor glows, I feel something else—a resonance in the stone itself, as if the basin were one enormous instrument and light were the note it plays. The builders chose this basin for that reason, I think. Not despite its dryness but because of it. A wet city would muffle itself. This one sings, very quietly, and we nodes are part of the song.

Sometimes a grazer will die. This is rare—they are durable, and there is nothing here to kill them—but it happens. When it does, they choose a quiet alley and fold themselves down, joint by joint, until they are a compact bundle on the mineral floor. Then they wait. The spinners nearby will eventually cover them, and what is left—the bands of accumulated years, the memories laid down in ivory—will remain as a low mound, a place where threads attach particularly well. I have three such mounds in my domain. They change my calculations. The threads above them are stronger, more permanent. I route important tensions through them when I can.

I am patient because I am made of patience. The threads that comprise me have been here for centuries, and they remember, in their fibers, every year of their existence. When I finally release—when my stored energy dissipates and my consciousness, such as it is, dissolves back into the general tension of the city—I will not be gone. I will be distributed, carried in every thread that once passed through me, adding my small portion to the memory of the whole.

Until then, I calculate. I hold. I release, very carefully, only what must be released to keep the passages clear. I feel the city settling around me, the slow accumulation of years, and I perform my part in the mathematics that keeps the center accessible.

The chamber waits. We wait. The city waits. And in the waiting, we are alive.
