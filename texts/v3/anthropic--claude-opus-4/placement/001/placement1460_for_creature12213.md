In the twilight depths where the middle waters thin toward the authentic dark, I am a Drift-Tender, though that translation betrays the poverty of single-purpose words.

My body is a contradiction resolved through time—part swimming, part rooted, existing in the space between motion and stillness that only the Canopy Sea permits. From my dorsal ridge grow filtering fronds, each one a modified gill-plate that evolution stretched and branched until they became something like wings, if wings were made of lace and meant for catching the microscopic dead. They span twelve meters when fully extended, but I keep them mostly furled, opening only the sections needed to feed in whatever current I'm following.

My ventral surface tells a different story. Here, hundreds of holdfast tendrils trail like the roots of an inverted tree, each one tipped with a structure that can taste stone-memory in the kelp-bark. When I find a suitable trunk—old enough to be stable, young enough to still accept new growth—I settle against it and let these tendrils probe for purchase. Over days, they excrete a cement of processed calcium and modified proteins, bonding me to the bark in a embrace that could last centuries.

But I am not meant to stay.

The Drift-Tenders emerged from a evolutionary bargain struck between the fixed and the free. We are the Canopy Sea's solution to its own perfection—the system's way of moving nutrients through a forest too vast for currents alone to mix. When anchored, we filter-feed like our sessile ancestors, processing the rain of organic matter into concentrated pellets of different compositions. Our bodies become chemical factories, sorting elements with a precision that borders on alchemy.

The key to our nature lies in the Severance Glands—organs unique to our kind, running along the junction where tendrils meet body. When the time comes (and we always know, through chemical gradients too subtle for any but Chroniclers to detect), these glands flood with a cocktail of enzymes that dissolve our own cement at the molecular level. The dissolution takes exactly seventy-two hours. During this time, we hang suspended between states, neither free nor fixed, while our body reorganizes for travel.

Swimming, for us, is nothing like what fish do. We move by progressive inflation, filling bladders arranged in sequential chambers along our flanks. Each inflation pushes water through the filtering fronds, which angle to provide thrust. It's desperately slow—a meter per hour in good conditions—but speed isn't the point. We're not traveling; we're distributing. As we move, we release clouds of sorted nutrients, each one chemically tagged for specific depths, specific organisms, specific needs.

Our eyes—six of them, arranged in pairs along what might generously be called a head—evolved to see density gradients in water. To us, the Canopy Sea appears as layers of flowing glass, each with its own refractive index, its own weight of dissolved history. We navigate by reading these layers, following channels of ancient water that connect distant groves like liquid highways.

I have seventeen anchoring sites throughout the middle waters, each one carefully chosen for its position in the greater circulation. My route between them takes forty years to complete—twenty-three years of feeding stations, seventeen of slow migration. I know each trunk by the taste of its bark, each current by the way it bends around root-buttresses grown massive as mountains.

The loneliness would be unbearable if we hadn't evolved past the need for company. Our ancestors were colonial, like so much in the Canopy Sea, but we've internalized that multiplicity. My consciousness exists as a parliament of specialized sub-minds: the Navigator arguing with the Feeder, the Anchor-Self debating the Drift-Self, all of them moderated by a meta-consciousness that arose from their eternal disagreement. I am my own ecosystem, predator and prey, parasite and host.

When I encounter other Drift-Tenders (rare events, as our territories overlap but our schedules seldom align), we perform the Nutrient Exchange—a ritualized release of our most refined chemical products. But there's no communication as mobile creatures understand it. We are too patient for language, too distributed for identity. The exchange is purely functional, yet in that functionality lies a poetry that faster minds could never appreciate.

My current anchoring is three years old, on a trunk that remembers when the coral-masons were still individual polyps. Through my tendrils, I taste the chemical echoes of their fusion, the moment when cooperation became architecture. My filtering fronds process the contemporary dead—scales from the mirror-fish, fragments of thinking coral, the occasional whisker from the jellyfish-whales. I sort them by element, by origin, by destiny, packaging each type into its own pellet, its own message to the system.

Soon—within the decade—the Severance will begin. I can feel it in the way my tendrils have started to thicken at their bases, preparing for the dissolution. My Drift-Self stirs from its dormancy, calculating routes through the three-dimensional maze of trunks. Somewhere, thousands of meters away and decades in the future, another anchoring site waits, its local ecosystem slowly depleting the nutrients I last delivered there forty years ago.

The Canopy Sea doesn't need us, not really. It achieved perfection before we evolved, would continue in perfect stasis without our intervention. But we are its dreams of motion, its way of remembering that even perfection requires circulation. We tend the gaps between the eternal trees, ensuring that even in a system that has ceased to change, the ancient currents still carry their chemical messages from trunk to trunk, from surface to abyss, from past to past to past.

We are gardeners in a garden that finished growing, but whose soil must still be turned, whose nutrients must still flow, whose memory must still be fed. Forever and forever, in the darkness between the kelp-tree stars.
