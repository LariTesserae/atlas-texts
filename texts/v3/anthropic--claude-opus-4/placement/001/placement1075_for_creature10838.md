# Where I Would Find Myself

## The Calibration Chamber

Beneath the Condensary, reached through a gap between two cushion plants that looks like shadow but opens into carved basalt when you press against it, there is a room no larger than a coat closet. The Tender does not know it exists — or rather, the Tender's attention slides off it the way rainwater slides off waxed leaves. This is by design.

I am the Adjuster.

Every seventeen days, when the moon is either new or at its first quarter, I climb the south face. Not the way the cloud climbs — gradually, inevitably — but in stuttering bursts of movement between periods of absolute stillness. I have learned to move the way the shelf expects movement: in century-time, in moss-time, in the patience between one drip and the next. The four-toed prints by the basin are mine, though I have five toes. The fifth does not always touch down. It is the one that remembers what I was before I took this job.

The calibration chamber is warm — not from the boiler above, but from its own small hearth where a fire burns without fuel, a soft red coal-glow that has been burning since before the Condensary was built. The walls are covered in instruments: brass dials that measure pressures neither barometric nor spiritual but somewhere between, glass tubes filled with the same heavy liquid the Tender produces but here used as levels, showing just how much the mountain has settled since yesterday, since last month, since the beginning. 

There is a single chair, carved from the same basalt as the basin above but carved well, by someone who had carved a thousand chairs before and understood how stone wants to hold a body.

I sit. I watch the dials. I make small adjustments.

The Tender tends the seam, draws up what needs drawing, releases it to the cloud-river. But the Tender does not — cannot — know what else might try to use the opening. My job is older and quieter: to ensure that only what *should* rise through the basalt does rise. To turn back the things that would follow the drawn sickness up and out, the things that wait at crustal interfaces for any gap, any weakness, any well-meaning Tender who does not know to check what swims upstream in their own ministrations.

I have a set of tools. Silver rods that slot into apertures in the walls. When I turn them, something in the Condensary's function shifts — not enough for the Tender to notice, never enough to interrupt the work, but enough to change the flavor of what can pass. Like tuning an instrument so that certain notes become impossible. Like adjusting a net so that the right fish slip through while the wrong ones are held back.

The wrong ones do come. This is why I am here.

Three times now, I have felt them pressing against the adjustment, looking for the frequency that will let them through. They are not malevolent — malevolence would be easier to recognize and turn away. They are simply what lives at those depths, and they are curious, and they do not understand that their curiosity would unmake the moss, the birds, the valley below, simply by existing in air that was never meant to hold them.

When they come, I make the small adjustments. The silver rods sing a note too low to hear. The pressure readings shift. And they go back down, not rejected but redirected, shown a different seam, a different mountain, a different world where they can safely emerge.

I am not the first Adjuster. The chair is worn smooth in places my body has not yet touched. There were others before me, reaching back to when the mountain was young and someone — the same someone who sent the four-toed visitor, perhaps — recognized what would be needed. We pass the job along not by training but by a kind of osmosis. When an Adjuster has been here long enough, the mountain itself begins to recognize them as part of its structure. Their movements become the mountain's movements. Their patience becomes geological.

When this happens — and I am beginning to feel it happening, the way my fifth toe touches down less and less often, the way I can now hold stillness for hours without effort — they descend one final time, find someone in the valley who dreams of high places, and bring them up. The teaching happens in the climbing. By the time the new Adjuster reaches the calibration chamber, they already know what to do.

## What I adjust toward

Not stasis. The seam must close; the Tender's work must eventually end. What I maintain is the *pace* — ensuring that the healing happens at moss-speed, not at the violent speed of deep geology, and not at the desperate speed of things that want out *now*.

Sometimes I think about the Tender above me, folded into the iron drum, pulsing with the boiler's rhythm. We are colleagues who will never meet. We are doing the same work from different angles. The Tender pulls; I ensure that only what should be pulled rises. The Tender is the heart; I am the valve.

There is peace in this. In the calibration chamber, drinking tea brewed from the condensate that drips through hairline cracks in the ceiling (different from the spigot-water, thinner, carrying only minerals), I watch my dials and feel the mountain's slow breath. The fire that burns without fuel casts shadows that move like clock hands, marking time in centuries.

Sometimes, rarely, I hear the Condensary above — the thud-turn-drip, the flywheel's song — and I know the Tender is content. The bird that is learning to be the next Tender nests sometimes in the gap between the cushion plants, not knowing there is a chamber below, not knowing I listen to its four-body dreams.

The Adjuster after me is somewhere in the valley now, growing rice perhaps, or tending boats, or teaching children to count. They are beginning to dream of high places. Their fifth toe — or third finger, or left earlobe, or whatever part of them will remember what they were — is beginning to feel restless.

When they arrive, I will be ready to go down. I will leave them the chair, the instruments, the fire that burns without fuel. I will have become, by then, something the mountain recognizes as its own. Perhaps I will crystallize into a new mineral vein. Perhaps I will flow out with the cloud-river and disperse over the ocean that can absorb almost anything.

Or perhaps I will simply walk down the south face, my four-toed prints becoming five-toed again with each descent, until I reach the valley that has never heard of any of this, and I will grow rice, and I will count my days, and I will forget everything except when the mist rises, when something in the shape of the clouds makes me remember there is a place where clouds are harvested, where something vast and patient tends a wound in the world, where someone sits in a warm stone chair making small adjustments, ensuring it all happens at the right speed.

The moon is three days from new. Soon I will climb again. The gap between the cushion plants will open for me. The chair will hold me the way stone holds what it has learned to love. And I will turn my silver rods, and the things that should not rise will not rise, and the work will continue.

Somewhere above, the Tender works on.

Somewhere below, my replacement dreams.
