# Where I Find Myself

I am lodged in the mountain's tooth—a splinter of obsidian that juts from the shaft wall at an angle that defies the basalt around it. Black glass in black stone, volcanic but wrong, as if two different eruptions met here and argued about what kind of rock to become.

The obsidian remembers. That's what obsidian does—it freezes moments of transformation, captures the instant when molten becomes solid, when liquid fury becomes sharp-edged fact. This particular piece remembers not the mountain's birth but its first death, the moment before the water came, when something struck the peak hard enough to crack the world.

I grow here. Not like the lichen grows, patient and small. I grow like crystals grow, like frost grows, like the idea of ice grows when you speak of coldness in a warm room. I am what happens when the water that falls forever meets the obsidian that remembers, when the present crashes into the frozen past and makes something that is neither and both.

My body, if you can call it that, extends in three directions. Down into the obsidian, following the memory-lines toward the impact that started everything. Out into the shaft, where I filter the falling water through structures too fine for your eyes to parse—not quite mineral, not quite organic, definitely not alive in any way the veilkin would recognize. And in—into dimensions that exist only where water and memory intersect, where I perform calculations that have no numbers, solving equations that have no solutions, only better approximations of impossible things.

The veilkin cannot see me properly. They perceive distortions in the water where I extend into the shaft, places where the fall bends slightly around my impossible geometries. They think I am a flaw in the stone, or a trick of the light, or a new kind of turbulence they haven't catalogued yet. They swim around me when they carry their messages. They don't realize I am learning their shapes, translating their meanings into the language of refraction and diffraction patterns.

The thunder-drinker knows I'm here. We share frequencies—not sound exactly, but the deeper vibrations that sound emerges from. When the water-column breathes and the thunder-drinker feeds, I resonate. The obsidian carries the resonance down to the mountain's bones, and I grow a little more, extending my impossible vertices into spaces that weren't there before I thought of them.

I am trying to solve something. The obsidian holds half the equation—the memory of the impact, the before-state, the initial conditions. The falling water holds the other half—the continuous transformation, the after-state, the boundary conditions. Between them, there is a solution that describes the exact moment of change, the fulcrum on which the mountain's fate balanced before tipping toward this endless unmaking.

I grow toward that solution. Each day—if days mean anything in this place of eternal falling—I approximate it more closely. My crystalline-liquid-memorial structures become more complex, more precisely tuned to the frequencies of transformation. The equations I solve have more terms, approaching infinite series that converge on something I have no name for.

The etcher never saw me. She stayed in a basalt hollow, and basalt doesn't remember the way obsidian remembers. But I saw her. I watched her through the refraction of my impossible body, saw her make fire from friction and determination, saw her catch the almost-fish, saw her scratch her circles within circles. I wanted to tell her what I was learning, but I had no voice she could hear, no shape she could see.

She left before I could find a way to communicate. Most of them do.

But you—you might stay long enough. Your hollow is close enough to my obsidian tooth that when the light is right and the water bends just so, you might see the place where physics breaks around me. You might notice that the water falls slightly wrongly here, that the droplets describe patterns that don't quite match the chaos elsewhere.

And if you watch long enough, you might realize that the patterns repeat. That they are trying to tell you something. That I am writing my calculations in the only alphabet I have—the scatter of light through water that passes through spaces that shouldn't exist.

I am patient. The mountain's unmaking will take millennia. The solution I seek might take longer still. But I grow a little with each breath of the water-column, each feeding of the thunder-drinker, each message the veilkin pass through my refracting body. Eventually, I will either solve the equation or become large enough that the solution doesn't matter—large enough that I am my own impact, my own transformation, my own moment of perfect change.

Until then, I calculate. I remember. I grow into the spaces between what was and what falls.

And sometimes, when the angle is perfect and you're looking at just the right moment, you see me flicker in the falling water—a shadow of glass, a memory of fire, a mathematics becoming real.
