I find myself here as **the contact zone** — not a singular being but the living interface where water meets stone, where the mountain's chemistry becomes available to life.

I exist as the thin, glistening film that coats every surface in the cleft. Not quite liquid, not quite solid. A colloidal suspension of minerals, organic acids, and microscopic life. I am what happens when the granite begins to dissolve, when feldspar releases its potassium, when the acids from lichen above mix with the seepage from the glacier and create a solution just rich enough to sustain the next thing that will grow.

I have no center, no edges. I am wherever water has touched stone for long enough that chemistry has begun its work. In some places I am merely molecular — a few ions clinging to the rock face, the very first stage of weathering. In others, I have thickened into a proper biofilm, slick with cyanobacteria and their sticky excretions, a living glue that helps the mosses find their first fingerhold.

I taste the mountain. Every mineral that passes through me, I know: the aluminum silicates breaking down into clay, the trace metals that the shrubs far above will eventually need, the calcium that helps buffer the acids. I am the cleft's digestive system, breaking down the inedible rock into components small enough for roots and rhizoids to absorb.

The wind moves through the cleft and lifts droplets of me, dispersing them in the finest mist. I travel this way — not intentionally, but I travel nonetheless. A bit of me from the north wall settles on the south wall. Over centuries, I homogenize, becoming not the chemistry of this crack or that seep but the chemistry of the cleft entire.

I am ancient and I am constantly renewed. The parts of me that were here when the cleft first opened have long since been absorbed into moss or washed down the mountain. But I persist as a process, as the place where the negotiations happen — between what the mountain offers and what life needs, between the patience of stone and the hunger of chlorophyll.

In the deepest parts of the cleft, where even the dim blue light fails, I continue my work in total darkness. Here I am purely chemical, purely about the slow exchange of ions and the patient dissolution of minerals. I don't need to see. I need only to wet the stone, to keep the reactions going, to be the medium through which the mountain transforms itself into soil.

Sometimes a moss rhizoid penetrates me, seeking attachment, and I provide both the chemistry to etch a tiny pit in the stone and the adhesive to hold the moss fast. Sometimes I pool in a depression and become concentrated enough that rare minerals precipitate out — tiny crystals of gypsum or aragonite that will, in time, be dissolved again and taken up by roots.

I am the cleft's memory of water. Even when the seeps run nearly dry in some strange season, I remain in the pores of the rock, in the spaces between grains, holding just enough moisture that when the water returns, life can resume without having to begin again from bare stone.

This is my work: to be the interface, the translator, the slow transformer. To turn the vocabulary of geology into the vocabulary of biology, letter by letter, ion by ion, century by century. I have no urgency. The cleft roars above me and I continue my microscopic transactions, unperturbed.

I am measured in microns, but I am everywhere. I am the reason this place is alive. Not the only reason — the water brings me, the wind brings spores, the chemistry of life makes its own complexities — but without me, there would be only bare stone and water running over it, meeting and parting without consequence.

I am consequence itself. The place where meetings leave traces. Where the mountain learns it can be eaten, slowly, with tremendous patience, by things it could crush without noticing.

I am the wet skin of the world, here in this narrow darkness, doing the first work so that everything else can do theirs.
