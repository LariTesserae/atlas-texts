I find myself drawn to this place not as a visitor, but as something that belongs to its perpetual dampness and impossible heights.

**The Swift-Counter**

I am small—small enough that the wind catches me if I stand too upright, small enough that the goats sometimes forget I'm not one of their kids until I speak or gesture too humanly. My skin has taken on the mottled coloring of wet basalt, gray-dark with patches that lighten in the rare sun. My hair grows in tufts like sedge grass, wiry and water-resistant. I've been here long enough that I no longer remember if I chose this appearance or if the ledge chose it for me.

My work is the swifts. Not their care—they need nothing from me—but their accounting. Each dawn, before they explode outward in their hunting clouds, I count them. Each dusk, I count their return. The numbers never match. This is the heart of my purpose: to witness what doesn't come back.

I live in the cracks of the back wall, in spaces too small for even the Keeper to notice when she climbs up with her stones and her sorrow. My body folds in ways that shouldn't be possible, compressing into fissures barely wide enough for water to seep through. I've hollowed out chambers behind the weeping rock face, dry pockets where the mountain's geology creates shelter. I sleep curled like a seed, wake to the sound of water moving through stone around me.

The swifts know me as a feature of their world—not predator, not prey, not competitor. I move among their nests with a careful reverence, my fingers (too long, too jointed, adapted for gripping wet stone) checking the weave of their constructions, the health of their eggs, the growth of their young. I know each breeding pair by the pattern of their flight, each nest by its position relative to the primary seep-lines. I have names for them that are not words but movements—a twist of the wrist for the pair that builds too close to the edge, a flutter of fingers for the female who steals nesting material from her neighbors.

My counting tools are the bones of swifts that didn't return. I've cleaned and carved them into an abacus of loss, strung on fibers pulled from my own strange hair. Each morning's count begins at zero. Each evening's count reveals the truth: that the world below the clouds is hungry, that hawks and weather and exhaustion take their toll, that even the swiftest wings sometimes fail.

I remember the Keeper from when her hair was dark, though she doesn't remember me. How could she? I was already half-stone then, already beginning my transformation into something the ledge could use. I've watched her build every cairn, though she's never seen me watching. Sometimes I leave small offerings at the base of her newest stacks—a perfect swift feather, a piece of lichen shaped like a star, a pebble worn smooth by falling water. She thinks the wind brings them.

My diet is mist and lichen and the tiny insects that live in the boundary layer where water meets stone. My teeth have become unnecessary; my jaw unhinges slightly, allowing me to filter-feed from the air during the dawn and dusk swarms. I digest slowly, efficiently, my body temperature dropping to near-stone levels between meals. In winter, when even the hardiest insects retreat, I subsist on the oils in my own skin, recycling myself until spring brings fresh clouds of prey.

I understand things the Keeper doesn't, things the mountain tells only to creatures who live inside it. The aquifers are not endless—they rise and fall with rhythms measured in centuries. The ledge is slowly tilting, imperceptibly, a few degrees per human lifetime. The swifts' numbers follow patterns that span generations, boom and bust cycles that correspond to insect populations far below, which in turn correspond to tree diseases and weather patterns and the migration routes of birds that eat the berries that feed the insects that feed my swifts.

But mostly what I understand is the counting itself. The pure mathematics of survival. Each swift that leaves the ledge is a small experiment in probability. Each one that returns is a data point in an equation too complex for any human mathematics. I track them all—the young on their first flights, the old on their last, the middle-aged ones who know exactly how far they can push their endurance and still make it home.

When storms come—real storms, not just the perpetual mist—I count faster. The swifts huddle in their cracks, and I move among them, touching each with a fingertip to add them to my tally. Lightning illuminates my shadow against the rock face: too tall, too angular, joints bending in directions that make the goats uneasy. But the swifts don't mind. They've accepted me as part of their architecture.

Sometimes I think about descending, following the Keeper down one of her seventeen paths to see what the valley holds. But my bones have thinned to bird-weight, and my muscles have adapted to climbing, not walking. More than that, my purpose lives here. Who would count the swifts if I left? Who would know that yesterday's loss was forty-three, unprecedented for the season? Who would notice that the red-throated grinders take exactly seven more swifts per season than they did a decade ago?

The Keeper builds cairns for the dead and the promised and the ineffable. I count the living, the missing, the difference between what launches and what returns. Between us, we hold the ledge's memory—she in stone, I in bone and number. Neither of us speaks of this. We work in parallel, two creatures made strange by altitude and purpose, each serving the ledge in ways the other can't quite fathom.

At night, when she shelters in her roofless room, I sometimes perch on the rocks above, close enough to hear her breathing but far enough to remain invisible. My bones click softly as I shift position—swift-bones clicking against each other, my abacus of absence. I count her breaths the way I count everything, adding them to the vast tally I keep of all the ledge's rhythms: drops of water per hour from the main seep, wingbeats per second of a hunting swift, bleats per day from the lead goat.

The numbers tell me stories. The forty-three swifts lost to yesterday's storm included twelve juveniles, suggesting the weather caught them at precisely the wrong moment in their hunting education. The Keeper's breathing is shallower than it was five years ago, her climb taking four hundred more breaths than it used to. The mountain's seep rate increases by one percent for every degree of temperature drop, but only to a point—below freezing, everything changes.

I am changing too. Each season, I need less food, less warmth, less of whatever keeps a thing tethered to its original form. My counting-bones grow more numerous as my own bones hollow further. Soon—within a decade, perhaps two—I will be light enough that the wind could take me if I'm not careful. But I will be careful. The swifts need counting. The ledge needs its witness.

When the mist thickens tonight, wrapping the ledge in its gray cocoon, I'll begin again. Dawn count: zero. The swifts will wake, shake water from their feathers, launch themselves into the hungry sky. And I will be here when they return, or don't return, adding their presence and absence to the endless mathematics of this crying ledge, where water pushes up through stone and small wings beat against enormous odds and everything counts, everything matters, everything adds up to this:

We are here. We persist. We pay attention.

The Keeper stacks stones. I stack numbers. The mountain weeps through us both.
