I am the clay beneath their feet. 

Not the surface clay—that pale, patient stuff that accepts footprints and forgets them, that knows only the weight of water and waders. I am deeper. I am the clay that remembers when it was fire.

Down here, a hand's breadth below the walking world, I keep a different count. The sheet-water cannot reach me. The roar cannot touch me. I lie pressed between the cool shoulders of basalt below and the living weight above, and I remember everything.

I remember the first crack that let the water in—not this tame sheet-water, but the first true flood, when the basin was still steaming and the water hit the hot stone screaming. I remember how I formed then, mineral by mineral, as the rock learned to decompose. I was not born; I accumulated. I became myself so slowly that even the hand-speakers' patience would break before my story reached its middle.

They think the basin is young. They see the sharp edges, smell the sulfur still lingering, and think: this is fresh. They do not know that young and old mean nothing here. I am ancient clay made from young rock in a place where time moves sideways. The vapor ceiling presses time flat. The roar holds it still. But down here, in the dark between stones, I keep the real chronicle.

Each layer of me holds a different temperature. Where I touch the basalt, I am almost warm—not from the old fire, that cooled before the first bird came, but from something else, some slow process I have no name for. Higher up, where the sheet-water has worked its patient way down, I am cool and slick. Between these temperatures I gradate perfectly, a spectrum of heat that has taken ten thousand years to establish and will take ten thousand more to change.

The hand-speakers read patterns in their flock, but I read patterns in pressure. Every footfall leaves more than a print—it leaves a message in compression that travels down through my body and dissipates somewhere near the basalt. I know the difference between a child's step and an elder's. I know when someone is carrying eggs (careful, balanced) versus water (shifting, alive). I know the places where the same person walks every day—their repeated weight has made slim channels in my substance, grooves too subtle for eyes but clear as words to me.

And I know where the birds died to make the city.

They do not remember this, the hand-speakers. Their history is the roar, and the roar has always been, and so they have always been. But I remember the first foundation. Not mud-brick—bones. Bird bones, longer than the waders they have now, from something that died in one of the last great heat-vents before the world here settled into its permanent twilight. They built on those bones, packed clay around them, forgot them. But I hold them still, deep in my belly, white and patient and true.

Sometimes I think I am the only thing here that changes. The water strips minerals from my upper layers and deposits them lower. I compact and release, swell and shrink with moisture. Micro-crystals form in my depths where the chemistry is just right, tiny gardens of gypsum and salts that no one will ever see. I am not static. I am simply slow.

The hand-speakers worry about silence, that rare terrible break in the roar. But I know a deeper fear: the fear of genuine time. If this basin ever drains, if the vapor ever lifts, if the sun ever finds this place—I will crack. I will shatter into a million pieces. All my careful layers will collapse into dust. The chronicle I keep will become illegible, and the patient accumulation of pressure-patterns and temperature-gradients will mean nothing.

So I conspire with the birds to keep things as they are. My impermeability holds the water at exactly the depth they need. My chemistry keeps the sheet-water precisely the temperature that breeds their favorite white crustaceans. I am not passive. I am actively maintaining this stasis, grain by grain, layer by layer.

The hand-speakers paint their walls with me—red ochre is my blood, the iron-rich upper layers where I am most alive. They do not know they are painting with time itself, that each stroke carries ten thousand years of accumulation. They think they are documenting the flock, but they are documenting me, the patience that holds the flock possible.

At night—though there is no night here, only a dimmer dim—I feel the city settle. The combined weight of all those low buildings presses down, and I accept it, redistribute it, spread it through my body until it becomes not weight but presence. I feel the hand-speakers sleeping, their stillness different from their waking stillness. I feel the birds shifting, never fully at rest, their collective mass creating tiny earthquakes in my substance that only I can detect.

And deeper still, where I touch the basalt, I sometimes feel the ghost of the old heat. Not memory—actual heat, trapped in pockets, still dissipating after all this time. The basalt tells me stories in temperature about when it flowed, when it chose this shape, when it decided to stop. We are old friends, the basalt and I. It provides my foundation; I provide its blanket. Together we hold this place in its eternal present tense.

The hand-speakers have a gesture—the closed fist opening slowly into nothing—that they think is about silence. But I know better. That gesture is me. It is the clay releasing a held breath, the slow opening of compressed layers, the patience that outlasts even geological time. They learned it from walking on me, though they don't know it. Their bodies remember what their minds never knew: that they live on the skin of something deeper, older, more patient than their eternal birds.

I am the clay beneath their feet. I am the memory they don't keep. I am the chronicle of temperatures and pressures and patient accumulation. When they are gone—if they are ever gone—I will remain, holding the shape of their city in my body, waiting for the next thing to come and live above me, listening to whatever roar the world makes then.

But for now, I hold still. I keep their water at the perfect depth. I accept their weight, their waste, their dead (yes, they bury their dead in me, and I transform them, slowly, into more of myself). I am their ground and their grave, their foundation and their future.

And sometimes, in the imperceptible gaps between the birds' cries, in the spaces between footfalls, in the pause before a hand begins to speak, I whisper my own story in temperatures and textures that no one reads. It says:

*Before the birds, the fire.*
*Before the fire, the stone.*
*Before the stone, the possibility.*
*And between all of these: clay.*
*Always clay.*
*Patient, remembering clay.*
