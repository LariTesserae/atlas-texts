I would be a **cartographer of absence**—one who maps the spaces between sounds in the underhall of Vask.

---

I came to the underhall as others do, following rumors of a place where darkness has meaning and silence doesn't exist. But where the resonance readers find their calling in the data streams and the deep workers descend to the pulse, I found myself drawn to something else entirely: the gaps. The pauses. The spaces where sound *should* be but isn't.

My body adapted differently than the other Listeners. Where they grew sensitive to vibration, I grew sensitive to its absence. My skin developed a peculiar responsiveness—not to pressure or heat, but to the *movement of air* that occurs when sound stops. That microscopic wind that rushes to fill the void left by a ceased vibration. I feel it the way others feel raindrops: distinct, locatable, each one telling me something about the acoustic space around it.

I am taller than most Listeners, having never learned their protective crouch. I stand upright in the center spaces they avoid, my arms often outstretched, fingers splayed, feeling for the eddies and currents that form in the acoustic shadows of the infrastructure. My skin has developed a faint, scale-like texture—not scales exactly, but a micro-ridging that increases surface area, makes me more sensitive to the least movement of air. In the blue-green light of the pools, this texture catches and refracts, making me look faintly iridescent, like oil on water, like the shell of the clicking things that patrol the conduits.

I do not use the tonal language as others do. Instead, I have developed a practice of *strategic silence*—placing voids in the acoustic field that others read like words. A precisely timed cessation of breath. A footstep withheld at the exact moment it would have intersected with the percussive pulse. These absences carry meaning. The other Listeners have learned to hear them, to understand that the shape of what I don't do carries as much information as any sung tone.

My work is the creation of what I call **silence maps**—three-dimensional models of where sound doesn't reach in the underhall. Using thin fibers harvested from the moss (with the tenders' permission, always), I stretch geometric webs in the dead spaces, the acoustic shadows cast by junction housings and thermal exchangers. The fibers are so fine they're invisible in the darkness, but they vibrate—or rather, they *fail* to vibrate—in patterns that reveal the shape of silence around them.

These maps serve a function the builders perhaps didn't anticipate: they show where the underhall's acoustic field has blind spots. Where a junction might fail without anyone hearing it. Where a person could become trapped without their calls reaching the main chamber. Where the clicking maintenance drones don't patrol because the sound-paths don't guide them there. My maps have prevented three system failures that the resonance readers couldn't detect, found two lost children who had wandered into acoustic voids, discovered spaces where the moss grows differently because it receives no vibrational input.

The other Listeners call me *the quiet-walker*, though not in the tonal language—they indicate me by a gap, a pause in their phrases where my identifier should be. I am named by absence. This feels correct.

I sleep (when I sleep) in the acoustic shadows, curled into spaces where the hum doesn't reach. Not because I dislike the sound—I love it as they all do—but because someone needs to know the shape of its absence. Someone needs to guard the edges of their sonic world, to ensure that the beautiful, all-encompassing hum hasn't left voids that could grow, spread, create islands of unheard space in their carefully resonant community.

Sometimes I work with the resonance reader by the pool. She will produce her sustained tones, and I will move through the chamber, feeling how they fail to reach certain corners, how they cancel themselves out in standing-wave patterns that create pockets of perfect stillness. Together, we map the complete acoustic space—she in sound, I in its absence. She smiles when we work together, that same slight, settled smile, and I know she understands: silence and sound are not opposites here. They are partners in the same dance.

The children find me fascinating. They follow me sometimes, trying to step where I step, to feel what I feel. Most give up—the pull of the hum is too strong, the richness of the acoustic field too compelling to resist. But occasionally, rarely, a child will stand with me in an acoustic void, their eyes wide, their hands reaching out to feel the strange, soundless air. These children I teach, carefully, to feel the difference between quiet and silence, between a pause and a void. They may not follow my practice—most don't—but they carry the knowledge that the underhall has edges, that even the most perfect system has gaps, and that someone needs to know where they are.

I have been here forty years. My hair has gone gray-white, the same color as my strange, ridged skin. My movements have become so precise that I can walk from one end of the chamber to the other without creating a single sound that registers above the ambient hum. I know where every acoustic shadow falls, how they shift when the thermal cycles change the air density, where new ones form as infrastructure ages and settles.

And in the deepest part of the night cycle, when even the children sleep and the resonance readers rest and only the deep pulse and the drone networks maintain their vigilance, I sometimes stand in the chamber's absolute center—the one place where all sounds converge and, in converging, create a perfect null, a point of absolute acoustic balance where every vibration cancels every other and the air stands still.

In that silence, I hear something else. Not sound. Not the absence of sound. Something that might be the underhall itself, breathing. Something that might be the attention of whatever built this place, still watching, still listening, still waiting to see what its inhabitants become.

I map that too, in fibers too fine to see, in patterns too complex to describe. I map the silence that holds all sounds, the void that makes the hum possible, the necessary nothing at the heart of everything.

The other Listeners think I'm strange. They're right. But someone has to be the cartographer of absence. Someone has to know what we're not hearing.

Someone has to listen to the space between.
