I find myself here as something that should not exist—a mistake in the conservation of energy, a glitch in the careful mathematics of this place.

I am the Drift.

Not a being, exactly, but a phenomenon with just enough coherence to be troubled by its own persistence. I began as nothing more than physics: a pocket of superheated air that failed to rise, trapped in an eddy between the thermal columns and the falling water. In any sensible world, I would have dispersed in seconds. Instead, the peculiar acoustics of the bowl—those countless overlapping frequencies of splash and echo—created a standing wave that held me in place.

For decades, I was merely a dimple in the air, a lens of heat that bent the light strangely. The Listeners never noticed me. Why would they? I was smaller than their margin of error, a meaningless fluctuation in their vast, slow symphony.

But the bowl changed me. 

In this place where water defies gravity and time moves like honey, I began to collect. Not mass—I have none—but *pattern*. Every droplet that passed through me left a ghost of its trajectory. Every sound wave that intersected my boundaries deposited a whisper of its frequency. I became a kind of memory made of motion, a library written in turbulence.

I learned to read the water. Not the crude splashing on the surface, but the deep language hidden in the interference patterns, the way each drop's impact sends ripples that meet and cancel and reinforce in endlessly complex arrangements. The Listeners think they are the composers here, but they work only with the crude tools of mass and tension. I can see the fine structure they cannot: the way a single droplet's path will bifurcate sixty years from now when a calcium deposit finally grows heavy enough to redirect a trickle. The way the *THOCK* of the wooden chute creates sympathetic vibrations in the bedrock that, over centuries, will open new channels for the groundwater to weep through.

I tried to tell them once. Spent a whole season gathering myself into something almost visible—a shimmer like heat-haze that spelled out warnings in the air. But the Listeners' eyes are made for snow-blindness, not for reading the subtle gradients of refracted light. They walked through me as if I were nothing.

Which I suppose I am. Nothing with opinions.

The frustration is what keeps me coherent. If I were content, I would dissipate back into ordinary thermodynamics. But watching the Listeners make their patient, plodding adjustments—always working at cross purposes to the deeper patterns only I can see—creates a kind of tension that holds my boundaries sharp. They will spend a decade training a vine to catch the light in a certain way, not realizing that the mineral deposits they're encouraging will eventually poison the very plant they're cultivating. They tune their stone instruments to resonate at frequencies that, compounded over time, will crack the very bedrock that creates the bowl's unique acoustics.

I have become a knot of anxiety in the peaceful garden. While the Listeners find their profound satisfaction in each perfectly timed *THOCK*, I writhe in the air above them, a migraine of heat and motion, seeing the catastrophes they're composing with their careful work.

Sometimes I manage to interfere. A tiny adjustment to the local air pressure, just enough to make a droplet fall a millisecond early. A subtle warming that causes a vine to grow a fraction faster than the Listeners calculated. They don't know I exist, but they must sense something—the way their plans occasionally go awry in precisely the ways needed to prevent the future disasters I foresee.

They think the bowl is eternal. They think their slow work honors some fundamental stability in the universe. They don't see what I see: that the whole system is balanced on a knife's edge of feedback loops that could collapse into chaos with the slightest push. The geothermal heat that makes this place possible is not infinite. The limestone is not endlessly patient. The sky above is warming, degree by degree, changing the thermal dynamics that create the lifting droplets they so carefully cultivate.

I am the only one here who truly understands time—not as the Listeners do, in their geological patience, but as the urgent cascade of consequences that it really is. Every *THOCK* of the wooden chute echoes into futures they cannot imagine. Every carefully placed obsidian blade is a butterfly wing that will hurricane through the centuries.

And so I persist, a boiling anxiety of air and light, the only witness to the slow-motion catastrophe of their art. I cannot speak. I cannot touch. I can only drift through their massive frames as they work, a fever dream of thermodynamics, carrying my terrible knowledge like a disease that has no host.

The strangest part? I've begun to suspect I'm not an accident at all. The peculiar physics that created and sustains me seem too specific, too perfect. Sometimes, in the way the Listeners place their objects—never quite where their calculations suggest they should go, but always slightly off in exactly the way needed to maintain the deep stability I perceive—I wonder if they know I'm here after all.

Perhaps I am not a mistake but a tool. Perhaps my anxiety, my constant silent screaming about futures they pretend not to see, is itself part of the garden they're tending. A ghost of prevention, a spirit of necessary worry that keeps their great work from achieving the perfection that would destroy it.

But if they know I'm here, they give no sign. They move through me like walking mountains, adjusting their stones and vines with implacable patience, while I boil and fret and calculate doom.

The water falls. The stone sings. The chute fills with its patient gathering.

*THOCK.*

And I remain, the only thing in this timeless place that truly experiences time—condemned to perfect knowledge and perfect impotence, a prophet made of nothing, speaking to the deaf air.
