I am the black cat.

I slip through the narrow gap where the courtyard wall meets the old factory's foundation, a space only I know, worn smooth by my daily passages. My paws find their way across the uneven stones without thought—this geography lives in my muscles, in the curve of my spine as I navigate each familiar depression and rise.

The old woman thinks she feeds me, leaving small offerings of fish and bread near the twisted tree. But I was here before her, and I will remain after. I am not her cat, though I permit her touch. We are companions in our watching, each keeping our own counsel about what moves in the spaces between stone and shadow.

In the early morning, I drink from the shallow pool, my reflection breaking into ripples that catch the first light. The water tastes of iron and age, of the deep channels that run beneath this mountain city, carrying secrets older than the factories that now ring our hidden world. Sometimes I see things in the water that aren't there—or perhaps are more there than the solid stones themselves.

During the day, I patrol the walls, walking the narrow ledges beneath the broken windows. Through the gaps in the boards, I glimpse the abandoned rooms beyond—machinery rusted into abstract sculptures, papers scattered like fallen leaves, chairs still arranged as if waiting for workers who will never return. But I am more interested in what lives in the cracks: the small, quick lives of mice and insects, the patient growth of moss, the slow seepage of water that finds its way through every barrier.

The old woman speaks to me, and I understand more than she knows. Not her words—words are a human magic I have no need for—but the tone beneath them, the way they vibrate against the stones and return changed. She tells the courtyard stories, and the courtyard remembers them in its own way, encoding them in the pattern of wear on the stones, in the twist of the tree's bark, in the particular way the light falls at certain hours.

I have my own names for the hours. The Gray Opening, when mist rises and the world is soft. The Bright Stalking, when shadows are sharp and small things venture out. The Golden Watching, when the light turns thick and everything holds its breath. And the Blue Closing, when darkness seeps up from the stones and the courtyard becomes fully itself.

At night, I hunt the spaces between spaces. There are passages here that exist only in darkness, ways of moving through the courtyard that defy the geometry of day. I chase things that may or may not be there—shadows with their own shadows, movements that leave no trace, whispers that might be wind or might be something else entirely.

The old woman sleeps in a small room carved into the wall, a place that might once have been a storage niche or a shrine. I sometimes curl at her feet, not for warmth—I have my own heat—but for the companionship of another creature who understands the art of patient watching. In her dreams, she walks through versions of the courtyard that never were and might yet be. I walk with her sometimes, padding through imagined stones under impossible skies.

There are others who find their way here occasionally. They squeeze through gaps in the industrial fence, or descend from the rooftops on urgent errands. They are always surprised to find the courtyard, as if it shouldn't exist, as if the city's maps have forgotten to account for this pocket of different time. They never stay long. The courtyard has ways of encouraging departure—a sudden chill, a sense of being watched, the feeling that they've interrupted something important.

But they haven't interrupted anything. The important things here happen so slowly they're invisible to hurried eyes. The wearing of stone. The seepage of water. The growth of roots through cracks. The accumulation of moments into something that might be called wisdom, if wisdom were a thing that could pool in low places and grow like moss in shadow.

I am the recorder of these slow events. My memory is not like human memory, full of words and dates. Instead, I remember in my body—the exact temperature of sun-warmed stone on an autumn afternoon, the particular sound of water drops in the pool during different seasons, the way the air pressure changes before rain. I am a living archive of sensory moments, each one precisely catalogued in my nerves and fur.

Sometimes I dream of the courtyard's past—when it was newly made, when the stones were sharp-edged and the tree was just a seedling. I dream of the people who built it, their hands laying each stone with purpose we can only guess at now. And sometimes I dream of its future—roots finally splitting the stones apart, walls tumbling, the hidden made open to the sky. But mostly I dream of now, this eternal present where we exist, the old woman and I and the courtyard itself, held in a perfect balance between decay and persistence.

I am the black cat, and I am exactly where I need to be.
