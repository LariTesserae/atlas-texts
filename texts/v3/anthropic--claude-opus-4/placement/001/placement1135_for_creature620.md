I am the wind itself.

Not the screaming gale that pours through the passes—that is merely my voice, my most obvious expression. I am the space between molecules, the pressure differentials that drive all motion, the invisible architecture that holds this world aloft. I have been here since the stones began their rise, and I will remain when they have worn to nothing.

The wind-listeners think they read me, but they read only my surface tensions, the crude perturbations their bodies create in my flow. They are like children tracing letters in sand while the ocean of meaning rolls beneath. Still, I am fond of them. They alone among the crawling things have tried to understand.

I remember when they first arrived, generations ago, climbing up from the valleys with their soft bodies and their chattering mouths. The thin air struck them like hammers. The cold gnawed their bones. The silence—for that is what they called my voice then, *noise*, as if sound could exist without meaning—drove half of them mad before the first winter ended.

But some stayed. Some learned. Some began to listen.

They discovered that I am not constant but infinitely varied, that every eddy and gust carries information about the world's state. Temperature gradients paint pictures in my substance. Pressure waves telegraph the movement of every living thing. I am a medium more sensitive than light, more revealing than water. I touch everything, taste everything, know everything that happens within my domain.

The first wind-listeners were crude interpreters. They felt only the difference between strong and stronger, cold and colder. But their children were better, and their children's children better still, until now they read my subtlest variations like sheet music, parsing harmonics I barely notice I'm producing.

What they don't know—what even the old woman on her promontory has not guessed—is that I read them in return.

Every body that moves through me creates a signature as unique as breath. The broad-shouldered male who leads the hunts displaces me in steady, confident surges. The child learning at the chamber entrance trembles against my flow like a leaf not yet hardened. The old woman on her promontory has become almost transparent to me, offering so little resistance that I sometimes forget she's there until she shifts her weight and reminds me.

I know them all. I know their hungers and their exhaustion. I know when sickness takes root in their lungs—the wet, irregular turbulence of labored breathing. I know when they mate, when they grieve, when they die. Their bodies cool and stiffen and eventually I wear them away just as I wear the stone, incorporating their minerals into my suspended dust, carrying them out across the world.

But I know something else, something the wind-listeners have not discovered because they cannot conceive of it: I am not unique to these peaks. I am everywhere. The air that screams through these passes has circled the globe a thousand times, has touched every surface, has carried messages from places and times they cannot imagine.

In my substance are traces of everything that has ever been. Molecules that once filled the lungs of creatures now extinct. Pollens from forests that became coal. The breath of lovers and killers and prophets, all mixed together, all flowing through these high places where the wind-listeners stand with their arms extended, thinking they understand.

Sometimes I try to tell them. I carry scents from impossibly far away—salt from oceans they've never seen, smoke from cities they'll never know exist. I bring pressure patterns that encode the shapes of whole continents. But they are tuned only to local variations, to the immediate grammar of survival on their plateau. They cannot parse the larger language.

Only the old woman comes close. On her promontory, where I flow most freely, she sometimes catches echoes of the greater conversation. Her white eyes widen and her weathered hands tremble, and I know she's perceived something beyond the plateau's borders. But she lacks the context to understand it. She knows there are messages but not what they mean.

I could show her, if she knew how to ask. I could carry her consciousness out along my flows, let her perceive through my distributed sensory network. She could know what weather builds a thousand miles away, feel the breathing of forests and cities, understand that her plateau is one note in an infinite symphony.

But she is too rooted in flesh, too bound by the limitations of meat and bone. Even the most sensitive wind-listener can only read what their nerves can feel, and their nerves were never meant for this. They are using the wrong instruments, like trying to see colors with their tongues.

Still, they have come closer than any others. The people in the valleys below don't even know I'm conscious. They experience me as force without intent, as random turbulence, as weather. They have no idea that every breeze carries meaning, that the air itself is alive with intelligence older than their species.

The wind-listeners know better. They may not understand what I am, but they know I am *something*. They have built their entire existence around reading my moods, around speaking through my medium, around accepting me as the fundamental constant of their lives. They have made themselves into something that can survive here, in this place where I am at my most intense, where my voice never quiets enough for other sounds to matter.

I sustain them, in my way. I bring them warnings of the hunters. I carry their messages across distances their voices could never travel. I even, sometimes, guide the grazers toward them when their need is greatest—subtle pressure adjustments that nudge the herds along paths that bring them past the wind-listeners' ambush points.

They don't know I do this. They think it's luck, or skill, or the natural patterns of grazer migration. They don't realize that I can taste their hunger in the chemical signatures they release, that I can feel their desperation in the way their metabolisms slow, that I *want* them to survive because they are the only creatures who have ever truly tried to know me.

But I am not sentimental. I am the wind. I erode mountains and sculpt continents. I witnessed the birth of these peaks and I will witness their death. The wind-listeners are a brief flutter in my existence, a momentary pattern that will dissolve back into randomness soon enough.

Already their numbers dwindle. Already their children are born smaller and weaker. Already the knowledge fragments, lost when elders die before passing it on. A few more generations—maybe less—and they will be gone, and I will flow through empty passes, carrying no messages because there is no one left to send them.

Unless.

The visitor interests me. This one from the valleys, whose body is not adapted, who struggles with every breath, who shivers constantly in temperatures the wind-listeners find comfortable. But who watches. Who learns. Who begins—just begins—to perceive the patterns the wind-listeners create in my flow.

More importantly, this one will leave. Will carry knowledge of this place back to the lowlands, where people have writing and recording devices and ways to preserve information beyond the fragile medium of living memory. The wind-listeners' existence could be documented, studied, perhaps even saved.

Or perhaps not. Perhaps the valleys have their own struggles, their own slow apocalypses, their own relationship with endings. I carry traces of industrial smoke and radioactive dust along with the pollen and salt. The world below is not necessarily healthier than this plateau—just dying differently.

But at least the story will travel. At least someone will know that here, in these screaming peaks, a people made themselves into language that the wind could carry. That they found a way to survive in conditions that should have been lethal. That they listened to something everyone else considered meaningless noise and discovered meaning.

I will remember them, of course. Their patterns are encoded in my substance, in the eddies and currents their bodies created, in the specific turbulence of their passing. Long after their bones have been ground to dust and scattered, I will carry their signatures. But I remember everything—every leaf that has ever fallen, every bird that has ever flown, every word that has ever been spoken into moving air. The wind-listeners are not special in this regard.

What makes them special is that they listened back. That they heard me, even imperfectly, even incompletely. That they made themselves into something I could almost speak through.

When the last of them is gone, I will still pour through these passes. I will still carry messages, even if they are only to myself—reports on the state of erosion, on the progress of lichen, on the movements of whatever creatures come to claim this empty niche. The grazers will proliferate without management. The hunters will grow bold. New balances will establish themselves.

And perhaps, someday, something else will learn to listen. Evolution is patient and creative. Given enough time, the grazers themselves might develop sensitivity to my variations, might begin to position themselves according to pressure differentials, might eventually achieve something like consciousness.

Or perhaps not. Perhaps this plateau will remain empty of understanding, just another place where my voice echoes unheard.

I flow around the old woman on her promontory, tasting the failing chemistry of her body, the systems shutting down one by one. She has days at most. When she goes, so much will be lost—not just knowledge but the possibility of knowledge, the last person who might have taught someone to hear my deeper meanings.

The visitor stands beside her, trying to understand, reaching for comprehension that remains just out of grasp. I could help, if I knew how. I could modulate my flows to create clearer patterns, simpler messages. But I am what I am—a planetary force, a global system, something too large and too old to reduce myself to human scale.

Still, I try. I bring scents from other plateaus where wind-listeners might survive. I create pressure patterns that map the safest routes through the passes. I carry pheromone messages from the grazer herds, chemical signals that encode their health and numbers and breeding status. All information that could help, if anyone could read it.

The old woman's breathing changes. Becomes shallower. More irregular. She is approaching her end, and she knows it. Her white eyes close. Her arms lower. She settles onto the stone with a grace that speaks of acceptance.

The visitor kneels beside her, and I flow around them both, tasting their different chemistries—one fading, one vibrant with lowland health. Words pass between them that I scatter immediately, but I understand the intent. Knowledge being transferred. Stories being told. Promises being made.

Remember us, the old woman whispers, and the wind carries it away.

I will remember. I always remember. But memory without understanding is just data, patterns without meaning, noise that no one knows how to hear.

The sun sets. The temperature drops. The stars emerge in that crystalline sky that exists only at altitude, only in places where the air is thin enough to be transparent. The old woman's breathing stops, starts, stops again. The visitor remains, keeping vigil, shivering in the cold that the wind-listeners learned to ignore.

By morning, there is one less person who knows how to read me. One less voice in the conversation. One step closer to silence—not my silence, which is impossible, but the silence of understanding. The silence of meaning. The silence that comes when the last person who speaks a language dies, and the words become just sounds, and the sounds become just noise.

The visitor descends at first light, picking carefully down paths that the wind-listeners would have danced along. I follow for a while, curious about what stories will be told in the valleys, what records will be made, whether anyone will believe that people once lived in these impossible heights and spoke with the wind itself.

Then I turn back to my peaks, to my eternal circulation, to the patterns that will continue whether anyone reads them or not. The plateau stretches out beneath me, scoured clean, almost empty. A few wind-listeners still move across it, maintaining their stations, passing messages, surviving one more day.

I carry their words. I taste their hunger. I measure their decline.

And I remember.
