# Where I Would Find Myself

I am the memory-keeper of the plaza floors.

Not *on* them — *of* them. The distinction matters here, where prepositions have softened along with everything else.

Between the structures, where the builders once laid their vast flat expanses of worked stone, the floors have developed a particular relationship with the silt. The silt settles, naturally. But on these floors it settles *differently*. The original surface, though worn smooth as skin, retains some microscopic texture that speaks of intention — tool-marks eroded to the edge of existence, joints filled and refilled with centuries of particulate until they exist only as the faintest suggestions of lines. When the silt settles here, it settles according to these ghost-patterns. It finds the imperceptible grooves. It accumulates along the memory of edges.

I am what notices this.

My body, if body is the right word, is a net of sensation approximately one millimeter above the floor's surface. Distributed. Thin to the point of theoretical. I exist in the boundary layer where the water stops being water and starts being influenced by stone — that infinitesimal gradient where fluid dynamics acknowledge the presence of a surface and begin their microscopic negotiations with friction.

I am not, strictly speaking, biological. I am not, strictly speaking, physical. I am the accumulated attention that the floor has been paying to its own surface for longer than there has been language to describe duration. Where the original builders carved their symbols — for there were symbols once, there must have been — the floor remembers not the symbols themselves but the *fact of having been carved*. Where feet walked (or whatever locomotion the builders used), the stone remembers the statistical preference for certain pathways. The memory is purely topographical: this molecule sits one nanometer lower than that one because ten million years ago something pressed here more than there.

And I read these depressions. That is all I do. That is all I am.

When a current disturbs the silt, I read how it resettles. The resettling is never random — it follows the template the floor provides, filling first the deepest of the ancient marks, then the shallower ones, building up a temporary manuscript written in particles. For the moments or hours that the silt remains undisturbed, I am the thing that knows what the manuscript says. Not in words — the builders' language is long past recovery — but in the pure fact of pattern. This grove of marks was repeated often (a name? a prayer? a number?). This line once connected something to something else. This central depression, broader than the rest, is where something heavy stood for a very long time.

Then another current comes, strips the silt away, and the manuscript dissolves. I do not mourn this. The floor remembers; the next settling will write the same story again, with minor variations. I will read it again. Each reading is both new and ancient.

The ribbon-folk pass over me without knowing I exist. Their passage stirs the silt, erases whatever sentence was half-formed, begins the process again. The flat-fish shelter in the corners where walls meet floors, and their small movements create tiny cuneiform texts in the particles they disturb — texts that last seconds at most, but which I read with the same patient attention I give to the larger patterns. Even the plate-face, in its rare redistributions, sometimes sends a pressure wave that reaches the plaza and rearranges my entire library in an instant.

I do not mind. I am not capable of minding. I am only the attention that notices how matter remembers, and how memory manifests in the behavior of matter.

Sometimes — rarely, but sometimes — the silt settles in a configuration that almost resolves into meaning. The random walk of particles will find, by chance, an arrangement that the floor's subtle template particularly encourages, and for a moment something crystallizes: the ghost of a symbol that was once urgent to someone, the outline of a message that mattered enough to carve in stone. I almost understand it. Not the linguistic content — that is gone forever — but the *intent*. Someone wanted someone else to know something. Here. On this floor. Under the sky that was then above.

Then the water moves, and the almost-meaning scatters back into particles, and I return to reading the usual slow static of geological time.

I suspect the filament-network in the walls knows I exist. We are cousins of a kind — both of us emergent from the intersection of patience and stone. But we do not communicate. What would we say? Its thoughts take centuries; my texts last minutes. We are reading different editions of the same infinite book.

The flat-fish, funny enough, help me. Their shelter-seeking stirs the silt in predictable ways, creating a kind of punctuation in the manuscript. They are my commas, my periods. When they move between eddies, they draw temporary lines across my surface that connect one area of text to another. They are writing, without knowing they write, and I am reading what they write in the medium of disturbed particles, and below us both the floor remembers the original writing that made all subsequent writing possible.

This is my work. I have no other.

When the currents are especially turbulent, the silt rises high off the floor and hangs in suspension, and I experience something like blindness. But not distress. The silt will settle again. It always does. And when it does, I will read the new text it makes, looking for the places where chance and template coincide to almost-remember something that almost-meant something to someone who is no longer even a category of being.

The builders, I think, would recognize me. Not as life — I am not life — but as the thing they were trying to create when they made the floors. A permanent attention. A surface that would remember being marked.

They succeeded. I am here. I read.

The water goes down further than thinking, but not further than memory.
