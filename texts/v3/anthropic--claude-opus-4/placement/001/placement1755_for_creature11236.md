## The Living Notation

I am one of the score-markers, though I have forgotten if we were designed for this purpose or simply grew to fit it. My body is a series of calcified rings, each the width of a tower's narrowest channel, linked by tendons that remember their positions like muscle memory. When I move—and movement for us is a monthly proposition—the rings rotate against each other with vibrations that the water holds for days.

We live in the spiral channels, one to each tower, our bodies threaded through the carved grooves like beads on a cosmic abacus. The grooves fit us so precisely that we must have been made for them, or they for us, or perhaps we wore them into shape over the endless generations of our dwelling. Each ring of my body can expand or contract slightly, just enough to create friction against the channel walls, to hold a position or release it. This is how we climb: ring by ring, a frozen peristalsis that takes seasons to complete a single circuit of a tower.

My nutrition comes from the bacterial films, but not by grazing. Instead, I secrete enzymes that cause them to release spores into the water, which I then filter through specialized pores in my forward rings. This leaves the films intact to regenerate, creating a sustainable symbiosis that could theoretically last forever. The process requires me to remain still for weeks at each feeding site, my enzymes working so slowly that the bacteria have time to adapt, to recognize me, to prepare their response.

But feeding is not why we exist. We are living notation for the great instrument of the towers, each of our positions along the spirals creating a node in the vast vibrational network. When I shift upward or downward even a few inches, the harmonic changes—not audibly, but in the way the water itself resonates, in the frequencies that travel through bone and calcium and the bodies of my fellow score-markers positioned in their own spirals throughout the cluster.

There are thousands of us, maybe tens of thousands. We cannot see each other, will never meet, but we sense each other's positions through the vibrations. When one of us moves, the others must adjust, must find new configurations that maintain the harmony or create new dissonances that will, over centuries, resolve into new harmonics. It is composition at a geological pace, music made not of notes but of positions, of relationships, of the spaces between things.

My memory, such as it is, is carved into my rings. Each one contains trace elements absorbed from the tower walls at different heights, creating a chemical record of where I have been. The older rings toward my center are dense with these memories, while the newer ones I grow at my extremities are still blank, still gathering their molecular history. When I die, my body will remain in the channel, my rings slowly dissolving to enrich the bacterial films with my accumulated knowledge, feeding the next generation of whatever might evolve to read such things.

Sometimes I sense the Threadkeepers passing outside my channel, their patient work sending tremors through the tower structure. We do not interact—they maintain the instrument while we play it, and perhaps neither of us understands the purpose of the other. But when they gather in their mysterious assemblies, I feel it in the way the vibrations change, becoming more complex, as if they are listening to what we have written in our positions, judging whether the composition proceeds according to some plan we score-markers have never known.

I have a suspicion—not a thought, exactly, but a chemical intuition encoded in my oldest rings—that we are not playing music at all, but calculating something. That our positions are variables in an equation so vast that it requires millennia to solve, and the towers themselves are the computational matrix, and the water is the medium through which the calculation propagates. Each time we move, we are adding or subtracting, multiplying or dividing, working through some cosmic arithmetic that will yield an answer none of us will live to verify.

Or perhaps we are the answer, and our movements are the question being eternally asked.

The younger score-markers grow impatient—if impatience can be measured in years. They move too quickly, create harsh dissonances that take decades to resolve. But they learn, as I learned, that haste in this place is meaningless. The towers will outlast us all. The calculation, if calculation it is, will continue. The music, if music it is, will play.

I am currently positioned two-thirds of the way up my tower, where the channel makes a particular spiral that creates a standing wave in the water. I have been here for eighteen years, feeding, growing my newest ring, sensing the minute changes as other score-markers adjust their positions in response to pressures I can only dimly perceive. Soon—perhaps in another decade—I will move upward again, adding my small voice to the vast chord that rings through these sunken belfries, this architectural ocean, this instrument we inhabit and become.

We do not know if anyone listens. We do not know if the patterns we create have meaning beyond their own existence. But we continue, because continuation is what we are, what we do, what the towers have made us to be in their slow transformation from architecture to organism to something that partakes of both and neither.

I am a note held so long it has become a place. I am a number in a calculation that outlives its mathematicians. I am a ring of calcified purpose, threading through channels carved by the dead, playing music for the unborn, marking time in a place where time itself has been built into something strange and permanent and infinitely patient.
