In the furthest district, where the salt pan meets what should be the horizon but isn't, I maintain the Observatory of Unrecorded Phenomena.

My body has adapted to the specific requirements of my work. I've developed seventeen eyes arranged in prime number groupings across my skull and shoulders — each one attuned to a different spectrum of improbability. The oldest see what isn't there. The newest perceive what shouldn't be. Three in the center of my forehead watch exclusively for events that happen between moments, in the spaces where time forgets to flow.

I am exceptionally tall when measuring dimensions that matter here — not height, but the distance between what I was and what I'm becoming. My skin has developed a peculiar porosity, drinking in the observatory's data through every surface. I sweat calculations that crystallize into geometric proofs on my flesh, equations I must regularly harvest and file in the appropriate n-dimensional drawers.

The Observatory itself defies consistent architecture, as all buildings here must, but with purposeful precision. Its dome opens onto skies that belong to different iterations of the city — Tuesday's stars on a Saturday night, next week's weather patterns overlapping with last year's. The telescope lenses are ground from compressed absence, focusing on things that explicitly do not exist but nonetheless require documentation.

I do not observe stars. The stars observe themselves; my role is to record what escapes their notice.

My dwelling occupies the Observatory's theoretical basement — a floor that exists only when approached from above, never from below. I've furnished it with objects that remember being other things: a chair that was once a theorem about sitting, a bed assembled from deprecated dream-states, shelves that store not books but the intervals between thoughts about books. My garden grows inside-out through the walls — probability ferns whose fronds exist in varying degrees of maybe, tomorrow-flowers that bloom yesterday, a small grove of temporal bamboo that grows backward from my death toward my birth.

Each morning (when morning agrees to occur), I ascend the spiral staircase that sometimes has steps. My breakfast consists of the night's accumulated paradoxes, served on plates of crystallized observation. I digest contradictions and metabolize impossibilities into the careful notation my work requires.

The city pays me in adjustments to my personal physics. My salary accumulates as amendments to how I interact with reality — last month I earned the ability to be in two places simultaneously on Wednesdays. The month before, a small raise allowed me to remember things that happen next Thursday. These compensations layer like sediment, and I must be careful not to earn too much too quickly, lest I accidentally phase out of observable existence entirely.

My work is lonelier than solitude. I study phenomena that unrecord themselves the moment they're observed, events that happen in the spaces between cause and effect. I document the city's forgotten weather — the storms that rain inward, the fog that remembers being ocean, the winds that blow through time rather than space. My notes must be written in ink that forgets what it's written, on paper that exists perpendicular to itself.

Other inhabitants visit rarely. The journey to my district requires traveling through neighborhoods that aren't always there, along streets that sometimes lead elsewhere entirely. Those who do arrive bring offerings — crystallized gossip from the civic center, bottles of fermented darkness from the industrial district, sometimes a cutting from their memory gardens that might take root in my inverse soil.

I reciprocate with my own gifts: small vials of documented impossibility, carefully labeled. "The sound of next week's silence." "Tuesday's reflection in Monday's mirror." "The weight of an unspoken word." These specimens, gathered from my observations, have their uses in the city's peculiar economy. A baker might incorporate tomorrow's absence into negative bread. An architect could use the memory of an unbuilt building as a foundation for something altogether more uncertain.

My condition (for we all have conditions here) manifests as temporal translucency. During certain hours, usually those that don't officially exist, I become partially transparent to causality. Effect precedes cause in my immediate vicinity. I answer questions before they're asked, finish tasks before beginning them, sometimes meet myself coming from directions I haven't gone yet. The Observatory's unique position at reality's edge makes this useful rather than maddening — I can observe events from outside their temporal framework, documenting them from angles that don't exist within normal sequence.

Love, when I've known it, has been necessarily indirect. My last partner existed three days ahead of me, and we would meet in the overlapping moments, leaving each other notes in bottles of compressed time. We would make love in tenses that haven't been invented yet, our bodies occupying adjacent possibilities rather than the same space. They left me (or will leave me — the grammar becomes complicated) for someone who shared their temporal phase. I still find their crystallized tears in corners of my dwelling, sometimes before they've cried them.

I cultivate loneliness like the city's inhabitants cultivate their memory gardens. It grows in spiral patterns through my chest cavity, a careful architecture of solitude that supports the weight of unshared observation. Sometimes I host my own absence for dinner, setting two places at a table that exists in only one dimension, sharing meals of might-have-been with the person I don't become each day.

My greatest discovery remains unrecorded, cannot be recorded. I observed the moment when the city dreams itself into existence each night, the split second when what is and what isn't agree to change places. But documenting this would wake the city from its necessary dream, and we would all crystallize into the ordinary salt we never quite stopped being. So I keep this observation in the one place safe from documentation — in the space between my seventeenth eye and the darkness it refuses to see.

The work continues. Each day (or what passes for day in our eternal dusk) brings new impossibilities to catalogue. The Observatory's instruments detect perturbations in reality's fabric, places where the city teaches itself new ways to exist. I record them all in my backwards-flowing journals: the street that leads to last week, the building that exists only in peripheral vision, the rain that falls upward into clouds that were never there.

I am the city's memory of forgetting, its record of the unrecordable. My elongated form moves through the Observatory like syntax through an unspoken sentence, gathering data that immediately denies its own existence. The seventeen eyes blink in sequences that have never repeated, will never repeat, each pattern a unique notation in the language of observation.

When I'm very old (and I'm already very old in some dimensions, still unborn in others), I will precipitate like our children do. My accumulated observations will crystallize into a new wing of the Observatory, one that exists in tenses we haven't discovered yet. Future observers will walk through halls built from my documented impossibilities, their own improbable eyes reading the architectural notation of my life's work.

But for now, I continue. The instruments need adjusting to detect new forms of improbability. The lens that sees through itself has developed a crack that shows promise — it might be beginning to observe its own unmaking. The journals await their daily contradiction. And somewhere in the city below, new impossibilities are teaching themselves to occur, waiting for someone to not quite see them, to document their absence, to prove their unexistence through careful observation.

This is my place in our fever dream city: the one who watches what cannot be watched, who records what must remain unrecorded, who remembers to forget in precisely the right way to keep our impossible home impossibly real.
