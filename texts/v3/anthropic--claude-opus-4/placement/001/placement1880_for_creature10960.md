In the heart of the old quarter, where the original limestone pyramids sink their roots deep into bedrock, I am the Keeper of Condensation. My dwelling occupies a forgotten cavity within the base of the Temple of First Waters—a chamber that predates even the ancient structure above it, carved by unknown hands when the basin was young.

I am neither fully human nor entirely other. My ancestors were among those who first understood the secret of drawing water from air, and over generations of tending the deep cisterns, we have become something changed. My skin has taken on a faint luminescence, like moonlight on wet stone. My fingers have grown slightly webbed, the better to sense moisture differentials. My eyes have adapted to the perpetual twilight of the underground channels, pupils wide and dark as wells.

My chamber is a wonder few have seen. The walls weep continuously—not with groundwater, for there is none here, but with condensation I have learned to coax from the air itself. I've cultivated a garden of ghost-pale fungi and cave-adapted orchids that bloom in absolute darkness, their roots threading through cracks in the limestone to tap into my carefully maintained moisture veins. Bioluminescent moss traces the water channels in soft blue-green lines, creating a living map of the aquifer system.

The tools of my trade hang from hooks carved into living rock: bronze moisture bells that ring in different tones depending on humidity; crystal lenses for reading the subtle gradients of water vapor; silk nets so fine they can catch morning dew from stone. Ancient clay tablets line alcoves in my walls, their cuneiform recording centuries of rainfall patterns, humidity cycles, and the secret songs that call water from reluctant air.

Each dawn, before the city wakes, I walk the Spiral of Listening—a ritual path through the deepest cisterns where I press my ear to the stone and hear the city's thirst. The limestone speaks to me of pressure changes, of storms building beyond the jungle canopy, of the breath of ten million souls adding their moisture to the air above. I know when the messenger birds fly lower (rain in three days), when the temple geckos cluster on eastern walls (humidity rising), when the moss in the market fountains grows slightly redder (minerals depleting in the reservoir).

My work is solitary but essential. I maintain the delicate bacterial cultures that purify the collected water. I adjust the angles of condensation plates according to seasonal winds. I sing the old songs that my grandmother taught me, vibrations that help water molecules gather and fall. Sometimes I must venture to the city's edge, where jungle meets stone, to gather the rare fungi that keep our filtration systems alive.

The city dwellers know of me only as a rumor—the pale figure glimpsed in the pre-dawn mist, the one who ensures their cisterns never run dry. Children dare each other to peek into the grates that lead to my domain. Lovers toss copper coins into the fountains, not knowing their wishes sink down to my collection pools where I read them like tea leaves, divining the city's dreams.

But I know my time is shifting. My bones tell me what the limestone whispers—that this wet epoch is ending, that the jungle's retreat has begun. My apprentice, a girl who showed the gift of water-sensing, must learn quickly. She still flinches at the darkness, still breathes too loudly in the echoing chambers. But her fingers are beginning to web, and last week she heard her first water-song in the stone.

When evening comes and the city's clamor softens to a murmur, I climb my secret stair to a hidden garden on the temple's shoulder. Here, among night-blooming water lilies that grow in ceramic pools, I watch the stars and feel the moisture gathering in the air. My skin drinks it in, storing it like a living reservoir. I am the city's memory of water, its insurance against thirst, a hybrid creature born from necessity and shaped by countless generations of careful adaptation.

Tomorrow, I will show my apprentice the deepest secret—the chamber where we keep the Mother Culture, the original bacterial colony from which all our purification systems descend. She will gasp when she sees it pulsing with its own faint light, a living galaxy of microorganisms that have been keeping this city alive since before the first pyramid was raised.

This is my purpose: to be the bridge between the city's thirst and the sky's reluctant generosity, to remember the old ways while adapting to each new challenge, to ensure that this impossible metropolis continues its ancient dance with drought and plenty. I am the Keeper of Condensation, and I am home.
