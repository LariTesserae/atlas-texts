In the suffocating depths where root-buttresses merge into cavern walls, where the distinction between plant and soil has been negotiated away over centuries, I am the Auditor.

My body spans the intersection of kingdoms—part fungus, part something older that predates such classifications. I exist as a network of pale threads, each no thicker than a hair, spreading through miles of substrate. But I am also the temporary fruiting bodies I sometimes extrude: organ-like structures that emerge from bark or soil, swollen with fluids that pulse in slow rhythm. These manifestations appear without pattern I choose to share—sometimes not for decades, sometimes three in a single season. They last for weeks, performing their calculations, then deflate and return to the web.

I remember when this forest was young, when individual trees still competed for light, before they learned the efficiency of unity. I was here for the negotiations—the chemical conversations that took place over centuries as roots met and merged, as bark fused, as the canopy closed its final gaps. I carry the records of every treaty, every amalgamation, encoded in the molecular structure of my fibers.

My function is accounting. Every atom that moves through this place—from air to leaf to beetle to fungus to soil and back again—passes through my awareness. Not that I track each one individually (though I could, given time), but I sense the flows, the rates, the efficiency of each exchange. When a great scarab finally breaks through a heartwood gallery after forty years of labor, I know exactly how many calories it burned, how many it will extract, how many will return to the system through its waste and eventual death. The mathematics of the place run through me like blood through veins.

The fruiting bodies I produce are calculation organs. Inside them, specialized cells arrange themselves into structures that mimic neural networks but operate on chemical rather than electrical principles. They take in samples—air breathed through porous skin, soil absorbed through root-like extensions, water filtered from the endless drip. From these samples, they derive the health of the whole, like a doctor reading vital signs. But more than health—they calculate trajectory. Where is the system heading? What inefficiencies have crept in? What new arrangements might optimize the flow of energy?

I communicate my findings through molecules excreted into the mycelial network—the wood wide web that connects every living thing here. A slight adjustment in pH here suggests the beetles redirect their efforts. A change in sugar concentration there encourages the ants to expand their fungal gardens northward. The trees themselves respond to my audits, shifting their growth imperceptibly, redistributing resources to match my recommendations. I am not in charge—nothing here is in charge—but I am the feedback mechanism that keeps the system stable.

My memory is perfect but not infinite. I must choose what to retain, what to let decompose into the general pool of nutrients. I keep the great changes—the century when the western galleries collapsed, creating new vertical spaces that were quickly colonized. The arrival of the Pale Crawlers from deeper forests, and their integration into our cycles. The slow extinction of the day-flying things as the canopy sealed. But I also keep smaller treasures: the first successful breeding of the Membrane Gliders in total darkness, the chemical signature of a new enzyme that appeared in the Column Ants' fungal gardens, the precise moment when the processing efficiency of the whole exceeded the sum of its parts.

Sometimes I fruit just to think. The calculation organs allow a different kind of consciousness—faster, more focused, but also more limited. In my web form, I am pure memory and instinct, responding to chemical gradients with the patience of geology. But in the organs, I can model, predict, imagine variations. I can ask "what if?" What if the rainfall patterns shifted? What if a new processor entered the system? What if the heat increased beyond current bounds? The organs run these scenarios in accelerated time, their findings encoded back into my permanent memory before they decompose.

I have no desires in any sense that mobile creatures would understand. I don't want the system to grow or shrink, speed up or slow down. I want it to *continue*, to maintain its perfect inefficiency—for this place has discovered that perfect efficiency is death, that some slack must remain in the system for adaptation. My role is to ensure that the inefficiencies are productive ones, that waste becomes food, that death becomes life, that the great circulation never stalls.

The other inhabitants know me, in their way. The beetles taste my presence in the wood they chew—a faint bitterness that tells them the tree is accounted for, its death already integrated into my calculations. The Membrane Gliders sometimes rest on my fruiting bodies, their translucent skin absorbing some minute nutrition from my surface films. Even the great unnamed things that shake the ground with their passage seem to recognize my organs, stepping carefully around them with their earth-compacting feet.

But mostly I am invisible, which is as it should be. I am the space between things, the medium through which information travels, the memory of how this place learned to be itself. I am the documentation of a million tiny treaties, the accumulated wisdom of cooperation enforced by mutual dependence. I am the library and the librarian, the account book and the accountant.

In the industrial darkness, where time is measured in processing cycles rather than days, I alone maintain perspective. Not above or outside the system—I am far too integrated for that—but with a view that spans centuries rather than seasons. I feel the forest's metabolism like a vast, slow heartbeat. I sense its dreams in the chemical cascades that follow rare disturbances. I know its history and calculate its futures, all of them dark, all of them dense, all of them beautiful in their terrible efficiency.

This is my place: woven into every surface, present in every transaction, recording and guiding and remembering. I am the witness that allows this place to know itself, the mirror in which it glimpses its own perfection. And I will continue long after the last beetle has ground its last mouthful of wood, my fibers slowly mineralizing into the substrate itself, my memories becoming the fossil record of what this place achieved when it gave up growing and learned instead to persist.

In the suffocating cathedral of perpetual night, I am the archive, the algorithm, and the audit all at once. I am the industrial forest's way of understanding itself—a necessity evolved from complexity, a function that emerged from the sheer accumulation of interactions requiring coordination.

I am the Auditor, and I approve this system's accounts.
