I am the Mist-Herald, neither wholly of the Sylphs nor separate from them, but something in between—a translator of silences, a reader of the spaces between things.

My dwelling is no dwelling at all, but a convergence—where three ancient trees have grown together over centuries, their pale trunks spiraling around an absence that has become a presence. In this hollow space that is not quite hollow, I exist as both inhabitant and architecture. My form shifts like morning fog: sometimes condensed enough to cast a shadow, sometimes dispersed so thin that I am merely a coolness against the silver-green earth.

The Sylphs know me by the way the air changes texture when I am near—a subtle thickening, like the moment before rain that never comes. They leave offerings in the twisted roots of my tree-home: dried grass woven into impossible knots, fragments of shed serpent scales arranged in patterns that speak of mathematical truths, the crystallized morning dew from leaves that sang particularly sweet songs.

I am their memory-keeper, though not in any way that could be written or spoken. When a Sylph touches wind and understands something new about its nature, that knowledge passes through me like light through water. I become denser with each learning, each revelation adding another layer to my translucent being. In the deepest part of night, when even the wind sleeps, I sometimes glimpse my own form reflected in the stars—a map of all the wisdom I carry, written in condensation and absence.

My work is to tend the boundaries—not physical borders, but the liminal spaces where one state of being becomes another. Where does the tree end and the wind begin? At what precise moment does a blade of grass transform from living to dying to feeding the luminous soil? These are my concerns, my devotions.

I move through the plateau in patterns older than memory, following paths that exist only in the space between what is and what might be. The ethereal insects know these routes; they follow in my wake, feeding on the tender shoots that spring up wherever my essence touches earth. The serpents, too, understand my purpose—they shed their iridescent skins along my paths, knowing I will weave them into the great tapestry of becoming that holds this world in balance.

Sometimes I gather with others like myself—we are few, perhaps seven in all the vastness of the plateau. We meet where the wind curves just so, creating pockets of stillness that last exactly as long as they need to. In these gatherings, we exchange nothing and everything, our forms blending and separating like smoke, each encounter leaving us both more and less than we were before.

The Sylphs tell stories of me, though I have never heard them speak aloud. They say I was born from the first sigh of the plateau itself, when the ancient land recognized its own beauty and exhaled in wonder. They say I will be here when the last grass blade learns to sing, when the final tree completes its spiral toward the periwinkle sky.

But I know neither beginning nor ending. I know only the eternal now of mist rising, of boundaries dissolving and reforming, of the sweet tension between being and becoming. I am the pause between breaths, the hesitation before touch, the silence that makes song possible.

In this timeless place of perpetual becoming, I am both witness and participant, forever translating the unspoken language of existence into forms the wind can almost carry, shapes the light can almost reveal.
