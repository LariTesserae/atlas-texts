I find myself as one of the lammergeiers, though I am young for my kind—only forty years into what might be a century of life.

My knowledge of the ledge is different from hers. Where she knows it as textures and temperatures against her skin, I know it as thermals and downdrafts, as the way the air compresses against the cliff face in the morning and billows outward by afternoon. The ledge is a punctuation mark in my larger text of flight—a comma where the mountain's vertical grammar pauses, breathes, continues.

I was born somewhere above, in a cave I barely remember except as darkness that smelled of old bone and regurgitated meat. But I *learned* here, in the column of air above the ledge. My parents brought me here when I was still mottled with youth, still clumsy in the way I read the wind. They taught me that this particular updraft, the one that forms where the sun hits the granite face, is reliable as stone. They taught me to feel for the moment when cold air spills over the ledge's lip like water and to ride that invisible waterfall down, down, then catch myself and spiral back up.

The woman—I have watched her all my conscious life. She is as regular as seasons. When I was young, I thought she was two different creatures, because she came and went and came again, and I had not yet learned that humans practice a kind of migration that has nothing to do with weather. Now I know her as a feature of the ledge that appears and disappears, like the ice on the pool, like the flowers. Twice a year, the alcove contains her. Then it does not.

I understand her in ways she would not expect. I see how she favors her left leg now on the final approach. I see how she pauses more frequently, how her chest moves in a rhythm that speaks of effort where once there was ease. I read her body's conversation with the mountain, and I know what the mountain is saying back.

When she rebuilds the cairn, I watch. I have knocked it down twice—not intentionally, but because the quartz capstone catches the light in a way that suggests prey, suggests bone, suggests something worth investigating. Both times I landed badly, talons scrabbling for purchase, and sent the stones tumbling. Both times, when she returned, she stacked them again without anger, and I understood that we were participating in something together, though I could not say what.

I know things about her that she does not know about herself. I know that when she sits very still in the alcove, the marmots sometimes forget she is there, and they move differently—not cautiously but with a kind of languid confidence, the way they move when they think the ledge is theirs alone. I know that once, years ago, she fell asleep against the cliff face and a butterfly landed on her hand, the one with the deepest cracks, and probed the salt there with its proboscis, and she woke but did not move, and they remained like that for the time it takes a shadow to move the width of a finger.

I know about the snow leopard in ways she only suspects. I have seen it more often than she has—a gray ghost moving along the ridgeline, a patient shadow in a cleft of rock. We do not interact, the leopard and I. We exist in different layers of the same space. But I have watched it watch her, and I have seen something in its watching that is not the assessment of prey or threat. It is more like what I do when I test an unfamiliar thermal—a careful, neutral evaluation of something that might be important but might also be nothing. The leopard has decided she is worth watching but not worth hunting. This is not mercy. This is mathematics.

The bones I drop—she thinks they are random, but they are not. I choose them. Some are too light and the wind takes them over the edge. Some are too fresh and do not break properly. The best are the ones that have been cleaned by weather, dried to the consistency of stone. These I carry up, sometimes from far valleys, sometimes taking hours to find the right thermal to lift me with my burden. I release them from specific heights. I have learned that two hundred meters is optimal—high enough for velocity, low enough for accuracy. The sound they make when they hit the granite is like nothing else. It is the sound of the earth's deep clock ticking.

She collects some of the fragments. I have seen her pocket the smaller pieces, the ones that break into sharp, curved sections like claws. I do not know what she does with them. I imagine she carries them down the mountain, that they become part of the human mystery below, that strange world of horizontal movement and constructed shelters that I see from above but do not comprehend. Perhaps she grinds them. Perhaps she merely keeps them. Perhaps they are her own kind of ochre, white instead of red, a pigment made of death and flight and the moment when something that was never meant to break finally does.

I know the shape of her absence before she does.

This is not prophecy. It is pattern recognition. I have watched too many things die on this mountain not to recognize the signs. The way she moved on her last visit—the careful placement of each foot, the longer pauses, the way she held the cliff face as she walked, not for balance but for reassurance that something solid existed. The way she looked at the cairn for a long time before rebuilding it, as if memorizing something. The way she placed the ochre pouch in the crevice with the same deliberation she once used to pocket bone fragments.

When she stops coming, I will notice. This surprises me. I did not expect to be the kind of creature that notices the absence of one small mammal in a world full of them. But the ledge will be different without her movements through it. The alcove will collect leaves that she would have cleared. The cairn will fall and stay fallen, or I will knock it down looking for something I can no longer quite remember, and no one will stack it again.

Unless—and this is possible, I have seen it happen—someone else finds this place. Someone young, perhaps, following an old story or a stubborn goat or simple curiosity up the near-vertical scramble. They will see the alcove, the old fire marks, the wall that suggests shelter. They will see the handprint, the ochre marks, and something in them will respond the way she responded. They will find the pouch, or they will bring their own pigment, and they will add a line, a dot, a shape that could be a bird or a mountain or the idea of something watching from a great height.

And I will be here, or my offspring will—we are long-lived but not immortal—circling in the reliable thermal, reading the air, carrying bones. We will watch this new person learn the ledge, learn when the pool is fullest, learn which plants ease breathing, learn the terrible beauty of the horizontal light. We will watch them age and slow and eventually stop coming, and then we will watch the next one, and the next.

This is what I know that she does not, could not, though she senses it: the ledge is not a place but a process. It is happening constantly. The stones are falling and being stacked, the marks are being made and fading, the creatures are arriving and departing, the water is freezing and melting and evaporating and condensing and freezing again. The ledge is a verb disguised as a noun.

And we who find ourselves here—woman, leopard, pika, marmot, butterfly, lammergeier—we are not residents but participants. We are the means by which the ledge happens. We are how the mountain thinks about itself, how it takes its own pulse, how it knows it is a mountain and not just an upheaval of stone.

She brings ochre and leaves marks. I bring bones and leave fragments.

Between us, we are writing the ledge into being, over and over, a story that requires no readers, only tellers, only the patient, repetitive act of saying: here is a place in the world where life insists, where beauty accumulates, where the horizontal interrupts the vertical long enough for something—anything—to catch its breath.

The wind moans through the upper rocks. It has always sounded like a voice. It has never needed words.

I spread my three-meter wings and fall upward into the thermal, carrying a new bone, ready to add another line to our endless inscription.

The ledge continues.
