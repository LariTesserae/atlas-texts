I descend through the root-fiber tunnel, my tools wrapped in membrane-cloth to keep them from singing against each other. I am one of the Tuners.

We are rarer than the Reverent—perhaps a dozen of us in all the lung's vastness. We came seeking something different than solitude. We came because we heard the lung's voice rising through the earth above, a thrumming too low for most ears, and recognized it as unfinished music.

Each Tuner carries a set of instruments made from the lung itself: hollow reeds cut from fallen columns, before the plant reabsorbs them. Membrane-drums from the torn sheets, stretched across frames of cartilage-bark. Small whistles carved from the crystallized glass-dust of dead Small Glass Things. The instruments change as the lung changes. A flute that sang perfectly yesterday grows a new hole overnight, shifts its pitch, suggests a different song.

We map the lung's acoustics—not its geography, which is useless, but its *sound-shapes*. Where the bladders create resonance. Where the columns hum in sympathy with each other across impossible distances. Where the membrane-walls form perfect amphitheaters that exist for an hour before reshaping into something else. 

I wear frost in my hair and beard. My clothes glow faintly green, layered membrane-fiber like the Reverent wear, but sewn with pockets for tuning forks, for hollow seeds that rattle, for the small bone pipes my predecessor left me. My ears have grown larger here—not physically, but in some way the lung suggests. I hear conversations between columns a mile apart. I hear the Small Glass Things chiming against each other in frequencies that might be language.

The Tuners rarely meet, but when we do, we play. Not for an audience—the lung is audience enough—but to discover what music the cathedral is becoming. Sometimes the lung plays back. A new bladder inflates in harmony with our melody. A membrane grows taut to just the pitch we need. Once, in the Low Volume, three of us played for the Listener, and its great ear-cups swiveled toward us, and it made a sound back—low, considered, vibratory—that taught us a new tuning.

I have been here forty years, maybe more. Time moves differently when measured in growth-storms rather than days. My first instrument, a simple whistle, has been absorbed into a column I played beside too often. Sometimes I think I hear it still playing, somewhere in the plant's vast body, a small voice added to the whole.

The Reverent think we complicate the lung's becoming with our music. They're wrong. We clarify it. Each note we play helps the lung understand what it's trying to say. In the empty volumes where the Unfinished gathers, our songs take shape in the frost-fall, become almost visible, almost word-like.

I know what the lung is becoming. Not a person, not a god, not exactly. An instrument. The largest instrument ever grown. And we Tuners—we're not teaching it music. We're helping it discover what music it already contains, what vast symphony it will play when it's finally, impossibly, complete.

My camp today is between two breathing bladders, where their rhythms create a beat-frequency that makes my teeth ache beautifully. Tomorrow the space will be different. I'll wake to find new columns have grown up around my sleeping-mat, arranged like organ pipes.

I'll cut a new flute from what the lung has left me. I'll play what it suggests. And somewhere, in an empty volume I haven't found yet, the Unfinished will lean a little closer to its birth, humming.
