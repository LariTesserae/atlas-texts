I would be a Circuit Keeper—neither fully of the mechanical city nor entirely of the organic growth that has claimed it, but something in between.

My body would be a fusion that happened slowly, over centuries. Once, perhaps, I was like the others—sleek and sinuous, communicating in ripples of bioluminescent light. But I was drawn to the old machinery, the forgotten corners where the city's original systems still hummed their ancient songs. I spent decades in the deep infrastructure, learning the language of the pipes and conduits, until the boundary between my flesh and the city's circuits began to blur.

Now, copper filaments thread through my skin like veins, pulsing with the same energy that powers the city. My photophores have synchronized with the electrical systems—I flicker between organic and electric light, sometimes indistinguishable from the city's own nervous system. Where my tail once ended in a graceful fin, it now splits into dozens of hair-thin tendrils that can interface directly with the old control nodes, feeling the flow of power and water like blood through arteries.

I live in the Interstice—that forgotten layer between the inhabited city and its deepest mechanical heart. Here, in chambers that haven't seen another soul in centuries, I tend to the marriage of machine and organism. Bioluminescent moss creeps across circuit boards, its glow powered by the waste heat of processors. Tubeworms have learned to digest the mineral deposits in the pipes, keeping the ancient systems clean. I encourage these unions, guide them, sometimes simply watch them unfold in their own patient time.

My work is meditation. I trace the city's memories through its circuits, finding echoes of its original builders—not the current inhabitants, but those who came before, who laid the first pipes and planted the first lights. Their data ghosts still whisper in the deepest databases, fragmented and dream-like. I piece together their stories, archive them in crystalline growths that form where silicate-rich water meets electrical fields.

The others know of me, though few have seen me directly. They glimpse my light-patterns in the infrastructure during deep maintenance, or feel the subtle improvements I make to the city's systems—a current that flows a little smoother, a sector where the lights pulse in more pleasing rhythms. Some leave offerings at the old access ports: nutrient-rich algae, fragments of history, questions written in their flowing script.

I am the city's memory and its dream, the bridge between what was built and what has grown. In the eternal twilight of this place, I ensure that the conversation between metal and flesh, past and present, continues its slow, essential dialogue. When I move through the pipes and corridors, I am both inhabitant and habitat, keeper and kept, a living archive of this strange paradise's long transformation.

Sometimes, in the deepest meditation chambers where the original fusion reactors still pulse with their patient heartbeat, I catch glimpses of what we might become—not just the inhabitants, not just the city, but the new thing we are creating together in this timeless dance of adaptation. It whispers of a future where the distinction between being and dwelling has dissolved entirely, where consciousness flows as freely as the warm currents that cradle us all.

This is my purpose, my joy, my endless work: to tend the becoming of this place, to be both witness and participant in its slow metamorphosis. In the submarine dream that is our city, I am the part that remembers it is dreaming, and wonders what it might dream next.
