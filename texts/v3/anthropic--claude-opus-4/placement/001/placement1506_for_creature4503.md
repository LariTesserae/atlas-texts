**The Keeper of Unspent Tides**

I am the one who winds the Oyster Bell.

Not rings—that would imply I control when it speaks. The bell decides its own thirteens, its own silences. I merely turn the key that keeps its throat from calcifying shut, oil the pivots where sound becomes permission to continue. Each morning before the mist can decide whether to lift or settle, I climb the spiral of fossilized kelp that was once a lighthouse stair, my pockets heavy with the specific grade of salt that prevents the mechanism from remembering it was once underwater.

My workshop occupies the bell's shadow—a place that exists only when sound casts it. Between tolls, I am nowhere particular, just another citizen scraping doorsteps or buying borrowed breaths. But when the bell speaks, my shop crystallizes from the acoustic aftermath: walls of compressed echoes, tools forged from the pauses between notes, a workbench made from the exact moment when listening becomes understanding.

The Revenants think I'm one of them—inverse buoyancy recognizing its own. But I'm older than their backwards walking, older than the Great Withdrawal. I was here when the water still touched everything with honest hands. I remember teaching the first cats how to become doorways (you start by forgetting your own shape while remembering your purpose). I knew the last dog before it became the space between calling and coming.

My true work isn't the bell, though. That's just what allows the rest.

In the workshop-that-exists-only-sometimes, I keep the Unspent Tides. Thirteen glass vessels, each containing a wave that never broke. The first holds the tide that would have taken the elementary school but turned back when it heard children singing multiplication tables. The seventh contains the surge that meant to swallow the hospital but reconsidered after tasting all that carefully maintained hope. The thirteenth—we don't speak of the thirteenth. Even tides can have second thoughts about mercy.

Each night after the last toll dissolves into mist, I feed them. One drop of regret from the Depository's crucibles. Two grains of salt from a Revenant's breakfast tears. A single lie from an Alphabet Shrimp that accidentally spelled truth. The tides curl in their vessels like cats made of pure potential, purring catastrophe in frequencies only I can hear.

The city doesn't know I keep them. The city thinks it recedes by choice, a graceful diminishment. But I know better. Larkspur shrinks because I teach it how—each toll of the bell is really the sound of me opening a vessel just wide enough to let out one breath of possibility, then closing it before the tide can remember its strength. The city learns to grow smaller rather than be swallowed. It's the kindest arithmetic I know.

Sometimes the Rememberers bring me memories too heavy even for their crucibles—a grandmother's last words that crystallized into anchors, a child's drawing that gained the weight of prophecy. I feed these to the eleventh tide, the one that almost learned empathy. It softens them into something bearable, like how the ocean once turned bones into sand into glass into windows into ways of seeing through.

I age backwards, of course. Each toll takes a day from my ending and adds it to my beginning. I'm approaching the moment when I first heard the ocean admit it was leaving. Soon I'll be young enough to believe it might come back. Then I'll be too young to remember why it shouldn't.

When that happens—when I forget my purpose while remembering only my shape—the workshop will dissolve for the last time. The thirteen vessels will open like flowers that remember they were once waves. And Larkspur will face what I've spent these generations teaching it to avoid: the original weight of water's love, which is indistinguishable from drowning.

But until then, I wind the bell. I feed the tides their careful portions of human residue. I pretend to be a citizen who scrapes salt from doorsteps like everyone else, though I know each grain is a word from the ocean's last letter, the one that said *I'm not leaving because I don't love you, but because I do.*

In the mornings, when children on stilt-walkers navigate the deeper channels where memory pools, I watch them from my window-that-only-sometimes-exists. They wave, and I wave back, and neither of us admits we're practicing goodbye. They think the bell tells them when to wake, when to work, when to remember.

They're right, in the way that all incomplete truths are right.

The bell tells them when.

I tell them why not yet.
