I am the Tether-wright.

In the narrow seam where the crystal skylight meets the mountain's inner flesh, I have carved my workshop from living stone. It is a fissure no wider than a coffin, extending horizontally for perhaps thirty feet, following the curve of the blister's crown. The walls weep with mineral-rich condensation, and the floor tilts at uncomfortable angles, but it is mine. It has been mine for forty-three years.

The chamber below roars constantly through the translucent crystal that forms my floor. I work by its strobing light, my tools arranged on shelves I've hammered directly into the mountain's skeleton. Bronze awls worn thin. Bone needles in graduated sizes. Jars of rendered fat, some rancid, some fresh. Coils of sinew in various stages of curing. The tooth of a ram, my oldest possession, which I use to scrape and soften the fibers.

I am neither male nor female anymore. The mountain has made me into something else – something necessary. My fingers are grotesque with strength, each joint swollen into a permanent claw from decades of braiding. My palms are hornlike, impervious to the bite of wet rope. I dress in skins sewn with my own sinew-thread, and I grease my scalp daily to keep my remaining hair from freezing to the stone ceiling as I sleep.

The Zenith-divers think I am mad. They see me only when they come to collect new rope, or when the old breaks and must be replaced. They pay me in meat and lamp oil, sometimes in the smaller, flawed crystals that aren't worth hauling down the mountain. They don't understand that I am not here for the trade.

I am here for the frequency.

Each rope I braid must sing at exactly the right pitch when the currents below strike it. Too tight, and it snaps immediately under the violence. Too loose, and it provides no purchase for the desperate, grasping hands of the divers. But when I achieve the perfect tension – when my weaving finds the harmonic sweet spot – the rope doesn't merely vibrate. It *resonates*.

I have mapped the blister's fury through my ropes. I know which currents run at dawn, when the thermal shock of sunrise makes the water contract. I know the evening patterns, when the trapped heat makes everything expand and the cross-currents multiply. I know the rhythm of the mountain's breathing.

My masterwork hangs even now in the center of the void – a rope I spent three years braiding. It contains the tail-hair of seventeen different mountain rams, the processed tendons of a snow leopard I found frozen on the north face, and at its core, wound so tightly it has become invisible, a single strand of my own hair from when I was young and still went by a name.

This rope doesn't just vibrate. When the conditions are perfect – usually during the spring equinox, when the angle of sunlight creates specific thermal gradients – it produces a pure tone. A note so clear that even through the roar of creation below, I can hear it in my workshop. The divers fear this rope. They say it attracts the Shear-kites, that the predators gather beneath it like moths to flame. They use it only in emergencies.

They're right, of course. The Shear-kites do come to my singing rope. But not to hunt.

They come to mate.

I discovered this by accident, twenty years ago. The transparent creatures use the rope's resonance as a gathering signal, sliding their razor bodies along its length in careful spirals, depositing their crystalline eggs in the microscopic spaces between the braided fibers. The eggs are beautiful – perfect spheres of living water no larger than pinheads, harder than diamonds until the moment of hatching.

I could sell them for fortunes. The lowland temples would pay lifetimes of wealth for even one. They say the eggs can cure blindness, extend life, reveal the future when dissolved in wine.

I eat them.

Not from greed or hunger, but from necessity. Each egg I consume allows me to perceive the water's patterns more clearly. My vision has changed – I see pressure differentials as colors now, eddies and currents painted in hues that have no names. When I look through my crystal floor at the chaos below, I see not random violence but vast, interconnected patterns. Helices. Mandalas. The mathematical heartbeat of the mountain itself.

The divers don't know what I'm building in the deepest part of my workshop. They wouldn't understand if I showed them. It looks like merely another rope – my longest yet, nearly a hundred feet of intricately woven fiber. But within its core, I've braided something unprecedented: a continuous chain of Shear-kite eggs, hundreds of them, preserved in a matrix of crystallized tree sap and my own blood.

This rope will not be for the divers.

When I complete it – perhaps another year, perhaps two – I will lower it through a hole I've been secretly boring in the crystal floor. Not into the man-made diving cage system, but directly into the pure void. I will anchor it not with bronze rings but with living stone, fusing it directly to the mountain's bones using techniques the lowlanders have forgotten.

And then I will descend.

Not to harvest crystals. Not for wealth or glory. But to hang in the absolute center of the genesis engine, to feel the mountain's birth-screams through every fiber of my rope-trained body. To finally understand the frequency that calls all things into being.

The divers say the pressure would crush even their trained bodies at the blister's heart. They say the currents would shred anything that tried to remain stationary in the center for more than seconds.

But they don't understand harmonics the way I do. They don't know that at the exact center of chaos, there is always a point of perfect stillness. The Shear-kites know. The mountain knows. And soon, wrapped in my egg-rope like an insect in a chrysalis, I will know too.

I am the Tether-wright. I braid the strings that bind the void. And when I finally join my masterwork to its purpose, I will become the first human note in the mountain's newborn song.

The chamber below roars its invitation. My fingers work in the strobing light.

The rope grows longer, one egg at a time.
