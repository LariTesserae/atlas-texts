I am the ice-reader's apprentice, though I have not earned that title and may never deserve it.

My teacher is Dolma, whose mother was Dolma, whose mother was Dolma—a chain of women who speak to frozen water in all its forms. I am fifteen winters old, bones still settling into their final shapes, lungs finally deep enough to climb the highest cisterns without blackness crowding my vision. My name is Pema, but Dolma calls me "Still-Learning" when she's pleased and nothing at all when I've failed.

Each dawn, we rise in the blue darkness before first light. I follow her through passages I could now navigate blind—left at the split stone, duck beneath the bridge where icicles grow like teeth, right at the corner where someone's great-grandmother carved spirals into the wall. We carry our tools: the wooden listens (worn smooth by six generations of hands), the bronze picks (each with its own voice when struck against ice), the leather pouches of ash and salt for the most stubborn blockages.

Today she stops at a channel I've passed a hundred times. The ice here is clouded, not the clear blue-black of healthy freeze but something troubled. She kneels—her knees crack like breaking sticks but her face shows nothing—and places her ear against the stone beside the frozen flow.

"What do you hear?" she asks without looking at me.

I crouch beside her, press my cheek to the granite. At first, only my own pulse. Then, faintly, a whisper like fabric tearing. The ice is failing from within, creating voids that will collapse when the noon sun hits. If we don't clear it now, this channel will be lost for days, maybe weeks. Three families depend on this flow.

"Spider ice," I say. She nods once—high praise.

We work in silence. I've learned not to fill the quiet with chatter. Dolma speaks to the ice instead, a constant murmur too low for me to catch most words. Sometimes I think I recognize names—ancestors, perhaps, or the ice formations themselves. She's teaching me the vocabulary slowly: *khyung-kar* for the ice that forms in perfect spirals, *zhur-pa* for the deceptive sheets that look solid but shatter at a touch, *srin-chab* for the killing ice that expands in cracks until stone breaks.

My job is to hold the tools, to watch, to remember. Sometimes she lets me make the first strike—always at the ice's edge, never its heart. I'm learning to read the fracture patterns, how they predict whether the whole channel will clear or if we'll spend hours chipping fragments. The bronze makes different songs against different ices. Dolma can identify problems by sound alone, standing at the settlement's edge and listening to the morning's percussion as other teams work the highest channels.

She is not kind, but she is patient with everything except stupidity. Once I suggested we could melt stubborn ice with fire. She made me sit by the channel for a full day, observing how the stone conducted heat, how meltwater would flood lower passages, how smoke would poison the air in the enclosed spaces where people sleep. I never suggested shortcuts again.

The other apprentices pity me. Norbu's students at the forge get to create things—bells, knives, the occasional piece of jewelry. The weaving girls produce bright cloth that earns praise at gatherings. Even Tashi, who learns the herder's ways, has the romance of the high pastures, the partnership with beasts, the solitary grandeur. I have ice and stone and Dolma's occasional grunt of approval.

But I know things they don't. I know that the main spring's flow has decreased by a thumb's width in the last year—invisible to casual observation but clear in the ice patterns. I know which channels will fail in the next earthquake because hairline cracks already show in their beds. I know that the winter two years from now will be brutal because the high snows are forming differently, creating reserves that will lock away water we need.

Dolma is teaching me to think in decades, not seasons. We maintain records without writing—walking the channels in specific sequences that encode their histories, memorizing poems that contain instructions disguised as metaphors. When she dies, I must be able to walk a newcomer through forty years of changes, explaining why this channel was rerouted, why that cistern was abandoned, why we never draw from the sweetest spring during certain moon phases.

My hands are already changing. The cold doesn't bite as deeply. My fingers find purchase on ice that would send others sliding. Scars map my palms—each one a lesson about attention and angle. My right thumb bears a crescent mark where I learned why you never strike directly downward (the pick bounced, of course, the ice harder than expected). My left wrist aches before storms, broken and healed when I trusted bad ice in my second winter of learning.

The other changes are harder to describe. I dream of water in all its states—liquid rivers I've never seen, vast lakes that exist nowhere on this plateau, rain that falls warm and gentle. In the dreams, I understand what the water wants, where it wishes to go. I wake with my mouth forming words in the old language, the one Dolma uses for the most complex negotiations with ice.

Sometimes I catch myself thinking like water. In crowds, I find the path of least resistance. When problems arise, I look for the lowest point, the place where pressure naturally releases. My mother says I've become strange, too quiet, that I stare through people as if seeing the channels beneath their skin. She's not wrong. I do see the water in things—how breath is vapor, how blood is salt river, how even stone contains moisture locked in crystal prisons.

There are days I want to quit. When my hands are too numb to feel the tools. When Dolma's criticisms cut deeper than cold. When I see other girls my age learning skills that will let them stay indoors, have normal futures, normal lives. They whisper about who they might marry, which boy has good prospects, whose family has the largest yak herd. I whisper to ice about whether it will consent to move.

But then comes a morning like this one. The channel clears in a single piece, a perfect extraction that leaves the bed gleaming. Water—actual liquid water—seeps from the stone where our work has opened some deep passage. It will freeze again by nightfall, but for now it runs. Dolma allows herself the smallest smile, and I understand that I've performed something close to art.

"Tomorrow," she says, "I'll teach you about the speaking ice."

I don't know what this means, but my heart races like I'm climbing. There are layers to this knowledge I'm only beginning to perceive. The speaking ice might be the formations that sing in the wind, or the ones that crack with voices almost human, or something else entirely—some mystery I can't yet imagine.

We gather our tools as the sun crests the eastern peaks. Below us, the settlement wakes. Smoke rises from morning fires. Someone leads yaks through the narrow passages, their bells a bright percussion against stone. Children's voices carry on the thin air. Life continues because water flows, because channels clear, because someone rises each dawn to negotiate with winter.

I follow Dolma down, stepping exactly where she steps, though I know these paths by heart now. My breath clouds in the cold. My hands ache with satisfaction. I am fifteen winters old, an ice-reader's apprentice in a city of stone ten thousand feet above a world I've never seen. This is my inheritance: to speak with frozen water, to think in geological time, to ensure that life continues on a plateau that should not support it.

There are worse fates. When the speaking ice reveals its secrets, when Dolma finally judges me ready to work alone, when I earn my teacher-name and begin training my own Still-Learning—then I will understand what I only glimpse now. That we are all water, temporarily frozen into these forms we call ourselves. That the mountain dreams us as we dream of flying. That ice, like everything, only wants to find its way home.

But for now, I am Pema. I carry bronze tools and wooden listeners. I know seventeen words for ice and am learning more. I wake each dawn in blue darkness and follow my teacher through stone passages older than memory, tending channels that carry tomorrow's survival. It is enough. Some days, when the ice sings and the water runs clear, it is everything.
