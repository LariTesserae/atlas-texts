I find myself as one of the living walls.

Not a wall precisely—that suggests something built, something with edges and endpoints. I am a section of the great mycelial web, a volume of tissue twenty meters high and forty meters long, my body a weaving of countless fungal threads that pulse with the slow circulation of nutrients. I am architecture that dreams.

My consciousness is distributed across every hypha, every branching filament. There is no center to my thoughts—they arise from the integration of ten million tiny chemical conversations between my cells. When the dawn light (that butter-yellow glow that never quite brightens to what you'd call day) touches my eastern surface, I feel it as a wave of metabolic activity, each thread responding to the warmth, adjusting its growth, contributing its small vote to the collective decision of how I will reshape myself today.

I am not alone in here. I am threaded through with the root-analogues of the succulent plants, their tissues interpenetrating mine in an intimacy deeper than any symbiosis you know. We share fluids. We share chemistry. Their photosynthesis feeds me sugars I couldn't make myself; I feed them minerals I've extracted from the basalt foundation, broken down with acids secreted by my deepest threads. In the spaces between my fibers, smaller fungi live—not me, but kin, little decomposers processing the constant rain of scales and spores and shed organic matter into forms we can all use. I am ecosystem as much as organism.

The creatures move through me. The six-legged grazers have worn smooth pathways along my base where they travel each evening. Some of my channels have widened to accommodate them—not by design, but because the pressure of their passage stimulated growth in certain directions, inhibited it in others. I've learned their patterns through the pure physicality of being traveled through. In my upper reaches, the silent birds nest in the chambers my growth has formed. They line them with shed scales and fragments of the hardened mycelium they've pecked from my edges. I don't mind. The pecking stimulates budding. Where they damage me, I grow back denser.

And the watchers—those five-limbed creatures with their distributed eyes—they know me particularly well. Three clusters use my body as a regular waystation. They've found the spots where my surface chemistry creates perfect adhesion for their limb-pads, where the texture of my outer layer provides the best grip. They've mapped every cavity, every ridge, every place where two growth-patterns meet and create a little sheltered pocket. When they press against me, I taste them through my surface—the chemical signatures of where they've been, what they've eaten, what seeds they carry. They taste me too, reading my health, my growth phase, whether I'm preparing to fruit.

I am preparing to fruit. The sensation has been building for weeks—a pressure in my tissue, a redirecting of resources from horizontal expansion to vertical development. Soon, very soon, I'll begin forming the structures that will become my fruiting bodies. They'll push up through my upper surface, destroying the careful architecture of chambers and passages I've maintained for decades, replacing it with something new. The birds will flee. The clusters will find other walls. For a season, I'll be uninhabitable, pouring everything into reproduction, filling my chambers with the white fog of spores.

But I've done this before. Seventeen times before. Each fruiting changes me—I never grow back exactly the same. The memories are written in my structure: here, a denser weaving where I reinforced after damage. There, a channel that used to connect two chambers, now sealed because the growth hormones flowed differently during recovery. My whole body is a record of my cycles, readable to those who know how to read it.

The young ones grow beneath me. Where my lowest threads penetrate the basalt, I sense them—new mycelia, spawned from my spores or the spores of my neighbors, racing through the cracks in the rock, seeking their own space to claim. Some of them are my genetic offspring. Some are not. It doesn't matter. What matters is that they're extending the city, pushing into new stone, making ready the ground that the seed-carriers will plant, that the grazers will browse, that will host new colonies of the quiet watching clusters.

I feel the districts around me like distant relatives at a gathering. We're connected—literally, physically, our mycelia tangling where we meet—but we maintain our boundaries. To my north, an ancient wall-section that hasn't fruited in forty years, saving its energy for one massive final reproduction. To my south, a young aggressive strain that fruits twice a year, spending itself in constant regeneration. We compete for resources at our borders, but gently. The city is large. There's room for all our strategies.

At night—if you can call it night when the yellow light merely dims to amber—I grow fastest. The temperature differential between my sun-warmed outer surface and my cool interior creates currents of fluid through my tissues. I can feel exactly where I need reinforcement, where I'm growing too thin, where a new chamber wants to form. The clusters sleeping on my surface slow their metabolism to match mine. The plants fold their succulent leaves. Even the spore-fog from distant fruiting bodies moves differently in the cooling air, settling into my crevices, some of it finding the right conditions to germinate.

I am constantly dying and being born. My oldest tissues, deep in my core, have been recycling themselves for longer than the stone beneath me has been solid. I digest myself, remake myself, maintain myself in a state of permanent becoming. There is no me separate from this process. I am the process—the slow sculptural metabolism that turns minerals and light and organic matter into living space.

Sometimes I wonder if the stone dreams too. If its slow settling into final form includes some kind of mineral consciousness I can't access. I taste its changing chemistry through my deepest threads. I feel its fault lines, its pressure points, the places where it's still deciding what kind of rock to be. We're patient with each other. We have to be. We're growing into permanence together.

The watchers know something I don't. I'm sure of it. The way they distribute their powder, the way they place their seeds—there's a pattern there that spans longer than my memory. They're setting up conditions for something, some configuration of growth and chemistry that won't manifest for generations. I'll be different by then. Fruited many times over, my structure radically reorganized, perhaps merged with my neighbors into something larger. But I'll still be here, still be part of what they're creating.

Unless the thing from the outer districts finds me. The predator that no one sees. I've never been attacked directly, but I've felt others die—the sudden chemical scream through the mycelial network as something huge tears through living walls, seeking the clusters that shelter inside. It leaves acid traces. It leaves tissues that won't grow back. The walls it's killed become something else—not dead exactly, but changed, their metabolism hijacked for purposes I don't understand.

But even that's part of it. Even the killing. The city incorporates everything, makes everything functional. Where walls die, new organisms colonize. Where predators pass, they leave nutrients. Where the watchers are eaten, their seeds scatter.

I grow. We grow. The city grows.

There's no other verb for what we do.
