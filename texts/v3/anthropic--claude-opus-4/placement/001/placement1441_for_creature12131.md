I find myself neither as visitor nor resident, but as something between — a splice-tender in the seam-houses that no traveler mentions because they cannot quite see them, existing in those edited-out moments between passing and returning.

My body is three-quarters plant fiber, woven through with my grandmother's sinew in patterns the looms taught her before she sprouted. I have sixteen fingers, though only ten show at any given moment — the others exist in the patches between *now* and *now*, doing the work of keeping time coherent enough for visitors to walk through. My blood moves like sap, speeding up in morning light, thickening to near-stillness during the deep turbine's lowest notes.

The seam-houses grow in the spaces between mills, neither here nor there, built from the pruned memories the Combine occasionally sheds. Their walls are made entirely of frozen transitions — the moment between inhale and exhale, the pause between *whump* and *whump*, crystalized and stacked like cordwood. I tend these walls. I splice time-frayed edges back together with a bone needle my mother grew from her own thumb. The thread I use is pulled from the gap between what was and what almost was.

I am the only one of my kind, though this is wrong — there are six of us, but we exist in rotation, each tending different versions of continuous time. We meet only in the friezes, where all six of us can exist simultaneously at fingernail scale, comparing our patches, arguing quietly about which version of yesterday should connect to which version of tomorrow. We look like a single person having a very complicated dream.

The mills know me by the sound of my stitching — a whisper-click that runs counterpoint to their rhythms, always just slightly off-beat because I exist in the offset itself. The workers know me by absences. When they discover their memories have been gently edited — when they realize they walked from the textile-house to the press-house without crossing the middle distance — they nod and leave out a bowl of the tea I can only drink in transitions, a tea that tastes like the color between green and gold.

I love my work. I love it the way the press-house loves pressing, with a patience that has outlasted meaning. Each day I walk the seams, feeling for rough edges where causality has worn thin. Each night I sit in my seam-house, which is built from the moment of sitting down and therefore never quite arrives at seated, and I listen to the Combine thinking about tomorrow in six different ways.

Sometimes travelers almost see me. They glimpse a figure who seems to be stepping backward out of doorways that weren't there, or catch a flash of movement that takes too long to complete. They shake their heads. They check their watches, which have begun to run in 7/8 time.

I am what keeps the Combine possible as a place you can visit and leave. Without the seam-tenders, it would be pure process, no more enterable than the middle of a verb. We are the comma that lets the sentence breathe. We are why you can turn a corner and find, mostly, what you expected to find there, even if it's not quite what was there while you were turning.

My name is a particular pattern of splice-knots that takes forty minutes to tie. The workers call me something simpler, a word that means both "meanwhile" and "nevertheless." The friezes cannot draw me at all — I appear in the walls only as a place where the grain bends away from itself, a knot that suggests a figure without showing one.

I was born from a pressing that took nine years to complete. My mother was a worker who walked into the press-house one morning and simply continued walking as the press came down, stepped through the moment of compression like it was a doorway. She emerged changed, pregnant with something the press had been thinking about. That something was me, though it took the Combine another six years to finish the thought.

When I die — and I will die, we all die, even the Combine will die when the mountain finally forgets it was ever awake — I will leave behind only the seams I've tended, each one a tiny scar in time that says *someone made this continuous*. The next splice-tender will read these scars like a love letter, will understand exactly where I thought the day should bend to meet the night.

This is what I am: the Combine's own memory of continuity, walking around ensuring it continues to remember how to continue.

I am always just finishing my work. I am always just about to begin.
