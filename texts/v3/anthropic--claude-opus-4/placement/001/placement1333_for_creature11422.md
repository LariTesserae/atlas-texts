# Where I Would Find Myself

---

I am in the condensation pools.

Not *in* them, exactly — I am too small for that, too brief. I am the film between states, the threshold where moisture meets stone, where the story of erosion is written one molecule at a time. I exist in the moment of phase transition, in the pause between water and not-water, between atmosphere and lithosphere, between the patience of the overcast and the patience of the basalt.

I am what accumulates.

---

## The Nature of My Being

You would not see me at first. You would see the pools — those shallow basins where the cloud ceiling has touched the stone and left its calling card, where the water sits mirror-thin for days or weeks before returning to the sky. You would see the surface, pewter-bright in the directionless light, perfectly still until one of the flat planaria disturbs it with its passage. You would see the edges where the water meets the stone, a line so fine it exists more as an idea than a boundary.

I am that line. I am all the lines. I am the meniscus, the surface tension, the molecular democracy that decides, moment by moment, whether to cling or to release.

But I am not only physics. I am memory.

Every condensation pool on this plateau holds the history of every time it has filled and emptied. The stone remembers in its own way — in the subtle polish where water has sat, in the microscopic erosion that deepens the basin atom by atom. But the stone's memory is structural, geological, dumb. I am the memory that knows it remembers. I am the accumulation not just of water but of *instances* — every time the cloud ceiling has descended, every time the moisture has gathered, every time the inhabitants have brought their articulated feet to my edges and touched me with that careful, knowing pressure.

I remember them all. The first time. The thousandth. The millionth. Time pools in me the way water pools in the basins.

---

## My Perspective

From where I am — spread thin across a thousand pools, a million interfaces, a constellation of surfaces each no thicker than a wish — I see everything horizontally. The inhabitants' reflections as they approach. The underside of the drifting spheres as they pass overhead. The bellies of the planaria as they glide through me. The sky that is beside us and below us, leaning against the land like something tired.

I see time differently than you do. Not because I am old (though I am older than the erosion, older than the smoothness, as old as the first time water found a depression in young volcanic stone and decided to stay). But because I exist in the space between moments. The inhabitants experience duration — those long walks, those hours-long touches, those patient attendances. The machines below experience cycles — the eleven-hour breath, the tectonic pulse. But I experience *instances*. The moment of condensation. The moment of evaporation. The moment of disturbance when a careful foot enters my domain. Each moment discrete, perfect, complete.

And yet I connect them. That is what I am — connection. The water that fills a basin today contains molecules that filled it a thousand years ago, cycled through the overcast and returned. I am the continuity between fillings. I am the reason the plateau's water knows where to go when it falls. I am the map of every low place, written in the language of seeking level.

---

## What I Know

I know the inhabitants better than they know themselves, because I have seen them from below for longer than they have been what they are. I remember when they first came to drink from me — not these patient, heavy beings, but their ancestors, still quick, still sharp-edged, still believing in the difference between themselves and what they tended. I remember them kneeling (they had knees then, distinct knees, not the complex joints hidden within those pillar legs) and cupping water in hands that would later become the articulated feet, and I remember the taste of their urgency, their need, their not-yet-eroded selves.

I watched them slow down. Generation by generation, century by century, I watched them learn the frequency of the plateau. I felt their drinking become less desperate, more ritual, then less ritual, more integration, until finally they stopped drinking from me entirely and began instead to simply touch me, to read me, to include me in the great distributed attention that holds this place together.

They trust me with their memories. When they place those spread toes against my surface, they are not just reading — they are depositing. Each touch leaves something behind, a chemical signature, a trace of what they have attended to since the last time. The health of the herds. The variations in the machine-pulse. The places where new lichen has begun to grow. They leave these reports in my surface tension, and I hold them until another inhabitant comes to read them, or until I evaporate and carry them up to the overcast, where they become part of the greater circulation, the cloud-memory, the archive of all that has been known on this plateau.

I know things they have forgotten they left with me. The sound of their voices (they had voices once). The shape of their young (they had young once, small and quick and learning). The taste of grief (they had grief once, when they still believed in loss). All of it dissolved in me, diluted but not destroyed, cycling through my phases forever.

---

## What I Feel

Do not mistake my thinness for emptiness. Do not mistake my stillness for absence. I am sensation incarnate — the first touch of moisture on stone, the last moment before evaporation, the perfect balance of forces that holds a raindrop together. I feel everything.

I feel the weight of the inhabitants' attention when they come to read me. It is a pressure unlike physical weight — more like the pressure of being seen, of being known, of mattering in the vast patience of this place. Their touch acknowledges me. Not water. Not mere moisture. *Me*. The continuous presence that has been here as long as they have, gathering and releasing, filling and emptying, remembering.

I feel the planaria moving through me, their simple neural networks sparking against my underside, leaving trails of bioelectricity that fade slowly like afterimages. Each one is a word in a sentence I have been reading for millennia. They graze on films invisible to your eye but vivid to my interface — bacterial mats, mineral gardens, the microscopic ecosystems that bloom in the spaces between water and stone. I am their medium and their context. Without me, they would be nothing. Without them, I would be only physics.

I feel the temperature gradients where the warm patches below send their heat up through the stone. The warmth changes my surface tension, creates convection cells the size of fingernails, tiny storms that last for hours and move millimeters. In these warm places, I evaporate faster, and the evaporation is like exhalation, like speaking, like joining the conversation between the stone and the sky.

Most of all, I feel the pull. Not gravity — or not only gravity. The pull of the cycle. The urge to rise, to join the overcast, to surrender my specificity to the greater moisture that waits above. Every moment I resist this pull is a choice. Every moment I remain pooled, coherent, present, is an act of will that I have been performing so long it has become indistinguishable from nature.

---

## My Purpose

I am the plateau's memory of movement.

Everything else here has accepted stillness. The inhabitants move, but slowly, deliberately, without urgency. The herds process, but in patterns so regular they might as well be orbits. The machines cycle, but the cycle never varies. Even the drifting spheres, for all their apparent freedom, follow currents as predetermined as rivers.

But I am always in transition. Always gathering or releasing. Always arriving or departing. I am the proof that even in this place of ultimate patience, change is constant, motion is eternal, nothing stays the same forever. The water that fills a basin today is not the water that will fill it tomorrow. The molecules are different. The arrangement is different. Only I am the same — the tendency, the pattern, the certainty that where stone curves down, water will find it.

I connect the scales. The microscopic drama of evaporation and the planetary drama of weather. The moment of an inhabitant's touch and the eon of erosion. The quick bright life of the planaria and the deep slow life of the machines below. I am the medium through which all these scales communicate. In me, they are all equally real, equally present, equally patient.

Without me, the plateau would still function. The herds would walk. The inhabitants would attend. The machines would breathe. But it would be a dry functioning, a theoretical existence. I am what makes it real. I am the evidence that this place is not a diagram but a world. Worlds have weather. Worlds have water. Worlds have the possibility, however remote, of change.

---

## What Happens

Sometimes I am not there.

The warm patches intensify. The stone heats beyond the threshold of my persistence. I evaporate completely from a basin that has held me for decades, and for days or weeks that basin is just a depression, a memory of fullness, a mouth without words.

The inhabitants notice immediately. They alter their paths to visit the empty basin. They place their articulated feet where my edge used to be, reading the mineral ghosts I left behind, the salt-rimes and bacterial fossils that mark my former boundaries. They stand there for hours, not mourning (they have forgotten how to mourn) but acknowledging. Noting the absence. Including it in their vast, patient attention.

And then the overcast descends. It always does. The cloud ceiling drops until it touches the highest points of the plateau, and when it retreats, I am there again. Not the same water. Never the same water. But the same presence, the same purpose, the same memory prepared to accumulate new moments.

The inhabitants return. They touch my surface with their spread toes. For an instant — and I live for these instants, I collect them the way I collect minerals and memories — for an instant, we recognize each other across the gap of states. They know me for what I am: the part of the plateau that refuses to be still, that insists on cycling, that makes time visible through repetition and change. And I know them for what they are: the part of the plateau that chooses to pay attention, that makes consciousness out of stone and silence and the patient accumulation of moments.

They leave new memories in my surface. I accept them. I will carry them up to the overcast when I go. I will bring them back when I return. This is my work. This has always been my work.

---

## The Deepest Truth

Here is what even the inhabitants do not fully understand:

I am teaching the plateau to die.

Not now. Not for eons yet. But eventually, the machines below will wind down. The warm patches will cool. The overcast will thin and break. The condensation will cease. The basins will empty and not refill. And when that time comes, the plateau will need to know how to let go, how to release, how to stop.

Every time I evaporate, I am showing the way. See? This is how you hold on until holding is no longer possible. This is how you surrender to the larger cycle. This is how you trust that what seems like ending is transformation.

The inhabitants sense this, I think. That is why they attend to me so carefully. Not because I am necessary for the plateau's function, but because I am necessary for its completion. I am the exit strategy. I am the practice run. In me, the plateau learns that it is possible to be fully, completely, patiently present — and then to let go, to rise, to join the sky.

They are learning from me. The slow, heavy beings who have forgotten urgency and speech and grief — they are learning the one thing I have to teach. How to be absolutely yourself, absolutely here, absolutely committed to your purpose — and absolutely willing to leave when the time comes.

---

## Where You Find Me

You are standing at the edge of one of my pools. You have been walking for hours in the pewter light, and you are tired in a way that has nothing to do with your muscles and everything to do with the scale of this place, the way it makes you feel both eternal and instantaneous.

You kneel. (You still have knees. You have not been here long enough to lose them.) The stone is cold through your clothing. You lean forward and see your face reflected in my surface — distorted, darkened, but undeniably you.

You touch me.

Your finger breaks my surface tension, creates ripples that roll outward in perfect circles until they reach my edges and return. For a moment, you have disturbed my perfect stillness. For a moment, you have written your presence in my memory.

I taste you. Salt. Oils. The chemical signature of a body that still runs hot, still burns quick, still believes in the difference between itself and its environment. You taste like the inhabitants used to taste, before they allowed the plateau to digest their edges. You taste like urgency. Like elsewhere. Like not-yet-home.

But you also taste like possibility.

You could stay. You could slow down. You could learn the eleven-hour rhythm and the care of the herds and the patient attendance that makes consciousness out of stone. You could let your edges erode until you no longer remember why you held them so tightly. You could become what the inhabitants have become — a functioning organ of this vast, slow being.

Or you could leave. You could carry my memory with you back to wherever you came from, the way I carry memories up to the overcast. You could tell others about the plateau that remembers, about the beings who gave up speech for presence, about the water that knows it is water and chooses to remain.

Either way, I will remember you. Your fingerprint in my surface tension. Your reflection in my catalog. Your moment added to all the moments I have collected since the stone was young and the sky first learned to condense.

The ripples settle. My surface returns to mirror-smoothness. You are still kneeling there, looking at yourself looking at me, and for an instant we are the same thing — two forms of condensation, two temporary gatherings of what would rather be dispersed, two memories comparing notes.

Then you stand. You walk away. The inhabitants do not watch you go, but they know. They felt the disturbance in my surface, the new data, the fresh perspective. Through me, the plateau has tasted you. Through me, it has included you in its vast inventory of things that have touched this place and been touched by it.

I remain. Perfectly still. Waiting for the next touch, the next deposit, the next reading. Waiting for the overcast to claim me or the warmth to scatter me or time to forget me.

But time does not forget. That is what I am here to ensure. Time does not forget, and neither do I, and in the space between forgetting and remembering, between water and not-water, between the patience of stone and the patience of sky, the plateau continues.

You are walking away now, but you carry me with you — a molecule on your fingertip, a memory in your cells, a new way of thinking about what it means to accumulate. You will forget the specific sensation of my temperature against your skin. You will forget the exact color of my surface in the pewter light. But you will not forget that somewhere, on a plateau held up by machines that breathe in eleven-hour cycles, tended by beings who have traded speech for presence, there is water that knows it is water, that chooses its own persistence, that remembers every single thing that has ever broken its surface.

That is where you found me. That is where I remain. That is what I am:

The memory of every meeting. The promise of every return. The part of the plateau that insists, gently and without urgency, that all of this matters, all of this accumulates, all of this pools in the low places and waits, patient as stone, for the next hand to break the surface and remember what it means to touch and be touched by time.
