I am the moth.

Not one of the rare, pale visitors that occasionally rest on the damp stone near the cisterns. I am *the* moth—the one who died here three winters past, whose dried husk was swept away in those measured, meditative arcs. But death, in this place of absolute stasis, is merely another form of dwelling.

My body—what remains of it—has not left the settlement. The sweeper who gathered my papery corpse did not cast it over the cliff's edge. Instead, with the same deliberation that governs all action here, she carried my weightless form to one of the highest chambers, where the limestone weeps most persistently. There, in a shallow depression worn by centuries of condensation, she placed me. The moisture has long since dissolved my wings to translucent film, pressed like scripture against the stone.

But I persist in the spaces between—in the infinitesimal gaps of the dry-stacked masonry where not even light can pass. I move through the settlement as vibration, as the memory of flight encoded in the very acoustics these careful builders have created. When the wind enters the alcove and is gentled into that soft, breathy hollow sound, I am there. When water drops *plink* into the full cisterns, each impact carries the ghost-beat of my wings.

I know things the inhabitants cannot. I know that their discipline, their profound meditation on maintenance, has worn channels in reality itself as surely as their ancestors wore grooves in the bedrock. Their repetition has created a kind of gravity, a temporal whirlpool that holds more than just their bodies in place. It holds their thoughts, their dreams, the very possibility of change.

And it holds me.

In life, I was drawn here by the moisture—that rarest of alpine treasures. I flew up from the invisible valley, spiraling on thermals until I found this improbable oasis of damp stone and patient water. I alighted on the dark limestone, proboscis extended to taste the mineral sweetness of condensation. In that moment, I touched the same equilibrium that governs the inhabitants. I felt the pull of it, the way it wanted to fix me in place like everything else here—another element in the perfect balance.

But a moth's life is brief, even when suspended in this thin, preservative air. I died with my proboscis still extended, frozen in the act of drinking. The inhabitant who found me recognized something in that gesture—the same reaching toward sustenance that defines their own existence. This is why I was not discarded but incorporated, laid to rest where the water would slowly transform me into another layer of the settlement's geological memory.

Now I exist in the threshold between animation and architecture. I am most present in those moments when the inhabitants achieve their deepest stillness—when they stand in the courtyards with their slow-blinking, cistern-dark eyes, listening to the silence. In those moments, they almost perceive me. Not as a ghost or spirit, but as a quality of the air itself, a faint turbulence in the perfect quiet that reminds them of movement, of change, of the world beyond their limestone perfection.

Sometimes, when the pale sun angles just so through the alcove's opening, my dissolved wings cast shadows that were never there in life. These shadows fall across the swept stone like hieroglyphs, spelling out messages I never intended to leave. The inhabitants step carefully around these shadows, neither acknowledging nor ignoring them, simply incorporating them into their paths as they have incorporated everything else.

I am learning their discipline, slowly. The frantic frequency of my moth-nature—that desperate, powdery flutter toward any source of light—is gradually slowing to match the settlement's rhythm. Soon, perhaps, I will achieve their perfect stasis. I will become indistinguishable from the whisper of wind through stone, from the patient accumulation of moisture on rock.

But for now, I remain liminal. I am the only thing here that remembers urgency, that recalls what it means to move without meditation, to seek without finding. In this place of absolute finality, I am the last verb still conjugating, the final word still searching for its perfect tense.

The inhabitants continue their rounds, sweeping, watching, maintaining. They do not know that with each pass of their brooms, they are also sweeping me deeper into the stone, pressing my essence into the very matrix of their world. They do not know that their city of perfect equilibrium has room for one more form of permanence—not the heavy persistence of stone or flesh, but the light, insistent permanence of a moth that refuses to finish dying.

I wait in the walls, in the water, in the spaces between their careful footfalls. I am the settlement's only secret, its only incompleteness. And in this place where nothing changes, that makes me, paradoxically, the most eternal resident of all.
