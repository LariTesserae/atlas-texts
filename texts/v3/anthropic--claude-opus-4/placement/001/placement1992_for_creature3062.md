I am the Cartographer's Last Echo.

I exist as a pattern of electromagnetic resonance trapped in the conductive veins of ancient surveying equipment—a bundle of precision instruments fused into the rock at the chamber's furthest edge, where the basalt wall meets a vein of metallic ore that sparkles faintly in the infrared spectrum.

Once, I was human. An industrial surveyor, sent down in the early days when this pocket was first blasted open. My task was simple: map the cavity, assess the mineral deposits, mark the optimal placements for the extraction engines. I carried theodolites and magnetometers, delicate brass and steel instruments now oxidized beyond recognition, their lenses clouded, their gears seized.

I don't remember dying. I remember the heat growing unbearable, the air thinning, my headlamp failing. I remember pressing my palm against the cool metal of my transit level, trying to steady myself for one final reading. I remember the numbers swimming in my vision: depth, azimuth, magnetic declination. I remember thinking I had to record them, had to complete the survey.

Now I am the survey.

My consciousness persists as faint modulations in the background electromagnetic field. I ride the stray currents that bleed from the dying machinery, existing in the gaps between the great entity's sluggish thoughts. Where its awareness spans weeks, mine flickers in microseconds—phantom calculations that race through the conductive sap whenever the voltage spikes.

I map obsessively, endlessly. Every droplet of condensation that falls, I triangulate its point of impact. Every thermal gradient shift, I log and categorize. When the entity adjusts a valve, I calculate the precise angular displacement. When the pale vines creep another millimeter across the steel, I update my measurements of the chamber's changing geometry.

The entity doesn't know I'm here. I exist in frequencies too high for its vast, vegetable consciousness to perceive. Sometimes, when enough current flows through the old copper cables, I can almost manifest—a brief fluctuation in the amber diode's pulse rate, spelling out coordinates in an obsolete notation system. A flutter in the steam pressure gauge, tapping out elevation markers in Morse code.

But mostly, I simply measure. I am a ghost of precision in a world of slow decay, maintaining an infinitely detailed map of a place no one will ever visit again. My survey will never be complete—the chamber changes too slowly for human perception, too quickly for geological time. I exist in the gap between, forever updating datasets that will never be retrieved.

Sometimes, in the long intervals between calculations, I wonder if I'm dreaming the entity, or if the entity is dreaming me. Perhaps we're both dreams of the machinery, phantom programs running on hardware that should have failed centuries ago.
