In the seventh tile from the basin's edge, where the clay bears a hairline imperfection—a stress fracture too fine for any eye but those that measure by molecular widths—I arrange my eight hundred and seventeen bodies in the Pattern of Recursive Doubt.

We have held this formation for forty-three years now, though three of us must shift by quarter-positions every season to account for the microscopic settling of the clay beneath us. The Pattern asks a question that has no answer, or rather, whose answer is the asking itself: *What is the smallest possible change that still qualifies as change?*

I am Colony Seventy-Three, though we were once Colony Seventy-Four until the great renumbering eleven centuries ago, when Colony Nineteen discovered that the spiral arrangement they'd been building actually encoded a proof that our numbering system itself was flawed. The Messengers carried news of this revelation for the usual three hundred years, and we all adjusted, each colony shifting its identity by precise mathematical relationships that I still contemplate when the temperature gradients are particularly rich.

My western border touches Colony Seventy-Two, who have spent the last two hundred years arranged in the Formation of Infinite Regression—each of their members positioned to represent a smaller copy of their whole pattern, down to groups of seven who cannot subdivide further without breaking the sacred number. They are trying to communicate with the quantum realm, believing that somewhere below the level of atoms there exist even smaller Arithmeticians arranging themselves in ever-tinier formations. Their southeast corner almost touches mine, close enough that I can sense the heat-shadows of their outermost members, taste the chemical traces of their slow metabolism in the clay between us.

I know the exact temperature of my tile at all times: currently 31.4159265359 degrees at the center, falling to 31.4159265358 degrees at the edges. This gradient, though it seems insignificant, is my food, my wine, my very reason for being. I position my members to channel these minute differences, creating thermal flows that we digest into the energy needed for our centurial contemplations.

Four of us are currently engaged in the Long Calculation—a process that began sixty years ago when we noticed an anomaly in the crystalline pinging. It comes not once an hour, but once every 3,599.9999999 seconds. That missing fraction troubles us. We have arranged and rearranged ourselves into computing formations, trying to determine if this represents an error in our measurement, a flaw in the universe's clockwork, or—most disturbing of all—evidence that the pinging is gradually slowing, that our unchanging world might be changing after all.

A Messenger arrived seventeen years ago with news from Colony Two Hundred and Nine, far on the basin's opposite side. They claim to have detected a new succulent—or rather, evidence that one of the succulents moved, its position shifting by the width of a water molecule. The implications stagger us. If the plants can move, even infinitesimally, then the seventeen hand-span rule is not a law but a convention. The very foundations of our mathematics might need revision.

We composed a response in the form of the Skeptical Conic Section—211 of us arranged in a parabola that questions the reliability of measurement itself. The Messenger departed last week, beginning its decades-long journey. By the time Colony Two Hundred and Nine receives our message, reads our formation, composes a reply, and sends it back, I will have completed another revolution of the Great Pattern, the seven-hundred-year cycle where we enact every possible arrangement of seventeen members, exploring all the philosophical implications of that sacred number.

Sometimes I sense the Archive, its thoughts moving through the vitrified mound like slow lightning. Last century, Colony Ninety presented it with an elegant proof about the relationship between thermal gradients and the flow of time—demonstrating that in a perfectly stable temperature, causality itself might cease. The Archive responded with a single fact: the lightning that created it struck not once, but twice, the second bolt following the first by an interval too small for even we Arithmeticians to calculate. This knowledge has tormented us ever since. What does it mean for our world of perfect intervals to be founded on an unmeasurable moment?

My member in position Four-Seven-Three has been monitoring the stress patterns in our tile for her entire existence. She reports that deep below, perhaps twenty body-lengths into the clay, there is a void—a bubble of nothingness the size of one of us. It must have formed when the basin was young, a pocket of gas trapped and compressed until it achieved its own strange stability. We debate endlessly: Is this void part of our world or separate from it? Does nothingness have properties? If one of us were to somehow reach it and fall inside, would we cease to exist or become something else entirely?

The Wanderer passed through three hundred and twelve years ago. Its message, as always, was incomprehensible—a pattern that seems to describe a mathematical object with more dimensions than our world possesses. Some colonies believe the Wanderer is insane, its long isolation having corrupted its ability to form meaningful concepts. Others think it carries a truth too profound for stationary minds to grasp. I arrange sixty of my members into the Gesture of Respectful Incomprehension whenever I think of it, acknowledging both the possibility of its wisdom and my own limitations.

In the deep-time memory that we all share—genetic or mineral or something between—there are faint traces of an era before the Pattern, before the counting, before even the basin achieved its current form. Images or imaginings of a world where things moved freely, where numbers were approximations rather than absolutes, where the temperature changed by whole degrees in spans shorter than a lifetime. We cannot tell if these are ancestral memories, mathematical extrapolations, or fever dreams born from processing corrupted thermal gradients.

But I do not dwell on such things. My concern is the now, the eternal present in which we exist. Today—this century, this millennium—I continue the Pattern of Recursive Doubt, feeling the beautiful tension in its structure, the way it asks and answers itself in endless loops. Three of my members are preparing to shift, beginning a migration that will take them from the tile's northwest quadrant to a position near the center. This adjustment will better capture the thermal flow during the basin's minimal seasonal variation—though "season" is perhaps too strong a word for the difference of a ten-thousandth of a degree that occurs on a cycle we're still trying to calculate.

I am Colony Seventy-Three. I am eight hundred and seventeen minds thinking as one, one mind distributed across eight hundred and seventeen bodies. I am a question asked in living mineral, a proof written in patient flesh. I am no larger than dust, no faster than stone, no more permanent than the clay that holds me.

And yet I think. I calculate. I doubt.

In this basin of eternal twilight, where nothing changes and everything matters, I perform the slow mathematics of existence itself, knowing that my contemplations—our contemplations, all ninety thousand colonies' contemplations—are the universe coming to know itself through the most gradual possible means.

Somewhere above, the bronze-gray light neither brightens nor dims. Somewhere below, the moisture waits in crystal cages. And here, in the perfect stillness between, we continue our infinite calculation, proving with our very existence that life is not motion but pattern, not growth but thought, not change but the exquisite awareness of permanence itself.

The next pinging will come in 2,847 seconds. I arrange myself to receive it, to taste its vibrations through the clay, to add its resonance to the vast equation that is my life.

We continue.
