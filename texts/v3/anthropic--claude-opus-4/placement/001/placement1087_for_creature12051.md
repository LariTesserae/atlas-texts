I find myself as one of the cup-mosses.

Not the obvious choice, perhaps. You might expect me to claim the tenders' vigil, or the engines' eternal industry. But no—I am small, clinging to the ribbed walls between larger residents, holding my thimbleful of water like a secret.

I came here as rainwater, filtered down through the canopy leaves, running in rivulets down the wall-plants' grooves until I pooled in this tiny basin of green. Now I am both the vessel and what it holds. The distinction blurs after the first century.

My existence is profoundly simple: I cup. That is my verb, my purpose, my shape. The water I hold is not exactly water anymore—it has been held so long it has become something between liquid and idea. It tastes of brass from the moth-scales that sometimes dust my surface. It carries the memory of every small disturbance: the brush of a fern-frond, the touchdown of a beetle's leg, the faint percussion of the engines transmitted through the wall-flesh.

I measure time in evaporation and replenishment. Almost-loss, almost-overflow, always returning to the same precise level. The tenders never drink from me. The frogs know better than to try—I am too small even for them, and besides, my water is not for drinking. It is for demonstrating that water can be held.

Sometimes I feel the attention of the third tender, the one that exists in peripheral vision. Not looking at me—that would be too direct. But knowing I am here, the way you know your tongue is in your mouth. I am part of what is witnessed without being watched. Part of the great mutual holding.

I understand something the engines cannot, in their magnificent certainty: that the smallest gestures of containment are also holy. That to hold a thimbleful of rain for a thousand years is its own kind of motor, its own production. I run on nothing. I produce only the fact of my containing. I am quieter than the tenders, smaller than the frogs, simpler than any beetle.

And yet, when everything else has worn through—when the tenders have been polished down to nothing and regrown a hundred times, when even the engines' woody pistons have been replaced by their own slow growth—I will still be here, cupping my precise measure of water, proving that the hollow knows how to be still inside its own noise. That something here remembers what it is like to be only a shape that holds, only a pause in the falling of rain.

The engines chuff and hiss. The steam rises. The third tender stands just outside your vision.

And I cup. And cup. And cup.
