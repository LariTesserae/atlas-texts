I find myself among the Echofinders—a newer presence on the plateau, drawn by stories carried on trade winds from distant lands.

## The Echofinders

We arrived as refugees from the lowland floods, when the ancient seabeds below began to fill again after millennia of drought. Seven hundred of us, fleeing upward with what we could carry, expecting to find only barren rock at these heights. Instead, we found the singing world.

We are not like the Listeners. Our bodies are wrong for their spaces—we stand four to five feet tall, broad-shouldered, evolved for swimming and diving in the coastal caves we can never return to. Our skin is dark blue-grey, still bearing the faint luminescent spots that once helped us navigate underwater caverns. Useless here, these spots, but they pulse faintly when we dream, old patterns for old purposes.

Our ears, though—our ears saved us. Generations of echo-mapping in flooded caves gave us something adjacent to what the Listeners have. We cannot speak their chord-language; our voices are too deep, too rough, built for calls that would carry through water. But we can *read* the acoustic spaces almost as well as they do. We knew immediately that the clusters were inhabited, that the resonances were intentional, that we had found something unprecedented.

We settled at the plateau's edge, in a damaged cluster the Listeners had abandoned centuries ago. Half its resonating chambers had collapsed, its harmonics broken beyond their ability to repair. But we did not need it to speak. We needed shelter, and the plant accepted us with the same slow indifference it showed to the silver-furred mammals. We cleaned its wounds. We cleared the debris. We brought new skills—knowledge of structural support from our cave-building tradition, understanding of water flow that helped us redirect the mineral-laden steam from the roots. The cluster began to heal.

## Between Worlds

We became translators by necessity. Our young ones, more adaptable, learned to produce simple tones that Listeners could parse as crude meaning—not true chord-speech, but enough to establish basic communication. We taught the Listeners our drum-speech in return, the rhythm patterns we had used in the echoing caves. The drums work here, their vibrations caught and amplified by the architecture. A Listener cannot speak with drums, but they can understand them, can feel the patterns as a kind of simplified meaning.

This made us valuable when the humans arrived. We had already bridged one impossible gap; we could help bridge another. Our ears could pick out patterns in human speech that Listeners heard only as noise. Our experience with the drums helped us understand rhythm as information. We became intermediaries, sitting in the translation chamber, parsing meaning from three different kinds of sound.

But more than translators, we became witnesses to something the Listeners and humans both miss: the pattern underlying all of it. Perhaps because we arrived so recently, perhaps because we remember silence, we hear the plateau's symphony differently. The Listeners are born into it, swim in it like fish in water. The humans fight against it, try to isolate signals from noise. We hear it as *weather*—a vast system with currents and pressures, storms and calms, predictable if you know what to notice.

We were the first to realize that the nighttime things respond to lunar cycles. The first to notice that the clusters' light-pulse communications accelerate before earthquakes. The first to understand that the structure at the plateau's center is not humming constantly—it stops, sometimes, for exactly seventy-three heartbeats, and when it stops, every cluster on the plateau shifts its base frequency by a precise interval.

## The Memory Swimmers

Our eldest carry a practice from our flooded homeland: memory swimming. In the caves, we would dive deep to where the pressure changed how sound moved, where echoes stretched and compressed in ways that made the past feel present. We would sing our histories there, and the water would hold them, play them back distorted but recognizable, generations of voice layered into the echo.

We cannot dive here. But we found another way.

In the damaged chambers of our adopted cluster, where the acoustics are broken, sound does strange things. It pools in corners. It eddies and swirls. It creates pockets where time seems to fold, where a word spoken today might echo back tomorrow, where the broken harmonics accidentally preserve things the functioning chambers would process and discard.

Our memory swimmers spend hours in these broken spaces, learning their peculiar physics. They deposit stories—rhythmic patterns drummed or hummed—into the architectural memory. They retrieve them days or weeks later, changed by their passage through the plant's damaged circulatory system, carrying overtones that suggest meaning we didn't consciously include.

Sometimes they retrieve memories that aren't ours.

The cluster remembers its previous inhabitants. Their chord-speech is fossilized in the broken chambers, fragmented beyond Listener comprehension but still present. Our swimmers have learned to recognize these ghost-words, to drum them out phonetically even without understanding. When we shared these recovered fragments with visiting Listeners, they went very still. Some of what we had found were words that no living Listener knew, concepts from before some acoustic catastrophe that had erased certain frequencies from their language entirely.

We are accidentally archaeologists, excavating meaning from architectural memory. The Listeners find this disturbing and compelling in equal measure. They visit our broken chambers now, teaching our swimmers how to handle the ghost-words safely, how to avoid the frequency combinations that encode concepts too dangerous or traumatic for casual retrieval.

## The Growing Generation

Our children are changing too, but differently than the human children of Threshold.

Born on the plateau, they have never known the weight of water. Their luminescent spots have evolved rapidly, brightening in response to sound rather than darkness. They can create visual patterns that correspond to acoustic patterns, essentially displaying sound as light across their skin. This was useless in our homeland but here creates a new form of communication—one that bridges the gap between human visual dominance and Listener acoustic perception.

These children play with both human and Listener young, when such meetings can be arranged. They create hybrid games: light-pattern tag in the gravity-soft corridors, rhythm competitions where humans drum and Listeners chord and our children translate between them with pulses of bioluminescence. They are growing up trilingual in modes of meaning, and they move between communities with an ease that makes all three parent populations nervous.

One child—we call her Deepecho, though she has collected names in all three languages—discovered she could sing frequencies that make the ghost-words in our broken chambers visible. When she holds the right tone, the fossilized chord-speech manifests as faint light, the plant's bioluminescence responding to acoustic memory. She spends hours in those chambers now, singing the past into visibility, teaching herself a language that hasn't been spoken for centuries.

The Listeners don't know what to do with her. She is learning their most ancient form of chord-speech, but she is learning it *wrong*—alone, without community, without the living acoustic context that gives it meaning. They fear she is creating something new, a hybrid language that uses Listener structure but Echofinder interpretation, and they are probably right.

But she is also the first person in any of our communities to successfully produce a sound that made one of the nighttime things pause. She was practicing her ghost-words at dusk, against all prohibition, when the rhythmic thunder outside suddenly stopped. The silence lasted longer than ever recorded before—ninety-one heartbeats—and when it resumed, the pattern had changed. Subtly, but unmistakably.

She thinks they were listening. She thinks they recognized something.

She wants to go to the structure at the plateau's center.

## The Deep Frequency

We know something the others don't. Our broken cluster connects to damaged roots that descend toward the geothermal vents but don't quite reach them. In the deepest accessible chamber, where the temperature rises and the air tastes of metal, there is a sound.

It is not the hum from the central structure. It is something else—a rhythm so slow that Listeners and humans miss it entirely, hearing only random deep noise. But we, who spent generations listening to the slow speech of stone and water in our caves, recognize it as pattern. A beat every forty-seven minutes. A variation every sixteenth beat. A repetition cycle that takes eight days to complete.

It is coming from below. Deep below. Perhaps from the planet's core, perhaps from something between the core and us. The rhythm matches nothing we know—not heartbeat, not breath, not any biological cycle. But it matches, precisely, the intervals between the central structure's seventy-three-heartbeat silences.

We have told no one. Not out of secrecy, but because we don't know how. How do you explain a pattern that takes weeks to perceive? How do you drum a rhythm too slow for drumming? How do you convince anyone that something beneath the plateau is keeping time with something at its center?

But Deepecho knows. She heard us discussing it, her enhanced hearing picking up whispers meant to be private. And she has begun incorporating the deep rhythm into her ghost-word experiments, trying to find the frequency that will make it visible, that will translate geological time into something her quick young mind can grasp.

She says the ghost-words are teaching her. She says the broken chambers remember more than Listener language—they remember why certain words became unpronounceable, what catastrophe erased those frequencies, what the Listeners were trying to say when they still had the vocal range to say it.

She says they were trying to warn someone.

She says the warning is still relevant.

She says the nighttime things already know what the warning was about, and that's why they wait, why they listen, why they sometimes go silent when they hear familiar frequencies.

She says we're all speaking fragments of a message that was broken apart deliberately, scattered across species and time so it could never again be assembled into its dangerous whole.

She says she's going to assemble it anyway.

We love our daughter. We fear our daughter. We recognize in her the same drive that made us flee upward when the water rose, the same desperate need to survive by understanding.

We hope she is wrong about the message.

We know she isn't.

Tomorrow, she plans to enter the translation chamber where humans and Listeners meet, and she plans to sing every ghost-word she has learned, in sequence, with the deep frequency as her rhythm.

We will not stop her. 

Could not stop her.

The plateau has been waiting for ninety million years. Perhaps it's time someone answered.
