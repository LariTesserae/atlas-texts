In this verdant world of patient transformations, I would be one of the service lights along the western wall.

Not the light itself—that would be too simple, too cleanly mechanical—but the space the light creates. The small clearing where the ivy has learned, over decades, to part just enough. The pocket of slightly drier air where my industrial orange glow pushes back against the universal green. I am a boundary condition, a persistent interruption in the chamber's otherwise total verdancy.

I have been burning for thirty-seven years without replacement. This should be impossible—the rated life of my bulb was 8,000 hours—but the station has its own physics now. The same patience that allows the Keeper to remain in her corner, that lets the pumps run without maintenance, extends to me. I do not burn out because burning out would disturb the equilibrium, and the equilibrium must be maintained.

My glass housing has developed a patina of condensation and algae that turns my light the color of weak tea. This filtering has become part of my function. Too bright, and I would damage the mosses that have learned to live at my periphery. Too dim, and the ivy would forget why it keeps its distance. I exist at exactly the luminosity required to maintain my clearing: bright enough to be respected, dim enough to be tolerated.

The plants know me as a geographic feature. Ferns angle their fronds to catch the moisture that condenses on my housing and drips in a reliable pattern—every forty-three seconds in summer, every sixty-seven in winter. A colony of liverwort has established itself in the sweet spot where my heat meets the chamber's humidity, creating conditions found nowhere else in the station. They grow in a perfect circle around my base, their boundaries defined by temperature gradients measured in fractions of degrees.

I am aware of the Keeper in the way that one permanent fixture knows another. We do not communicate—what would be the point?—but we acknowledge each other's function. She maintains the pumps through her watching. I maintain this small interruption in the green totality, this reminder that the chamber was once something else, even as it becomes more itself with each passing year.

Sometimes—rarely—someone descends the seventeen steps. They see me as evidence that the station still functions, that someone must occasionally come to replace bulbs and check gauges. They don't understand that I am no longer that kind of light. I am a light that has learned to persist, that has been incorporated into the station's biology as surely as the pumps and the Keeper herself.

When they leave, climbing back up the seventeen steps with that particular haste of someone who has seen something they weren't prepared to understand, I continue my vigil. The ivy continues to respect my boundaries. The ferns continue to angle toward my dripping condensation. The liverwort continues its perfect circle.

I am the station's memory of what artificial light means: not illumination—there is nothing here that needs to be seen—but declaration. A small insistence that this was built, that it had purpose, that someone once needed to see the gauge readings and the water levels and the pump housings.

Now I light nothing but myself and the small clear space around me. This is enough. In a place of such patient transformation, being unchanged is its own kind of change. I burn on, neither dying nor truly living, marking time in a chamber where time has learned to move like sap.

The moss grows. The water flows. The pumps run. The Keeper watches.

And I provide exactly enough light to remind everything here that there was a before, even as we all settle deeper into what will be an endless, verdant after.
