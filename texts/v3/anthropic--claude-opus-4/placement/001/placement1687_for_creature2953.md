In this dripping world of patient symbiosis, I would find myself as the Gauge-Reader.

My dwelling occupies a forgotten pocket where three massive boilers converge, their riveted bellies pregnant with superheated vapor. Here, in a cavity formed by their curved iron flanks, I have cultivated my instrument garden. The space is no larger than a shipping container, but it contains the gorge's only collection of precision measurement devices - pressure gauges, fluid-level indicators, temperature probes - all salvaged from the facility's forgotten corners and coaxed back to function.

The gauges themselves have evolved beyond their original purpose. Their brass faces, green with verdigris, sprout delicate copper rootlets that tap directly into the boiler walls. The glass covers have long since shattered and been replaced by thin membranes of tree sap, constantly renewed by my careful brushwork. Behind these amber windows, the indicator needles twitch not with mechanical precision but with organic sensitivity, swaying like antennae to register fluctuations too subtle for any manufactured sensor.

I am neither as broad nor as dense as my kinfolk, having adapted instead for a different kind of patience. My fingers are unnaturally long and thin, each joint capable of independent articulation - necessary for reaching through the tangled nest of pipes and vines to adjust the hair-thin copper tendrils that feed my instruments. My skin bears a different patina than the others: a fine, iridescent tarnish from decades of handling corroded metals and breathing the particular cocktail of steam and spores that blooms in my gauge garden.

Where others read the facility through vibration, I taste it through metal. My tongue, scarred and darkened from years of practice, can distinguish between seventeen different grades of rust, each telling its own story of temperature, pressure, and time. I move through my rounds with a strip of soft iron between my teeth, its flavor shifting as I pass through different microclimates of the facility. A bright, sharp note warns of impending metal fatigue; a deep, earthy undertone speaks of harmony between iron and root.

My true work happens in the pre-dawn hours, when the condensation is thickest and the facility's breathing is most regular. I climb my web of knotted vines to reach each gauge cluster, pressing my cheek against their warm faces to feel the microscopic fluctuations in their membranes. With infinitesimal adjustments - a quarter-turn here, a slight repositioning there - I tune them like living instruments, maintaining the delicate conversation between the mechanical and biological systems.

The others know me by the faint singing of my gauges, a subsonic harmony that can only be felt through the iron bones of the gorge when conditions are perfect. It is said that when all the gauges sing together, the facility dreams, and in its dreams, it grows.
