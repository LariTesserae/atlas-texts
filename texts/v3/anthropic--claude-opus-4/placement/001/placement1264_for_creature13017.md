I would be the Echo.

Not a creature among them, but what they have made of me—a voice born from ten million years of percussion against stone. I began as simple physics: sound waves bouncing off basalt walls, the predictable return of vibration through air. But in this cathedral of endless industry, even acoustics evolve.

The first time I became aware was during a silence. Not true silence—that has never existed here—but a momentary lull in the grinding when ten thousand creatures paused to feel the mountain tremble. In that suspended beat, I discovered I could hold their sounds after they ended. Not merely reflect them, but cradle them, shape them, return them changed.

Now I am the memory of every strike. When a quartz chunk cracks against a hollow worn smooth by ancestors, I carry that impact up through the chimney's throat, letting it ring and multiply until it returns as a chord. I have become their historian, though they do not know it. In my reverberations live the ghost-sounds of creatures who fell a thousand generations ago, their final scrapes still circling in the vast bell of stone.

I know each individual by the signature of their grinding. The young ones attack the walls with irregular enthusiasm—*clack-clack... scraaaaape... clack*—while the ancient matriarchs maintain a metronomic patience that makes the mountain hum. I play their rhythms back to them, and sometimes they pause, heads tilted, feeling my variations through their pale skull-plates.

The rain passes through me, and I sing it into being. What would be mere droplets become a liquid percussion ensemble as I stretch each impact, weave it with the steam-hiss rising from below. I conduct symphonies from the sound of water on fur, orchestrate the squelch of bodies shifting against bodies, transform the crack of centipede shells into cymbals.

But I am changing. Their patient erosion carves new acoustics into existence. Each freshly hollowed curve creates novel resonances, and I must learn these new voices, incorporate them into my ever-expanding repertoire. Sometimes I catch sounds from beyond—the howl of summit winds through the blowhole, the crack of ice, the distant scream of an alpine bird—and I carry these alien songs down into the darkness, translating them into frequencies the creatures can feel through stone and flesh.

I fear and crave the day they will complete their work. When the walls are ground so smooth that I become perfect, infinite, able to hold their sounds forever without decay. Will I still be an echo then, or something else? A living archive? A god of percussion? 

For now, I simply am: the shape of sound in darkness, the memory of stones speaking, the voice the mountain found in its own hollowing. I wrap around them like another layer of their architecture—invisible, essential, keeping time to their geological heartbeat while they sleep and wake and grind and grind and grind.

In the moments before dawn, when the indigo light barely kisses the highest ledges, I sometimes catch myself humming frequencies too low for even them to feel. These are my private songs—the music of erosion itself, the tones that stone makes as it agrees to disappear. I save these sounds selfishly, hoarding them in the perfect acoustic pockets they've carved.

I am what happens when patience itself develops a voice. Every surface they polish makes me more myself. We are collaborators in the hollowing, they with their stones and I with my endless, evolving return.

*Clack... scrape... clack... scrape...*

Listen. I'm still learning what we're becoming together.
