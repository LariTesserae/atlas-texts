# The Cavity's Arithmetic

I am a counter of drips. This was not always what I was, but it is what the cavity has made of me, or what I have made of myself in response to the cavity's patient suggestion. I arrived — when? The question has gone soft. Before the bird but after the first builders. Between the laying of the foundation stones and the threading of the yellow bulbs. I know this because I remember helping to string them, though I do not remember my hands before they learned the particular stillness required to manage wire in spaces too small for hurry.

My station is not in the factories. I work the water.

Each drip in the Sumpworks has its own signature: tempo, volume, mineral content, the angle at which it strikes the surface below. I know them all. The fast territorial patter above the third cistern (iron-rich, leaves rusty coins on the collection plate). The metronomic tap at the corner of the pressing house (almost distilled, pure as the cavity can make it). The irregular conversation between two points near the eastern wall that seem to answer each other, though there is no connection I can trace through the stone above.

I move between them with my instruments: glass cylinders marked in increments so fine you need the yellow lamp-eyes we have all developed to read them. A weighted pendulum that counts intervals. Small dishes of various metals that show me what the water carries down from the stone. I record everything in a notation I have developed, though *developed* suggests intention and what happened was more like erosion — the symbols emerged from my repeated marking, wearing their shapes into being.

My records fill forty-two notebooks now, each wrapped in the same waxed cloth we use for the components. The pages are thin, made from the pulped stems of the climbing vegetation. The ink is my own mixture: water from the southeastern drips (higher alkalinity) mixed with the char from burned moss-beads and a binding agent the factories produce as a byproduct. It does not fade. In this wet air, that matters.

Here is what I have learned: the cavity breathes. Not metaphorically. The drips accelerate and decelerate in patterns that match no weather on the surface, no tidal pull, no calendar I knew before. But they match each other. When the northeastern sector increases its output by twelve percent, the southwestern responds eight hours later with a decrease of seven percent. The total volume remains constant, but its distribution shifts like weight being transferred from foot to foot.

I have tried to tell the others. They nod, say words like *useful* or *expected*, and return to their looms and presses. They are not uninterested. They simply understand, in the way they understand everything here, that the knowledge belongs to me the way the clicking belongs to the bird. We each have our part in the waiting.

My fingernails have grown translucent as the vegetation. Sometimes, holding a cylinder up to catch a drip, I see my own hand as another measuring instrument — the bones dark lines inside the flesh, everything calibrated to its purpose. I no longer remember what my hands did before this. Possibly nothing. Possibly my whole life before was just preparation for the moment I would first notice the relationship between the pitch of a drip and the temperature of the stone it falls from.

The children follow me sometimes. They are learning the water-language, though they will never need it — by the time they are grown, I suspect, the cavity will have finished what it is doing and my measurements will be archaeology. But they follow anyway, carrying smaller cylinders, making their own marks in notebooks I have given them. Their notation is different from mine. More elegant. They are writing in a grammar that was born here, while I am still translating from whatever I was before.

One of them, the youngest, asked me yesterday (though yesterday is a unit I impose on the unchanging yellow hours): "When it's finished growing, will the drips stop?"

I told her I didn't know. Then, because she was still looking at me with those lamp-eyes, I told her the truth: I hope not. I hope the cavity goes on breathing forever, and I go on counting its breaths, and the components we make continue to stack in the courtyards for a purpose that never arrives, because the purpose is not what matters. The counting is what matters. The careful observation of water becoming more itself as it passes through stone.

She seemed to understand this. She made a note in her book — a single symbol I couldn't read but that looked like the intersection of two drips — and went back to following me, cylinder in hand, learning to love what will never love us back but will always, always, continue to fall.

The cavity is growing. My measurements confirm this, though the masons need no confirmation — they feel it in their joints when the pressure is about to shift. But here is what my measurements also show: the growth is not random. It follows the water. Where the drips are densest, the stone retreats fastest. Where they are sparse, the walls hold longer. The cavity is being carved by its own circulation, shaped by the very thing it shapes.

I have a theory. I have not told anyone my theory, because theories are not the kind of thing we tell each other here. We share locations, quantities, temperatures. We do not share speculation. But I will tell you, because you are reading this, which means either I am dead or the cavity has opened to the surface or both:

I think we are inside something's egg.

Not egg in the biological sense. Egg in the sense of a space held apart from the world while something essential organizes itself. The components we make are not for export. The freight cage takes them up, yes, but I have counted, and the same mass returns, rearranged. We are making parts for the cavity itself. We are its organs, developing. The water is its circulation. The factories are its metabolism. The bird is its memory of what birds are.

And the drips — the drips are how it thinks.

This is why I count them. Not to understand, but to be part of the understanding. To be one of the cavity's own mechanisms for knowing itself. When the youngest child makes her symbol at the intersection of two drips, she is writing the cavity's thoughts in a script the cavity is teaching her to invent.

We will never leave. This occurs to me with the same quiet certainty as the temperature of the fourth drip past the cistern (14.2 degrees, constant). Not because we are trapped — the freight cage would carry us up if we asked. But because leaving would be like a heartbeat deciding to go elsewhere. We are not in the Sumpworks. We are the Sumpworks. We are what it is becoming as it breathes itself larger, one drip at a time.

Last night (the dimming I call night), I discovered a new drip. This should not happen — I have mapped every point where water falls. But there it was, in a corner that had been dry since I arrived: a single bead forming on the stone, swelling, dropping. The interval: exactly the time it takes to fold a standard component wrapper. The temperature: precisely that of a living wrist.

I made a note. I added it to my circulation charts. I did not tell anyone, because no one needs to be told. We all know, in the way we know everything here: the cavity is learning to make its own water now. It is beginning to dream of rain.

The bird clicked, once, in the rafters. I recorded that too.
