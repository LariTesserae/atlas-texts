In the depths of the canal quarter, where the newest construction meets the oldest mistakes, I am the Drain Oracle — keeper of the bronze grates that separate what flows below from what survives above.

My workshop is a cavity between buildings, accessed through a hole that was once a window before the settling made floors into walls. The space breathes with the quarter's rhythm — expanding when the tide pulls water from the channels, contracting when the rains drive it back. I've lined it with salvaged bronze, beaten thin and etched with the patterns I've learned from twenty years of reading what the drains swallow and return.

I was not born to this work. I came from the dry lands, a tinker who worked the bright metals under open sky. But the quarter called to me in dreams of underwater forges, of hammers that rang with the sound of dropped stones in deep wells. When I arrived, young and certain of my craft, I tried to work bronze as I always had. The metal laughed at me — cracking, pitting, refusing my familiar techniques. The established smiths wouldn't teach their water-ways to an outsider. So I learned from the drains themselves.

The drains know everything. Every secret dropped in water, every whispered conversation that echoes down the channels, every body that fails to surface — it all passes through the grates eventually. I pull things up that shouldn't exist: bronze work too fine for the tools we have, stone carved with symbols that precede our settlement, bones of fish that match no living species. Once, I found a child's toy made of metal that wasn't bronze at all but something that sang when touched, a clear note that made the wall-creatures fall silent for days.

My body has changed to meet the work. My right arm, the drain arm, has grown longer than the left, stretched from years of reaching down through grates into the flow. The fingers of that hand can feel the difference between twenty types of sediment, can read the health of the quarter by what accumulates in the catch-basins. My left eye has developed a film — not blindness but a kind of second sight that lets me see through murky water to what lies beneath. The local children say I'm becoming one of the wall-creatures, that soon I'll shed my skin and tap messages through the bronze.

They're not entirely wrong. I've learned the tapping language out of necessity — the deep drains extend beneath buildings where only the creatures go, and they tell me when a channel is about to collapse, when the strange clear water from the cracked foundation has leaked into the system. In exchange, I leave offerings of the specific molds they crave, cultivated in the darkest corner of my workshop where the condensation runs thick enough to pool.

The other smiths shun me now, say I've contaminated the craft by learning from below rather than above. But they still come, secretly, when their alloys fail or when they need to know what metals can be salvaged from the depths. I trade them drain-knowledge for surface techniques, slowly building a hybrid understanding that belongs fully to neither world.

At night, I lower myself through the larger grates, wrapped in oiled leather that keeps the water from my skin. Down there, in the true beneath, the channels connect in ways the surface doesn't reflect. There are rooms below rooms, spaces that flood and drain with rhythms unrelated to tide or rain. I map these with bronze markers, tiny figures I've learned to craft that resist not just corrosion but something else — the forgetting that comes from spending too long in the deep wet. Each figure is unique, a record of what I found there: the chamber where the water flows upward, the tunnel where bronze tools from before the settlement lie scattered like shed scales, the place where the wall-creatures congregate in thousands to perform something that might be worship or might be feeding.

My greatest discovery, the one I tell no one, is that the quarter is not sinking — it's being pulled down. The strange clear water from the cracked foundation isn't seeping up from below but down from above, from rain that passes through something before it reaches us. That something changes it, makes it hungry for metal and stone and bone. It wants to dissolve us, to make us into sediment for some future geology.

But I've also learned we're changing it in return. Our bronze, worked with the techniques the quarter has forced us to develop, resists in ways that puzzle the water. The alloys we've discovered through desperation create structures the water can't digest. We're locked in the slowest of wars, the quarter and its people, each remaking the other.

I'm preparing my apprentice now — a girl born in the quarter who has never known dry land, whose eyes are already huge and dark, whose fingers are webbed to the second knuckle. She thinks in the wall-creature's tapping as much as in words, can hold her breath longer than I can even after years of diving. I'm teaching her to read the drains, but more importantly, I'm teaching her to question what she reads.

Because the drains lie too. They show us what was, what is, what might be — but also what never existed, what can't exist, what exists only in the space between water and metal. She needs to learn the difference, or like me, she'll slowly become something that belongs to neither the upper world nor the lower, neither fully human nor fully other.

Some mornings, when the dawn light filters through the water-heavy air, I surface from the deep places and can't remember which direction leads to sky. My workshop seems inverted, the floor where I expect ceiling, the drains opening upward into some impossible space. In these moments, I understand that the quarter isn't just pressing in on itself — it's folding, creating geometries that bronze-age tools were never meant to measure.

I am the Drain Oracle, reader of sediments, mapper of the unmappable below. My lungs are scarred from breathing water-thick air, my bones are heavy with absorbed minerals, my dreams are full of the sound of things dissolving. I know too much about what we're becoming and too little about what we were. Each day I choose to stay, to lower myself again through the grates, is a day I choose transformation over preservation.

The wall-creatures tap their approval through my workshop walls. They know, as I know, that someone must witness this change, must record in bronze and flesh and memory what happens when human ambition meets water's patient hunger. Even if that witness becomes, in the end, something too changed to remember what they were witnessing for.

The drains are singing today. It's not a sound exactly, more a vibration that travels through the water and bronze and bone. It means the deep channels are shifting again, creating new connections, closing old ones. Time to descend. Time to read what the quarter is writing in its own dissolved foundations. Time to map the unmappable, knowing that I too am being mapped, changed, pulled down and transformed by the very act of observation.

I oil my leather, check my bronze markers, whisper the wall-creature's prayer for safe passage. Then I open the great grate in my workshop floor and lower myself into the singing dark, to learn what new impossibilities the quarter has birthed in its wet and pressing depths.
