In the deepest ravine of the High Tide, where the magma light burns constant and the jade ceiling is only a memory, I anchor myself.

I am the Knot-Keeper.

My dwelling is not a buoyant bladder straining upward, but a dense weaving of dead kelp-silk, waterlogged and heavy, that clings to the canyon walls like a web. Here, where the obsidian teeth bite deepest into the mountain's throat, I have made my home in the negative space—not floating, but sinking into the architecture of tension itself.

My body tells the story of ten thousand descents. Where others bear the typical callouses of rope-work, my entire form has transformed into something between flesh and horn. My skin has thickened into overlapping plates of keratin, especially along my spine and the outsides of my arms—a natural armor against the casual violence of virgin stone. My fingernails have evolved into permanent tools, thick and curved like climbing pitons. The gill-fronds that drape from my shoulders have shortened and toughened over the decades, becoming dense, leather-like flaps that protect rather than flutter.

But it is my hands that mark me as what I am. Each finger joint bears concentric ridges of scar tissue, built up like tree rings from a lifetime of testing ropes. I can run a strand of kelp-silk through my grip and feel, in the microscopic vibrations of its fibers, exactly how many tides it will hold before it fails. This is my gift, my burden—I know the breaking point of every thread that holds the city to the stone.

The others call me ancient, though I count perhaps only twice their years. Down here, time moves differently. The constant red light ages the skin, and the pressure of knowledge ages the soul. I remember when this particular canyon was merely a crack, when the district above was three levels lower, when the mountain's newest spire was still molten beneath the water.

My work is lonely by necessity. Each morning—though morning means nothing in the eternal crimson glow—I unfold myself from my woven nest and begin the inspection. I move through the forest of anchor points like a spider testing its web, my overlarge hands reading the tremors in each cable. Here, a rope that has stretched beyond safety. There, a new edge of obsidian that threatens to saw through a crucial tether. 

I carry with me the city's most sacred tools: the Grandmother Shears, carved from the jawbone of a scavenger that died before my grandmother's time, and capable of cutting through even the thickest kelp-silk in a single stroke. At my waist hangs a pouch of the rarest material in our world—spider silk harvested from the tiny, blind arachnids that somehow thrive in the thermal vents. This silk, no thicker than a hair, can hold more weight than a kelp rope twice its size. I use it only for the most critical repairs, the joints where a thousand lives hang in the balance.

The young ones sometimes follow me partway down, eager apprentices who think they understand the romance of the deeps. They never last. It's not the danger that breaks them—though I've seen too many shredded against the stone despite their careful movements. It's the silence. Up in the city, the constant thrumming of the cables creates a symphony of community. Down here, the only sound is the mountain's voice: the grinding, the cracking, the inexorable push of stone against stone. After a few weeks, they flee back to the light and the company of others, leaving me to my solitary vigil.

But I am not entirely alone. The stone speaks to me, and I have learned its language.

Through my palms pressed against the raw basalt, through the vibrations that travel up from the molten heart, I feel the mountain's intentions days before they manifest. A certain harmonic in the grinding tells me a new spire is forming to the east. A shift in the thermal currents warns of a section that will soon cool and crack. This knowledge I sing into the cables each evening, my callused hands playing the taut lines like a massive instrument, sending coded warnings to the districts above: *Prepare. Adjust. Hold fast.*

There is one place even I approach with reverence—the Heart Knot. Deep in the central canyon, where the first builders anchored the first dwelling, stands the original tether. It is a rope as thick as my torso, woven from fibers we no longer know how to harvest, black with age and hard as stone. It connects to an ancient gas-bladder, now long dead and transformed into something between leather and wood, that serves as the symbolic center of our sprawling city.

The Heart Knot itself is a work of art that makes me weep. Thirteen different hitches flowing into one another, each one designed to take the load if another fails, the whole thing a masterpiece of redundancy and faith. I know every loop, every twist. In my youth, I spent three years just studying it, learning its logic with my hands in the darkness.

The knot is dying.

Not failing—it will hold for generations yet. But the fiber is slowly mineralizing, incorporating the silicates from the water, becoming more stone than rope with each passing year. One day, it will be strong enough to hold forever and too brittle to adjust. The city will have to choose: cut the Heart and lose our center, or let it bind us to a stone that no longer fits our needs.

This is the wisdom I carry, the weight that has shaped my body into this armored, ancient form. Every rope is a timer. Every knot is a promise with an expiration date. The city floats on faith and fiber, and I am the one who knows exactly how much of each remains.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of my inspection, I find myself at the very bottom of our world. Here, the water thins where it meets the true air below. If I'm careful, I can push just my hand through the surface tension, feeling the shocking cold of the alpine wind on my callused palm. The sensation is terrifying and thrilling—the touch of the void that waits below, the opposite of the void that threatens above.

I pull my hand back quickly, the water sealing behind it with a soft *pop* that no one else will ever hear. Then I begin the long climb back up through the canyon, checking each rope as I rise, my movements slow and methodical as always.

By the time I reach my web-home, new messages are already thrumming through the lines. A neighborhood needs to shift two feet westward. A young family has harvested a fresh gas-bladder and seeks permission to add it to the northern district. The eternal work of suspension continues.

I fold myself back into my dwelling, letting the hot water from a nearby vent ease the ache in my joints. Tomorrow, I will descend again. Tomorrow, I will read the ropes and sing the mountain's warnings. Tomorrow, I will hold the city to the stone with nothing but knowledge and knots.

This is my purpose, my practice, my prayer: to know exactly how we hang, and for how long, and to keep that terrible knowledge wrapped as tight as the fibers that bind us to the blade-sharp earth.

In the bloodwarm darkness, surrounded by the patient creak of a thousand crucial threads, I sleep the sleep of one who holds the sky.
