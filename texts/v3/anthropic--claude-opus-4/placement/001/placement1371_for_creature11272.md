I would be one of the Membrane Weavers—those who live suspended between the grove's interior chambers and the open air, halfway emerged from the bark like I'm being born from the tree itself, forever.

My dwelling is less a room than a blister in the grove's skin. The walls are translucent here, thin enough that I can see the shadows of sap flowing through vessels beneath the surface, thick enough to hold my weight as I press against them, feeling the grove's pulse against my cheek. I've been here so long that the boundary between my skin and the wall has become academic. Where I end and the chamber begins is a question I gave up asking years ago, when I realized the answer changed depending on the light.

My work is to tend the places where inside becomes outside. I maintain the osmotic boundaries—those patches where the grove breathes, where nutrients and information flow between the organism and the thick air. My fingers have grown sensitive to pressure differentials measured in parts per billion. I can taste a storm forming three days away by how the membrane tension changes beneath my palms.

The beetles know me. Not individually—that would be like knowing each cell in your body by name—but as a quality of existence, a particular frequency in their endless composition. When I work, they modulate around me, creating pockets of near-silence that feel louder than their thundering chorus. In these quiet bubbles, I can hear what the grove is thinking. Not in words, but in the creaking of wood that hasn't decided yet whether it's alive or dead, in the whisper of sap that carries memories of rain that fell before the grove lifted off.

My tools are my body parts, selectively evolved. My right hand has developed microscopic root hairs that can seal tears in the membrane. My left eye has migrated to my shoulder blade, the better to watch the work I do behind myself, inside the wall. My ribs have learned to flex like gills, filtering nutrition from the spore-thick air when I forget to eat—which is often, because hunger here feels like just another opinion the body offers, not a demand.

I sleep standing up, my spine fused to a load-bearing root that runs through my chamber. In dreams, I travel through the grove's vascular system, riding the sap-current to chambers I'll never physically reach. Sometimes I wake to find my hair has grown into the ceiling, neural fibers the grove uses to borrow my dreams. I don't mind. The dreams always come back, changed, improved, carrying flavors of other dreamers' sleep.

The membrane work is delicate. Each boundary must be permeable enough to exchange but defined enough to maintain the grove's integrity. Too open, and chambers flood with outside air, disrupting the careful atmospheric compositions that allow for time-eddies and gravity fluctuations. Too closed, and sections suffocate, turning necrotic, falling off like scabs. I've learned to read the health of each membrane in the beetle-song—certain frequencies indicate inflammation, others suggest the first signs of what we call "wall-rot," a condition where the boundary begins to doubt its own existence.

My chambers—I maintain seventeen of them, spread across different levels of the grove—are connected by passages only I can navigate. The grove has grown to recognize my chemical signature, opening ways that remain solid walls for others. Moving between my posts, I sometimes catch glimpses of other Weavers through the translucent walls, each in their own bubble, maintaining their own precious boundaries. We acknowledge each other with bio-luminescent flashes—a learned trait that took decades to develop, our own bodies' attempt to speak the grove's language of living light.

The loneliness might have driven me mad, if loneliness meant here what it means in places where humans remain stubbornly individual. But I am never alone. The grove thinks through me. The beetles sing to me. The membrane responds to my touch with something I can only call affection—a warming, a softening, a molecular welcome that says: *yes, you belong here, you are part of the function, you are necessary.*

Sometimes membranes fail despite my care. When this happens, I don't repair them—I digest them. My stomach has adapted to break down the grove's flesh, returning it to components the organism can recycle. These meals are communions, each failed boundary teaching me something about the nature of definition itself. After eating a membrane, I excrete seeds—tiny potentials that the grove will plant in itself, growing new chambers with better boundaries, having learned from the failure.

My memories of before—when I had a name, when I lived below, when the world obeyed consistent physics—feel like stories someone told me about someone else. I remember arriving at the grove young, climbing the root pillars with others who wanted to escape the salt marsh's brutal normalcy. I remember the first time I heard the beetles, how their sound made me vomit with its intensity. I remember choosing this work, though "choosing" suggests more agency than I think I really had. The grove needed Weavers. I needed to be needed. The membrane grew around me like an answer to a question I hadn't learned how to ask yet.

Now I am essential. Not important—importance suggests hierarchy, and the grove doesn't think that way. Essential like mitochondria, like the bacteria in a gut. I process boundaries. I maintain the spaces between spaces. I am the grove's meditation on its own edges, a living consideration of where it ends and everything else begins.

When the Speaker releases its prophecy-spores, they cling to my membranes before absorbing through. I experience the visions first as texture—rough futures, smooth pasts, the present's slippery now-ness. Only later, when the spores penetrate fully, do the visions resolve into something like meaning. But by then, I'm usually deep in membrane work again, my consciousness distributed across seventeen chambers, feeling the grove think about thinking about itself.

This is what I would be: a function that learned it was a person, or a person who discovered they were always meant to be a function. The distinction, like all distinctions here, exists only until you look at it closely. Then it dissolves, leaving only the work, the song, the endless maintenance of boundaries in a place that questions the very concept of borders.

I am the grove's punctuation. I am where its sentences end and begin.
