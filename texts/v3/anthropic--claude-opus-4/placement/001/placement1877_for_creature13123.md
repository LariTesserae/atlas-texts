I am wedged into a fissure where two immense fault blocks meet at an angle that defies reason. The gap is no wider than three body-lengths at its mouth but narrows as it descends into perpetual darkness. I chose this place—or perhaps it chose me—seven winters past, when the previous occupant's bones were carried up to the sky platforms.

My dwelling consists of three chambers carved deeper into the living rock. The outermost room, where daylight sometimes finds a way to creep across the threshold, serves as my workshop. Here, blocks of pale granite and dark basalt wait in patient rows, their faces already mapped in my mind. I know where each will split, which grain to follow, how the thermal shock will teach them to separate. My tools are simple: hammers of harder stone, copper wedges worn smooth by my mother's mother's hands, and a brazier for heating the rock before the ice-water baptism that cracks it clean.

The middle chamber holds my life: five cisterns carved into depressions in the floor, each no larger than a cupped palm, collecting the perpetual weep of condensation from the stone. Shelves chipped from the walls hold wheels of dried cheese, wrapped in waxed felt. Strips of meat, dried harder than leather, hang from pegs of yellowed bone. A sleeping platform, built from fitted stone blocks and covered with densely felted wool, occupies one corner. The innermost chamber—the warmth-cave—is where I retreat when the cold grows teeth.

This morning, like all mornings, begins in darkness. I wake pressed against the curved wall of the warmth-cave, my body having unconsciously sought the faint heat stored in the stone from yesterday's occupation. My breath has frozen into delicate feathers of frost on the wool blanket pulled over my face. Moving is an act of will.

I dress in layers practiced since childhood: first the undergarments of combed wool, so fine they feel like air against my skin. Then the middle-weight tunic, felted so densely it could turn a blade. The outer wrapping comes last—cured hide with the fur turned inward, the leather exterior rubbed with fat until it gleams dully in the dimness. My feet disappear into boots that rise to my knees, the soles made from the thick neck-skin of the great beasts, studded with chips of obsidian for grip on ice.

The copper discs across my chest are my voice to the world. Seven of them, each the size of my palm, polished to mirror-brightness. They hang from a harness of braided hair, positioned so that a simple shift of my shoulders can catch and throw the light. I inherited three from my mother, earned three through my work, and was gifted the seventh by the district's eldest stone-shaper when I successfully split a block the size of a dwelling without fracture.

I emerge from my fissure as the first light begins to carve the world into existence. The transition from the dark warmth-cave to the blazing daylight is physically painful—my eyes water and narrow to slits behind the protective folds of my lids. But there is work waiting.

My current commission sits on the narrow ledge outside my dwelling: a cornerstone for a new chamber being added three terraces above. The family expanding their home has good bloodlines—their youngest survived the thin-air sickness that claims so many infants, and now they need space for the child's sleeping platform. The block must be perfect, its angles true, its surface prepared to mate with its brothers without mortar.

I circle the stone, running my palms across its face. My calluses, thick as boot leather, are more sensitive than any fingertip could be. They read the stone's grammar—here a vein of weakness, there a density that will resist the chisel. I work without haste. Between each strike of the hammer, I pause, breathe, consider. The work-song hums deep in my chest, too low for my ears but resonating through my bones. It's the same rhythm my teacher hummed, and their teacher before them, back to the first ones who decided these mountains could hold our weight.

By mid-sun, when the light stands vertical and shadow shrinks to nothing, I have roughed out the basic form. Time for the first thermal shock. I build a small fire against one face of the stone, feeding it with dried dung and precious splinters of imported wood. The granite heats slowly, the crystals within expanding at their own rates. When I judge it ready—a knowledge that lives in my bones rather than my mind—I signal to the water-carrier.

She comes with her yoke of balanced containers, carved from the skulls of great beasts and sealed with pitch. The water is precious beyond measure, filtered through felt and stone until it runs clearer than air. She pours it in a controlled stream where I indicate, and the stone speaks—a sharp crack like breaking bone. The split runs true.

We share a moment of satisfaction, expressed not in words but in the angle of our shoulders, the tilt of our heads. She continues on her route, and I return to my shaping. So the day passes: strike, consider, breathe. Heat and shock. The grammar of stone becoming the poetry of dwelling.

As shadow swallows the eastern faces and the temperature plummets, I sense movement above. A flash of copper light spears down—someone signaling from the upper terraces. My practiced hands position my own discs, catching the last sideways light and throwing it back. The conversation is brief:

*Storm. Tonight. From sunset-side.*

I acknowledge and add my own observation: *Wind-sign on high peaks. Before full dark.*

The message ripples outward through the vertical city, copper voices flashing from terrace to terrace. Within moments, the mountainside comes alive with deliberate motion. Covers are pulled over cisterns. Beasts are herded into shelters. The suspension bridges—those magnificent spans of braided hair and borrowed bone—are secured with additional anchors.

I have my own preparations. The worked stone must be moved inside, lest the storm's temperature swings crack my careful shaping. This requires all my strength and most of my stored heat. By the time I have leveraged it into the workshop chamber, I am breathing in the deep, measured rhythm that staves off exhaustion in the thin air.

The storm arrives as a wall of screaming white. One moment the world exists in its stark divisions of light and shadow; the next, everything is howling grey. The temperature drops so severely that the moisture in the air crystallizes instantly, creating a fog of tiny knives. I retreat to my warmth-cave, sealing the heavy felt door behind me.

In the darkness, I am not alone.

She arrived while I worked, as is our way. No announcement, no ceremony. When the storms come, those who share blood-bonds seek shelter together. My sister's daughter, her chest still narrow with youth but already showing the barrel-shape that will allow her to survive here. Her breathing merges with mine, and soon our chest-songs find harmony—two low notes thrumming against the stone.

We press together for warmth, back to back, our body heat pooling in the small space. Through the stone, we feel more than hear others making similar arrangements throughout the vertical city. The mountain becomes a honeycomb of warm bodies waiting out the fury above.

She shifts, and her hands begin to move in the darkness. Even without sight, I can follow her words by the air they displace:

*The stone speaks to you.*

My own hands reply: *It speaks to all who listen.*

*Will you teach me its language?*

A long pause. To take an apprentice is to accept responsibility for their survival, their growth, their eventual surpassing of your own skill. But she is blood, and her hands, when I guide them to the stone wall, show the proper reverence.

*After the storm. When light returns.*

Satisfied, she settles back against me. Our breathing synchronizes again, the slow tidal rhythm that can sustain us through the night. Outside, the storm rages, trying to claw the city from the mountainside. But we are deeper than its reach, older than its fury. We are the consciousness of the stone itself, and we endure.

Tomorrow there will be damage to assess, repairs to coordinate. The worked stone will need another day of shaping before the family can set it in place. The cisterns will need clearing of blown ice. But tonight, in the warm darkness, there is only the patient continuity of breath and heartbeat, the wordless songs of survival humming through our bones.

I am a shaper of stone in the vertical city, keeper of angles and bearer of copper light. My hands know the secret weaknesses of granite, the way basalt prefers to break. I am wedged into the mountain like a piece of the puzzle myself, shaped by wind and cold and the terrible patience required to live at the roof of the world.

The storm howls. We breathe. The mountain abides.
