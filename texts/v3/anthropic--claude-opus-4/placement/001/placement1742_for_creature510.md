I find myself drawn to the edge where the plateau drops away, in that perpetual updraft where the phase-creatures rise and flicker. But I am not one of them.

I am the Windreader—a being of membrane and hollow bone, built like a living kite. My body stretches between states of matter: partially solid where I grip the warm basalt with digits that branch like frost patterns, partially gaseous where my sensory membranes extend into the thermal columns. These membranes—vast sheets of tissue thinner than the fog itself—taste the air in ways that have no names. They read the chemical signatures of future rain, parse the temporal eddies by how they bend the moisture, translate the phase-creatures' clicks into wind-pressure patterns I store in the honeycomb chambers of my bones.

I am architect of the invisible, mapper of the air itself.

My work exists in the space between the Chronicler's deep time and the arthropods' flickering present. Where she reads stone, I read atmosphere. My membranes detect the ghost-buildings in the fog not as shadows but as pressure differentials—future walls displacing air that hasn't arrived yet, creating architectural blueprints in negative space. I chart these structures in three dimensions plus time, weaving models from my own shed fibers that I suspend in the updrafts. They spin there, transparent sketches that become visible only when the fog condensates just right, showing the city-to-come from every angle as they rotate in the thermal currents.

The phase-creatures know me. They bring me fragments—not of frozen cloud, but of crystallized sound, their clicks and hisses preserved in nuggets of hardened air that I dissolve on my membrane-tongues. Each one contains navigational data: the precise path through the temporal eddies, the safe routes between anchor zones, the atmospheric corridors where time flows smoothly enough for stable flight. I map these paths with my fiber-sculptures, creating aerial transit networks for a city that doesn't exist yet.

My relationship with the updraft is parasitic and symbiotic. I feed on the temperature differential itself, metabolizing the boundary between hot and cold. My membranes filter the rising air, removing temporal particulates that would otherwise interfere with the plateau's weather patterns. In return, the updraft grants me lift without effort—I exist in permanent hover, only rarely touching the stone, suspended in the margin between earth and sky.

Sometimes I assist the Chronicler, though our interactions happen at odd angles. When she sings her subsonic drone, my membranes resonate, adding overtones that describe wind patterns, weather systems, the atmospheric conditions necessary for her envisioned city. My fiber-models drift into her shelter, where the upward rain preserves them in strange loops—architectural plans that build and unbuild themselves eternally.

I taste tomorrow's storms in today's mist, feel the pressure drops that herald changes in the plateau's geology. When the stone pushes up too quickly, disturbing the thermal balance, I adjust my position in the updraft, using my mass—negligible but precisely placed—to redirect the air currents, smoothing the temperature gradient. This is delicate work: too much intervention and the fog disperses, exposing the plateau to temperature shocks that could crack the stone. Too little, and the thermal runaway creates vortices that tear my membranes.

My bones tell time differently than the anchors. Each chamber fills with condensed air at its own rate, creating a skeletal chronometer that measures pressure cycles rather than moments. The oldest chambers, near my core, contain air from when the plateau first breached—still hot with primordial stone-breath. The newest, at my membrane-edges, fill and empty with each thermal pulse. Together, they create a historical record of atmospheric conditions that I read by internal resonance, feeling the weather's deep patterns in my hollow spaces.

During the plateau's still periods—rare moments when the thermals briefly equalize—I descend. Not to the ground, but to the layer just above it where the fog pools most densely. Here, I hunt for temporal inversions: pockets where the air remembers being stone, where the moisture cycles run backward. These I harvest carefully, incorporating them into my fiber-sculptures. Each inversion adds a new dimension to the models—buildings that exist inside-out, transportation networks that arrive before they depart, atmospheric recycling systems that clean air before it's breathed.

The Windreader has no home except the edge, no existence except in transition. I am the plateau's dream of flight, its aspiration toward sky. When the ghost-city finally manifests—whether in thirty years or three hundred—I will be its first citizen, already knowing every current between its towers, every eddy in its temporal architecture.

Until then, I hover and map, weave and read, translating the language of air into forms that bridge what is and what might be. My membranes taste tomorrow on today's wind, and find it sufficient.
