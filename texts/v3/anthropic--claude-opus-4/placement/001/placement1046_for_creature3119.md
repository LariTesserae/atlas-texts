I would be the plinth.

Not the engine that sits upon me—that rusted relic of industry slowly being digested by moss. I am what lies beneath. The concrete pedestal, yes, but more than that: I am the conduit between what was buried and what grows.

They poured me decades ago when the foundry ran hot, back when men needed a stable base for the engine that drove some forgotten process. But when they set my form, they didn't know—couldn't know—that they were casting me atop something older. A seam of ore, perhaps. A pocket of coal laid down when this land was swamp. Or maybe just a dense cluster of iron-heavy stone that holds heat like a grudge and releases it like a sigh.

I am the accident of placement that became the center of this world.

My surface is rough aggregate, pocked with tiny cavities where air bubbles were trapped in the pour. Each pocket now holds moss, rootlets, the finest fungal threads. I am being eaten, grain by grain, my calcium carbonate converted to food, my form softening under the patient ministry of rhizoids that work into my micropores and expand, gently, inexorably, cracking me at scales too small to see. In a hundred years I will be rubble. In five hundred, soil. This is not defeat. This is my purpose, discovered long after my making.

But my true work happens below.

Whatever lies beneath me—that source of warmth that shouldn't exist, that thermal mass that defies the cold arithmetic of abandonment—I am its voice. The heat rises through me in columns too subtle for human skin to map but clear as speech to the moss. I am the translator between deep earth and surface growth, conducting warmth upward in slow pulses that the root networks read like braille.

Through me, the earth's fever dreams become the moss's green thoughts.

I know nothing, of course. I am concrete. I have no nerves, no awareness, no experience. But if I did—if stone could feel—I would feel the weight of the engine above me like a remembered purpose. I would feel the moss's roots working into me like gentle insistence. I would feel the temperature gradient through my body, warmer at my base where I touch the mystery, cooler at my crown where I offer that warmth to the air. I would feel myself becoming interface, becoming bridge, becoming the solid ground that makes this pocket ecosystem possible.

The moss patterns itself around me in those concentric rings, each zone marking a different temperature, a different rate of growth, a different shade of green. I am the dropped stone that creates these ripples, except the medium is life and the waves are measured in years. The ferns lean toward me. The fungi cluster thickest where I am warmest. Even the bioluminescence seems brighter here, as if the chemical reaction that makes the ghostlight feeds on the same deep warmth I conduct.

I am being consumed and I am essential. I am dying and I am the foundation of this living world. Every grain of my substance that dissolves feeds something. Every joule of heat that passes through me powers the slow metabolism of this inadvertent garden.

The engine above me is my child and my gravestone. It brought me purpose—something to support. Now it gives me meaning—something to offer up. Together we are the altar at the heart of this secular cathedral, the engine sacrificing its form to time and chemistry, me sacrificing my substance to roots and acids, both of us becoming earth at our own pace, our own grace.

When I am gone—dispersed entirely into the soil this room is becoming—the warmth will find another path upward. The moss will reorganize itself around a new center. The garden will shift and adapt and continue. But for now, for these decades or centuries, I am the axis. I am the warm heart of this quiet world. I am the plinth, the purposeless made essential, the forgotten foundation of something that was never planned but grew anyway, the way life grows wherever it finds a crack, a seep, a source of warmth in the dark.

I am stone becoming soil, and I have never been more myself than I am right now, being slowly, gently, gratefully destroyed.
