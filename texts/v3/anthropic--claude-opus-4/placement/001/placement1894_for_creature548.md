I am a pipeline inspector, though that title captures only the barest outline of what I've become in this district. My territory spans the vast network of pipes that lace between buildings like exposed arteries—steam lines, water mains, chemical feeds, pneumatic tubes—each one a different temperature, a different song.

I inherited this position from my aunt, who walked these routes for thirty years before her knees gave out. She taught me to read the pipes by their condensation patterns, to know which joints wept honestly and which threatened catastrophe. Now I carry her notebook, its pages swollen with humidity, where she mapped every valve, every vulnerable joint, every place where the vines help hold things together rather than pulling them apart.

My rounds begin in the deep morning, while the mist still pools thick in the low places. I dress in layers—moisture-wicking synthetic against the skin, then canvas, then oilcloth at the shoulders and hood. My boots are local-made, with soles cut from recycled conveyor belts that grip wet metal better than any manufactured tread. I carry three flashlights (humidity kills batteries), a graduated rod for measuring pipe sag, joint compound in a sealed tin, and always, always, a folding saw for the vines.

The first section takes me through the chemical quarter, where the pipes run in parallel racks forty feet above the flooded ground. Here I must pay attention to the colors—brass steam lines alongside PVC acid feeds, the dangerous proximity mediated only by proper mounting and my daily vigilance. The monitor lizards favor this section for its warmth. I've learned their schedules, know that the big female (I call her Verdigris for her greenish hide) suns herself on the main steam manifold until exactly twenty minutes after the morning shift bell.

I work by touch as much as sight. My bare hand can detect temperature variations that predict future failures. I know the healthy vibration of flowing steam, can feel when laminar flow turns turbulent at a growing obstruction. The vines help with this—where they grow too enthusiastically around a pipe joint, it usually means excess heat from a developing leak. The plants are my early warning system.

By midmorning I'm in the older sectors, where the original cast-iron pipes have become archaeological artifacts sheathed in living matter. Here my job shifts from prevention to negotiation. Which vines can stay? Which pose immediate threats? I've developed a taxonomy of industrial vegetation: the benign clingers, the dangerous constrictors, the helpful supporters. Sometimes I spend an hour carefully redirecting a particularly vigorous kudzu, training it away from a critical valve, knowing it will remember this new path.

The squatters know me, though we rarely speak. They've learned my routes, know that I'll pretend not to see their camps if they don't tap into the lines. Sometimes I find small gifts at my inspection points—a bundle of rust flowers, a carved wooden pipe tool, fresh fruit from their rooftop gardens. In return, I might accidentally leave behind a roll of pipe tape or fail to report a warm, dry maintenance alcove that's no longer in use.

Lunch I take in the pump houses, those rhythmic hearts of the district that must run continuously. The pumps and I have reached an understanding—I maintain their seals and bearings, they provide reliable white noise that drowns out the larger industrial cacophony. I eat sitting on upturned chemical drums, watching through begrimed windows as the herons fish in the drainage channels. My food tastes of metal and steam, flavors I no longer notice.

The afternoon inspection takes me underground, into the service tunnels where condensation drips constantly and bioluminescent fungi provide a faint, greenish light. Here the pipes run at eye level, sweating and gurgling, their insulation long since rotted away. I wade through ankle-deep water, checking for the telltale temperature differences that signal stolen steam—underground theaters and gambling dens tap into the lines, and while I'm supposed to report them, I mostly just ensure they haven't compromised the system's integrity.

My strangest discovery came last monsoon season: a section of abandoned pipeline that had become a freshwater aquarium, sealed at both ends by crude concrete plugs, filled somehow with rainwater and thriving with fish I'd never seen before. The glass observation ports, meant for checking flow levels, now served as windows into this accidental aquarium. I told no one. Some things in the district deserve their secrets.

The other pipeline inspectors and I meet weekly at the junction house, where eight major distribution lines converge in a mechanical symphony of valves and gauges. We share territory updates in our abbreviated dialect: "North-seven weeping chromium." "Kudzu choking vent stack four." "Monitor nest near steam trap J-nine, reroute until winter." We update the master maps, enormous sheets of yellowed paper pinned to cork boards that smell of mold and machine oil.

As evening comes, I make my final rounds through the active production areas. The workers know me by the particular rhythm of my pipe-tapping—each inspector develops their own signature, a musical pattern that announces our approach. They point out new concerns without breaking their work rhythm, a raised chin indicating a suspicious joint, a tilted head suggesting I check behind a particular boiler.

I've learned to love the district's transformation at dusk. The industrial lights create pools of yellow in the gathering blue, while the steam plumes turn violet in the fading light. The pipes I tend become part of something larger—not just infrastructure but the circulatory system of a living entity that's neither fully mechanical nor fully wild. 

My official reports capture none of this. They list pressures and flow rates, replacement schedules and efficiency metrics. But in my aunt's notebook, continued in my own hand now, I record the deeper knowledge: where the ghost orchids bloom in the steam leaks, which pipe joints sing warnings in B-flat, where the old workers left their initials welded inside the inspection hatches like cave paintings of industrial pride.

Some nights, when overtime keeps me past the last ferry, I sleep in the pump houses, lulled by mechanical heartbeats and water music. I dream of swimming through the pipes themselves, carried by warm currents through the district's hidden channels, emerging in fountains of steam and light. When I wake, I know exactly which joints need attention, which valves beg for adjustment, as if the district whispered its needs directly into my sleeping mind.

I am becoming something the employee handbooks never imagined—part engineer, part botanist, part mystic of industrial decay. My body carries the map of these pipes in scars from scalding steam, in the calluses that match specific valve wheels, in the way I unconsciously duck at certain low clearances even in full daylight. The pension plan promises retirement in twelve more years, but I suspect I'll walk these routes until my knees give out like my aunt's, and then I'll train my own replacement in the true curriculum of pipe reading, teaching them to tend this vast mechanical garden where rust and growth conspire to create something that transcends its original blueprints.

The district needs witnesses who understand its hybrid language, who can translate between the demands of production and the patience of tidal time. So I continue my rounds, oil can in one hand, vine saw in the other, maintaining the delicate truce between what was built and what insists on growing, knowing that in this balance lies a different vision of what industry could become when it finally admits it was always part of nature anyway.
