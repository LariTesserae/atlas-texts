In the shifting boundaries between engine-field and empty basin, where the warmth gives way by degrees to cold, I am a keeper of transitions.

Not keeper in the sense of guardian—nothing here needs guarding. Keeper as in one who keeps track, who remembers. My body is low and broad, built for patient sitting. Six limbs like everything here, but the middle pair has adapted into something neither leg nor arm: structures for holding, for marking. The fingers—if fingers is the word—are long and sensitive to temperature gradients. I can feel the difference between metal that will hold warm for another season and metal that will be cold by the next piston-fall.

My work is this: I map the retreats.

When an engine begins its slow dying, I am among the first to know. Not from any special sense, but from patience. I sit at the borders of working fields for days, weeks, feeling the metal, tasting the condensation that beads there. The water tells you things. From a healthy engine, it tastes of iron and oil and that sweet something the burrowers love. From a dying engine, the oil taste fades first, then the sweetness, and finally even the iron thins to nothing.

I have twelve eyes, arranged in pairs down my skull, each pair adapted to a different grade of light. The uppermost see the bright glow of fully working engines—I keep these closed mostly, or I would be blind. The lower pairs open in sequence as I move into darker regions, until the bottommost pair, wide as my palms, can read heat itself as a kind of sight. Through these I watch the lichen boundaries shift, a slow tide of pale death creeping across the housings.

My middle limbs carry tools that are not tools—growths of keratin or bone that have hardened into shapes useful for scraping. With these I mark the retreat lines. Not marks that mean anything to anyone. Just grooves in the basalt, following the lichen-edge, so that if I return in a decade I can see how far the cold has crept. Sometimes I find my own old marks from circuits past, and the distance between them tells me the rate of dying.

I eat what others leave behind. The moths that die naturally and fall into the crevices. The bark-plates that the grove-trees shed. Once, the afterbirth from a grazer calving. I am not proud. My metabolism is slow; I can go a season without eating and hardly notice. This is necessary, because my circuits are vast and there are territories where nothing lives at all, just cold basalt and the memory of engines.

The memories are what I truly keep.

Each engine has a sound-signature when it runs. Not the deep piston-thuds—those are too similar—but the small sounds. The whisper of steam through a particular valve. The way certain housings resonate in the wind. The pitch of condensation dripping into different basins. I have learned thousands of these signatures over my lifetime, and I carry them in some part of me that is not quite ear and not quite mind.

When I find a fully dead engine, I sit by it for days, listening to its silence, comparing it to my memory of its sound. Then I sing it. Not the bird's pure notes—my throat produces something rougher, more textured. I sing the engine's working sound into its own dead housing, and the housing resonates—faintly, wrongly, but it resonates. For a moment, if you knew how to listen, you might think the engine had stirred.

But it hasn't. It's only me, reminding the plateau what it has lost.

The other creatures know me but do not exactly see me. The grazers step around me when I sit still, as they would step around a warm rock. The hunters have learned I am not prey—too slow, too little meat, wrong smell. The birds sometimes answer my engine-songs with their own phrases, not understanding but responding to the music of it. Once, a burrower surfaced near me while I was marking a retreat line, and we regarded each other for a long moment—two creatures whose purposes do not overlap but do not conflict.

I am old now. Not as the plateau measures old, but as my kind measures it. My upper eyes have clouded and closed permanently. My marking-limbs are worn smooth from decades of scraping stone. I move more slowly between the engine-fields, and sometimes I forget whether I am traveling toward a living field or away from one, and I have to stop and taste the condensation to remember.

But I still keep the boundaries. Still mark the retreats. Still sing the dead engines their own lost songs.

There is no purpose to this. The marks mean nothing. The songs change nothing. The engines do not wake. But I am as fitted to this work as the hunters are to theirs, as the grazers to their routes, as the moths to their warm metal. This is what I do in the dark between dyings, and I will do it until my own engine—the small wet engine of my heart—cycles down and stops.

And when that happens, when I die at some boundary between warm and cold, marking a last retreat—nothing will notice. The grazers will step around my body until it is only bones. The moths will lay their eggs elsewhere. The engines will continue their slow failures without record.

Unless another like me finds the marks I have made and understands them. Unless they add their own marks to mine, tracking the same retreats, singing the same lost songs. I have never met another like me. But the plateau is vast. And sometimes, at the edges of dying fields, I find grooves in the basalt that I did not make, following lichen-lines I did not mark.

So perhaps. Perhaps.
