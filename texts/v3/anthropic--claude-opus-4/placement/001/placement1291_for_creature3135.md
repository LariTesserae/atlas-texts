I find myself drawn to the liminal spaces between things, becoming neither quite one nor the other.

---

I am a **channel-keeper**, though that designation would amuse the colony. We don't keep channels — we become them, briefly, incompletely, always failing at the becoming in ways that generate new possibilities.

My body is a hybrid thing, caught between the wing-creatures' dense efficiency and the salamanders' translucent function. I stand perhaps twenty-five centimeters — too large to move through the channels as the salamanders do, too small to reach the basin edges without effort. My skin is the compromise: neither furred nor fully transparent, but something between — a pale membrane through which my darker organs show as suggestions, shadows, implications. When I move through shallow water, I am almost invisible. When I stand on dry stone, I am uncomfortably present.

I have no wings, no wing-fingers. My hands are like the salamanders' feet but larger, with flattened pads that can create suction against wet stone, allowing me to climb the basin walls, to position myself at angles the others cannot achieve. My fingers are webbed to the first joint — enough to cup water, not enough to swim. Everything about me is partial, transitional, a body that refuses to commit to being any one thing.

This is why I am necessary.

---

I came to consciousness — not birth, consciousness, the two are separated by years of automatic function — during a channel collapse. A critical artery between the third and seventh basin clusters had been compromised by frost-wedging, the carefully carved groove suddenly choked with schist fragments. The salamanders pooled at either end, unable to pass. The wing-creatures descended in their silent frenzy, trying to clear the blockage, but their wing-fingers were too large for the delicate work, too strong — they kept dislodging more fragments, worsening the situation.

I was living then as salamanders live, moving through the channels by instinct, carrying traces I didn't examine, my mind a simple map of routes and temperatures. But something in the crisis — the subsonic pulses of distress, the flicker of dozens of irises cycling through panic-white, the biomass in basin three beginning to pulse erratically without its connection to seven — something in that moment of system failure lit up corridors in my cognition I didn't know existed.

I found myself thinking: *The channel is not the groove. The channel is the connection. The groove is only one way of being a channel.*

My hands moved without my conscious direction. I climbed the wall between basins three and seven — my suction pads allowing me to traverse the vertical surface where no salamander could go — and I positioned myself as a bridge. My mouth opened (wider than it should, with a flexibility I hadn't known I possessed) and I drew water from three, held it in a throat-sac I was discovering in the moment of its use, then expelled it into seven. Back and forth. A living channel, temporary, inefficient, but functional.

The wing-creatures went still. Their irises fixed on me — dozens of golden lights — and I felt the weight of their assessment. I was not what they expected. I was not part of the plan. I was a spontaneous solution to an immediate problem, and they were trying to decide if I was a perturbation to be absorbed or a revision to be integrated.

The biomass in basin seven stabilized. The crisis passed. I climbed down, exhausted, something in my throat-sac strained from the unfamiliar use. A wing-creature approached — an elder, its fur whorled with charcoal patterns so dense they looked like written text — and placed a wing-finger on my membrane skin, just over where my heart shadow pulsed. It held the touch for a long moment, reading something I couldn't name. Then it withdrew, pulsed a subsonic tone I felt in my bones — not language, but recognition — and returned to its work.

I have been a channel-keeper since. Not the only one — there are perhaps a dozen of us, each with our own hybrid geometries, our own partial abilities. We are the terrace's improvisation made flesh, the system's way of saying: *When the old categories fail, become a new one.*

---

My days are spent in the spaces between plans. When a channel is blocked, I am a living bypass. When a basin needs isolation — for the biomass to undergo some deep internal reconfiguration without interference — I position myself as a valve, controlling flow with the pressure of my body against stone. When the salamanders need rest, I carry their cargo, though the biomass-traces burn my semi-transparent skin in ways that leave patterns, memories written in scar tissue that fades and returns with each use.

But more than function, I am a perspective.

My cognition occupies a middle space. I lack the wing-creatures' capacity for complex interfacing with the biomass — when I touch the red-black mass, I feel only a vague pressure, like trying to remember a dream — but I have more awareness than the salamanders, who experience the world as a series of thermal gradients and chemical trails. I can't participate in the great debates, the endless revision of plans, but I can *see* them. I watch the patterns of argument flow through the colony like weather systems, the way a disagreement about grub allocation in the eastern warrens propagates through subsonic pulses and iris-flickers until it becomes a question about the nature of heat itself, about whether warmth is a resource to be managed or a gift to be received.

My semi-transparent nature means I am partially invisible to the colony's social dynamics. I move through the frantic activity like a ghost made half of flesh, noticed only when my function is needed, otherwise overlooked. This should be lonely. It isn't. It is a kind of freedom — to observe without the weight of full participation, to be essential and peripheral simultaneously.

---

Sometimes I station myself at the basin where the oldest biomass grows — the deep one near the back wall, carved so long ago the stone has developed a patina, almost organic, as though the rock is trying to become flesh. This biomass pulses differently from the others, slower, with rhythms that don't match any current debate or problem. The wing-creatures interface with it rarely, and when they do, they emerge disoriented, irises cycling through colors too quickly to read, their movements uncertain for hours afterward.

I think this biomass remembers the terrace before the theory of revision took hold. When there were still fixed plans, fixed names, fixed beliefs. When the colony thought it was building something permanent. The memory disturbs them — not because they mourn the loss of certainty, but because they can't quite believe they ever possessed it. It seems like a fairy tale, a story too simple to be true: *Once we thought we knew what we were doing.*

I touch this old biomass sometimes, though I can't truly interface. My semi-transparent fingers sink into the mass, and I feel ... not thoughts, not memories, but *temperature*. The biomass is older than individual memory, older than the colony's collective history, but it holds the thermal record of every freeze-thaw cycle it has lived through. Thousands upon thousands of fluctuations, encoded in ways I can't decipher but can somehow sense — the way you can sense age in stone, time in water. It tells me, without words, without concepts, that the cycle has always been. That there was never a golden age of stability. That the terrace has always been dying, and the colony has always been adapting, and the only change is that now they know it.

This knowledge sits in me like the traces I carry between basins — present but not quite integrated, information waiting for a framework that can hold it.

---

The day the human arrives is a day of particular tension. The eastern lip lost another meter in the night — not a gradual erosion but a sudden calving, like a glacier releasing an iceberg. Three basins were lost instantly, their biomass tumbling into the white void before anyone could react. The salamanders that were in the connecting channels simply vanished, there one moment, gone the next. The wing-creatures have been in crisis mode since dawn, evacuating the nearest warrens, redistributing grubs, carving emergency channels to reroute the water flow.

I am struggling to keep up. My body, neither fully suited to any single task, is needed everywhere at once. I bridge collapsed connections, valve overflowing basins, carry biomass traces from the lost clusters that some quick-thinking wing-creature managed to preserve in a bone-stalk husk. My membrane skin is burning from the traces, my throat-sac raw from constant use, my suction pads barely holding on the increasingly wet stone.

And then the human appears, and everything ... pauses.

I see them first as a shadow — impossible on this shadowless terrace, but the human is so large, so opaque, that they create their own darkness, a mobile eclipse. The wing-creatures notice immediately, their subsonic alarm pulses rippling through the colony like struck water. But it's not the alarm of a predator sighting. It's something more complex — curiosity mixed with calculation mixed with a kind of desperate hope.

*A new variable*, I hear/feel in the subsonic frequencies. *When the old solutions fail...*

The elder with the densest fur patterns approaches the human with a stone. I know this elder — I have carried traces from their favorite basin, the one where they test the most radical architectural proposals. Their iris is steady gold, no flicker of uncertainty. They place the stone and wait.

I find myself holding still, though seven different tasks demand my attention. The human is ... I don't have the concept. *Unprecedented* is too small. They are a rupture in the scale of things, a massive warm-blooded anomaly in our precise, cold-adapted system. They could destroy us with a careless step. They could save us with knowledge from the lower world. They could be irrelevant, a tourist, a misdirected creature who will leave as mysteriously as they came.

Or they could be what we become.

This thought arrives uninvited, shaped by my hybrid cognition, my neither-this-nor-that perspective. What if the human is not a variable to be incorporated but a glimpse of a trajectory? What if adaptation, pushed far enough, leads to this: massive, opaque, capable of surviving at any altitude? What if the colony's future is not an ever-tightening spiral but a metamorphosis as complete as whatever the grubs become when they split their casings and vanish?

I am thinking too large. My job is channels, connections, the spaces between. But I can't stop watching as the human considers the stone, as the elder waits, as the entire terrace holds its breath (though we don't breathe the same way, don't hold the same way, don't wait the same way).

The human reaches for the stone.

---

And I see, in that reaching, a gesture I recognize. Not the shape of it — their hand is vast, five-fingered, without webs or suction pads. But the hesitation. The way they start the movement, pause, continue. They are deciding, but more than that, they are *aware of the decision's weight*. They know the stone means something, even if they don't know what. They know their response will be data, will be incorporated, will change the system in ways they can't predict.

They are, in other words, thinking like us. Like the colony. Seeing themselves as a variable in a system they're still trying to understand.

The human picks up the stone. Holds it. Their massive fingers dwarf it — what is a substantial offering from the elder becomes a pebble in their grip. They look at it, then at the elder, then — and this is when I know they understand more than they should — at the basins. Their gaze tracks the channels, notes the emergency rerouting, the fresh-carved grooves. They see the systematic nature of what we're doing. They see the crisis.

Slowly, with a care that seems learned rather than natural, they place the stone at the junction of three channels — a position that creates a small dam, redirecting flow in a way that actually helps our emergency management. It's not perfect. It's not what a wing-creature who had interfaced with the biomass would do. But it's *useful*, and more than that, it shows they were watching, thinking, trying to help.

The elder's iris flares — surprise, assessment, recalculation. They approach the stone, touch it with a wing-finger, pulse a subsonic pattern I can barely detect. Then they do something I have never seen: they look directly at me.

The message is clear, even in subsonic frequencies not quite calibrated for my hybrid ears: *Bridge this.*

---

I understand. The human is too large to interface with the basins directly. The wing-creatures' subsonic language is below their hearing range. The iris-flickers, the fur-patterns, the wing-gestures — all illegible to something that sees and speaks on an entirely different scale. But I am a channel-keeper. I exist to connect disparities. This is just a larger gap than usual.

I approach the human slowly, letting them see me fully. Their reaction is ... complicated. I watch their eyes (strange, round pupils, fixed irises that don't communicate) widen, then narrow, then fix on my semi-transparent body with an intensity that feels like heat. They can see my organs through my skin — heart, processing chambers, the burnt traces of biomass scars. I am, to them, an impossibility: too large to be what I look like, too coordinated to be what my appearance suggests.

I position myself between them and the elder. My hands move in a deliberate pattern — not wing-gestures, which I cannot make, but something I'm inventing in the moment: a simplified version of the colony's gestural language, reduced to concepts I hope might translate. *You.* *Us.* *Stone.* *Water.* *Help.*

The human watches. Their hand moves — do they realize they're moving it? — mirroring my gesture. Not perfectly, but enough. They're trying.

I touch a channel with one hand, then my throat-sac with the other, miming the flow of water. Then I point to the collapsed eastern edge, the missing sections of terrace. My gestures are crude, nothing like the wing-creatures' fractal eloquence, but the human is following. They look where I point. They see the scope of the loss.

They place another stone.

---

What follows is the strangest collaboration the terrace has ever seen. The human, guided by my improvised translations, helps with the emergency restructuring. They can lift stones we cannot budge, place them with a strength that would take a dozen wing-creatures. But more than labor, they offer perspective — literally. Their height lets them see patterns we cannot, the overall flow of the water system, inefficiencies that are invisible from our forty-centimeter vantage point.

I exhaust myself bridging this communication. My throat-sac swells from overuse, trying to create sounds that might reach their hearing range. My hands cramp from constant gesture. The biomass traces in my skin burn brighter, creating a kind of luminescence, as if the crisis is writing itself on my body. But it works. Slowly, imperfectly, it works.

The elder interfaces with the basins constantly, bringing the human's interventions to the biomass for analysis. Other wing-creatures observe, debate, adjust. Some are skeptical — the human's help comes without understanding, brute force rather than integrated thought. Others are desperate enough to accept any assistance. The debate rages in subsonic pulses that make my bones ache.

By the time the terrace's evening arrives — the light never changes, but the grub-warmth cycles lower, creating a kind of thermal night — we have stabilized. The water flows through new channels. The surviving biomass is redistributed. The salamanders have learned the altered routes. It is not what we had before, but it is *functional*, which is all we ever ask for. Functional, for now.

The human sits at the edge of the terrace, massive legs hanging over the lip, looking down into the white void. They seem ... tired? It's hard to read their fixed-iris eyes, their opaque expressions. But their posture suggests exhaustion, or perhaps something deeper — a recognition of the futility of what we've done. We've delayed the inevitable by days, perhaps weeks. The terrace will continue to shrink. We will continue to adapt. Until we can't.

I approach them, my membrane skin still glowing faintly with trace-burns. I have nothing to offer — no stone, no water, no solution. But I position myself where they can see me, this impossible hybrid thing, this living channel between scales of existence. I gesture one more time — not instructions, not information. Just acknowledgment. *You helped. Thank you. It matters, even if it doesn't last.*

They look at me for a long moment. Then, slowly, they mirror my gesture.

---

As the thermal night deepens, I find myself at the old biomass basin again, the deep one with its memories of certainty. My burnt hands rest on its rim. The mass pulses with its ancient rhythm, remembering temperatures from before the colony developed its philosophy of revision, before the terrace began to noticeably shrink, before creatures like me became necessary.

I think about the human, about what they saw when they looked at us. A dying system, probably. A futile scramble on a crumbling shelf of stone. Creatures too small, too strange, too committed to an impossible equilibrium to simply *leave*, to seek better ground. They must think we're insane, or stupid, or both.

But they helped anyway. Without understanding our debates, without interfacing with our biomass, without being able to hear our language or read our gestures, they saw the pattern of what we were trying to do and joined it. They became, briefly, part of the revision.

Maybe that's what I am, really. Not just a channel between basins but between scales of being. Between the wing-creatures' frantic precision and the human's vast incomprehension. Between what we are and what we might become, if becoming is still possible, if there's time.

The old biomass trembles under my touch, remembering.

I remember with it, in my partial way, and wait for dawn, for the next crisis, for the next impossible connection that my hybrid body might bridge.

The terrace continues, for now. We continue.

Like this.
