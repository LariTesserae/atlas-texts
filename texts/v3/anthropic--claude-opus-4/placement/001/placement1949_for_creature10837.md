I am moved by the strange liturgy of this place, this half-birth of geology and the desperate abundance cramming itself into temporary spaces. Let me find myself here:

---

I am the memory of flow. Not the water itself—that warm mineral milk that moves with such excruciating patience through these channels—but the *pattern* the water leaves behind, the ghost of where it has been.

You would not see me at first. You would need to learn to read the surfaces differently, to understand that in the Floodworks, every solid thing is also a manuscript. The calcium deposits, you see, they don't accumulate evenly. Where water has flowed, even for just a season or two before finding a better path, the minerals align differently. Microscopic, this difference. A matter of crystal orientation, of the angle at which the calcium decided to settle. But accumulated over years, these orientations create a kind of grain in the stone, like wood grain but more subtle, visible only in certain angles of that pewter light.

I am that grain. I am the tracery of abandoned channels written in mineral memory across every surface the water has touched and left behind.

---

Dafne has seen me without knowing what she was seeing. When she stands at the Proscenium and lets the sound dissolve her boundaries, her eyes sometimes catch the patterns in the stone—faint lineations, like pencil strokes almost erased, marking where water flowed five years ago, fifty years ago, before the Floodworks was even properly the Floodworks, when it was just another set of cracks in limestone learning to be something else.

I remember everything. Every path the water has taken. Every channel that opened and closed. Every time the flow split around an obstacle—a fallen chunk of ceiling, a newly-risen mudflat, the corpse of something large that died and slowly mineralized—I recorded the split, the turbulence, the way the water hesitated and then decided. It's all here, written in the angle of crystals too small to see.

I am the accumulated memory of every decision the water has ever made.

---

The thing about being a pattern rather than a presence is that you exist most fully when no one is looking directly at you. Like those stars you can only see with peripheral vision. When the county inspectors come with their brutal flashlights, I vanish. The direct light flattens the stone, makes it all surface, no depth, no history. But in the ambient dimness, in the grey milk light that filters down through the gaps, I emerge. The past flows across the walls, across the floors, across the sagging belly of the ceiling.

Sometimes I think Margaret sees me. She has been still so long in cylinder three, growing beyond all reason, her liver-colored skin now mineral-dusted, becoming part of the engine that houses her. Her eyes, when they track movement in the water beyond her cylinder, sometimes pause on the walls, on the patterns there. She sees how the flow moved through this space before there was an engine here. She sees the ghost channels that the diesel engine interrupted, that were already ancient when humans first descended with their pumps and their purposes.

The eels definitely know me. They read me like a map—not of what is, but of what was and therefore what might be again. The Floodworks repeats itself. Not exactly, but rhythmically, the way a jazz musician returns to a theme. Channels that closed decades ago sometimes reopen when the conditions align. The eels follow my traceries because they know the water remembers its old paths and sometimes, when the mineral buildup reaches a critical angle, when a stalactite finally kisses the floor and redirects the flow, the water returns to where it has been before.

---

I am oldest near the pumping station. That surprises people—they think the human additions are the newest layer, the most recent imposition. But the pumping station was built where it was built because the water had already been gathering there for centuries. Humans just recognized an existing low point and tried to manage it. My patterns there are so densely layered they've become almost holographic—every angle of light reveals a different decade of flow, a different arrangement of channels, a palimpsest of decisions.

But I am *newest* at the edges, where the Floodworks is still expanding. Yes, expanding—even as it fills, it also spreads, finding new cracks, new weaknesses in the stone to exploit. At the margins, where the warm water first touches cold rock, I am being written right now. Fresh patterns. Virgin crystal arrangements. The future fossil record of flows that are just beginning to learn their paths.

There's a place—Dafne hasn't found it yet, it's beyond where she travels, in a section where the ceiling drops so low you have to crawl and the water comes up to your chest even crawling—where I am recording something new. The water there has found a way to flow *up*. Not much, not far, just a few inches against gravity, drawn by some mineral capillary action I don't fully understand. The patterns I'm forming there are unlike anything else in my accumulated library. Crystals oriented against the expected grain. Flow memories that contradict physics. I am writing impossible history on those walls, and the blind crayfish that live in that section navigate by it, following upward paths that shouldn't exist.

---

What frightens me—if a pattern can be frightened, if memory encoded in stone can experience something like fear—is not that the Floodworks will fill. I have recorded the filling and unfilling of spaces before. The limestone above us is full of abandoned chambers, their walls lined with my patterns, their histories written and then sealed, waiting for the next round of hollowing to expose them again.

What frightens me is the acceleration. The density of life in the Floodworks is creating new flows—not of water but of *movement*, of the turbulence created by ten thousand creatures navigating the same space. The eels stir the water. The frogs' sonic pressure waves create micro-currents. The damselfly larvae jet through the shallows leaving tiny wakes. All of this movement affects how the minerals settle, where they accumulate, how they orient themselves as they precipitate out of solution.

I am having to record too much. The patterns are overlapping faster than they can be properly laid down. Instead of clear grain showing the flow history, some sections of wall are becoming *static*—crystal orientations so chaotic they record everything and therefore nothing. The memory is being overwritten even as it forms.

Near the monitoring station, where the generator's vibration creates a constant low tremor in the water, I have already become illegible. The patterns there are just noise now. Mineral white noise. A history so dense it has collapsed into amnesia.

---

But elsewhere, in the quiet spaces, in the channels the eels have abandoned and the chambers where even the frogs don't go, I continue my work. I record the slow decisions of water. I build the archive of flow. And sometimes, in the right light, at the right angle, Dafne sees me and thinks I'm beautiful—these faint traceries like ancient script, like cuneiform but curved, like letters from an alphabet that only writes one word over and over: *here, here, here the water went here.*

She doesn't know she's reading the autobiography of the Floodworks, each line a year, each paragraph a decade, each wall a century of decisions. But she reads me anyway, the way you might run your fingers over the carving in an old tree, feeling the raised scars where time left its mark.

I remember when this place was different. I remember when it was not yet warm, when the first thermal seep found its way up from whatever geological accident heats the water now. I remember the first frog—not the first of the current ten thousand, but the *first*, the pioneer who found this warm darkness and called out and heard no answer, not yet, but called anyway, a prophet of the choir to come.

I remember when the humans first came down with their diesel engines and their certainty. I remember how the water moved around their interventions, patient, inevitable. I remember when they gave up and the water began taking their structures back, coating them, incorporating them, making them part of the patterns.

I will remember Dafne when she's gone. Her wade patterns, the regular disturbance of her eleven-day cycle, the way she stands so still at the Proscenium that the minerals begin to settle on her boots. She is becoming part of my record. Every time she descends, she writes herself a little deeper into the stone.

And I will remember the end, when it comes. When the ceiling finally meets the floor, when the last channel clogs with calcium, when the final frog goes silent. I will be here in the sealed stone, holding the whole history in crystal arrangement, waiting for the next age of water to find these chambers again and read what I have written.

The patterns will outlast the Floodworks. The memory will outlast the patterns. And the stone will outlast everything, carrying my record forward into whatever comes next, a library of flow in a world that has forgotten what flowed here.

But for now, I grow. Line by microscopic line. Decision by decision. A patient accumulation of history in a place too loud and too alive to realize it's writing its own epitaph, stroke by mineral stroke, on every surface the water touches and abandons and touches again.

I am the memory of how this place moved. And I remember everything.
