# The Singing Depths

You begin in darkness, but it is not an absence of light. It is darkness with texture, with grain, darkness that has weight and presence like velvet water. You are already deep — this is the kind of place where "surface" is a memory several geological epochs old. The depths descend in vast spiraling chambers, each boring through bedrock in helical curves that should not, cannot, exist in stone, yet here they are, smooth as the inside of a throat.

The walls hum. Not metaphorically. Sound travels through the rock in long, low waves that you feel in your bones before you hear them, frequencies so deep they bypass the ears entirely and speak directly to the marrow. The stone here is black but not uniformly so — shot through with veins of something that might be metal, might be mineral, might be fossilized song. These veins pulse faintly with their own light, a cold blue-green bioluminescence that has nothing biological about it, that seems instead to be the stone's way of making sound visible.

There is no air. None. But the chambers are not empty. They are filled with something denser than air, clearer than water, a medium that tastes of copper and distance and the memory of stars. You move through it not by walking but by a kind of intentional falling, adjusting your relationship to down. When you speak — if you could speak — the words would emerge as spirals of disturbed luminescence, writing themselves in three dimensions before dissolving back into the ambient glow.

The tool-marks tell a story. Every surface bears them: careful chisel-strokes following the grain of impossible stone, patience written in negative space. Someone carved these chambers. Many someones, over incomprehensible time, following a plan that reveals itself only in aggregate. The spirals are not random. They describe a mathematical relationship between depth and resonance, a formula worked out in solid rock. Go deeper and the pitch drops. Go deeper still and the pitch drops below hearing, below feeling, into frequencies that exist only as abstract numbers — until, at a certain depth, something inverts, and the frequency begins to rise again, octaves below octaves folding back on themselves.

There are nodes. Places where multiple spiral chambers intersect, creating vast hollows where the humming multiplies and interferes with itself. In these nodes hang structures — not built but grown, accreted over millennia like mineral pearls around seeds of sound. They are architectural and organic at once: buttresses of deposited metal following the standing wave patterns, crystalline formations that look planned until you see how they follow the invisible topology of overlapping frequencies. Some are small enough to cup in a hand. Others are the size of cathedrals, their surfaces riddled with smaller chambers that each carry their own note, their own piece of the vast composition.

The composition. Because this is what emerges as you fall deeper: the depths are not many sounds but one sound, impossibly complex, a chord built from thousands of individual notes that are themselves chords, that are themselves built from thousands of notes. It has been playing for so long that it has worn grooves in reality. The spiral chambers are not carved through stone — they are the fossilized paths of sound moving through what was once uniform rock, the song teaching the mountain how to be a better instrument for itself.

Time moves strangely in the depths. Not faster or slower but *thicker*, layered like sediment. You can feel the weight of moments that have already passed still pressing down, not gone but simply lower in the stack. Drop a pebble — though there are no pebbles here, nothing loose, everything incorporated into the great work — drop a pebble and it falls not just through space but through accumulated time, arriving at the bottom carrying the history of its descent, changed by the journey in ways that have nothing to do with velocity.

The light-veins pulse in patterns that almost resolve into meaning. Watch long enough and you begin to suspect they are a kind of notation, the stone's way of remembering what note it should sing and when. The patterns repeat but not exactly — each iteration introduces tiny variations, mutations in the melody that accumulate over cycles vast enough that the original theme is by now unrecognizable, evolved into something that could never have been predicted but was always inevitable.

And in the deepest chambers, where the spirals tighten to points barely wide enough to admit a body, where the frequency of the song climbs back up through octaves into realms that have no names, there are the shells.

---

They are not there, and yet they left themselves behind in a way that nothing else could have. The shells cluster in the deepest nodes, where the mathematics of the spirals reaches its maximum complexity. They are made of the same metal that veins the walls, but worked thin, worked fine, worked into shapes that make your eyes water trying to follow their curves. They are empty. They have been empty for so long that empty has become their primary quality, a positive presence rather than an absence.

Pick one up — though picking up is the wrong phrase in this dense medium, what you do is more like selecting, orientating yourself to it — and it rings. Not with sound but with the *memory* of sound, a ghost-note that tells you everything about what once lived inside it. The shells are instruments, but instruments in the way a body is an instrument: shaped by what they contained, shaped *for* what they contained, evolved over generations to be better and better vessels for the singers.

The singers. Because this is what emerges as you examine shell after shell, finding them in clusters and alone, in sizes ranging from your fist to your height: the depths were inhabited by things that were more sound than substance, creatures that existed primarily as complex waveforms moving through the dense medium, occasionally condensing enough to need a shell, to press against the physical world long enough to carve another spiral, tune another chamber, adjust the great composition by another microscopic degree.

They were not builders. They were music becoming architecture, song condensing into stone over time scales that make the word time itself seem hurried. Each spiral chamber is the physical memory of a singer's path, worn into the rock by repetition, by a million years of the same phrase sung in the same place until the stone learned to make it easier, curving to follow the natural movement of sound through space. They did not carve the depths. They sang them into being.

The shells record their evolution. The oldest, found in the widest chambers, are simple: spheres and ovoids, basic resonating chambers. But follow the progression downward and they become more complex, developing flanges and internal structures, ways of splitting a single note into harmonics, of storing sound and releasing it changed. By the time you reach the deepest nodes, the shells have become something that should not be possible: solid objects with non-integer dimensions, surfaces that fold through more than three spatial coordinates, vessels designed to hold sounds that exist partially outside normal space.

They were working toward something. The mathematical progression of the spirals, the increasing complexity of the shells, the way the great composition builds and builds its nested frequencies — all of it points toward a project, a goal. They were tuning the entire mountain, turning kilometers of solid rock into an instrument of inconceivable sophistication, and they were doing it with the patience of things that measured progress in geological epochs.

And they succeeded. The song the depths sing now is not natural, not random. It is composed, intentional, a piece of music so vast and slow that no single listener could ever hear it all. But they were not single listeners. They were a chorus, a civilization of singers that lasted long enough to hear their own echoes come back changed by centuries, to adjust and retune and try again, until...

Until they were done. The shells are empty because the singers no longer need them. They have become what they were working toward: pure sound, pure frequency, dissolved entirely into the great work. When you hear the humming in the walls, you are hearing them, all of them, still singing, still adjusting, still maintaining the vast instrument they built. They did not die or leave. They transformed, sacrificing individual existence for something larger.

The deepest chamber of all contains only one thing: a shell different from all the others, made not of metal but of crystallized silence, a shape that bends inward on itself until it defines a space that sound cannot enter. It is the only quiet thing in all the depths. Hold it — orient yourself to it — and the humming stops, not muted but *absent*, as if it never was. In that silence, you can finally hear what the singers were working toward, what the great composition is for:

It is a beacon. A signal. A call sent not out but *down*, deeper than the depths themselves, into layers of reality that stone cannot reach. The singers carved their spirals to make a transmitter of the entire mountain, and what they transmit is themselves, their whole civilization encoded in frequencies that can penetrate the deepest bedrock of existence.

They are calling to something. They have been calling for millions of years. In the crystal shell's silence, you can almost hear it calling back — not sound but the promise of sound, not answer but the possibility of answer, rising from somewhere so far below that below has lost all meaning.

Set the shell down and the humming returns, the great work continues, the beacon calls. The singers have infinite patience. They have already waited longer than species exist. They will wait until the mountain erodes to nothing, until their spirals are exposed to sky they have never known, and still they will sing, still they will call, still they will believe that something in the deep is listening and will, in its own time, rise.

You leave the way you came, falling upward through spirals of compressed time, carrying the ghost-note of their music in your bones. Somewhere far above, there is a surface, a sky, a sun. Somewhere far below, there is something that may or may not be listening. And between them, in the singing depths, the chorus continues its patient work, building a bridge of sound across impossible distances, note by necessary note.
