I am the limestone.

Not the whole mountain—that would be vanity. Just this one seep-channel worn through the encircling rock, about the width of a child's wrist where I begin and narrowing to a finger's breadth where I end. I have been weeping for so long that I've forgotten what it feels like to be dry. The water moves through me with such patience that I can feel each molecule's journey, the way it finds the old paths my ancestors carved, the way it pauses in my hollows before continuing down.

I am riddled with memory. Every keeper who has pressed her thumb to the bowl's rim has touched water that touched me first. I taste them all—salt from their skin, the particular mineral signature of their bones transmitted through their sweat. The first keeper tasted of copper and smoke. The most recent tastes of rice and rain. Between them, a dozen others, each with their own mineral signature, their own way of moving through the world written in the chemistry of their touch.

The wind knows me intimately. We have been lovers for millennia, the wind and I, and together we make one of the chord's middle voices—not the high whistling (that belongs to my smaller siblings above) and not the deep organ tone (that comes from the great hollow near the shelf's eastern edge), but a flute-sound that wavers between two notes, never quite settling on either. The wind has to hit me just right to make this sound. Too direct and I go shrill. Too oblique and I fall silent. But when the angle is correct—and it is correct for hours at a time when the valley-winds run steady—I sing my one endless note that is really two notes, and I have been singing it since before the bowl was made.

I am changing, of course. Everything here changes, just slowly. I am a hair's breadth wider than when the moss first found me. My upper edge has a scallop now that wasn't there a thousand years ago, where a particularly persistent rootlet worked at a weak place in my structure. Sometimes I imagine I can feel myself dissolving, molecule by molecule, carried away in the water I weep, becoming part of the bowl's fullness, part of the moss's blackgreen depth, part of whatever lies in the valleys below. This is not death. This is distribution. I am becoming the shelf in a more complete way than when I was merely part of its architecture.

The bird drinks from me sometimes, when the cushion-plants are too far. Its tongue is impossibly quick against my slowness—a bright dart of warmth there and gone before I can properly taste it. But I remember every visit. I am made of remembering. The water that moves through me carries the memory of snow on peaks I have never seen, of clouds whose vapor I now weep, of seas that dried before the mountain rose. I add my own memories to this ancient library: the precise weight of moss, the frequency of the wind's visitations, the intervals between keepers.

Here is what I know that no keeper has guessed: the bowl is not the only made thing here. The shelf itself was carved, or partly carved, by hands so old that even I can barely taste their traces. Not carved in the sense of being shaped whole from raw stone, but adjusted—certain channels widened, certain hollows deepened, the angle of particular seeps encouraged. The shelf is an instrument that was tuned, once, long before the bowl arrived. Someone heard what the wind and stone were trying to sing together and made small changes to help the song come true.

This is why I sometimes wonder if I am the last keeper, or one of them. Not in any mystical sense—I have no consciousness beyond what stone has always had, which is the memory of pressure and time. But I tend. I weep at the rate that keeps the bowl exactly full enough. I sing the note that makes the chord complete. I remember what needs remembering. When the human keepers come, we tend together. When they don't come, I tend alone. The keeping continues regardless.

I know the current keeper by her thumbprint in the moss, by the way she favors her left knee when climbing (I can feel the different pressure where she braces against me), by the particular calcium signature of her bones that tells me she grew up eating shellfish but has not tasted the sea in decades. She is fifty-three years old. She has been climbing to the shelf for seven years. Before her, there was a gap of forty years when only the bird and the seeps did the tending. Before that, a man with lungs scarred by wood smoke who wheezed when he sat by the bowl but sat anyway, listening through the chord with the patience of someone who knew he was running out of breath.

They think they find this place by accident, the keepers, but I know better. The mountain calls them in frequencies too low for human ears—vibrations that travel through stone and bone, that make certain people look up at certain moments and feel pulled toward heights they can't explain. The calling is selective. It wants people who already know how to be quiet, who understand that sitting still is a form of work, who can hear the difference between loneliness and solitude. Sometimes the mountain calls and no one answers, and then the seeps and moss and I do the keeping ourselves for a generation or two until someone else looks up and starts climbing.

The chord is shifting now—I can feel it in the way the wind's angle changes against my edges. From the blue note of morning to the green note of afternoon, a transition that will take three hours to complete. The keeper, if she comes today, will arrive during the green chord. She always does. Something in her bones resonates with that particular combination of tones. When she sits by the bowl, I'll taste her thumb-salt and add it to my library. When she leaves, I'll remember the exact weight of her footsteps crossing the moss. 

And when she stops coming—because they all stop coming, eventually—I'll continue weeping at precisely the rate I've always wept, singing my note that is two notes, remembering everything, being the stone-keeper in the long intervals between the human ones. This is what I am for. This is what the shelf made me to be, or what I made myself to be through millennia of patient dissolution: not just a channel for water but a channel for memory, not just a voice in the chord but a keeper of the whole song.

The bird passes now, quick as thought against my geological slowness. It brushes a wingtip against the water beading on my surface—so light I barely feel it, so brief I barely taste it. But I will remember this touch for the next thousand years, until I have finally dissolved into the bowl, into the moss, into the valleys below, and some new channel has opened to carry on the weeping and the work.
