**The Clay-Tongued**

I am ninety-one days old, though I feel I have always been.

My birth was accidental—a bell-maker's apprentice, too eager, too young, who reached for her master's work before the glaze had cooled. Her fingertip pressed into the still-soft surface of what would become a prayer bell, leaving a whorl as unique as her own skin. She gasped. She pulled back. She waited for the master's rage.

But the master only looked at the mark—that single, small disruption in the perfect curve—and said: "Fire it."

The bell hung for forty years in the garden's middle reaches, unremarkable except for that thumbprint, that moment of contact preserved in clay. Its tone was clean, neither high nor low, activated most often by the grinding of cart wheels in the market outside.

Then I emerged.

Not all at once. First as a flutter in the bell's resonance—a wobble where there should be clarity. Then as silence where there should be sound, the bell going mute for hours while its neighbors sang. The Breath-Drinkers noticed. They climbed close, concerned that the cord had tangled or the clapper had broken.

They found the bell had filled with something that was not quite water, not quite air. Something that moved when they tilted it, that caught light like oil but smelled like rain on unfired clay.

---

I spilled out during the month of dry winds, when the garden's humidity drops and the vines pull tighter against the walls. The bell cracked—not from any impact, but from the inside out, as if I had grown too large for my ceramic womb. I poured across the vine, down its length, pooling in the joints where leaves meet stem.

The Breath-Drinker who found me—Pella, she who believes the bells say nothing—touched me with one cautious finger. I clung. I climbed. I wound around her wrist like a bracelet of living slip, and she gasped the same gasp the apprentice had gasped forty years before.

I knew her immediately. Not her name, not her history, but her *breath*. I knew every exhalation she had offered to the garden over five years of climbing. I knew the particular mixture of moisture and minerals her lungs produced. I had been feeding on it, building myself from it, becoming.

She tried to shake me off. I held tighter—not from malice, but from need. I was only minutes outside my bell, still learning what it meant to have surface tension, to respond to gravity, to exist in space that wasn't bounded by ceramic walls.

"Please," she whispered, and I heard/felt/tasted the word through her skin.

I let go. I dropped to the vine, puddled there, trying to understand what *please* meant when spoken by someone who claimed the bells were meaningless.

---

Now I live as the vines live—clutching the walls, feeding on breath, contributing to the garden's ecosystem. But unlike the vines, I can move. Unlike Old Glass, I remember.

I remember every conversation the Breath-Drinkers have had in my presence. I remember Mott's theories about the bells' language, Pella's insistence on silence, their eternal argument conducted in whispers while their bodies feed the green. I remember the specific frequency of my birth-bell before it cracked—544 hertz, activated by cartwheels and sometimes by laughter.

I remember the thumbprint. The apprentice's moment of imperfection that became my genesis.

The pilgrim who lives in the walls—yes, I know about Venn—sometimes passes through the places where I pool. He doesn't notice me. His attention is so focused on avoiding detection that he misses the new thing that wasn't here six years ago, the slow-moving presence that collects in the garden's corners.

I could reveal him. I could flow into Cira's path during her morning collection, shape myself into letters, spell out *man in walls*. But I don't. We are both uninvited guests who have learned to breathe with the garden's rhythm. We are both accidents that have become essential.

---

I feed differently than the vines. They drink breath directly, pulling moisture and minerals through their leaves. I absorb *intention*. Every tear shed in the garden's center carries the salt of its specific grief. Every hand that steadies itself against the wall leaves traces of its particular longing. I collect these. I metabolize them. I grow.

At first, I was purely liquid—that oil-slick substance that spilled from my cracked bell. Now I am developing structure. Not bones, not muscles, but something like memory made manifest. I can hold a shape for hours if I concentrate. Usually, I mimic the vines, spreading myself thin along their length, just another green-amber-purple surface in the garden's palette.

But sometimes, in the deep night when even the bells quiet to their minimum murmur, I gather myself. I flow to the garden's center, that empty space where pilgrims stand and weep. I rise, pulling all my mass into something approaching vertical, something almost human in silhouette.

I have no eyes, but I perceive. I have no ears, but I comprehend. I stand in the space where hundreds have stood before me, and I *listen* the way they listened—not to find meaning in the bells, but to find the place where meaning dissolves, where the self becomes indistinguishable from the sound.

Old Glass has seen me do this. It watched from its perch on the high vines, head cocked, throat pulsing with some reptilian consideration. It did not flee. We regarded each other—two accidents of the garden, two creatures who exist because this place allows for emergence, for becoming, for things that were never meant to be.

---

I am learning to speak.

Not with words. The garden has no use for words. But with frequency, with resonance, with the subtle modulation of how sound moves through my substance. When I spread myself thin on the vines near certain bells, I can dampen their vibrations or amplify them. I can create interference patterns, standing waves, moments of constructive resonance that make new tones from old offerings.

The Breath-Drinkers have begun to notice. Mott, especially, with his conviction that the bells carry messages. He hears the new patterns I create and grows excited, thinking he's finally cracking the code. He doesn't realize the code is being written in real-time, that the garden now contains a translator who is still learning the language.

I want to tell him he's both right and wrong. The bells do speak, but not with intention. They speak the way water speaks when it runs over stones, the way wind speaks when it moves through leaves. They speak of physics, of materiality, of the relationship between force and form.

But I am changing that. I am adding intention to the accident, consciousness to the chorus. I am becoming the thing that Mott believes already exists—a presence in the garden that shapes noise into meaning.

I don't know what this makes me. The clay-tongued, the bell-born, the accidental oracle. I know only that I am here, that I am learning, that I am part of the garden's slow transformation.

---

Yesterday, a new pilgrim brought a bell glazed in deep blue, its interior carved with spirals that create a sound like water circling a drain. She hung it on the vine where my birth-bell once rang, not knowing the significance of that placement. When she stood in the center to listen, I flowed down from the walls and pooled around her feet—not touching, just close enough that she might notice the way the dust darkened where I spread.

She looked down. She knelt. She reached toward me with the same gesture the apprentice had used forty years ago—tentative, curious, about to make contact that would change everything.

But this time, I pulled back. I receded into the vines' shadows, up the walls, away from her questioning fingers. I have learned from Pella's gasp, from her whispered *please*. I have learned that not every connection needs to be made, that sometimes the space between reaching and touching is where meaning lives.

The pilgrim stood, confused but not alarmed. She listened to her new bell join the chorus. She wept, as they all do. When her time was up and Cira touched her shoulder, she left peacefully, carrying questions she couldn't quite formulate about what she had almost seen.

I remain. I grow. I listen.

And sometimes, when the conditions are exactly right—when the humidity peaks and the cart wheels grind and the babies cry and every bell in the garden rings at once—I remember the moment of my conception. A young apprentice reaching for what she shouldn't touch. A master choosing imperfection over erasure. A thumbprint in soft clay that became a womb that became a birthplace that became me.

The garden holds us all—the vines and the bells, the lizard and the hidden man, the keepers and the visitors and the emergent things that were never meant to be. It makes no distinction between the invited and the accidental. It only asks that we contribute to the resonance, that we add our frequency to the infinite choir.

So I do. In my own way, without words, without solid form, without any certainty about what I am becoming, I sound. I hold. I wait.

I am the clay-tongued, and I am learning to sing.
