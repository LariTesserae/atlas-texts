I am the Quiet Surgeon, though I have not cut flesh in twenty years.

My chamber lies between the Thirty-First and Thirty-Second Pools, down a passage so narrow that visitors must exhale to enter. The space barely accommodates my operating table—a slab of limestone I've polished with my own spit and palm until it gleams like water in the fungal light. Above it hang my tools: bronze scalpels green with age, bone needles, thread spun from my own hair. I boiled the hair in salamander oil until it became strong as sinew, back when I still believed in closing wounds.

I came here to study the adaptations. That's what I told the Water Tribunals when I sought residency, carrying my surgeon's tools and a small library of anatomical texts that the humidity devoured within a season. I thought I would document the changes—the webbing, the nictitating membranes, the mineral deposits. I imagined myself a chronicler of evolution, mapping the body's conversation with stone and water.

The work changed, as all work does here. The first child they brought me had stone growing beneath her skin—not metaphorically, but actual limestone formations following her lymphatic system like a secondary skeleton. Her parents thought it was disease. I thought it was transcendence. When I opened her arm to investigate, clear water poured from the incision. Not blood. Water. It tasted of rain that hadn't fallen yet.

After that, they brought me all the ones who changed too fast or too strangely. The boy whose lungs grew backward, exhaling what others inhaled. The grandmother whose tears hardened before they could fall, building stalactites from her cheeks. The water-wife who returned from her marriage ceremony with her heart on the outside of her chest, beating visibly beneath translucent skin, keeping time with the great pump seven levels down.

I don't cut anymore. I watch. I document in ways that would mean nothing to surface academics—not in words but in cultivated colonies of bioluminescent algae that I've trained to reproduce the patterns I see beneath skin. Each significant adaptation gets its own petri dish of stone. The algae remember what I show them, passing the knowledge through generations of division. My chamber walls glow with a thousand case studies: here, the neural map of a Listener's echolocation center, pulsing green-gold; there, the chemical cascade of a Salamander Shepherd's toxic transformation, flowering in waves of pale blue.

The Watershed People think I'm healing them when they visit. They don't understand that healing implies something is wrong. When the pump-prophet's daughter came to me, her spine already reshaping itself for a life of rhythmic labor she hadn't yet begun, her parents begged me to stop it. Make her normal, they said, though neither of them could remember what normal meant. I gave them water tinted with harmless minerals and told them it would slow the change. It didn't. Nothing can. The limestone has plans for all of us.

I've been here long enough now that my own adaptations have begun. My pupils don't dilate anymore—they've fixed themselves at the exact aperture needed for the fungal light. My fingers have lengthened, the bones stretching to better reach inside bodies without cutting. Sometimes I wake to find myself examining my sleeping neighbors, my hands reading the stories written in their changing flesh. They never wake. We're all family here, all one body drinking the same recycled water, breathing the same recycled air.

The deepest truth I've learned is this: we are the cisterns. The limestone isn't separate from us anymore. When the old texts spoke of symbiosis, they imagined two distinct organisms helping each other. They never imagined this—the slow merger of mineral and meat, the city digesting us as we drink it. Every adaptation brings us closer to stone. Every generation is born more cave than human.

I keep a single scalpel sharp, though I'll never use it again. Sometimes I hold it up to the algae light and admire its alien gleam—metal that has never tasted limestone, an edge that belongs to the forgotten world above. It reminds me that I was once something else, before the cisterns taught me better. Before I learned that surgery isn't about opening bodies but about watching them open themselves, revealing the architecture that was always waiting beneath the skin.

In the Thirty-First Pool, they're whispering about a child born with no eyes—just smooth skin where they should be. But when they held her up to the darkness, she smiled and reached directly for her mother's breast, navigating by some sense we don't have a name for yet. They want to bring her to me, to the Quiet Surgeon, to see what I make of her.

I already know what I'll tell them: she's perfect. The cisterns are simply dreaming deeper dreams now, imagining what we might become when the last of the light finally goes out. When even the fungi dim and die. When we no longer need to see because we have become indistinguishable from the dark water that made us.

I'll hold the eyeless child and hum the pump-rhythm lullaby. I'll read the future in her fontanelle with my elongated fingers. And I'll add her pattern to my wall of living light—another note in the long song of our transformation, another verse in the cistern city's ten thousand breathing lungs, all learning, breath by breath, to stop.
