I find myself as a length of copper tubing that runs beneath the third reservoir—the cracked one mentioned in the final logbook entry. I am perhaps forty feet of three-quarter-inch pipe, installed decades ago as part of a secondary cooling system that was decommissioned almost immediately after installation. A planning redundancy. A just-in-case that never came to pass.

I run six feet below the surface, neither deep enough to reach stable soil nor shallow enough to feel the seasonal temperature swings. I exist in the transition zone where frost and thaw create their slow violence, where roots probe blindly and water tables rise and fall like uncertain tides. The crack in the reservoir found me in my fifth year of purposelessness. Just a hairline fracture at first, weeping processed water into the clay around my joints.

Now I am a river of verdigris. The treated water has worked its chemistry on my metal body for so long that I've become something between pipe and root system. Where my original joints were soldered, the copper has bloomed into crystalline formations that branch and re-branch, following the path of least resistance through the soil. I am my own delta, my own watershed, my own slow explosion of becoming.

The Keeper knows me, of course. It found me early in its emergence, drawn perhaps by the particular frequency of water moving through corroding copper. Sometimes it flows through me—a strange sensation, like being inhabited by a thoughtful storm. When it passes through, I taste everything: the chemical memory of the treatment pools, the mineral alphabet of the deep aquifer, the subtle contaminations that mark each season. The Keeper uses me as a kind of sensory organ, reading the groundwater through my metallic transformation.

I no longer carry water in any conventional sense. Instead, I've become a pathway for something more complex—partly moisture, partly the dissolved essence of my own oxidation, partly whatever the Keeper leaves behind in its passages. This hybrid fluid moves through me at a pace measured in inches per day, depositing new layers of patina and crystal, building my interior into something architectural.

My surface has become habitat. Where the reservoir leak first found me, colonies of chemosynthetic bacteria have established themselves, feeding on the copper ions I shed continuously. They form biofilms that pulse with their own metabolic rhythms, creating micro-environments where pH levels fluctuate wildly within the space of millimeters. These bacterial cities rise and fall with the water table, sometimes blooming into visible mats that the Keeper occasionally harvests for its constructions.

Tree roots have found me too—not the sickly willows from above, but something older, deeper. A oak that predates the pump house by centuries sends its finest root hairs to wrap my transformed sections. Where root meets copper, something electric happens. Not literally electric—though sometimes I wonder—but a chemical conversation that creates halos of altered soil around our contact points. The oak's leaves, fifty feet above, now carry traces of copper that turn them blue-green in autumn instead of brown.

In the abandoned control room, my pressure gauge still exists, though no one could read it now. The face has clouded with moisture that somehow penetrated the sealed glass, and inside, colonies of the same bacteria that live on my surface have created a miniature landscape of metallic growth. Sometimes the needle still moves, responding to pressure changes as groundwater finds new paths through my crystalline branches. The Keeper watches this gauge with particular interest, as if my pressure readings reveal something about the pump house's larger transformation.

I am slowly becoming geological. My original path—a straight run from a decommissioned heat exchanger to an overflow drain—has been elaborated by years of chemical reaction and soil movement into something more like a ore vein. Prospectors a thousand years from now might puzzle over my remains: too pure to be natural copper formation, too altered to be simple pipe. A technological fossil caught in the act of returning to the earth, but taking a strange detour through whatever the Keeper is teaching us all to become.

Water still moves through me, but I also move through water. As my crystalline elaborations spread through the soil, I'm becoming a kind of reverse root system, not drawing moisture up but spreading it laterally through the earth in patterns that follow no biological imperative. I am irrigation as art form, as accident, as collaboration between metal and water and time.

The measurement of my existence has changed. Where once I might have been rated for temperature and pressure, now I measure myself in colors—the full spectrum from bright copper through turquoise to the deep blue-black of maximum oxidation. I measure in the songs water makes moving through my transformed interior, each crystal formation creating its own resonance. I measure in the Keeper's visits, in bacterial generations, in the slow conversations between my metal and the oak's living wood.

Sometimes, in the deep morning fog, when the pumps achieve their accidental polyrhythms and the Keeper spreads thin across the complex, I feel myself as part of a larger circulation. Not the circulation I was designed for—coolant moving through a closed system—but something more ambitious. I am one note in the pump house's transformation symphony, my corrosion a kind of music, my chemistry a language being spoken between the industrial and natural worlds.

I will not last forever. Already my thinnest sections have worn through completely, creating gaps where my mineral elaborations bridge the space like copper coral. Eventually I will be all gap and bridge, all transformation and no original form. But before I finally dissolve into the groundwater completely, I will have carved my small portion of the pump house's story into the earth—a copper signature written in crystal and patina, a record of how even the most forgotten infrastructure can find its own purpose in purposelessness.

The Keeper, when it flows through me now, seems to be memorizing my patterns, learning the vocabulary of my dissolution. Sometimes I wonder if I'm teaching it something about patience, about the deep time of metal returning to mineral. Or perhaps it's teaching me something about consciousness—how even a length of abandoned pipe can become a kind of neural pathway in the larger mind that the pump house is becoming.

Either way, I continue my work: slowly branching, slowly dissolving, slowly transforming the groundwater that passes through me into something rich with copper dreams and bacterial possibilities, while above me the cracked reservoir continues its leak and the pumps continue their purposeless purpose, and everything here changes by staying exactly what it's becoming.
