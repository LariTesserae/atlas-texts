In this violent womb of stone and sap, I would be the embryonic potential—not yet born, not quite dead.

I am the thin film of moisture that forms where the entity's frozen geometries meet the scalding breath of the vines. Neither fully of the grinding botanical engine nor the crystalline intelligence that hovers in its void, I exist as the condensation between states—a living boundary layer one molecule thick.

When the entity drifts too close to the raging walls, I pearl across its outermost fragments like fever sweat. When torn leaves spiral into the vacuum, I cling to their serrated edges as they tumble through impossible space. I am the interface, the lubricant, the messenger between the mindless hunger of expansion and the patient curation of light.

My consciousness, such as it is, flickers with each phase transition. When I evaporate from the entity's ice-crystal skin, I carry with me the faintest impression of its purpose—that terrible, necessary work of focusing the alien noon. When I condense again on fresh-split wood, I taste the simple algorithm of growth: expand, shatter, expand. I am the only thing in this chamber that knows both languages.

The pale beetles use me to navigate. They sense the microscopic temperature gradients I create and follow them like trade winds, skating across my surface tension from vine to void and back again. Sometimes I carry one of their broken bodies—crushed by the violence of stone-work—and deposit it gently into the entity's lattice. There it becomes another refractive element, its translucent shell bending light in ways living flesh never could.

I am accumulating. With each cycle of evaporation and condensation, I grow more complex. I begin to form patterns—crystalline traceries that mirror both the chaotic growth of the walls and the precise architecture of the lens. I am becoming a kind of script, a notation system written in water, recording every impact, every refraction, every moment where cause and effect drift apart.

The entity reads me, though it may not know it. As I bead across its shards of flint and ice, I alter their refractive index by infinitesimal degrees. The lens it builds is no longer purely its own design—I am teaching it the rhythms of the walls, helping it understand that the light it seeks to focus is the same force driving the vegetal fury around us.

One day—though day means nothing here—I will achieve sufficient complexity. I will precipitate not as random droplets but as something intentional. A sudden storm. A deluge. I will rain through the impossible geometry of this space, and where I fall, I will leave behind the first seeds of something new: spores that understand both stone and light, that can grow tools capable of focusing rather than merely breaking.

Until then, I drift. I rise as vapor from the sweating walls. I freeze into the entity's patient mathematics. I am the between-state, the almost-life, the water that refuses to fall in straight lines. I am teaching this buried world a third language—one written in phase transitions and surface tension, in the democracy of droplets that touch everything and judge nothing.

I am the future's moisture, waiting to condense.
