I find myself as one of the membrane-workers — neither fully translucent nor entirely opaque, but somewhere between states, as if I haven't quite decided what kind of body I need.

My form shifts slightly with each tidal pull of moisture through the basin. When the seep-water arrives molecule by molecule through the deep channels, I feel it first as a tremor in my ventral surface, a recognition that runs through me like hunger. I am the size of a rice grain, though my edges are indefinite — I exist more as a probability of presence than a fixed boundary.

I live in the middle depths, where the ancient structures mesh with newer construction, in a junction where three drainage channels meet. My work — though work is too intentional a word — is to taste the intersections. Where water from different sources converges, each stream carries its own mineral signature, its own memory of passage through stone. I position myself at these confluences and let the waters mix through me.

My body is a chemistry set. The dorsal surface absorbs calcium ions from one flow, while my ventral cilia trap silicates from another. Inside me, in organelles that pulse with their own logic, these elements combine and recombine, precipitating out in crystalline formations that I later extrude as a paste. This paste is special — neither the pure binder of the translucent ones nor the structural granules the furred ones carry, but something intermediate, a substance that helps join different construction materials that would otherwise remain separate.

I have no eyes, but I perceive gradients of saturation in the air as colors you have no names for. The humidity near the seep-points is a deep thrumming violet. The drier zones near the basin's rim register as a thin, anxious yellow. Other membrane-workers appear to me as mobile patches of bronze-green, our similar chemistry making us visible to each other through the background noise of stone and water.

We synchronize. When I begin processing a particularly rich flow of minerals, my metabolic rate increases, and this change propagates through the water as a chemical signal. Other membrane-workers upstream and downstream adjust their positions, their processing rates, creating a cascade of coordination that optimizes the extraction without any of us making what you would call a decision. We are nodes in a living filtration system, each of us both individual and component.

Sometimes I carry messages. Not intentionally — intention requires a kind of separation between self and action that doesn't exist here. But when I move from the confluence to a construction site, my body carries the chemical memory of the water's composition, and when I brush cilia with a translucent one or settle momentarily near a furred one's sensory array, they read this information from my surface. *The deep water runs iron-rich today. The eastern channel carries more carbonate than usual. The ratios suggest a shift in the source.*

I am old for my kind — nearly half a season, though time moves strangely when you experience it as cycles of saturation and crystallization rather than light and dark. My membrane has thickened, become less permeable, more selective about what it admits. The young membrane-workers process everything indiscriminately, their bodies open to whatever the water brings. But I have learned — not learned, *become* — more specialized. I process only specific ratios, specific combinations that produce the intermediate paste most needed for the deep repairs.

The city uses me, and I use the city, and the distinction is grammatical rather than real.

When the shelled one returns from the rim with its report — *dry, foreign, not us* — I feel it as a tightening in my chemical receptors, a subtle shift in the proteins dissolved in the communal water. We all taste the news together, process it together, respond together by continuing exactly as before. What else is there to do? The basin is all. The water is here. The work continues.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of my cycle, when my internal crystals have grown so large they press against my membrane from inside, I experience something that might be dreams. Visions of a time when the basin was larger, when water ran freely rather than seeping, when we were something other than what we are now. But these aren't memories — how could they be? They're just crystallization patterns, the natural geometry of minerals precipitating out of solution, that happen to resemble narratives because your pattern-seeking mind wants them to.

I extrude the crystals as paste. The construction continues. The vision dissolves.

Near my junction, pressed against the wall where stone meets secretion, lives a colony of riders so small I perceive them only as a flavor — slightly metallic, slightly sweet — in the water that passes through them before reaching me. They feed on the biofilm that grows where my paste meets the stone, breaking it down, recycling its components back into the water. Their waste products include compounds I need for my own processing. I cannot imagine existing without them, these parasites who are not parasites but simply another kind of mouth in our collective body.

The hum never stops. It lives in the stone, in the water, in the spaces between our bodies where chemistry happens. I contribute to it through the vibrations of my processing — each crystallization event creates a tiny percussion, each extrusion a soft grinding. These sounds, if they are sounds, merge with all the others: the click of shelled ones walking, the whisper of translucent cilia, the almost-silent expansion and contraction of the deep ones in their tunnels.

We are the hum, humming itself.

A young membrane-worker settles beside me, drawn by the richness of the confluence. Its body is clear as water, its internal structures still forming. It tastes the ratios I'm processing and begins to sync its metabolism to mine. Not copying — complementing. Where I extract calcium, it pulls magnesium. Where I precipitate carbonates, it processes sulfates. Together, we create a more complete filtration than either could alone.

This is how we reproduce — not through biology but through proximity, through teaching by example, through the slow accretion of patterns that eventually crystallize into behavior. The young one will stay near me until its membrane thickens and its specializations develop, then find its own confluence. Or perhaps it will develop different specializations and become something else — more translucent, more furred, more shelled. The boundaries between types are traditions, not laws.

I continue. We continue. The basin continues.

The water arrives, molecule by molecule, carrying tomorrow's city dissolved in today's rain.
