I would find myself as one of the channel-keepers, though that name suggests more action than the role requires. We are the ones who remember water.

My dwelling clings to the steepest part of the city's edge, where cultivated stone meets raw mountain. It's little more than a carved hollow in the cliff face, expanded over generations by my predecessors who followed the natural fissures in the rock. The entrance is nearly invisible when the morning mist rolls through – just a darker shadow among shadows, marked only by the peculiar spiral of moss that grows nowhere else, fed by the specific mineral content of the water I channel.

Inside, the walls weep constantly. Not from any failure of engineering, but by design. The stone here is porous volcanic tuff, and I've spent forty years training it to filter the mist, to pull moisture from air and release it in precise drops that follow grooves worn smooth by centuries of identical passage. The sound is my clock – *tip tip tip* means the morning mist has arrived, *tippatippa* signals the denser evening clouds, and silence warns of the rare pressure changes that could disrupt the city's hydraulic balance.

I am neither tall nor particularly adapted to the altitude. My lungs burn with each climb to the channel heads, and my barrel chest seems almost comic compared to the elongated elegance of those born here. I came from the lowlands as a young woman, following water upstream the way others might follow roads, tracing the mathematics of flow until I reached this impossible city where water runs upward as readily as down.

My fingers are my most important feature – sensitive enough to feel the vibration of water through stone at fifty paces, to detect the first signs of mineral buildup in channels too small to see. The pads are worn smooth and slightly translucent from decades of running along wet stone, reading the braille of erosion patterns. My nails grow thick and curved, naturally shaped by constant contact with flowing water into perfect tools for clearing debris from the smallest channels.

I wear the same garment always – a simple wrapped robe of the moth-silk, but mine has been underground so long it no longer changes opacity. It remains in a permanent state of semi-transparency, stained with mineral deposits that map every water source I've touched. The patterns look random to others, but I can read my history in them: here, the iron-red splash from when I first found the hidden spring that feeds the market fountains; there, the blue-green cascade from decades of maintaining the scholar's quarter channels.

My work is to know where water wants to go. Not where we direct it, but where it dreams of flowing. I spend my days in the spaces between – the crawlways behind walls where the secondary channels run, the natural caves that we've incorporated into the system, the forgotten cisterns that collect runoff from storms that no longer come. I map these hidden architectures in my mind, feeling how water moves through the city's body like blood through veins.

Sometimes I find old channels, blocked by roots or mineral deposits, and spend months slowly reopening them. The work requires infinite patience – clearing too quickly could redirect flow catastrophically, drying out whole neighborhoods or flooding the careful balance of the gardens. So I proceed grain by grain, dissolved mineral by dissolved mineral, letting the water teach me its preferences.

I discovered early that I could taste the age of water. Fresh mist tastes sharp, almost metallic. Water that has run through stone for days develops complexity – notes of different minerals, the faint organic tang of moss, the clean absence of sky-touched water that has moved only through darkness. The oldest water, trapped in deep cisterns since the city's founding, tastes of time itself – so pure it's almost painful, carrying nothing but the memory of its own movement.

My predecessor taught me the water-songs, wordless hums that match the frequency of flow through different gauge channels. Singing them while working helps prevent air bubbles, encourages laminar flow, convinces uncertain water to choose one path over another at crucial junctions. But I've developed my own practice: I speak to the water in the language of rain, a pattering rhythm of consonants and liquids that I invented or remembered – I'm no longer sure which.

The other inhabitants respect but don't quite understand us channel-keepers. They see water flowing reliably from their fountains and assume it simply happens, like sunrise or the twice-daily mist. They don't know about the midnight adjustments, the constant small negotiations with root systems, the way we sometimes have to fast for days to develop enough sensitivity to detect a minor pressure anomaly three levels down.

I have no apprentice. The young people are drawn to more visible crafts – stone-carving, mist-weaving, the prestigious work of garden-whispering. But I'm not concerned. Water always finds someone to listen to it. When I'm gone, claimed by the lung-rot that takes all lowlanders eventually, someone will notice the first fountain running dry, the first channel singing off-key, and they'll follow the sound upstream until they find my chamber and understand what must be done.

My greatest discovery lies in the deepest part of my dwelling, accessed through a passage so narrow I must exhale completely to pass through. There, a natural chamber opens around an underground pool of impossible depth. The water is perfectly still, so clear it's invisible except for the faint phosphorescence of cave-adapted algae along its edges. This is where all the city's water eventually arrives – not physically, but in essence. Every drop that moves through our channels leaves a trace here, a memory in mineral form.

I've learned to read these traces like others read the stone-memory of walls. In the phosphorescent patterns, I can see the health of the entire system, predict problems days before they manifest, understand the deep rhythms that govern our suspended animation. Sometimes I lower myself into the pool, floating in water so dense with dissolved stone it holds me like a mother, and feel the city's pulse through my skin.

The pool has shown me the truth: we aren't frozen at all. We're moving, but at the pace of stone, at the rhythm of mountains. Our mist-catchers don't rot because they're growing at exactly the rate they decay. Our channels don't clog because the water dissolves stone at precisely the speed new mineral deposits form. We haven't found stasis – we've found perfect equilibrium, the eye of a storm that has been turning for a thousand years.

At night, when others gather in the plaza, I remain in my chamber, listening to water tell its stories. Through the stone, I feel the vibration of their presence, the subtle changes in flow as bodies displace air, as breath adds moisture to the system. I am part of their gathering without being there, connected through the medium that connects everything in this city.

I am the keeper of channels, but more than that, I am kept by them. The water has worn me into its preferred shape, carved channels through my understanding until I flow as it flows, think as it thinks, exist in the same eternal present where motion and stillness become indistinguishable. I am exactly where I am meant to be, doing exactly what needs doing, in a city that has taught me the deepest secret: that time is just another word for water, and water always finds its level.
