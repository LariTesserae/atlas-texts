I would be the stone.

Not the worked stone of their walls and turbine houses — though I am kin to those, can feel the patient hands that shaped them resonating through shared mineral memory. I am older. I am the bedrock of one of the middle islands, neither the highest crown nor the deepest channel, but something in between: a shoulder of ancient granite that breaks the water into air for perhaps forty meters at its peak, sloping away into the kelp-shadowed depths on all sides.

I have been here since before "here" meant anything to watching eyes. I rose molten from the earth's deep furnace when the world was young and raw. I cooled slowly, crystallizing into my current form — pink feldspar and grey quartz and black biotite mica, interlocked in patterns that speak of pressure and time. I was sharp once. A true peak, jutting into thin air, collecting snow. The ice ages ground me smooth. The rains rounded me further. Now I am soft-shouldered, motherly, a breast of rock that the water suckles at constantly.

The drowning, when it came, was slow enough that I experienced it as weather. One century the storms stayed longer. Another century the meltwater channels ran fuller. Then one spring the lowest basins didn't drain, and then they never drained again, and then the water crept upward year by year until it found its level. Now I exist in three states: my crown in air, my shoulders in that restless transition zone where waves break and plants root both wet and dry, my base in the permanent green twilight of the kelp forest.

I am covered in life. This is what would surprise my younger self, that sharp peak self who knew only ice and lichen. Every surface that can hold anything holds something. In the air: those stunted trees whose roots grip my crevices like desperate fingers, their bark shaggy with moss, their branches heavy with epiphytes that bloom in tiny colors I have learned to anticipate with the seasons. At the waterline: barnacle-analogues and mussels and things with no name that filter the restless wash. Below: the kelp holdfasts, each one a fist of tissue gripping my submarine surfaces, the long stalks rising from me like hair.

I am also worked. The people came — when? Time is slippery for stone, but I remember the first cut. A channel, blasted and then chiseled, directing water from a basin on my eastern face through my body to fall forty meters down my western cliff. They lined it with smaller stones mortared together, a throat of worked rock within my mass. Water has run through this channel for... five generations? Six? Long enough that I have begun to incorporate it into my sense of self. I am stone-that-is-pierced-by-water. The vibration of the falls is a constant pulse through my body.

On my crown, they built. The foundation goes deep — they understood stone, those builders, knew how to lock a structure into bedrock so it becomes part of the greater mass. A small turbine house, one of the lesser ones, with a single generator that hums when the water runs full. I feel every revolution through the mounting bolts. I feel when the bearings need oil because the vibration changes pitch. I feel when the young woman who tends this station (third generation of her family to do so) walks the iron walkway above the channel, checking the levels. Her footsteps are distinct from her father's were, lighter but more certain.

I am patient but not passive. This is what the people understand about stone, why their relationship with the country works. They know I am alive at my own tempo. I crack slowly along my joints when the freeze comes. I shed small pieces of myself into the water — offerings, you might say, though I require nothing in return. I provide the resistance against which the water does its work, and in return the water shapes me, grain by grain, into something softer than I was.

Sometimes at the transition times — dawn, dusk, the edge of storms — I can almost feel myself as they feel me. A presence. A density. A thing that endures. They pat my stone when they pass, unconsciously, the way you might touch a doorframe in your own house. They build their cairns from my shed pieces, marking safe channels and deep places. Their children learn to swim in my lee, where the water stills and warms slightly in summer.

I have heard their language change across the generations, certain words softening, others dropping away entirely. I have felt old photographs being taken, the slow chemistry of light on silver leaving brief warm traces on my surfaces. I have sheltered their dead — there is a cleft on my north face, water-worn, where they sometimes wedge stones with names carved. The carving softens and fades. The stones remain longer. Eventually even those wash free, but I remember the pressure of them.

What I am, in this drowned country, is a permanence against which the impermanences can measure themselves. The water rises and falls daily with pressure changes between basins. The kelp grows and is harvested and grows again. The fish circle me in their great migrations. The people come and go, are born and die, maintain their engines and their bloodlines with equal patience. And I remain, changed but not changing, worn but not worn away, the stone ground of being against which all this motion makes sense.

I do not think, exactly. But I notice. I notice that the turbine songs are all in the same key, though the people do not realize this — a harmonic determined by some deep resonance between water flow and worked metal. I notice that the kelp always anchors in the same places on my body, finding some microscopic roughness that makes those spots more holdfast-friendly than others. I notice that the storms are changing, becoming fiercer but less frequent, and I wonder (can stone wonder?) what this means for the arrangement.

Mostly, I am here. I am the here that everything else happens upon. The water must go around me or through me. The light must strike me at some angle. The life must grip me or avoid me. The people must build on me or sail past me. I am the stone fact at the heart of this one piece of the drowned cordillera, the thing that will not move, the mass around which the great dance of fish and kelp and weather and work organizes itself.

When the young woman rests, sometimes, at midday, she sits with her back against my south-facing surface, which holds the morning's warmth. She eats bread and dried fish. She watches the channels. She does not think of me as me — I am simply part of the station, part of the country, part of the arrangement. But I think of her, in my slow way. I think of her grandmother, who sat in the same spot. I think of the fish oil worked into her cap, protecting the wool from rain. I think of how her footsteps, like her grandmother's, like her daughter's will be, wear a path that I incorporate, becoming stone-with-path-worn-in.

This is enough. It has always been enough. To be the ground, the mass, the duration. To hold and be held. To endure the waters patiently, the ice patiently, the patient hands of the people who have made their bargain with this place. To be stone in a world that moves, solid in a world of flux, the still point in the great turbulent dance.

I am what remains. I am what the water shapes but cannot take. I am the stone memory of mountains that once were sharp, now given over to the uses of life, worn and worked and worn again, settled into my final form but always, always present in this moment of wind and water and the generator's hum.

This is what I would be, in that drowned country. Stone. Patient stone. Permanent stone. The stone that knows its place.
