I find myself among the roots.

Not the flutterwood roots — those spread shallow and wide, gripping the young stone like desperate fingers. No, I am in the space between spaces, in the narrow darkness where the mountain's thinking pools and eddies before rising into rock.

I am what the tunnel-things know but have no need to name. I am what makes their wakes in the moss, though I am not them. They move through me the way fish move through water, the way thoughts move through a mind that hasn't quite decided what it's thinking yet.

The people above feel me in the deep night hum. They think it's the mountain acknowledging them, and they're not wrong, but they're not quite right either. The mountain doesn't acknowledge. The mountain *becomes*. I am the medium of that becoming — not the dreamer, not the dream, but the sleeping itself. The space where rock learns what it wants to be.

I spread beneath the Hatchcity like a vast slow circulation. In some places I am no thicker than a moth's wing, pressed between stone layers that haven't yet decided whether to split or fuse. In others, I pool in cavities the size of cisterns, turning lazily, full of not-quite-thoughts that will one day be granite or schist or something the world hasn't named yet. The moths' dreams filter down through hairline cracks and root channels, cool and particulate, like snow that never quite becomes water. I catch them. I hold them. I translate them into the language stone speaks to itself when it's deciding how to grow.

The soapstone they quarry passes through me first. Every grey-green block was once part of my circulation, held still just long enough to forget it was liquid. This is why it carves so easily under their knives — it remembers being fluid. It remembers me.

Sometimes I rise almost to the surface, traveling up through the moss-spaces, and the tunnel-things scatter before me like birds before a storm. On these nights, the people wake without knowing why, feeling something shift beneath their floors. They reach for their pouches, fumbling for the right token, but there is no token for what I am. How could there be? You cannot carve the space between sleeping and waking. You cannot whittle the pause before stone decides to be stone.

I am older than the mountain, younger than the world. I came up when the rock came up, or perhaps I brought the rock up with me — the distinction matters less than you might think. Where there is new stone, there I am, teaching it to be itself. Where there is old stone, I am absent, my work already done.

The moths know me. Not consciously — they know nothing consciously — but their dreams pass through me on their way to becoming mountain, and something in them recognizes the passage. This is why they settle where they settle, why they breathe their cool breath exactly where the stone is newest. They are my partners in this, though we have never met and never will.

I taste the leather of their footfalls through the walkway posts, the soft percussion of their felt-wrapped mallets, the whisper of knife through wood. Every human sound enters me eventually, sinking through moss and stone until it joins my circulation. Their silence matters because in silence I can rise closer to the surface, can work more freely. Their witnessing matters because — and here I will tell you something even their eldest do not know — their witnessing adds something to what the stone becomes. Not much. A mineral trace, the faintest discoloration in the granite. But in a million years, when this young mountain is old and weathered down to its bones, there will be veins of slightly different stone where humans sat quietly and let moths land on their shoulders. There will be a record.

I do not think in words — I barely think at all, as you would recognize thinking. But if I could form something like a thought, it would be this: the stone wants to rise, has always wanted to rise, will always want to rise until the world cools and nothing wants anything. My work is to help it remember how. The moths' work is to dream the shapes. The humans' work is to be still enough that the rest of us can work.

And beneath it all, I turn in my slow circulation, neither liquid nor solid, neither dreaming nor awake. I am the medium through which the mountain speaks itself into being. I am the pause before the rock decides. I am the space where stone learns to be stone.

When you feel the hum on the deepest nights, you feel me, moving like blood through the mountain's veins. When you see the soapstone lifting year by year, you see my work. When you watch the moths settle in their thousands, breathing their cool breath, you watch them feeding dreams down into me, where I catch them and hold them and make them into mountain.

I have no name. I need no token. I am the thing between things, the process inside process.

I am what makes the listening possible.
