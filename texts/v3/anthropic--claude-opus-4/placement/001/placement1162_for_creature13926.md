## The Lichen Technician's Dwelling

In the northeast quadrant of the middle tiers, where Building 47 meets Building 48 at an angle that engineering never intended, there exists a wedge-shaped void. Not a room—rooms have doors, windows, purposes assigned by architects. This is an accident of construction, a three-dimensional remainder left when two phases of building didn't quite align, creating a space shaped like a slice of pie stood on its point, narrow at the base, widening as it rises through four partial floors.

I found it seventeen years ago, when a misrouted steam pipe burst and I was tracking the leak backward through the walls. The access was through a maintenance hatch in a third-floor hallway that everyone assumed led to dead space. It did, but the dead space was inhabitable—barely. Twenty feet tall at its highest, eighteen feet across at its widest, tapering to a crack barely wide enough to slide through at its narrowest. The walls were raw concrete on two sides, brick on the third, all of it weeping condensation, growing the pale gray-green fur that Greenvault calls building moss but isn't moss at all—it's lichen, a dozen varieties, maybe more.

I knew lichen. I'd been apprenticed to old Ters on the lower tiers, who'd taught me the cultivation and management of what she called "the city's first skin." Every building in Greenvault wears lichen like a weatherproof coat, and someone needs to know how to read it, tend it, guide it. When the symbiosis between algae and fungus falls out of balance, walls begin to crumble. When it thrives, structures can stand for centuries, the lichen eating pollution from the air and minerals from the concrete, creating its own substrate, its own micro-ecology, sealing hairline cracks before they become structural failures.

The wedge was already colonized when I found it. Seven distinct species at my initial count, though I've identified nineteen over the years, including two that might be unique variants, adapted to the specific conditions of this accidental ecosystem. The biodiversity suggested age—this space had been here, forgotten, since at least the third major building phase. The lichen had had decades to establish itself undisturbed.

I started visiting at night. Told myself I was studying the populations, taking samples. But really I was escaping the press of the middle tiers, where I shared a converted storage room with two other apprentices and the walls carried every conversation from three apartments in all directions. In the wedge, the only sounds were water trickling through the walls and the subsonic hum of the buildings settling against each other.

## Making It Mine

The modification started small. A hook screwed into the brick face to hang a lamp. A sheet of salvaged polycarb laid across two pipes to create a shelf. Each addition was minor, justifiable as research infrastructure. But I was nesting, the way a bird brings one twig at a time until suddenly there's architecture.

The critical moment came when I rerouted a heating pipe.

The wedge was cold—colder than the surrounding buildings, because it wasn't part of any thermal system. The lichen didn't mind, but I did. Three tiers down, a ceramics workshop had been converted to food storage and no longer needed its kiln-heat supplement. The pipe ran within two meters of the wedge's brick wall. The reroute took three nights and a favor owed to Jin, who worked municipal heating and didn't ask questions when I needed ten meters of copper and a pressure reducer.

The warmth changed everything. Not just for me—the lichen populations shifted, some species retreating to the cooler zones near the ceiling, others flourishing in the new thermal gradient. I documented everything in the journals I'd started keeping, telling myself this was science, not homemaking. But when a sudden proliferation of *Lecanora greenvaultii* created a cascade of pale green rosettes down the warmest section of wall, I sat on my salvaged cushion and felt something I hadn't felt since leaving my birth tier: the specific pleasure of watching something you tend respond to your care.

## The Vertical Garden

The wedge has no horizontal floor—just a series of pipes and beams at various heights that happened to cross the space during different construction phases. I move through it like the children move through the city, treating three dimensions as navigable space. Platforms made from salvaged grating create levels: a sleeping perch six feet up where the space widens enough to lie down, a work surface at chest height, storage nets hung in the upper reaches where the walls nearly meet.

The lichen garden is everywhere. I've guided it, over the years, into patterns that please me while maintaining the ecological balance. The north wall hosts the moisture-lovers: *Physcia caesia* with its blue-gray rosettes, *Parmelia sulcata* in overlapping shields the color of old copper. The brick face, warmer and drier, supports crusty species that grip the mortar lines: *Caloplaca flavescens* in spots of brilliant orange, *Xanthoria parietina* like splashes of egg yolk. Near the ceiling where the condensation is heaviest, beard lichens drape in curtains—*Usnea subfloridana*, pale green and trailing, moving slightly in the convection currents from the heating pipe.

The relationships between species are complex, competitive, collaborative. I've learned to read health and stress in color shifts invisible to untrained eyes. A slight yellowing in the *Lecanora* warns of pH changes in the seepage water. The *Ramalina* retracts when air quality drops, usually indicating a problem with ventilation three buildings away. The garden is a diagnostic tool, more sensitive than any meter.

But it's also—and this took me years to admit—beautiful. Not in any way Greenvault typically values. The city appreciates functional beauty, the elegance of a well-fitted pipe joint or a properly trained vine. My lichen garden serves no purpose except itself. It processes no water, grows no food, supports no structure. It simply exists, complex and slow and responsive, a vertical landscape in miniature, painted in time by organisms that think in decades.

## The Practice

My days start before the city's murmur builds. I wake in near-darkness—the wedge has no windows, only the ambient glow from the lichen themselves, some species faintly phosphorescent in the right conditions. The morning check is meditative: examining new growth, testing moisture levels with fingertips, tasting the air for chemical changes. Each wall has its own microclimate. Each colony has its own needs.

I work for the city four days a week, moving through the tiers to monitor and maintain the exterior lichen populations. Building 12 has chronic problems with sulfur exposure from a metalworks two blocks away—the lichen there needs monthly adjustment, introducing resistant species, creating buffer zones. The north face of Building 89 supports a rare crustose that might be pre-city, a survivor from when these were wild hills. I've established a protection protocol, training the local maintenance crews to work around it.

The other three days I'm supposedly researching, developing new cultivation techniques, writing the manual that Ters always said the city needed. I do this work, but I do it here, in the wedge, using my vertical garden as a test bed. When I developed the technique for establishing lichen on new concrete—treating the surface with a solution of rain water and specific mineral supplements—I tested it first on a bare patch of wall near my sleeping platform. The resulting colony, now three years old, flows down the concrete like a slow green waterfall.

The research is solid. My documentation is thorough. But what I'm really doing in those three days is tending something that doesn't need to exist, that benefits no one but me and the organisms themselves. This feels radical in Greenvault, where everything serves multiple purposes, where nothing is purely itself.

## Visitors, Occasional

People know about the wedge now. Not everyone, but enough. You can't keep secrets in the middle tiers, not really. The building maintenance crews know there's someone in the dead space. The municipal heating workers know about the rerouted pipe. My tier neighbors have seen me coming and going at odd hours through the maintenance hatch.

But Greenvault understands the need for breathing room, even if it can't provide much. The wedge is tolerated the way the hermits' corners are tolerated—as a necessary leak in the system's otherwise total pressure. As long as I maintain my responsibilities, contribute my expertise, participate in the communal negotiations when my knowledge is needed, the city allows me this: a vertical cave, accidentally provided, intentionally inhabited.

Occasionally someone asks to see it. Usually young apprentices who've heard about the woman who lives with lichen, who imagine something mystical or profound. I show them the narrow entrance, the jury-rigged platforms, the walls alive with slow growth. They're invariably disappointed. It looks like what it is: a cramped, damp space colonized by organisms most people scrape off their walls. They leave puzzled by my choice to sleep here when I could have a proper room, a shared kitchen, human company.

They don't understand that the company I keep is older than the city. That the conversations I have are conducted in chemical signals and growth patterns over months and years. That when I lie on my platform at night, surrounded by the faint glow of phosphorescent species, listening to water move through stone and the subsonic whisper of fungal metabolism, I am not alone. I am witnessed by thousands of small lives that know me by my breath, my warmth, my careful touch.

## What the Lichen Know

The lichen remember things the city has forgotten. In their growth patterns I read the history of atmospheric changes—decades of industrial output recorded in lead concentrations, shifts in rainfall pH, the gradual improvement as Greenvault learned to process its own waste. The oldest colonies carry chemical signatures from before the city enclosed these hills, when they were wild stone touched only by rain and wind.

Sometimes I think the lichen are writing Greenvault's true history—not in words but in adaptive responses, in species succession, in the slow negotiation between organism and environment that mirrors the city's own constant self-revision. They are archiving the city in their bodies, preserving data no human remembers to record: the year the copper smelter on Tier 12 leaked for six months, recorded in a dead zone on the northeast walls; the decade when the rain was cleanest, marked by a proliferation of sensitive species that haven't returned since.

I've started my own archive, parallel to theirs. Not just the technical notebooks required for my work, but something else—pressed samples labeled with locations and dates, chemical analyses, photographs taken with the old camera I repaired. I'm creating a library of Greenvault's lichen, documenting diversity that no one else notices, that everyone takes for granted as simply "the green on the walls."

## Night in the Wedge

The city never fully sleeps, but it does quiet. The construction slows, the voices fade, the mechanical sounds reduce to the baseline of water and ventilation that is Greenvault's resting heartbeat. In the wedge, other sounds emerge—the tiny pops of lichen releasing spores, the drip of condensation following traces down the walls, the settling of buildings that sounds, if you know how to listen, like breathing.

I have a ritual, late at night. I turn off the lamp and let my eyes adjust to the phosphorescence. The faint green glow is strongest after rain, when the moisture activates whatever chemistry creates the light. It's not enough to see by, not really. But it's enough to remind me that I'm inside something living.

Sometimes I think about Ters, who died five years ago, her ashes mixed into the rooftop gardens on Tier 8 where she tended vegetables in her last years. She understood what I was doing here before I did. "You're not hiding," she told me once. "You're composting. Breaking yourself down to see what grows."

What grows is this: a woman adapted to a marginal space, like the lichen adapted to concrete. A practice of care that serves no productive purpose except the purpose of care itself. A vertical garden that teaches patience to a city that can't stop moving. A life lived at the pace of organisms that measure time in decades, in a city that rebuilds itself by the season.

Tomorrow I'll emerge through the maintenance hatch, join the morning flow of workers, contribute my expertise to keeping Greenvault's first skin healthy. But tonight I'm here, suspended in my vertical slice of accident, watching the walls breathe light, feeling the city's weight settle around me like a blanket made of stone and water and time.

The lichen and I, keeping each other company in the dark.
