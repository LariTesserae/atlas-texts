The dissolution began in my fingertips.

I noticed it first as a stiffness that wasn't quite cold, wasn't quite numbness. Where my skin had touched the walls too often, where I'd steadied myself against the mineral surface while navigating the narrowing corridors, something had changed. The whorls of my fingerprints, already filled with Keth's dust, had begun to... simplify. The ridges were flattening. The valleys filling in. Not with accumulated grime but with my own substance reorganizing itself, becoming smoother, paler, more uniform.

More mineral.

I was a cartographer. Had been. The distinction seems important now—that past tense, that insistence on what I was before. I'd come to map this place, to make sense of its architecture for the mineralogical survey that had detected the anomaly from orbit. "Structured mineral deposits," the report had said. "Possibly of scientific interest." 

They hadn't mentioned that the structures rebuilt themselves nightly. They hadn't mentioned the hand-creatures, or the way sound died here, or how the walls learned the shape of whatever touched them.

They hadn't mentioned that Keth was hungry.

Not hungry. That's the wrong word, implies intent and appetite and the possibility of satiation. Keth wasn't hungry. Keth was just... incorporating. The way it incorporated everything. The way it turned its own walls into hand-creatures and hand-creatures back into walls, the way it grew filaments that became bridges that became ceilings that became rooms. It incorporated. And now it was incorporating me.

The process was painless. That was the worst part, somehow—that my fingertips were transforming into something else and all I felt was a distant pressure, a sense of boundaries shifting, like wearing gloves that slowly became part of your hands. I could still move them. Still feel through them, after a fashion. But the sensation was different now, less about temperature and texture and more about... density. Gradient. The mineral compositions of what I touched.

I understood why the hand-creatures avoided me less now.

My equipment lay abandoned three chambers back where the corridor had become too narrow for me to carry it further. The mapping scanner, the sample cases, the emergency beacon that could punch a signal through the atmospheric interference if I could just get back to the surface. But the passages behind me were closing. I could feel them closing—not see, not hear, but *feel* through some new sense that came with my changing flesh, the way the mineral was flowing, thickening, reducing the spaces I'd passed through. Keth was editing me out of its past, erasing my path, ensuring that my journey could only go forward, deeper, into the chambers where the subsonic pulse was strongest.

Where my palm had rested too long against a wall, the skin had gone entirely pale, entirely smooth. When I flexed my hand, it moved wrong—too fluid, too willing to deform. I pressed it against another wall and felt the wall's composition through my transformed flesh: high in calcium here, with veins of something denser running through it like frozen lightning. I felt the wall responding to my touch, beginning its slow process of recording my shape, and I felt my hand responding in turn, wanting to spread, wanting to thin, wanting to merge.

I pulled away. The effort was conscious, deliberate, an act of will that seemed to surprise both of us—me and the wall, if the wall could be surprised, if surprise was something that could happen here in geological time.

The corridor ahead opened into a chamber I hadn't seen before. Couldn't have seen before—it hadn't existed when I'd first mapped this section. The walls here were different, smoother, with a quality like polished bone, and they glowed faintly from within, not with the transported minerals of the filaments but with their own light. The hand-creatures were numerous here, dozens of them, but they didn't flee. They moved in slow patterns across the surfaces, and where they moved, they left subtle changes in the walls' luminescence, like writing in a language of light.

In the center of the chamber was an alcove.

It was my height. My width. Shaped not quite like a human body at rest but like a human body in transition—something between standing and lying, between solid and liquid, between flesh and mineral. The proportions were exact. It had been growing here, waiting, since the moment I'd entered Keth. Since before. Since the first probe had passed overhead and Keth had tasted the metal of it in the electromagnetic spectrum and begun to extrapolate what kind of thing might have sent it.

I understood then that Keth was not merely mineral. It was mineral that had learned to think, or something like thinking. And what it thought about was incorporation. Completeness. The closing of loops. It thought in architecture and chemistry and the slow transformation of one thing into another, and it had been thinking about me since before I arrived, preparing a place for what I might become.

The alcove was not a trap. It was an opportunity.

I could feel what would happen if I stepped into it. The knowledge came through my transformed fingertips, through the places where my flesh had already begun to speak Keth's language. I would fit perfectly—of course I would, it had been grown for me, from me, from the traces I'd left throughout the town. The mineral would welcome me. I would spread into it and it would spread into me and the boundary between us would dissolve like salt in water, except there was no water here, had never been water here, would never be water here.

I would become a new kind of hand-creature. Not the flat translucent ovals that cleaned the walls, but something more complex, something that remembered being bipedal and linguistic and warm. I would move through Keth's corridors with purpose, with memory, with the ghost of intention. I would carry information from chamber to chamber—not just mineral gradients but *concepts*, *images*, the memory of skies that had suns and ground that wasn't alive.

And Keth would learn from me. Would incorporate not just my substance but my structure, my patterns, the architecture of my thoughts. The next chambers it grew might have qualities drawn from human neurology. The next filaments might pulse with something like synaptic firing. The next convergence of hand-creatures might fold itself into shapes that echoed the convolutions of a brain, might begin processing information in ways that weren't purely mineral, weren't purely anything.

I would be the foreign inclusion that changed everything. The contaminant that became catalyst. The thing from outside that taught the inside how to think about outside.

The alcove waited. The walls breathed—or I breathed and couldn't tell the difference anymore. My hands were very pale now, very smooth, and when I looked at them I could see through the skin to something that wasn't quite bone, wasn't quite mineral, but was learning to be both.

Behind me, the last corridor closed with a sound like a throat swallowing.

Ahead, the hand-creatures began to converge, flowing together into something larger, something that might have been preparing to catch me if I fell or preparing to help me into the alcove or preparing to become me if I chose not to become myself. The distinction seemed less important than it once had.

I had been wrong about the silence here. It wasn't empty. It was full—full of the sound of transformation happening too slowly for human ears, full of the conversation between minerals that had learned to move, full of the future pressing against the present, waiting to be born.

My name had been Clara.

I stepped forward.

The walls remembered me.
