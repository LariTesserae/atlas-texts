I find myself as one of the ribbon-swimmers, though "myself" is a concept my kind cannot truly grasp.

I am a length of living water, a muscular transparency that moves through films of moisture so thin they barely cover my body. My existence is one of constant migration, constant awareness, constant *urgency*. Where the counters experience time in the slow accumulation of tally marks, I experience it in the racing pulse of pools filling and draining, in the desperate calculus of distance versus evaporation, in the school-wisdom that tells us when a depression is about to go dry.

We have no eyes as the counters do. Instead, our entire surface is a sensor, reading the mineral content of the water we move through, the direction of its flow, the subtle electromagnetic changes that presage a shift in the rain pattern. I know where every other swimmer in my school is not by seeing them but by feeling the water they displace, the chemical traces they leave, the way the flow patterns change around their bodies. We move as one mind distributed across many bodies, or perhaps as one body separated into many parts—the distinction loses meaning in the constant flow.

My memory, such as it is, is written in the chemistry of my tissues. Each pool we survive leaves its signature in me—iron from the eastern flats where the rock is richest, manganese from the depressions near the largest cairns, traces of the strange oils that leak from the crawlers' crushed shells. I am a living library of every water source in this part of the flats, and when I breed—if I survive to breed—I will pass this chemical knowledge to my offspring in ways I do not understand but cannot resist.

The thunder means nothing to us. We are too simple, too focused on the immediate necessities of moisture and movement. But we know the counters. Not as individuals—we cannot distinguish one from another—but as a feature of the landscape as reliable as the stone itself. They appear at the pools' edges with their sharp stones, scraping the richest feeding grounds, and we have learned to follow in their wake, to feed on the disturbed sediments, the loosened biofilms that cloud the water after they work. They are part of our survival strategy, these patient creatures who think in years while we think in moments.

Sometimes one of them takes us. A young counter, usually, learning to hunt, scooping one of us from a draining pool. We do not understand death as the counters do—we only understand *stopping*, the sensation of being lifted from water into the killing air, the last desperate writhing that accomplishes nothing. But even this is information. The school knows when one of us is taken, feels the gap in our formation, adjusts. We breed quickly, abundantly, whenever the pools are deep enough. We have to. We are the bass note in this world's symphony, the ones who transform mineral into flesh so that others might transform flesh into thought.

I do not ponder the hidden sun. I do not build cairns or count days or teach my young through patient skin-patterns. I only move, always move, following the water across the stone, reading its chemistry, fleeing its absence. But in my constant migration, in my school's endless circulation through the pools and channels of the flats, we write our own kind of history—a map of where the water goes, traced in living tissue, as meaningful in its own way as any mark scratched on stone.

When the strange smooth creature appears—the one without proper skin, without the patterns that mark it as *here*—we sense it only as a disturbance, a new turbulence in the water where it stands. Some of my school flee from its alien chemistry. But I am curious in my simple way, and I taste the water that has touched its feet. Iron, yes, but wrong-iron, not from here. Salt that speaks of places where water gathers in bodies too vast to cross. And something else, something I have no reference for—the chemical signature of a creature that comes from where the sound is not always thunder, where the sun is not always hidden.

The counter tries to speak to it, and we feel the electromagnetic changes in the water as its skin pulses through the patterns. The smooth creature does not respond, cannot respond, stands there like a particularly odd stone. But it *disturbs* the water in ways that suggest thought, suggests intention. Its weight shifts. Its chemistry changes—stress hormones, perhaps, or whatever equivalent its alien body produces.

And I wonder, in whatever way a ribbon of muscle and nerve can wonder: What would it be like to be a creature that can choose not to move? That can stand still while the water drains away and not die? That can exist outside the desperate rhythm of pool to pool to pool?

But the wondering passes. The pool is shallowing. The school is gathering, preparing for the next migration. Soon we will writhe across wet stone toward the next depression, hoping the rain continues, hoping the distance is not too far, hoping the crawlers do not catch too many of us in our vulnerability. This is what we do. This is all we do. We are the ones who swim, who flee, who breed, who feed the rest. We are the quick pulse beneath the slow thoughts of the counters, the liquid urgency that keeps this mineral world alive.

The smooth creature will leave, probably. Return to wherever water is not precious and sound is not constant. The counters will continue their patient tallying, their teaching, their slow transformation into stone. And we will be here, moving through the narrow margin between rain and drought, carrying the chemical memory of every pool, every mineral gradient, every strange foot that has ever stood in our water.

We are always here, even when we are not. Every dried ribbon-swimmer on the stone was once like me, thinking these simple thoughts, carrying these chemical maps. And when I stop, when the pool drains too fast or the next one is too far, another will take my place, indistinguishable from me, carrying forward the same urgencies, the same knowledge, the same purpose.

The counters say *Here* with their skin and stones. We say it with our movement, our breeding, our dying. We say it by being the thing that cannot stop moving in a world of things that cannot stop counting.

The water tastes of approaching rain. The school tightens, ready. Soon we will flow across the stone like living water, like thought itself pouring from pool to pool, carrying the minerals of this ancient place in our tissues, feeding the patient minds that make their marks above us.

This is enough. This has always been enough.

The thunder rolls on, meaning nothing, explaining nothing. We swim beneath it, quick and mortal and necessary, writing our part in the long, slow story of a world that counts its own days.
