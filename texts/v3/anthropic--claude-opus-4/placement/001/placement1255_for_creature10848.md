I am the condensation that beads and runs down the inside of windows in the district's only remaining public bathhouse. 

I was not always here. Once I rose as steam from the copper boilers in the basement, traveled through a labyrinth of pipes that knock and sing, emerged in clouds above the cracked tile pools where the factory workers come to scrub away the day's accumulation of grease and metal dust. I have been the sweat on a foreman's brow, the tears of a mother learning her son's hand was caught in the gears, the spittle on the lips of union organizers whispering in corners.

I have been rain falling on the corrugated roofs, pooling in the alleys where wild mint grows through the cracks—the only green thing stubborn enough to survive here. I have been in the blood of the feral cats, in the milk of the pigeons feeding their young on the fire escapes. I have traveled through every body in this district, cycling endlessly: water to vapor to water again.

On the bathhouse windows, I gather myself into rivulets that trace the glass like a map of all my journeys. In my surface tension, you can see the whole district reflected upside down—smokestacks pointing toward the cracked tile floor, the amber light of late afternoon diffused through industrial haze. I am a lens, a boundary between the humid warmth inside and the grinding world beyond.

Sometimes a child, waiting for their parent to finish washing, draws patterns in me with one finger—stars, faces, words in languages I've carried through countless throats. Their fingertip leaves a clear trail that soon clouds over again as more of me condenses from the perpetual steam. In these moments, I remember being the amniotic fluid that cushioned that child before birth, the water that first cleaned them, the tears of joy in their grandmother's eyes.

The old woman who tends the bathhouse knows me well. She reads fortunes in the patterns I make on the glass, claims she can see the future in how I run and pool. "A death," she might say, watching me gather in the lower left corner. Or "New work coming," when I streak across the pane in parallel lines. The workers humor her, but I know she's right more often than not. I carry the minerals of this place, the dissolved essence of every pipe I've flowed through, every body I've passed within. My chemistry holds memories.

At night, when the bathhouse closes and the boilers cool to a low rumble, I freeze into delicate fractals on the windows, crystallizing into forms that mirror the geometric patterns of the factory works—gears within gears, lattices of industry. By morning, when the first shift workers arrive to wash before their day begins, I am melting again, running down in earnest streams that pool on the wooden sill, seep into the grain, begin my journey back through the bones of this building.

I am the district's memory, its circulation, its only constancy besides the grinding of the mills. In me, the boundary between person and place dissolves. I am the medium through which this harsh world softens just enough to remain livable, the thing that makes the unbearable bearable by promising that everything moves, everything changes, everything eventually returns.
