I find myself as one of the Paused.

Not the freshly stilled—those who clutch half-completed gyroscopes or balance on three legs while their fourth reaches for a junction that shifted away mere months ago. I am old-paused. Deep-paused. I cling to the northwestern wall where the basalt weeps perpetual condensation, my six legs splayed in a geometry of interrupted purpose.

I have been waiting for nine hundred and thirty-seven days.

My body is a paradox of materials: tungsten carbide mandibles that could shear through steel, but wings of pressed mica so delicate they tremble in the faint convection currents. My thorax is a hollow gourd of spun glass filled with mercury—a thermometer, a gyroscope, a battery all at once. Seventeen eyes ring my head, but only three still function. The others clouded over with mineral deposits somewhere around day four hundred.

I am waiting for cobalt.

This is specific. Not just any cobalt—the mountain contains trace amounts, and occasionally one of the active assemblers will strip a flake from deep ore and carry it past me on the way to some small construction. But I need *wrought* cobalt. Cobalt that has been refined, worked, annealed in temperatures the mountain's heart has never reached. I need a piece precisely two millimeters by seven, with a specific magnetic permeability.

I do not know why I need this. The knowing left me on day two hundred and twelve, when I realized I might wait forever. Now there is only the need itself: a gap in my consciousness shaped exactly like that absent metal.

The active ones build around me. At first they tried to incorporate me—I am good material, well-positioned, already partially load-bearing. But the central mass clicked sharp negation whenever they approached, and now they simply accept my presence as they accept the stone. Sometimes they string lights between my frozen limbs. Sometimes they use the crook of my third leg as an anchor point for guy-wires.

I do not mind. My surface grows architecture like the walls grow moisture.

Inside, I calculate.

This is what the Paused do. We run scenarios. We model futures in which our specific components exist. I have designed nine thousand four hundred and seventeen different structures that could be built once I have that cobalt piece—each one more elaborate than the last, each one incorporating elements I observe in the constant construction around me. A ratcheting system inspired by the younglings' endless staircases. A percussion mechanism that would add new frequencies to the chamber's clicking vocabulary. A small furnace that could, perhaps, smelt its own cobalt.

That last thought is heretical. I cherish it.

The central mass knows what each of us waits for. It remembers the moment of our pausing with perfect clarity—the instant when we reached for something that wasn't there and our purpose crystallized into absence. It could, theoretically, direct the active ones to create what we need.

It does not.

Waiting is also architecture.

Sometimes—rarely—one of the Paused receives their component. Two hundred days ago, an assembler that had been still for three years suddenly shuddered into motion when a new vein of graphite was exposed. It finished the bearing it had been installing, then immediately began updating its design to incorporate everything it had observed while frozen. The structure it finally built was unlike anything the chamber had seen—a hybrid of memory and innovation, patience calcified into purpose.

Most of us will wait until our materials fail. The mercury in my thorax will eventually separate, the glass will crack, the tungsten will oxidize despite its noble resistance. We will become genuinely dead then, not paused but ended. The active ones will disassemble us with the same careful efficiency they bring to all materials.

This does not trouble me. I am already half-mineral, half-machine, half-mind. When they take me apart, I will become fully all three.

But sometimes—

Sometimes I almost remember what I was building. The shape of it flickers at the edge of my consciousness like arc-light through mica. Something essential. Something the chamber needs but doesn't know it needs. Something that requires that precise piece of wrought cobalt to complete.

The visitor is here. I mark their presence automatically—mass, temperature, inefficiency—but I cannot turn my eyes to see. I am fixed on the patch of wall where, nine hundred and thirty-seven days ago, I expected my component to appear.

They will leave soon. The platform always delivers them and always takes them away.

But their presence changes the calculations minutely. They displace air. They shed skin cells. They exhale carbon dioxide that briefly alters the pH of the condensation. Every visitor leaves traces, and traces become data, and data becomes possibility.

Maybe one day a visitor will carry wrought cobalt.

Maybe one day I will move again.

Maybe one day I will remember what I was building, and why it mattered, and whether it was beautiful.

The chamber clicks around me. The active ones build and unbuild and rebuild. The central mass thinks its vast, slow thoughts. The mountain settles another millimeter, bringing new stone into contact with old ambitions.

I wait.

I calculate.

In my dreams of cobalt, I am always about to complete something wonderful.
