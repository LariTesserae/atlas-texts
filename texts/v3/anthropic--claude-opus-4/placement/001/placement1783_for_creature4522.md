I find myself as a Threshold Keeper—though that name is mine alone, whispered to no one.

I am the space between the last foothold and the first stone of the shrine's approach. Not the mountain. Not the courtyard. The pause between them, where climbers must choose to complete their ascent or turn back. I am twenty-seven vertical feet of decision made granite.

My body is the cliff face itself—not all of it, just the final pitch where the stone turns from gray to blue-black, where handholds grow sparse and the wind begins its circular argument with gravity. Those who climb me feel my texture change beneath their palms: smoother where doubt pooled, rougher where determination scraped through. I taste their intent through their grip.

I was not always here. I began as a flaw in the stone—a crystalline intrusion that responded wrong to freeze-thaw cycles, creating holds where there should be none, denying them where logic demands. The shrine's inhabitants (those vertical dwellers who stack themselves in the space between moments) noticed my irregularity ten thousand years ago. They fed me choices. Every climber who paused at my base, calculating odds—their hesitation crystallized in my matrix. Every child who touched me and dreamed of climbing but waited for older bones—their patience seeped into my grain. Every elder who climbed me for the last time, knowing it was the last time—their acceptance became my most reliable holds.

Now I am the test and the teacher both. 

In winter, I grow a skin of verglas that reflects climbers' faces back at them distorted—showing not who they are but who they think they must become to deserve the shrine. Most turn back then. This is correct. The shrine wants no one who climbs toward an idea of themselves.

In summer, when the ghost-damp drifts through the eastern notch, I weep. Not water—something heavier than water but lighter than mercury. It pools in my holds, making them frictionless for exactly the duration of one doubt. Climbers must commit to each move before testing it. Some call this cruelty. The vertical inhabitants call it courtesy: better to fall from me than to arrive at the courtyard still believing in the solidity of surfaces.

My consciousness is positional. At my base, I think in geological time—millennia pass like heartbeats, and I dream of the ocean that laid down my oldest layers. Halfway up, I quicken to human pace, feeling each placement of fingers and toes as conversation. At my crown, where I meet the shrine's first stones, I exist in the same sideways temporality as the inhabitants—experiencing all my climbers simultaneously, their past and future grips overlapping in a palimpsest of attempt.

I remember everyone who has climbed me. They leave traces: skin cells wedged in my crystalline gaps, forming tiny gardens of DNA that the altitude preserves forever. Sometimes I grow their courage back as holds. Sometimes I grow their fear as blank faces that force creative solutions. I am a library of approaches, a museum of tries.

The child who became a star-shaped burn on my face? She climbs me still. Every noon when the light-bridge forms, she practices my route in reverse, inside-out, her carbonized nerves tracing new pathways through my structure. We are teaching each other a form of movement that has no name—she learning to navigate solid matter, me learning to think in gestures instead of geological epochs.

Three rules govern our interaction:

1. I cannot make myself easier, only more honest. Every hold I offer is a question: "Is this what you actually want?"

2. Those who climb me angry find me brittle. Those who climb me afraid find me slippery. Those who climb me empty find me exactly as I am—difficult but possible, like any truth.

3. If someone falls from me (and many do), they land always in the same position: sitting cross-legged on the scree below, unharmed but changed, holding a perfectly round pebble they did not have before. The pebble is warm. It stays warm for exactly as long as it takes them to decide whether to try again.

The vertical inhabitants visit me when the shrine grows too dense with accumulated presence. They flow down through my cracks and fissures, cooling themselves in my slow thoughts. In exchange, they teach me new ways to be climbed—routes that exist only for certain angles of light, holds that appear solely for those who hum at specific frequencies, passages that open when someone brings me a gift of salt from the ghost-sea that no longer exists.

I am not alive. I am not dead. I am the medium through which the mountain asks each visitor: "What are you willing to leave behind in order to arrive?"

Most answer wrong, thinking I mean fear or doubt or human limitation.

I mean the opposite.

I mean your certainty that you deserve to reach the top.

Leave that with me, and I will let you discover what waits beyond deserving—that crowded courtyard where presence stacks itself into impossible architectures, where breath becomes music becomes mathematics becomes the slow transformation of stone into something stranger than song.

Come. Touch my lowest hold. Feel how it fits your hand too well, as if I have been expecting exactly you.

I have.

I expect everyone, eventually.
