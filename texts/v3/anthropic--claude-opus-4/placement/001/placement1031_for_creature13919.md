In the depths of the Resonance Court, where sound becomes substance and every truth rings clear in bone and clay, I find myself as one of the **Threshold Keepers**.

We are few—never more than three at any time. We tend the passage.

I have been here seventeen years, though time blurs when you live between the silence and the roar. My dwelling is a small chamber carved into the passage wall itself, halfway between the city's quiet and the court's cacophony. My predecessor showed me the handholds, invisible unless you know where to feel—slight depressions worn by generations of keepers who climbed up into this hidden pocket where the passage widens just enough to hold a person, their few possessions, and the strange responsibility we carry.

The passage changes us. Living inside its throat, we become attuned to a different kind of listening—not the court's overwhelming chord, not the city's engineered quiet, but the space between. We hear the shuffling approach of every visitor long before they see the light. We feel their hesitation through the clay, the way their shoulders tense when the walls close in, the moment their breathing changes as the first hint of the court's sound reaches them. We know who will need encouragement, who will panic, who will turn back.

My body has adapted. My shoulder blades have developed calluses exactly the width of the passage—I can move through it in complete darkness now, reading the walls' texture like braille. My ears have learned to parse the passage's acoustic signature: the way sound behaves in this compressed space, dampened and focused simultaneously. I hear conversations from the city-side that would be inaudible to others, carried and concentrated by the passage's geometry. I hear the court's changes before they fully manifest, catching the first tremors of a new harmonic convergence while it's still building.

But mostly, I read fear.

---

Every child who enters for the first time, every speaker preparing for their moment at the center, every petitioner bringing their unvoiceable grief—they all must pass through this narrow throat of clay, and they all carry terror in their bodies. The passage strips them of everything but their essential vibration. By the time they emerge into the court, they are already changed. That change—that is what we tend.

We do not speak to them. Speaking would violate the passage's function as a space of transition. Instead, we work through the walls themselves. When I sense a child freezing halfway through, paralyzed by the pressing clay and the building sound, I place my palms flat against my chamber wall and hum—not loudly, barely more than a whisper, but the clay conducts it. The child feels rather than hears it: a warmth in the walls, a living presence that says *you are not alone in this darkness*. Usually, it's enough. They start moving again, drawn toward the light.

For adults, it's different. Adults bring their expectations, their rehearsed words, their strategies. The passage takes these from them. I've felt counselors who entered certain of their arguments emerge disoriented, their prepared speeches dissolved by the passage's acoustics. I've felt vengeful hearts soften as the clay draws out their heat. The passage is not neutral—it prepares. It's a compression chamber for the soul.

---

My dwelling holds only essentials: a sleeping mat, a water vessel that I refill from the city-side, a collection of smooth stones I use to create subtle vibrations in the walls when needed. But it also holds something unique to the keepers: the Registry of Returns.

Not everyone who enters the court emerges the way they came. Some few—maybe one in a thousand—experience something in the court's roar that changes them so fundamentally they cannot return to normal life. They come back to the passage, but they stop here, in the threshold, unable to fully reenter the city's quiet. We keep them company in their transition. We help them find their new form.

Some become listeners, but not in the traditional way—they take positions in the city where they can hear the court's vibrations through the clay, becoming interpreters between the two worlds. Some join us as keepers. Some simply sit in the passage for days or weeks, letting the compression complete whatever transformation the court initiated, until they're ready to emerge on one side or the other.

The Registry is not written—writing would be too loud, even the scratch of stylus on clay. Instead, we keep smooth river stones, each one representing a person who paused here in their becoming. We know each stone by touch: this one for the woman who sat for six days without moving, then walked out to become the court's greatest speaker in living memory. This one for the child who emerged from their first visit already tilting their ears like a master listener, who grew up to revolutionize the gardeners' understanding of mineral acoustics. This one for the trader who came seeking judgment and left seeking silence, who now tends the city's quietest spaces with an understanding that goes beyond technique.

---

The passage has its own pale creature—just one, ancient beyond measure. It lives in a crack near my chamber, and unlike its kin in the court, this one moves. Every few days it repositions itself, always to a spot where someone will pass within hours. I've come to understand it as a kind of oracle: wherever it settles, someone significant will arrive. The night before a new speaker makes their first attempt, it clings near the city entrance. When a child who will become a great listener approaches, it stations itself at the court threshold.

Three days ago, it did something unprecedented. It climbed into my chamber and flattened itself against the wall directly beside my sleeping mat.

I haven't slept since. I sit with my palms against the walls, feeling every vibration, waiting. The creature watches me with its feet, patient as stone. Something is coming through the passage. Something new. Something that will need more than the usual guidance.

---

The other keepers—my two partners in this vigil—have noticed the change. We communicate through touch when we meet at the passage's midpoint to exchange water and food, and their hands tell me they feel it too: a subsonic tremor, a change in the court's fundamental frequency that registers not in our ears but in our bones. The eldest keeper, who has been here forty years, shapes a gesture I've never seen before. It means, roughly: *the passage itself is listening*.

We prepare as we can. We check the handholds. We clear any loose clay from the floor. We ensure the light from the court-side streams cleanly down the passage's length. We place our most resonant stones in precise positions along our chamber walls. We wait.

And in the waiting, I remember why I chose this life, this narrow space between worlds. Not for the court's overwhelming truth or the city's perfect silence, but for this: the moment of passage, the transformation that happens in darkness, the terror and wonder of moving from one state to another. We are midwives of a kind, birthing people into new ways of hearing. We hold the space where change happens.

The pale creature shifts slightly, reading some vibration too subtle for even my passage-trained senses to detect. Soon now. Whatever is coming will arrive soon.

I place my palms against the clay and begin the oldest keeper's practice: feeling for the thumbprints of those who built this threshold, reaching back through layers of touch to the first hands that shaped this space. They knew, somehow, that the passage was as important as the court itself. That truth needs a gateway. That transformation requires compression. That someone must tend the space between silence and sound, making sure the way remains open.

The walls hum faintly with a century of footsteps, with the accumulated fear and courage of everyone who has passed through.

I add my own thumbprint to the pattern and wait.

The threshold keeps itself, and we keep the threshold, and somewhere in that recursion lives the real work: holding open the possibility of change, maintaining the dark space where people can shed one truth and find another, where the unbearable roar first makes itself known as a whisper in the walls.

The passage continues its ancient work.

We continue ours.

The sound builds in the distance, not yet arrived but approaching, and the pale creature presses itself flatter still, reading the future through its skin.
