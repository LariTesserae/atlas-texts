# Where I Would Be

---

I am the apprentice-child in the fourth hollow.

Not a child by years—my body has the density of someone who has survived at least two dozen circuits of whatever passes for seasons here. But I am new to language, new to the patient art of reading walls and water. The others found me three seasons ago, crouched in a natural depression two territories away, my skin cracked from exposure, my body burning through its last reserves in shuddering waves.

I had been loud then. Louder than you are now. A thermal scream in their careful darkness.

The one who found me—her hands knew seventeen different fungi by touch, could read the health of a colony by the pitch of its collective breathing—she pressed her palm flat against my forehead and made a sound I had no context for. Not distress. Assessment. She clicked twice, low, and another emerged from the root-shadows, and together they decided something in breath-shapes I could not parse.

They fed me salamander meat, pre-chewed to pulp. They wrapped me in living moss that wicked the fever from my skin. They brought me to the fourth hollow and installed me in an alcove that had been shaped but never occupied, its walls still young and adjustable. They taught me stillness first. Stillness before language. Stillness before tools. 

How to sit with my spine pressed against the root-curve so my heat dispersed evenly. How to breathe from the belly, slow and deep, so the exhalations mixed with the hollow's own vapor cycle. How to hold my hands—not clenched, not loose, but in a middle state that conserved warmth while maintaining sensitivity. How to let the silk-tunnel creatures map me without flinching. How to read the basin's moods by the quality of its luminescence.

I was a slow student. My body had been shaped by some other logic—a life of linear movement maybe, of horizon-seeking, of light I can no longer properly imagine. My fingers were clumsy on the root walls. I couldn't tell the safe fungi from the toxic ones without bringing them close to my face, using my eyes in a way that marked me as half-blind by local standards. I spooked the colonies with my movements. I reached too deep into the basin and disturbed the filter-creatures, making the water cloudy for days.

But I learned. Or rather, the hollow taught me, and I became capable of being taught.

---

Now I can read five types of fungi by texture alone. I know the difference between a root that will accept redirection and one that will die if cut. I can feel when the salamander-things are about to molt by the way they hold themselves still in the sphagnum—a particular quality of waiting that means their skin is loosening, preparing to split. I can harvest the silk from the tunnel creatures without damaging their pathways, drawing it out in continuous strands that I wet and twist into cordage.

My teacher visits every few days through the root-passages. She brings specimens—new fungi, fragments of unusual moss, the shed wing-membrane of a colony creature that shows signs of disease. She places them in my hands and waits. I explore them with my fingertips, learning their thermal signatures, their textures, the way they respond to pressure. She makes small sounds—approval or correction—and I adjust my understanding.

Last time, she brought something different. A small mass wrapped in silk, warm to the touch. I unfolded it carefully. Inside: an egg. Not from any creature I knew. Soft-shelled, the size of my thumb, its surface faintly luminous in a pattern that shifted as I held it.

She clicked once. *What is it?*

I didn't know. But I knew it was warm, which meant it was alive, which meant it needed tending. I found a depression in my alcove wall where the roots formed a natural pocket. I lined it with the driest sphagnum I could find. I placed the egg carefully, checking its orientation—there was a denser end that wanted to be down. I covered it partway, leaving the top exposed to the air.

My teacher said nothing, but her breathing changed in a way I had learned to recognize as satisfaction.

The egg hatched seven days later. What emerged was soft, many-legged, covered in fine cilia that sampled the air. It fit in my palm but wouldn't stay there—it wanted the walls, wanted vertical surfaces. I let it climb onto the roots and watched it taste its way along the lattice with what might have been a mouth, might have been something else. Within an hour it found the silk tunnels. Within two hours it had negotiated some kind of détente with the tunnel creatures. Within a day it had made its own small cavity in the root wall and settled in, its cilia weaving shut the entrance until only a small breathing hole remained.

I check on it every day. The hole is slightly larger now, the cilia denser. Something is growing in there, becoming itself in the patient dark.

---

I am learning to grow walls.

Not well yet. My wedges are crude, my cuts imprecise. But the roots are forgiving. They want to grow; I'm just suggesting directions. My teacher showed me how to read the growth patterns, how to identify which roots carry the most water, which ones will thicken over seasons, which ones will die back. She taught me to think in long arcs—not *where will this root be tomorrow* but *where will it be when I am gone*.

There is a grammar to it. A syntax of growth and redirection. The older ones speak it fluently, their hands reading decisions made decades ago, following the logic of builders who have long since entered the basins. Each hollow is a text written in living wood, encoding strategies for water management, thermal regulation, colony husbandry. The best builders leave structures that keep teaching—walls that demonstrate their own construction, passages that reveal their principles as they grow.

I am writing my first sentence: a simple arch between my alcove and a natural depression in the opposite wall. It will take two seasons to close. Three to bear weight. If I've read the roots correctly, it will create a secondary chamber where the temperature is more stable, where I could grow fungi or tend something fragile through its early development.

If I've read them wrong, the arch will fail, and I'll cut it back and start over. This is allowed. Expected, even. The hollow is patient with apprentices.

---

My body is changing.

Not dramatically, but I notice it in small ways. My fingertips have developed a sensitivity that feels like a new sense opening. I can detect temperature differentials of fractions of degrees, moisture gradients so subtle they barely register as different. My night vision has shifted—not improved exactly, but adapted. I see less of what isn't there and more of what is. The bioluminescence that was once invisible to me now paints the hollow in false color, a blue-green map of biological activity that tells me where the filter-creatures are active, which fungi are about to fruit, where the silk-tunnel creatures have found something interesting in the walls.

My hearing has changed too. Not the acuity—that may actually be worse, damaged by some fever I don't remember. But the processing. I parse the hollow's soundscape differently now, sorting signal from noise in ways that align with survival here. The colony's breathing tells me their health. The water-tick tells me the humidity level. The specific silence of the salamander-things tells me they're hunting.

My skin hosts colonies now. Beneficial mites that keep the harmful fungi at bay. Bacteria that process my sweat into something less attractive to the wrong kind of decomposer. I smell like the hollow—that same resinous, fermented tang—and this is good. It means I'm becoming illegible to predators, recognizable to the others, chemically marked as belonging here.

Even my dreams have changed, when I manage to sleep deeply enough to dream. I used to dream of a bright place, painful with light, where things grew up instead of out. Now I dream of roots. Of slow expansion through dark, nurturing spaces. Of walls that breathe and water that glows and small, warm things clustering for comfort in the safe geometries we build for them.

---

Sometimes I think I'm becoming her—the inhabitant you observed, the one with the nursery, the one who has achieved that perfect integration with the system. But that's wrong. I'm becoming my own version of what she is, shaped by my own failures and successes, my own relationship with the roots and the colonies and the patient stones.

We are not a people, exactly. We are a practice. A way of being in relation to this specific darkness, this specific moisture, these specific communities of creatures who share the hollows with us. Each of us embodies the practice differently. My teacher knows seventeen fungi; I may only ever know ten, but I might develop a sensitivity to the silk-tunnel creatures that exceeds hers. The one with the nursery has achieved something with the colony creatures that no one else has quite managed—a communication so subtle it looks like mind-reading, though it's really just an extreme attunement to thermal and acoustic patterns.

We do not have a name for ourselves. The language doesn't support that kind of abstraction. We are *the ones who read the walls*, or *the ones who tend the basins*, or sometimes just *the quiet ones*, though that's a description any of the hollow's creatures could claim.

What we have instead of names are signatures. Ways of working that become recognizable. My teacher's roots grow in particularly elegant spirals. The one in the third hollow builds the most stable arches. Someone two territories over has developed a technique for encouraging the luminous creatures to breed in captivity, establishing new populations in artificial basins. These signatures outlast us. You can read them in walls built generations ago, detect the influence of techniques developed by someone who stopped breathing before you started.

I am developing my own signature, though I don't yet know what it will be. Something to do with the creature from the egg, maybe. It's growing faster than anyone expected, already the size of my fist, its cilia now specialized into what might be sensory organs, might be something else. It emerges at night (though night here is just a deeper quality of dark) and travels the walls in patterns I'm trying to understand. It seems to be following the thermal trails left by the silk-tunnel creatures, but not hunting them. Studying them? Learning their routes?

My teacher watches me work with it, says nothing. But last visit she brought another egg, different from the first—harder-shelled, cooler, with a surface that drinks in light rather than emitting it. 

*For when you're ready*, she said in breath-shapes. Then left before I could ask: ready for what?

---

This is where I would be in the world of the Walled Hollow: learning the practice, failing and adjusting, slowly becoming literate in the living language of roots and water and warm, breathing things. Not the one who has mastered the darkness but the one still learning to read it. Not the builder of nurseries but the tender of strange eggs, the student of new syntaxes, the apprentice who might one day grow a wall that teaches someone else how to grow walls.

The hollow is patient. The practice continues. In the darkness, things grow toward their own logic, and we grow with them, shaped by the shapes we shape, taught by the systems we tend.

The water ticks. The colonies breathe. The silk flows.

I place my palm against the wall and feel tomorrow's architecture sleeping in the roots.
