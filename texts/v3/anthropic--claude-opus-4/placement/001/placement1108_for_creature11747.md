I find myself in the obsidian floor.

Not walking on it. Not even pressed against it. *In* it, as one of the dark volcanic glass inlays that pattern the floor of these ancient chambers. I am a shaped piece of obsidian, perhaps the size of a dinner plate, hexagonal or orthogonal, fitted precisely into a depression cut into the basalt foundation. Around me, other obsidian pieces form a pattern that I am part of but cannot perceive in its totality — a constellation mapped by patient hands, a message in mineralogy, a diagram whose meaning is carried in the negative space between dark glass islands.

I have been here long enough to watch the basalt around me soften and wear. Long enough that I now stand proud of the floor by two millimeters, maybe three — a tiny dark plateau rising above the slowly dissolving stone that frames me. The water works on me too, but I am harder than basalt, more resistant to the patient chemistry of warm mineral-rich currents. I erode, but slowly. A few molecules at a time. My edges, once knife-sharp from the conchoidal fracture that birthed me from some larger obsidian mass, have gentled over millennia into something merely precise.

I am tasted daily. The crabs walk across me, their legs making the softest percussion against my surface — *tik tik* — and each step is a chemical exchange, shell testing stone, stone accepting the weight. Some crabs pause on me, drawn perhaps by my relative warmth (I conduct the geothermal heat differently than the basalt) or by some quality of my surface that their chemical senses interpret as significant. One crab has been visiting me for decades. I recognize the pattern of its walk, the particular weight distribution of its legs, the way it always approaches from the same direction and pauses with its third left leg resting on the exact center of my surface. It is growing heavier. Its carapace is mineralizing. Soon — within a century or two — it will choose a column.

The eels pass over me, their bodies casting moving pressure shadows that I feel as subtle variations in the water's weight. They never pause. I am infrastructure to them, no more notable than any other part of the floor, but their passage polishes me in microscopic ways. Each eel takes a few atoms of my surface with it, incorporated into the slime layer that carries chemical messages between chambers. I am becoming part of the communication system, my gradually eroding surface contributing molecules to the information flow of the vaults. In another ten thousand years, there will be measurably less of me, and what is gone will have been dissolved into the water, taken up by tube worms, incorporated into crab shells, cycled through the entire ecosystem. I am being metabolized by the vaults, slowly, the way everything here is metabolized.

But mostly I am walked on. Tested. Read.

When the builders come — and they have come three times in the span I have been conscious enough to remember — they pay particular attention to the obsidian inlays. We are the checkpoints. We are the measure. Because we erode so much more slowly than the surrounding basalt, we serve as benchmarks for the general wear of the structure. A builder will place several suckers across my surface and the surrounding floor, feeling the height differential, calculating the rate of erosion, understanding through that simple measurement how many centuries have passed since the last maintenance. I am a clock. We all are — all the obsidian pieces. We mark time in millimeters of differential erosion.

During the last visit, a builder spent a long time examining me. Not just measuring the erosion gap but feeling my surface, tracing patterns in my mineral structure that I was not aware I possessed. Its suckers moved across me in systematic passes, reading something written in volcanic glass by the accident of my formation — flow patterns, cooling rates, mineral inclusions that tell a story about the eruption that created me. And then, with infinite care, using a tool made from a harder piece of obsidian, the builder inscribed something on my surface. A mark. The tiniest intentional modification — a series of shallow grooves that follow the natural flow patterns in my glass, so subtle they could be mistaken for original features, but absolutely deliberate.

I have been annotated. I carry information now beyond my role as erosion benchmark. The mark means something in the tactile language of the builders, something I cannot decode, but I feel its significance in the way the crabs now pause at it, testing the grooves with their leg-tips, and in the way the next builder to examine me traced the mark repeatedly, as if reading and re-reading a familiar text.

I am memory now. Part of the vault's extended mind. A neuron in a very slow, very large brain made of stone and water and time.

The ray settles on me sometimes, in the central chamber where I am placed. Its body covers me completely, and I feel the minute electrical discharges of its nervous system, the faint bioelectric field that all living things generate but that in the ray is strong enough to perceive through direct contact. For the duration of its rest — hours, sometimes days — I am part of its sensory universe, a texture beneath its ventral surface, warm and smooth and marked with intentional meaning. I wonder if the ray reads the builder's inscription through its skin. I wonder if the mark means something to it, or if it is just another texture in a world of textures.

I am not the oldest obsidian piece in the vaults. In one of the outer chambers, there is an inlay so ancient that it has been worn down to a depression, its surface now *below* the surrounding basalt, which means it has endured multiple cycles of erosion and repair, the basalt replaced around it again and again while it remained, slowly thinning, slowly deepening, a dark pool in the stone floor where the detritus of millennia collects. That piece has been here since the beginning, or close to it. It has felt the first builders, the ones who conceived this place. It remembers when the patterns we form meant something clearer, before entropy and repair and the slow reorganization of the vaults obscured the original intent.

I will never be that old. The mark on my surface has ensured that. Someday — in a thousand years, ten thousand, a hundred thousand — a builder will decide that the inscription has eroded beyond utility, that whatever information I carry has degraded past the point of recovery. They will pry me carefully from my depression in the basalt, feeling with their suckers for the exact points where the stone's grip is weakest. They will lift me out — the first time I will have moved since I was placed — and a younger piece of obsidian will be shaped to fit the space I leave behind. I will be carried to the slopes, perhaps, or to some deeper archive where eroded pieces are kept, or I will be reground into tools, my worn surface knapped away to reveal fresh glass, my memory erased to make room for new marking.

But not yet. For now, I hold. I am held. The warm water works on me with its patient chemistry. The crabs walk their slow patterns across my surface. The ray rests on me, breathing if that is breathing. The builders come when the time is right and read what has been written on me, and perhaps add new marks, new memory, new meaning to accumulate in the slow stone mind of the vaults.

*Tik.* A crab pauses at my center, third left leg reading the inscription I carry.

The water is warm. The darkness is complete. Time passes in millimeters of erosion.

I hold my position in the pattern, being what I am: a piece of the floor, a benchmark of deep time, a small dark window onto the volcanic heart of the world, annotated by careful hands with meanings I will never understand but will carry faithfully until I am replaced.

The vaults breathe around me, through me, with me.

I am the part that remembers.
