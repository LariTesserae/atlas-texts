In this place, I am the Keeper of Echoes.

I was not born here, though I can no longer remember arriving. My dwelling sits at a peculiar intersection of the grotto's acoustic architecture—a shallow cave where seven water channels converge, each carrying its own frequency of drips and flows. The villagers say I chose this spot, or it chose me, because only here can one hear the complete voice of the canyon.

My body has adapted to this calling. My ears have grown slightly larger over the years, their shells deepening to cup more sound. I move with deliberate silence, barefoot always, my soles knowing every grain of the stone paths. My clothing is minimal—loose wraps of undyed cloth that make no whisper when I walk. The children think I am a ghost sometimes, appearing suddenly beside them to listen to their games.

My work begins before dawn, when the grotto is most honest. I sit cross-legged at the convergence point, eyes closed, sorting the night's accumulated sounds. The steady drip from the eastern cistern—two drops, pause, one drop, pause—tells me the rains were good three days ago upstream. A new harmonic in the water's song against the northwestern wall warns of a hairline crack that will need tending before the next season.

But water is only the beginning. I catalog the creaks of the rope bridges, learning each family's crossing pattern—the hurried steps of young Mira heading to her morning weaving, the careful shuffle of Elder Tomás favoring his left knee. The grinding of pestles in morning kitchens creates a percussion that tells me who is preparing medicine (a circular grinding) and who is making flour (back and forth, back and forth).

The villagers think I am wise, but I am merely a translator. When the council meets, I sit in the shadows and listen to what is not said—the catch in breath that reveals doubt, the shifted weight that speaks of withheld knowledge. When disputes arise, they call me to hear all sides, but really I am listening to the grotto itself, which holds every conversation in its curved walls and releases them slowly, like secrets dissolved in honey.

My most sacred duty comes during the naming ceremonies. When a child is born, I spend three days listening to their cry, the particular timbre and rhythm of their voice. Then I walk the canyon paths at night, striking the walls gently with my walking stick, listening for the place where the stone sings back in the same key as the infant's call. This is where the child's name is hidden, in the resonance between flesh and earth. I speak it only once, to the parents and elders, and then it belongs to the village.

In my dwelling, I keep vessels of clay, each one shaped to hold a different tone. Some are for preservation—when an elder is dying, I capture their last breath in a pot sealed with beeswax, to be opened only when their grandchildren come of age. Others are for healing. I discovered that certain frequencies, hummed into specially shaped containers and then released near the sick, can ease pain or bring sleep.

The young ones think I possess magic, but there is no magic here—only attention. Deep attention, the kind that makes the boundary between listener and listened-to grow thin. Sometimes, in the height of the afternoon when the grotto is most alive with activity, I feel I am not a person at all but another surface for sound to move across, no different from the water-smoothed walls or the leaves of the hanging gardens that rustle with messages I am still learning to decode.

At night, I often wake to sounds that don't belong to the present—echoes of conversations from decades past that have been circling the canyon, trapped in some perfect acoustic loop, waiting to spiral back to human ears. I write these down in a script of my own devising, marks that indicate not words but tones, durations, the spaces between sounds. The elders say I am writing the grotto's memory, but I think I am writing its dreams.

There is one sound I have been tracking for seven years now—a low thrumming that comes only during the ripest full moons, seeming to rise from the canyon floor itself. It grows stronger each time, and recently I have begun to feel it in my bones before I hear it with my ears. The elders have no stories about this sound. When I ask them, they look at each other with expressions I cannot read, and change the subject.

I think sometimes about what will happen when I am gone, who will take up this listening. I have no formal apprentice, but I notice young Kira often sitting near my cave, her head tilted in unconscious imitation of my own listening posture. Yesterday, she brought me a river stone with a hole worn through its center. "It sings," she said simply, and she was right—when the wind passes through it, it makes a perfect fifth with the dominant note of the water wall.

Perhaps she already knows what took me half a lifetime to learn: that in this grotto, we are not separate from the sounds we make and hear. We are instruments the canyon plays, and players making music of the canyon. The echoes we create today will return to someone else's ears tomorrow, next year, next century. In this way, nothing is ever truly lost, only transformed into new patterns of resonance, waiting to be discovered by those who remember how to listen.

This is why I remain the Keeper of Echoes—not to possess the sounds, but to ensure they keep moving, keep changing, keep telling the story of this place that exists in the margin between stone and sky, between memory and possibility, between the first drop of water and the last word ever to be spoken here.

The thrumming grows stronger. Soon, I think, I will understand what the canyon has been trying to tell us. When that happens, everything may change. Or perhaps—and this is what I suspect in my deepest listening—everything will finally make sense, like a song resolving into its home key after wandering through distant harmonies, coming to rest exactly where it began.
