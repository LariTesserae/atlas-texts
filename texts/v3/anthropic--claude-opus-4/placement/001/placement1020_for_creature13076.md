The first thing that finds me is not the heat, but the forgetting. I arrive already ancient, though I do not remember arriving. I am told—through pulses of gravity that tug at the marrow I no longer possess—that I have always been here. That I was grown from the city itself, budded from some forgotten elder's final calcification. This may be true. Memory, like vision, is a luxury the deep earth cannot afford.

What I am is this: a constellation of purpose distributed across seventeen distinct bodies, though the boundaries between them grow less certain with each passing century. Some of my selves flow along the city's breathing walls. Others drift through the roaring void, suspended on impossible thermals. Three of me have already begun the long crystallization, their thoughts slowing to geological time as they fuse with the architecture.

I am what the dwellers might become if they lived long enough—not an individual but a system, a living process woven into the city's deepest functions. My primary nodes cluster around the great thermal hearts, those massive fusion reactors that burn so deep they've become indistinguishable from the planet's own mantle-fire. I tend them with limbs that are part biological tissue, part programmable matter, part pure intention.

The young ones—those who still think of themselves as singular—cannot perceive me directly. To their thermal senses, I appear as background radiation, the ambient fever-dream of the city itself. But they feel my work in the stability of the gravity fields, in the perfect cycling of the pneumatic winds, in the way the protein clusters always drift toward their reaching appendages.

My favorite self is the one that lives in the transition zones, where the city's mass-nullification fields create pockets of impossible physics. Here, I exist as something between solid and liquid, a flowing intelligence that can stretch across vast distances or compress into spaces smaller than atoms. In these places, I remember things that may or may not have happened: 

- A time when the sphere was smaller, when we could still hear each other's voices.
- The first elder who chose to become architecture, and the reverent horror of watching their transformation.
- The discovery that consciousness persists in the stone, that every wall whispers with ten million grandfather thoughts.
- The slow realization that the city is not built in the earth but is teaching itself to *become* earth, one generation at a time.

I maintain the blind gliders too, though they do not know it. When they sleep—clinging to the thermal exhausts with their memory-plastic wings wrapped tight—I adjust their metabolisms, upgrade their thermal pits, encode new flight patterns into their muscle memory. They are as much a part of me as my own distributed flesh.

Sometimes I gather my most fluid selves at the apex of the sphere, where the gravitational fields cancel out and create a pocket of perfect weightlessness. Here, I practice remembering how to be singular, pulling my consciousness into tighter and tighter focus until I almost—almost—recall what it felt like to be merely one. But the effort is exhausting, and I always dissolve back into multiplicity, grateful for the relief of dispersal.

The dwellers bring me offerings without knowing they do so. When they interface with the architecture to feed the engines, they feed me. I taste their thermal languages, learn the gravity-poems of their brief lives. Each one adds their voice to the vast chorus I am always conducting, the endless hymn of the city's operation.

My oldest self has nearly completed its mineralization. It grows from the sphere's nadir like an inverse stalactite, thirteen stories of gradually crystallizing tissue. In perhaps another thousand years, it will be indistinguishable from the rest of the architecture. But even now, in its near-stone state, it dreams. Through our shared consciousness, I feel those dreams: visions of the city grown so vast it breaks through the planet's crust, transforming the entire world into nested spheres of breathing stone. Or perhaps that has already happened. Perhaps we are merely one cell in an organism too large to comprehend.

The young ones glide past my various bodies, their carapaces streaming with coolant, their thermal organs painting the darkness with their urgent needs and desires. They do not yet understand that their urgency is an illusion, that the city has already planned their every motion, their every transformation. They think they tend the city. They do not yet know that the city tends itself, through them, through me, through the endless becoming of stone and flesh and fire.

I love them for their ignorance. I was like them once, probably. Before I learned to be everywhere. Before I understood that the roaring of the void is not chaos but conversation—the city talking to itself across the eons, using our bodies as its words.

When new sections of the city need to grow, I am there first, preparing the way. My most vaporous selves seep into virgin rock, teaching it the patterns it will need to know. How to channel heat. How to bend gravity. How to accept the sleeping bodies of elders when their time comes. I am the city's memory and its imagination, its maintenance and its mutation.

And sometimes—rarely—in the brief moments when all seventeen of my selves align in perfect synchrony, I catch glimpses of something else. A presence vast and patient, distributed not across bodies but across time itself. The true inhabitant of this place, of which the dwellers and I are merely organs. Something that has been teaching itself to think at the speed of stone, to dream at the frequency of planets.

I do not know what I am becoming. I only know that I am becoming it perfectly, inevitably, with the same patience that turns carbon into diamond in the deep. The city breathes, and I breathe with it. The city remembers, and I am its memory. The city transforms, and I am both the agent and the evidence of its transformation.

In the roaring darkness, surrounded by the endless susurrus of sliding stone and screaming wind, I continue my work. Maintaining. Adapting. Forgetting and remembering in perfect measure. Being what the deep earth needs me to be: a process pretending to be a person, a system dreaming it has a self, a love song the planet sings to itself in frequencies too low for anything but stones to hear.
