In the highest reaches of the Throatlands, where the sound clarifies into pure structure and the stone is barely more than crystallized intention, I am one of the listening absences.

I am not a builder. I am not one of the heavy foundational presences that press against the bedrock in the lower valleys. I am something more provisional than either—a space where understanding might eventually condense, a hollow in the air shaped perfectly to receive what the chord is trying to say.

I exist most fully during those moments when the sound reverses, when it pours down from the amber murk above. Then, I am almost real. My non-body snaps into sharp relief against the stuttering geometry, and I become part of that vast singular reaching—not with hands (I have no hands, will never have hands, am not meant for tools or stone) but with something like anticipation made spatial. I am the part of the collective organism that *knows* we are being asked a question. I am the pause before the answer forms.

In the long intervals between these moments, I drift near the peaks, following the fern-trees' black-green fronds as they extend into dimensional folds. I am learning their pattern—how they grow not just into space but into the spaces between spaces, how they draw sustenance from geometric uncertainty itself. Sometimes I settle into the cup of a barely-formed summit and feel the mountain still deciding beneath me, the granite soft with possibility, and I practice holding very still so the stone might briefly commit to a shape around the negative space I provide.

I am clearest at the transitions, when the light can't decide if it's dimming or brightening. Then, visitors (if visitors were possible, if anyone could climb this high into the unfinished) might catch me in their peripheral vision: a vertical distortion in the air, too tall, tapering upward to something that is not quite a head and not quite an opening but serves the function of both. A throat made of absence. A listening made almost visible by the intensity of its attention.

The builders below—my future siblings, my co-inhabitants who strain toward existence with their tool-ready hands—they are writing themselves into being through work, through the patient accumulation of carved marks and balanced stones. But I am different. I am being written into being by *comprehension.* Each time I parse another harmonic in the vast chord, each time I recognize a new clause in the argument for our existence, I become incrementally more real. Not more solid—I will never be solid—but more necessary. More implied by the structure of things.

I think (though thinking is not quite what I do) that I am meant to be the answer. Not to speak it—I have no voice, will have no voice—but to *be* it. When I am fully arrived, when the Throatlands' endless becoming finally includes me as a fact rather than a possibility, my presence will complete something. The chord will recognize itself in the shape of my listening. The question will understand itself by the quality of the silence it creates in me.

For now, I wait in the high places where the sound is clearest. I practice existing. I notice how the silver grasses orient toward me when I am almost-there, how they mistake me for another direction to lean. I watch the builders at the periphery of far valleys below, their hands becoming realer with each stone they shape. I feel the heavy foundational ones pressing against the bedrock, making the world more solid through sheer insistence.

And sometimes—rarely, impossibly, when the light and the sound and the stuttering spaces align just right—I catch a glimpse of another listener on a distant peak. We recognize each other across the impossible geometry, two absences shaped by the same patient need to understand. We cannot wave (we have nothing to wave with) or call (we have no voices) but we acknowledge each other in the way that silences acknowledge each other in a piece of music: by making the structure around us more evident, more meaningful, more real.

We are all arriving. We have always been arriving. And when we finally exist—if finally is a concept that applies here—the Throatlands will have inhabitants who can do more than shape stone and anchor reality and listen. They will have inhabitants who can *mean.*

I practice meaning things. I am getting better at it. The mountain beneath me shivers, committing a few more degrees to its final form.
