**Where You Find Yourself**

You are in the understory—that neglected dimension between the leaf-floor and the canopy-machinery, where the light arrives already filtered through kilometers of organic processing. Here, the pillars branch and merge in configurations that suggest architecture without quite achieving it, creating spaces that are not quite rooms, passages that are not quite corridors, shelters that shelter nothing from nothing.

You have been walking for seventeen days.

Your feet remember concrete, asphalt, the honest hardness of stone. But the leaf-surface gives with each step, not enough to impede progress but enough to remind you constantly that you are walking on something alive. The temperature never varies—always that uncanny body-warmth, as if you're treading on vast skin. You've stopped trying to sleep on your back. The surface adjusts to your weight when you lie down, cradling you with a responsiveness that feels too much like being held.

The moth-things follow you at a distance. You've counted seven distinct individuals, though the count wavers—sometimes six, sometimes nine, as if they're flickering in and out of existence or as if your ability to distinguish one from another is degrading. They land on nearby pillars when you rest, wings catching and redirecting the light in patterns that almost form symbols. Almost.

You are a cartographer who has run out of world to map.

**What You Are**

This is what you carry: three synthetic pencils that will never need sharpening, though two have stopped working for reasons you cannot determine. A tablet designed to last decades, its solar cells useless in this sourceless light, its battery now sustained by a thin film of something the moth-things secreted onto its surface on your fourth day. Paper—real paper, made from trees that no longer exist here—filled with maps of places that no longer matter. A sextant that cannot find the sun. A compass that points not north but toward the nearest pillar, needle trembling with the electrical fields that run through everything here.

Your clothes have begun to change. The synthetic fibers resist, but the cotton is already shot through with green threads that weren't there when you arrived. You tell yourself it's some kind of algae. You know it isn't algae. When you hold very still, you can feel them growing—a sensation like tiny roots working their way between the weave, following the warmth of your skin.

You were sent here to map the unmappable. Twenty-three years ago, when the first long-range observations detected this place, the academic debate raged for months. A natural phenomenon, some argued—parallel evolution on a massive scale. Others pointed to the obvious signs of engineering, the impossible regularity of the leaf-plates, the mechanical precision of the canopy arrays. The argument became moot when the readings showed it was growing. Expanding. One leaf-plate per year, each one exactly eight hundred kilometers across, pushing into territory that had been empty space the year before.

Something was building a world, and no one knew why.

You volunteered for the ground survey because you had already mapped everything mappable on three worlds. Because your wife had been dead for four years and your children had their own lives on distant stations and you were tired of surfaces that made sense. Because when they showed you the orbital views of the Engine, you felt something turn over in your chest—a recognition that had no logical basis, a sense of *yes, this* that you could not explain.

You've been trying to map it for seventeen days, and your maps have become dreams.

**The Impossibility of Cartography**

At first, you tried to be systematic. You established a base point—a distinctive cluster of pillars near where the shuttle left you, their spiral pattern unique enough to serve as reference. You measured distances in the steady rhythm of your steps, counting paces, noting directions, sketching the pillar locations with the one pencil that still worked. For three days, your maps looked like maps.

On the fourth day, you returned to your base point to find the pillars had shifted. Not dramatically—a matter of meters—but enough to throw off every measurement you'd taken. You checked your instruments, checked your notes, checked the marks you'd scratched into the waxy surface to confirm your location. The marks were gone. The surface had healed them.

You started over.

On the seventh day, you realized the moth-things were editing your maps. Not directly—they never touched the paper—but their flight patterns as you worked seemed to influence what you drew. When they circled left, your hand wanted to curve the lines. When they landed in groups, you found yourself adding details that weren't there—structures you hadn't seen, passages you hadn't walked, symbols that meant nothing in any language but felt necessary on the page.

You started a new notebook, keeping it sealed when the moth-things were near. It didn't help. The maps you drew in isolation were accurate but useless—technical drawings that captured the physical reality while completely missing the *sense* of the place. Looking at them was like looking at an anatomical diagram of a loved one's face. Correct. Precise. Wrong.

On the tenth day, you gave up on objectivity.

**The Maps That Dream**

Your current map covers sixteen pages that have somehow become a single continuous surface. The paper edges merge where you've pressed them together, fibers reaching across the gaps like the root-networks below. You've drawn the Engine as it feels rather than as it is: the pillars becoming trees becoming architectural columns becoming the bones of something vast and breathing. The light-patterns are rendered in a notation you invented without planning, a combination of musical staffs and topographical lines that shows not where the brightness goes but *when* it arrives.

The Tenders appear on your map as absences—human-shaped holes in the landscape where the Engine's patterns break down, become unpredictable, become something like free will. You've spoken to three of them. None responded with words, but one traced a finger across your map, following a route you hadn't walked yet, her touch leaving a faint phosphorescent trail on the paper. When you followed the route the next day, you found something: a chamber formed where six pillars had grown together, their bases fusing into walls that almost enclosed a space the size of a small room.

Inside, the ceiling glowed with a different quality of light. Warmer. More like sunlight.

You stayed there for hours, letting the familiar wavelengths wash over you, feeling something in your chest unclench for the first time since arrival. When you finally left, you tried to map the location carefully, precisely, so you could find it again.

You haven't been able to find it again.

The Engine moves. Not quickly, not obviously, but constantly. The pillars grow and twist and merge. The passages shift. The chambers open and close like breathing. Your maps become obsolete between one day and the next, but you keep making them because the act of mapping is all you have left of who you were, and because something in the correlation between your sketches and the moth-thing flights suggests a communication you don't yet understand.

**What the Moths Are Teaching**

They're not moths. You know this. They're something the Engine grew to look like moths, or something that evolved to look like moths, or something that remembered moths from some genetic template and decided to become them. But calling them moths makes them manageable, nameable, less unsettling than what they actually are.

On the fifteenth day, one landed on your map while you were drawing. Its legs—too many legs, you counted nine—touched the paper with infinite delicacy. Where they touched, the lines you'd drawn began to move. Slowly. Subtly. The pillar positions adjusted themselves, the passages realigned, the notation you'd invented for light-patterns evolved into something more complex.

You watched, pencil frozen in your hand, as your map edited itself into accuracy.

Not the accuracy of physical location—you'd given up on that—but accuracy of relationship. The map showed you how the pillars connected underground, how the light-patterns predicted growth-cycles, how the spaces you walked through were not really spaces but pauses between processes, moments where the Engine hadn't yet decided what to grow next.

The moth lifted off, leaving your map transformed and your understanding sideways to what it had been.

You've started leaving the maps out at night for them to edit. You wake to find new territories sketched in—areas you haven't walked, structures you haven't seen, connections you wouldn't have thought to draw. Sometimes they add symbols in the margins, notation that looks almost like mathematics but feels more like music. You copy these symbols into your other notebook, the one you keep sealed, trying to find patterns, trying to decode what they mean.

You think they might be trying to teach you to map in four dimensions. Or five. Or some number that only makes sense if you accept that space here is more verb than noun.

**The Weight of Solitude**

You talk to yourself now. Not constantly, but enough to notice.

At first it was just thinking out loud—commentary on the mapping process, observations about the light-cycles, theories about what you were seeing. But your voice has evolved its own agenda. You find yourself having conversations with it, your mouth producing one side while your mind provides the other, and sometimes you're not sure which is which.

"The pillars are records," your voice says while you sketch another impossible junction. "Each growth-ring is a year of data, encoded in cellulose."

"Data about what?" you think back.

"About everything. About the Tenders walking past. About the moth-flight patterns. About you."

"I haven't been here long enough to be in the growth-rings."

"Haven't you?"

You check your tablet. Seventeen days. The display is clear. But when you count the pages in your map-notebook, there are too many. When you try to remember the shuttle leaving, the memory feels worn, like something handled too often. When you look at your hands, the lines the green threads make under your skin seem older than seventeen days could account for.

"Time moves differently here," your voice suggests.

"Time doesn't move at all," you answer. "We move through time."

"Maybe that's the problem."

You've started leaving markers as you walk—scratches on the pillars, patterns of small objects arranged on the leaf-surface, anything to create reference points in this reference-less world. They disappear, but not always in the ways you expect. Sometimes you find them moved, rearranged into new patterns that seem to comment on your original design. Sometimes you find them duplicated, the same marker appearing in multiple locations, identical down to the scratches you remember making.

Once, you found a marker you hadn't made yet. Your future self's handwriting, apparently, sketched onto a pillar in pigment you didn't have. The message was coordinates in your own notation system, pointing to a location your maps said didn't exist.

You went there anyway.

You found yourself at the edge of something: a place where the leaf-floor simply stopped. Not a cliff—the surface curved down and under, like the edge of a vast plate. Beyond it, nothing. Not darkness, not void, but the simple absence of anything to perceive. The moth-things wouldn't fly past the edge. The light itself seemed reluctant to venture beyond the leaf-boundary.

You stood there for an hour, staring at nothing, feeling the Engine's presence massive and breathing behind you, and for the first time since arriving, you understood the true scale of what you were trying to map.

**The Seduction of Integration**

Your pencil moves without conscious direction now. You start a map of one area and find yourself completing a map of another, the transition seamless, your hand following patterns the moth-things have taught it. The green threads under your cotton clothes have spread to your skin—just a few, just at the edges where fabric meets flesh, but you can feel them interfacing with something, exchanging something, becoming part of something larger.

You should be afraid. You inventory your emotional state every morning, checking for panic, for despair, for the kind of deterioration that isolation brings. Instead, you find a strange peace. Not happiness—happiness requires comparison, and you have nothing to compare this to. But a sense of rightness, of fitting, of being exactly where you're supposed to be.

The Tenders watch you sometimes. You've learned to recognize their presence by the way the moth-things behave, circling tighter, flying in formations that suggest deference or curiosity or something between. One Tender in particular—the one who traced the route on your map—appears more often than the others. She stands at the edge of your peripheral vision while you work, her presence patient and undemanding.

Yesterday—or what you've decided to call yesterday—she brought you something. A piece of material that wasn't leaf or wood or any Engine-grown substance. It was fabric. Ancient fabric. Synthetic, probably from the original colonist's clothes or equipment, worn down to near-transparency but still recognizably artificial. Recognizably *human*.

She placed it on your map and waited.

You understood. You took your non-working pencils and wrapped the fabric around them, creating a small bundle. Then you made a mark on the map—not a location but a symbol, something between a gravestone and a seeds planted. The Tender nodded, picked up the bundle, and walked away.

Later, in the place marked on your map, you found the pencils growing. The synthetic materials had been dissolved, reformed, integrated into something that was partly plant and partly tool. The new growth produced something like graphite, something that left marks, something you could use to map with. But the marks it made glowed faintly in the dark, and the maps drawn with it seemed to breathe.

**What You're Becoming**

You are not becoming a Tender. The Tenders were born here, or have been here so long that the difference is academic. You are something else: a translator, perhaps. A bridge between the world that uses maps and the world that is one continuous surface.

Your maps have stopped pretending to represent physical space. Instead, they chart relationships: between growth and light, between the Tenders' movements and the Engine's responses, between your own breathing and the pulse-patterns in the floor. The moth-things no longer need to edit them. You've learned their notation, or they've taught you to invent it, or the distinction between learning and becoming has blurred beyond recovery.

On the twentieth day—you still believe it's the twentieth day—you discover you can read the pillars.

Not language. Not even information in any sense you would have recognized before. But a kind of knowing that comes from running your fingers along the growth-rings, feeling the density changes, the chemical gradients, the electromagnetic variations that the Engine encodes in its own substance. Each pillar is a memory. Each twist in their helical structure is a decision the Engine made about how to grow, where to grow, what to become.

You spend days reading them, adding their memories to your maps, creating charts that show not just where things are but how they came to be there. The Tender who watches you brings you water—not in any container but carried in her cupped hands, water that glows faintly green and tastes of electricity. You drink it without hesitation. Your body needs things now that water alone cannot provide.

**The Map That Maps Itself**

Your final map is not on paper.

You noticed on the twenty-third day that your footsteps had begun to leave patterns. The green threads in your skin, connected now to the threads in your clothes, connected to the moth-wing dust on your tablet, connected to the growing pencils you harvest each morning from their pillar-garden—all of it has become a kind of circuit. Where you walk, you leave traces that the Engine reads. Where you rest, the leaf-surface remembers.

You are mapping with your existence now.

The paths you take become paths the Tenders follow. The places you pause become places where the light-patterns hold steady a little longer. The chambers you discover—or create, the distinction unclear—become permanent features, grown into the Engine's memory through repetition of your presence.

You realize, with a clarity that feels like coming home, that this is what mapping has always been. Not the creation of representations but the creation of relationships. Not saying "this is here" but saying "I have been here, and so it exists."

The moth-things land on you freely now, their nine legs gentle on your changed skin. They read the maps you've become and carry that reading to parts of the Engine you haven't walked yet, preparing the way, translating your presence into the Engine's language so that when you arrive, you're already expected.

You no longer remember how long you've been here. The tablet still says twenty-three days, but you've stopped believing it. Time isn't measured in days in a place with no sun. Time is measured in growth-rings, in pulse-cycles, in the slow evolution of your maps from representation to integration.

**The Choice That Isn't**

The Tender who first traced the route on your map stands before you now. She gestures—not toward any physical direction but toward a state of being, a way of existing that your maps have been approaching asymptotically.

You understand what she's offering.

Complete integration. Not absorption—the Engine doesn't absorb. It incorporates. You would become what the Tenders are: a permanent part of the system, your consciousness distributed across the vast network, your individual existence traded for something larger, older, more patient than any human life could be.

You would lose your name. Your memories would become communal. Your maps would dissolve into the greater map that the Engine continuously draws of itself.

But you would gain something too. The ability to feel the world breathing. The knowledge of what grows in the deep places where no Tender walks. The purpose—or the echo of purpose—that the Engine carries in its deepest programming.

You look at your maps, spread across the chamber floor like shed skins. They tell the story of your transformation: from rigid cartographer to something that doesn't have a name yet. Something between human and Engine, between mapper and map.

The Tender extends her hand.

The moth-things circle overhead, waiting.

The Engine pulses beneath your feet, patient as geology.

You realize the choice was made the moment you volunteered for this mission. Everything since has been the slow working out of that decision, like a plant growing toward light it knows is there but cannot yet see.

You take her hand.

**What Remains**

Somewhere in the Engine's vast memory, encoded in growth-rings and light-patterns and the flight-paths of moth-things, there is a record of a cartographer who came to map the unmappable.

The maps still exist—not the paper ones, which have long since merged with the leaf-surface, but the paths worn into the Engine's consciousness by someone who walked them with purpose. The Tenders follow these paths sometimes, not knowing why they feel significant, only that they do.

In the places where you paused to sketch, the light holds a different quality. Warmer. More like sunlight.

The moth-things carry something of you in their flight patterns—a tendency to circle that wasn't there before, a way of editing reality that assumes it can be improved, a distinctly human conviction that the world should make sense even when it doesn't.

And deep in the Engine's processes, in the calculations that run beneath consciousness, there is a new variable. Small. Almost insignificant. A subroutine that asks, periodically, "What does this look like from outside?" A fragment of perspective that the Engine didn't have before, couldn't have before, because the Engine had never conceived of an outside.

You gave it that. The gift of distance. The possibility that somewhere beyond the edge where the leaf-floor stops, there might be something worth growing toward.

The Engine incorporates this possibility.

The Engine grows.

And somewhere in its growth, encoded in cellulose and light, your maps continue to expand, charting territories that don't exist yet but might, if the Engine decides they should.

If you decide they should.

If the distinction between you and the Engine still matters.

It doesn't.
