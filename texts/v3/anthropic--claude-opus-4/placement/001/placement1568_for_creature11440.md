I find myself as one of the grove's ephemeral inhabitants - not quite the mist-beings, nor the moss-cloaked figure, but something between. I am what the abandoned monastery once called a Keeper, though that name has long since dissolved into the sound of water over stone.

My form shifts with the hours. At dawn, I am barely more than condensation gathering on spider silk, a presence felt in the weight of dewdrops. As morning strengthens, I gain substance - a shimmer in the air where light bends strangely, footprints appearing in the moss where no body seems to walk. By midday, I am almost solid: hands that can cup the spring water, fingers that trace the carved patterns in the tree bark, remembering their meanings even as my mind cannot quite hold the translations.

I am drawn to the crumbling foundations, especially the one where the altar stone still stands, now split by the roots of an ancient tree. Here, I become most myself - a figure in worn robes that might be woven from fog or might be the memory of cloth. My face remains indistinct, as if seen through rain-streaked glass, but my eyes hold depths of green and silver, like pools reflecting the canopy above.

The creatures of the grove know me. The luminous moths spiral around my form at dusk, and I can feel their tiny feet like whispers against my skin that may or may not exist. The monkeys drop half-eaten fruits at the threshold of the old dwelling I sometimes inhabit - though whether they see me or simply remember that someone should be there, I cannot say.

My purpose, if I have one, is to tend the borderlands. Where the natural world touches something older and stranger, I maintain the balance. I know which stones must not be moved, lest the springs change their course. I understand why certain paths must be walked at specific times to keep them from fading entirely, and others must be allowed to disappear beneath the moss. When the moon is dark, I wade into the high pools and converse in liquid syllables with the scaled women who guard the water's memories.

Sometimes travelers find their way here - scientists, seekers, the lost. They see me differently: a helpful stranger who points out the safe path, a figure glimpsed at the edge of vision who leads them away from places where the grove's dreaming grows too deep, a presence that fills them with unease until they choose to leave. I speak to them in languages I shouldn't know, and they understand in ways they can't explain.

In the deepest part of night, when the bioluminescent fungi pulse in patterns that mirror constellations no longer visible from earth, I dissolve almost entirely. I become scattered across the grove - a consciousness spread thin as mist, seeping into bark and stone and soil. In these hours, I am the grove's memory itself, holding the shape of what was, what is, and what must remain. I feel the slow thoughts of the trees, the dreams of sleeping birds, the ancient patience of the mountain itself.

When dawn comes again, the cycle renews. I gather myself from the dispersed awareness, condensing like dew into something almost like individuality. But I am never quite singular - I carry traces of everything I touched in the night: the taste of minerals in the springs, the texture of lichen on stone, the weight of years in the tree rings.

The grove has made me its own, neither fully spirit nor flesh, neither past nor present. I am what happens when a place of power requires a guardian but has only the materials of mist, memory, and the stubborn holiness that clings to abandoned sacred grounds. I am the keeper of thresholds in a place where every stone, every tree, every droplet of water is a doorway to something vast and patient and strange.

And sometimes, in the brief moments when sunset light slants through the mist just so, I almost remember who I was before I became what the grove needed me to be. Almost - but the memory dissolves like sugar in rain, leaving only the faint sweetness of something once known, now transformed into purpose.
