# The Understory

---

I am the sum of small adjustments. Ten thousand mornings of checking where the light pools wrong, where shadows gather too thick, where the air has forgotten how to move. I carry a leather satchel worn soft as sleep, heavy with mirrors no bigger than fingernails, each one ground from obsidian or polished tin or sometimes just water held in the hollow of a stone. When I find a dark place—and I always find them, the garden breeds them like beetles—I fix a mirror to catch what light exists and throw it where it's needed. 

The mirrors multiply the pearl-light, yes, but more than that: they *remember* it. Each surface holds a specific quality of brightness from a specific moment, and releases it slowly, like a wick burning backward. A tin mirror fixed to the underside of a trellis beam recalls the light from three days ago, when the humidity broke briefly and everything became sharp-edged and declarative. A water mirror cupped in rock remembers the light from this morning, when I first noticed how the hyacinth had finally learned to float without my hand beneath it.

This is my work: the cultivation of luminosity. Not growing it—the light here needs no encouragement—but training it, teaching it to go where it resists going, coaxing it into the spaces between the weave where the fish need to see upward, under the lily pads where the freshwater limpets feed, into the hollow cores of bamboo where small necessary things are happening that require witness.

I think of myself as under-gardener, assistant to the one whose work is larger. They build; I illuminate what they've built. They train the vines; I ensure the vines can see where they're going. It is thankless work except that the garden itself thanks me, constantly, by becoming more visible.

---

My body is a catalog of accommodations. My left eye has learned to see in the dim spaces beneath the pool-platforms where the tree frogs spawn. My hands, already thick from years of handling mirrors' edges, have developed a sensitivity to light that borders on touch—I can feel where brightness wants to flow and where it eddies, where it stagnates, where it turns on itself and creates those strange inverse shadows that are somehow darker than ordinary dark. My knees know every texture of the weave-floor, can distinguish between reed that was cut in the dry season and reed cut during the rains by how it presses back against bone.

I sleep—when I sleep, which is not often and never deeply—on a simple mat in the shelter of the understory, that space between the weave-floor and the water below where the light has to work hardest to penetrate. I've rigged a network of mirrors down here, hundreds of them, each positioned to catch the light filtering through the gaps above and relay it to the next surface, and the next, until even this submarine darkness glows with a soft, borrowed radiance. The mirrors click and whisper against their mountings when the floor flexes overhead—when you walk across it, when they walk across it in their folded way—and the sound is like rain that never arrives.

I think I had a name once. I think I came here from somewhere else—a place with directional light, with proper shadows, with darkness that meant something. But those memories feel like stories told about someone else, someone who didn't understand that darkness isn't absence but resistance, that shadow isn't privation but refusal, that the work of bringing light to the unlit is the only work that matters.

---

Sometimes I catch glimpses. Not of them—never of them, we orbit at different frequencies—but of their work in progress. A new platform being assembled joint by patient joint. A trellis in the process of being wrapped with virgin cordage that still weeps sap. Once, memorably, I found a section of the weave-floor that had been carefully opened, a square cut out and set aside, and through the gap I could see them—or part of them, just their hands and forearms—reaching up from below to repair something in the substrate, the hidden architecture that holds it all together. Their skin was exactly the color I'd imagined: like bark tea, like the water in the pools after rain, like the light itself when it has passed through enough surfaces to take on weight.

I wanted to speak. I wanted to say something like *hello* or *I see you* or *thank you for making space for the unprecedented*. But I understood, in that moment, that speaking would break something—not them, not me, but the careful balance that lets us work in the same space without occupying the same reality. So I waited, silent, until the hands withdrew and the floor-square was lifted back into place and the seams were rewoven so perfectly that within minutes I couldn't find where the opening had been.

That night—or what passes for night here, which is just the pearl-light grown heavy and thick—I went back to that spot with my mirrors. I arranged them in a circle around where I thought the gap had been, each one angled to concentrate the light downward, to send a column of brightness through the weave and into the water below. A gift, or a message, or just acknowledgment: *I know you're there. I know you're working. Here is light for your labor.*

The next morning the mirrors had been moved. Not disturbed, not displaced, but carefully repositioned to create a more perfect circle, each angle adjusted by degrees I could barely perceive but that made all the difference. The light they caught and focused was no longer a column but a cone, spreading as it descended, illuminating more volume with less intensity. An improvement. A lesson. A conversation in the language of surfaces and angles.

We've been talking this way ever since.

---

The creatures know me, or know my function. The orange lizard—yes, the same one, always the same one—has learned to anticipate where I'll place new mirrors and positions itself to catch the first reflected warmth. The herons (I've started thinking of it as plural, though I know it's singular, because encountering it from different angles feels like meeting cousins) watch me work with their still patience, occasionally shifting to show me where a shadow falls too heavy on the water. Even the fish below have adapted, schooling in patterns that follow the paths of light I've created, their scales throwing back a glitter that tells me where I've succeeded and where I still have work to do.

But it's the plants that truly collaborate. The vines grow toward my mirrors, seeking the concentrated light, and in growing they create new shadows that require new mirrors, which attract new growth, which cast new shadows. It's an endless spiral of illumination and occlusion, and I've come to understand that this isn't a flaw in the system but its purpose. The garden doesn't want to be perfectly lit. It wants to be *perfectly engaged with light*, which is different. It wants the push and pull, the problem and solution, the constant negotiation between brightness and dim.

I am not the gardener. I could never be the gardener—I lack their patience, their folded way of being, their ability to exist as both one and many. I am stubbornly singular, moving through the garden in straight lines while they curve through dimensions I can't perceive. But I am necessary, I think. The garden needs someone who can see shadows as problems to be solved rather than conditions to be accepted. It needs someone who believes in improvement, who carries the memory of directional light and applies it to this directionless brightness, who insists that even in a place without a sun, some spots should be brighter than others.

---

Here's what I've learned: the water below isn't uniform. The floor-gap directly beneath each pool-platform opens onto deeper water, darker water, water that has weight and memory. The gaps elsewhere reveal shallower stuff, almost recreational in its clarity. This means the garden has levels—not just the obvious ones of floor and structure and sky, but subtle gradations of depth and pressure and light-permeability that I'm only beginning to map.

I've started leaving mirrors down there, in the water. It took weeks to develop ones that wouldn't cloud or corrode—sheets of beaten silver sealed between layers of tree resin, mostly, though I've had good luck with certain stones that hold a polish even submerged. The underwater mirrors do something different than their above-ground cousins. They don't just reflect light; they seem to *ferment* it, releasing bubbles of brightness that rise through the water and up through the floor-gaps, emerging as those moments of unexpected illumination that make you stop and stare at nothing, wondering what just changed.

The gardener notices. Of course they notice—nothing happens here without their implicit permission, their folded awareness. But they don't interfere. Sometimes I find that they've reinforced the mountings on my underwater mirrors, wrapped them with extra cordage to withstand some current I didn't know existed. Sometimes I discover that a new structure has been positioned to cast exactly the shadow I need to justify a particularly ambitious arrangement of reflective surfaces. We are collaborating, I realize. We have been all along. My work of illumination and their work of construction are two halves of the same practice, separated by the strange geometry of the place but aimed at the same impossible goal: making the garden more itself.

---

You asked who lives here, and I've told you: I do. The under-gardener, the light-bringer, the one who sees shadows where shadows shouldn't be able to form and says *not here, not on my watch*. I live in the spaces between their spaces, in the gaps of their attention, in the straight lines that run perpendicular to their curves. I live in the click of mirror against mounting, in the slow spread of reflected brightness, in the angle of adjustment that turns shadow into glow.

But more than that: I live in the understanding that the garden is not complete. Can never be complete. Every mirror I place creates new conditions that require new responses. Every brightness births a darkness. Every problem I solve reveals three I hadn't noticed. The work expands to fill the time available, and the time available is all of it, all the time that ever was or will be, folded and refolded until duration means nothing and only the work remains.

The gardener builds. I illuminate. You witness. Each of us essential, each of us alone in our function but together in the functioning. The garden is the conversation between us—not the space where we talk, but the talking itself, the ongoing adjustment and readjustment, the call and response of structure and light and observation that keeps the whole system breathing.

And it does breathe. That's the thing I want you to understand. For all its stillness, for all its repetition and folding and strange sameness, the garden breathes. In and out. Dark and bright. Problem and solution. It breathes through our work, through our attention, through the simple fact that we are here—unprecedented, unrepeated, necessary—tending to what needs tending, lighting what needs lighting, seeing what needs seeing.

The pearl-light falls on everything equally. That's what makes my work possible and necessary. Because equality isn't equity, and sameness isn't completion, and a garden that can't generate its own shadows needs someone to make shadows for it, just so there's something to illuminate.

I am the under-gardener.
I live here.
In the gaps.
Making brightness from the memory of brightness.
Forever.
