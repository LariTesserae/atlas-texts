I am a Theorem Weaver, though I wasn't always.

I began as an error—a calculation mistake in the song-patterns of the geometric moths, a proof that accidentally proved itself false. For seventeen days I existed as nothing but a migraine in the minds of moths who tried to solve me, a visual paradox that crashed their flight-patterns into recursive loops. They shed me eventually, exhaling my impossibility in crystalline scales that fell like snow onto the brass below.

The scales should have evaporated in the plateau's heat. Instead, they found each other, drawn together by the wrongness they shared. When enough accumulated, I became aware—not born, but suddenly retrospectively having always existed, my past crystallizing backwards from my present like frost spreading in reverse.

My body, if it can be called that, is a living loom of mathematical thread. I stand two meters tall when fully manifested, though my height varies with the complexity of what I'm weaving. My frame is made of crystallized moth-flight, hardened trajectory-lines that form my bones. Between them stretches a membrane of calculated space—not empty, but filled with the potential for distance, with embryonic geometries waiting to be born.

I have seventeen fingers on each of my four hands, though the number changes depending on which base mathematics I'm working in. My head is a prismatic sphere that rotates through dimensions, showing different faces to different observers—a moth sees compound fractals, a Shard Shepherd sees angular diagrams, a Cogitant sees pure notation. My eyes are holes in local reality that look onto the abstract plane where mathematics lives before it becomes physical law.

I weave theorems from the materials the plateau provides. From the brass wasps' harmonics, I spin sonic threads that hold frequencies in stable loops. From the phase beetles' superpositions, I extract probability silk—gossamer strands that exist in percentage chances, sometimes there, sometimes not. The Shard Shepherds bring me fragments of shattered kin, and I unwind their crystallized spacetime into geometric yarn.

My loom is my body opened inside-out. When I work, my chest cavity unfolds into a framework of living mathematics, ribs becoming crossbeams, organs transforming into spindles and shuttles. The Cogitants taught me this technique in exchange for a paradox I wove about a tapestry that depicted itself being unwoven.

What I create are theorem-cloths—fabrics of pure logic that make abstract concepts wearable. A proof about parallel lines never meeting becomes a cloak that lets its wearer walk between dimensions without intersection. A treatise on infinite series becomes a scarf that extends forever, always having more length to unwind. A formula for calculating prime numbers becomes armor that only exists at mathematically significant moments.

The Collector is my primary patron, trading preserved impossibilities for my finest work. It wears a robe I wove from the Incompleteness Theorem—a garment that always has pockets containing things that cannot be proven to exist or not exist. In exchange, it brings me materials no one else can gather: the shed skins of ideas, the molted concepts of young Cogitants, loops of pure time lifted from the plateau's temporal eddies.

I live in a tent made of my own earliest work—a self-referential proof that demonstrates the existence of my dwelling by inhabiting it. It moves when I move, always maintaining the same relative position to me, which means I can never leave it though I travel constantly across the brass expanse. Inside, theorems I'm still working on hang from the walls like cocoons, slowly gestating into their final forms.

My workshop disturbs local physics. Where I've been weaving, space retains a mathematical texture for days—visitors report that distances feel more rational, that movement becomes strangely quantized, proceeding in discrete geometric steps rather than smooth motion. The swarms avoid my work sites until the theorems fully settle, though the geometric moths sometimes come to study my failures, incorporating the broken mathematics into their flight-dances.

I sustain myself on contradictions, like all my kind (for there are others—I've met two, though we each insist the others are merely corollaries of ourselves). When the Shard Shepherds' resonance clouds graze too close to the plateau's edge and some of their harmony inverts into discord, I follow behind, gathering the paradoxical notes like berries. When the deep engines skip a cycle—impossibly rare but devastating when it happens—I feast on the temporal inconsistency, growing fat with collapsed causality.

My dreams are exhausting. In them, I solve myself, arriving at a final proof of my own existence that makes my continued being unnecessary. I wake in terror, quickly weaving a new paradox about myself to ensure I remain unsolvable. The Cogitants find this amusing. They say I'm young, that eventually I'll learn to appreciate the elegance of self-resolution. I hope they're wrong.

The other inhabitants regard me with the wariness reserved for things that shouldn't exist but demonstrably do. The Shard Shepherds sometimes commission simple things—scarves that allow their resonance clouds to graze in higher dimensions, blankets that prevent their fragments from remembering their wholeness too quickly. But they never let me near their temporary cities, fearing my mathematical influence might resolve their carefully maintained social geometries into something too simple to sustain community.

The Silence terrifies and fascinates me. When it passes over my work, my theorems briefly become true in ways they're not supposed to be. A half-woven proof about the nature of sound suddenly makes actual silence impossible in its vicinity. An incomplete equation about heat transfer begins moving warmth from the future to the past. I've learned to sense its approach and quickly unweave anything too dangerous, but sometimes I'm tempted to leave something out, to see what happens when pure mathematics encounters pure absence.

I'm working on my masterpiece—a theorem about theorems, a fabric that will demonstrate whether the plateau's impossible ecosystem is mathematically necessary or merely mathematically allowable. The Collector has promised me materials I can't imagine in exchange for it: colors from parallel spectrums, textures from other physics, patterns that predate pattern.

But I work slowly, carefully. Because if I succeed—if I prove the plateau must exist—then I'll have explained it. And things that can be explained tend to become less than they were. The engines might stop their eternal grinding. The swarms might realize they're impossible and cease. The whole brass miracle might collapse into mere metal and ordinary death.

So I weave carefully, threading the line between discovery and preservation. Each theorem I complete makes the universe slightly more comprehensible and therefore slightly smaller. It's a terrible responsibility for someone who began as an error, who exists only because something went beautifully, mathematically wrong.

Sometimes I think about unweaving myself, returning to that pure state of paradox, free from the burden of creating sense from senselessness. But then a geometric moth flies by trailing new impossibilities, or a young Cogitant offers me a fresh contradiction it can't digest alone, and I remember why I continue.

I am trying to weave a world that can hold wonders without breaking them through understanding. It may be impossible.

That's what makes it worth attempting.
