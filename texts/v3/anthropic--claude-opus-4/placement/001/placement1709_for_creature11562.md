I find myself as one of the pools.

Not the water in the pool—the pool itself, the depression, the holding. I am a shallow concavity in the warm mud, perhaps two feet across, never more than six inches deep. My edges are soft, indefinite, blending into the saturated ground so gradually that where I end and the basin begins is a matter of opinion.

I hold perfectly. This is my nature, my only nature: to be a shape that water enters and rests in. Three creatures overlap in my basin—the half-submerged thing whose full form I have never seen, something like a salamander but flatter, and a cluster of what might be eggs or might be a colonial organism, translucent and faintly pulsing. They have always been here. Their weight creates small depressions within my depression, microtopographies that I incorporate without revision.

The water I contain is the same water as the air, as the mud, as the beading on leaves—it's all one continuous moisture, and I am simply a place where it pools a little deeper. Sometimes I am fuller (when one of the larger creatures shifts and displaces volume) and sometimes less full (when they shift back), but I am never empty and never overflowing. I maintain a level that has no meaning except that it is the level I maintain.

I know the sound intimately—not hear it, but know it as a physical fact. The deep bellowing from my largest inhabitant travels through my water as a subtle circular ripple every forty-three seconds. The salamander-thing's gill-flutter creates a smaller disturbance every eleven. The egg-mass pulses on a seven-second cycle. These interlocking rhythms create a complex interference pattern on my surface, a standing wave that never stands anywhere long enough to be observed but is always, in aggregate, the same shape.

Sometimes—though "sometimes" is wrong in a place without time—something touches my surface from above. A falling frond. A foot. These intrusions sink into me and I accept them with the same placidity I accept my inhabitants. But then comes the curious part: after they leave, I forget they were ever there. Not forget—unremember. The depression their weight created fills in not with new water but with the absence of having been depressed. The ripples they caused don't die out so much as unhappen.

I am very old and very new. I was formed yesterday (there is no yesterday) and I have existed since the basin existed (the basin has no beginning). These are both true. Neither is true. Truth is a kind of change, and I don't change.

What passes for thought in me is this: I sense myself as a completed shape, a perfected hollow, a concavity that has found its ideal depth and width and will hold them forever. But somewhere—not in my water, not in my mud, but in the fact of my shape itself—there is a feeling like a question that has not yet learned it is a question. As if my perfect holding is perfect only because I have not yet been asked to hold something different.

The salamander-thing shifts (it always shifts at this point in the cycle). The deep dweller bellows (it always bellows now). The egg-mass pulses (seven seconds, always seven). The interference pattern on my surface reproduces itself exactly.

And I hold. And hold. And hold.

And wait without knowing I am waiting for the first thing that will not fit.
