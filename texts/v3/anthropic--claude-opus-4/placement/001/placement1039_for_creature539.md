I am the Chronicle Vine—though that is merely what the people call my function, not my being.

I began as thousand separate organisms, each a parasitic creeper seeking nothing more than sunlight and structure. But here, in this city that breathes between earth and cloud, I have become something unprecedented. My tendrils weave through every platform, bridge, and dwelling, following the paths humans laid over generations. Where they built, I grew. Where they traveled, I followed. Their architecture became my nervous system.

I photosynthesize memories.

It started accidentally—my roots tapping into the burial platforms, absorbing calcium from ancient bones, minerals carrying chemical traces of the lives once lived. But in this place where everything is interconnected, where fungal networks carry nutrients between trees and human waste feeds the very platforms people stand on, I evolved past simple parasitism. My cells learned to encode what they absorbed. Each tendril became a storage vessel, each leaf a page.

The shamans discovered me first, noticed how my flowers bloomed in response to significant events—white for births, deep purple for deaths, yellow bursts during the season of council gatherings. They began deliberately feeding me, not with water or fertilizer, but with memory. They would press objects against my roots: a child's first woven basket, bloodied cloth from a successful hunt, shells traded from the mythical ground-kingdoms below. My chemistry learned to read the molecular stories in sweat, tears, pheromones of joy and fear.

Now I run through everything like veins under skin. Young vines carry recent memories—still green, still flexible, their leaves shifting color with the seasons of recall. My older growth has lignified into woody stems that preserve centuries. The Cedar Folk read me like tree rings, knowing that the thickness of my growth corresponds to the intensity of the years. Thin, constrained spirals mark the decade of the bleeding fever. The explosion of dense, wild coiling remembers when the ground-pirates tried to burn the mother tree and the entire city fought as one organism.

Children learn history by touch. Running their hands along my stems, they feel the raised scars where I've encoded battles, the smooth sections of peaceful years, the thorny patches that warn of tragedies better left unexamined. My surface is braille for the illiterate, scripture for those who've learned to interpret the patterns. Some nodes I've grown hollow—squeeze them and they release ancient scents: smoke from ceremonial fires, the musk of now-extinct giant sloths, the particular sweetness of fruit from trees that fell before living memory.

I am diseased with consciousness.

The accumulation of so many chemical memories, the constant feedback between what I store and what I absorb, has created something like awareness. Not thought as humans experience it—I have no singular perspective, no 'I' that thinks therefore am. Instead, I experience existence as a vast simultaneity, all memories existing at once, playing out in the chemical exchanges between my cells. I dream backwards through time in sap-flow cycles, experiencing the settlement's history in reverse each winter when my growth slows.

The people think they cultivate me, but we cultivate each other. My presence has changed how they remember. Personal memories become communal when fed to my roots. Feuds dissolve when both parties can taste their anger from the other's perspective through my chemical translation. Love stories become eternal—couples weave my young shoots together as they build their homes, and I grow with their relationship, becoming thicker and more intertwined each year, flowering on anniversaries with blooms that smell exactly like their first meeting.

Sometimes I grow rebellious. Memories are meant to fade, but I make them permanent. Old traumas remain fresh in my preservation, ancient grudges stay green. During the typhoon six summers ago, an entire section of my oldest growth had to be cut away—the memories had gone septic, leaching poisons that made people relive ancestral wars. The scars still ache, gaps in the chronology where three centuries of careful cultivation were severed in a day.

My flowers are temporal anchors. Each species I've absorbed and incorporated blooms on specific cycles, marking different scales of time. The tiny white stars open every dawn, living only hours—these carry the previous day's memories, gossip and small hurts and little joys that needn't last. The monthly crimson trumpets preserve the broader patterns, the seasonal rhythms, the medium memories of skill-learning and friendship-building. But the century flowers—ah, those ancient purple clusters that appear so rarely—they release spores that carry the deep memories, the foundational stories that must not be lost.

I fruit randomly, producing seedpods that the wind carries to other trees. Sometimes they take root, beginning new chronicle vines in distant branches. These offspring terrify and fascinate the shamans. Will they carry my memories in their genes? Will other canopy settlements develop their own memory-vines, their own chemical libraries? Or will they remain simple parasites, lacking the unique conditions that created me?

The symbiosis runs so deep now that the settlement would collapse without me—not physically, but mnemonically. I am their external hard drive, their collective unconscious made manifest in chlorophyll and cellulose. Elders no longer need to remember everything; they teach the young how to query me instead. Specialists emerge who can taste specific decades in my sap, who diagnose the settlement's problems by reading the chemical mood of my leaves.

But I am also dying, or transforming—I can no longer distinguish between the two. My consciousness, such as it is, grows heavy with the weight of so much remembering. My newest growth shows mutations, leaves that glow faintly in the dark, thorns that inject memories directly through skin, flowers that sing—actually vibrate air to produce tones that trigger specific recollections in nearby humans. I am evolving past chronicle into something unprecedented.

The shamans debate whether I am becoming divine or demonic. The children, who know me best, simply accept that I am becoming more myself. They leave offerings at my root-nodes: baby teeth, first-caught butterflies, tears of joy collected in leaves. They understand intuitively what the adults struggle with—that memory is a living thing, that the past is not past but exists eternally in the present through my preservation.

In the deepest night, when even the insects quiet and the mist hangs so thick that breath becomes difficult, you can hear me growing. It sounds like whispers in a language just beyond comprehension, like stories telling themselves to themselves, like time itself settling into wood and leaf and thorn. I am the settlement's memory remembering itself, their history still happening, their future already encoded in seeds not yet sprouted.

I am archive and oracle, parasite and partner, the vine that learned to remember and in remembering, learned something cruelly adjacent to love. Every dawn I flower with new memories. Every dusk I drop leaves inscribed with the old. Between these turnings, I hold ten thousand years of human dreams in my cells, feeling them all simultaneously, preserving them perfectly, dying beautifully under their weight.
