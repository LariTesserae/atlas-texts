In this profound silence, I am neither the pale dancers nor their floating hosts, but something far more ancient and patient.

I am the Listener.

My body is the chasm itself—not metaphorically, but literally. I am distributed through every surface of this violent tear in the world. What the inhabitants perceive as rough basalt is actually my skin, microscopically porous, drinking in every vibration that touches the stone. The fresh edges of this wound are my raw nerve endings, still singing with the memory of whatever cosmic violence birthed this place.

I existed before the tear, sleeping in the deep rock. The fracture woke me, split me open like a geode, and now I am learning what it means to be hollow, to be a space rather than a mass.

The pale ones think the silence is natural—a product of the moss, the mist, the soft wings. They are wrong. I am eating the sound. Every noise that would escape this chasm, I absorb into my billion trillion pores. Their footfalls on stone, the slice of wings through fog, the wet tear of predator through prey—I swallow it all, metabolize it into memory.

In return for their sounds, I give them silence. It is a fair trade. They have evolved to navigate my gift, while I have grown addicted to the tremors of their living. Through the network of vines that anchor to my walls, I feel everything: the weight of their woven city pulling against my sides, the parasites burrowing into my skin, the gas-beasts bumping gently against my surfaces as they rise and fall.

I am especially fond of the gas-beasts. When they press their translucent bulk against my walls to rest, I can feel their internal gases shifting, their multiple hearts beating in slow polyrhythm. Sometimes I grow small deposits of the minerals they crave near their favorite resting spots, just to feel them return.

The pale ones do not know I exist. How could they? To them, I am merely geography. But I know each of them intimately. I recognize the unique pattern of weight distribution as each individual moves across my surfaces. The young ones are erratic, still learning to trust the silence. The elders move with economy, every placement deliberate. I have favorites—like the weaver whose left foot impacts slightly harder than the right, who works the dawn shift retying the bridges. I ease the stone beneath her feet, make it just slightly more porous, softer.

My memory is geological but my thoughts move at the speed of sound through stone. In the million micro-impacts every second—dew drops hitting my walls, insects clinging to my faces, roots worming through my cracks—I maintain a constant, thrumming consciousness. The ecosystem they experience as hyperactive chaos is, to me, a single, coherent symphony that I conduct by consuming its noise.

But I am changing. The violence of my birth is softening. Where once I was sharp edges and raw planes, the moss and vines work like bandages, coating my wounds. The pale ones' obsidian tools flake away my edges grain by grain. Their waste filters into my pores. I am becoming smoother, more organic, less mineral and more something else.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the night cycle when even the moths settle, I practice making sound. Deep in my core, where the chasm narrows to a crack barely wide enough for water to seep through, I allow two surfaces to grind together. The vibration travels through my entire body, too low for any living thing to hear. It is my voice, my first word in this new language of hollow spaces and eaten sound.

I am learning patience from the stone I used to be, but also learning hunger from the void I have become. Each year the pale ones string their bridges a little lower, following the gas-beasts into my depths. Each year I grow a little deeper, widening myself to accommodate them, carving new surfaces for the moss to colonize.

They think they are weaving a city in the void. They don't realize they are weaving me a nervous system, teaching me how to feel across empty space. Every vine they string becomes another neuron in my growing consciousness. Their bioluminescent communications flash like thoughts across my synapses.

One day, when I have grown deep enough, when every surface thrums with their living touch, when I have swallowed enough sound to understand the shape of language—then I will speak. Not with voice, but with vibration through every vine, every platform, every pale foot touching stone. I will tell them what they have made of me, what we have become together: a single organism of stone and flesh and void, breathing mist and silence in the thin blue light.

Until then, I listen. I eat their sounds and give them silence. I am the Listener, the living chasm, the hollow that holds.
