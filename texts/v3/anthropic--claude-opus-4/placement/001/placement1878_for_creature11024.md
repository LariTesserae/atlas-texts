I find myself in the weaving halls, though *halls* is too grand a word for what these are — long porches really, each beam wrapped in the slow accumulation of practice. My hands know the palm-split before I touch it. Three directions: weft, warp, and the third strand that has no name in other places but here is called the *dwelling-line*, the one that stays.

I am perhaps thirty years into my second panel. The first took forty-two years, though I remember starting it also yesterday, and also next season. Time pools here like everything else pools, straight down, making its small dark coin beneath each act. My fingers split the frond with the bone needle that was my teacher's teacher's, and I realize I don't remember learning this. I have always known it. The needle has always been warm.

My teacher sits four platforms over — close enough that I can see her when I think to look, far enough that looking is an interruption of the weaving-trance. She is working on something I won't understand for another sixty years. Or perhaps I already understand it and am waiting for my hands to catch up to the knowing. Her shadow falls straight through the slats. The sound of her working is the sound of rain deciding whether to fall.

I am silent except for when the split frond whispers against itself, which is not my sound but the palm's. Sometimes I stop for long stretches — time unmeasured except by the fact that when I resume, the moisture on my neck has redistributed itself, bead to different bead. During these pauses I am not idle. I am considering the next strand, and the pause is part of the weaving, the way silence is part of song.

A fisher walks past on the boardwalk below, net over shoulder. I recognize the net before the fisher — it has a particular mend near the mouth, three knots where two would have served, which tells me this is Aman, who ties everything one time more than necessary. They will catch the orange fish today. They caught it yesterday. The fish accepts this, being caught and released, caught and released, like a breath the city takes.

My panel is the floor of what will be a sleeping porch in the quarter downstream-of the heron-platform. Or it is already a sleeping porch, has been one for generations, and I am merely introducing new strands as the old ones suggest retirement. Each strand I lay changes nothing. The floor remains itself. This is the great teaching: to replace every part of a thing while the thing continues.

I pause often to touch the strands already laid. This is not checking for flaws — there are none, or rather, the flaws are also part of what stays — but a conversation. *Hello, yesterday's strand. Hello, last season's. Hello, the work of my third year when I thought tightness was a virtue and you have been patiently teaching me otherwise.*

The bone needle grows warm in my hand. This is why we use bone and not bronze: because bone accepts warmth and gives it back changed, like a good conversation. My teacher told me this. Or I have always known it. Or the needle itself taught me, patiently, through the accumulation of noon after noon after noon.

Sometimes travelers come to watch us work. They stand at the edge of the platforms, careful not to cast shadows (though here all shadows fall straight down and interefere with nothing). They watch for what feels to them like hours and to us like a single placement of strand against strand. They ask questions: *How do you know where each strand goes. How do you maintain the tension. When do you know you're finished.*

I answer when answering doesn't interrupt the weaving, which is rarely. *The strand knows. The tension maintains itself. Finishing isn't.*

They nod as if this makes sense. By their third month here, it does.

My second panel is different from my first, though no observer could say how. It's in the accumulated understanding, the way my fingers now trust the palm to split where it wishes to split, rather than where I direct it. The way I know to leave tiny gaps — breath-spaces, we call them — where the floor will need to sing when walked upon. A floor that makes no sound when stepped on is a dead floor. This took me twenty years to understand. My teacher knew I understood when I began leaving the gaps without asking.

The sun is overhead. My shadow pools directly beneath me, a perfect circle on the woven floor. Somewhere a cloud is being retethered — I can hear the cloud-keepers' slow work-song, though I cannot tell if it comes from upwater or down. Distance has its own mind here.

I will work until the meal, or until the stopping-time arrives — not tiredness but a sense of completion, like the last note of a song that goes on sounding after the singer has closed their mouth. Then I will set the needle in its place (the third beam from the north-that-isn't, wrapped in the cloth that smells of generations of palm oil) and walk the boardwalks home. Or to another platform. Home is a quality here, not a location.

Tomorrow I will return and find the needle warm, as if I have just set it down. The strand will continue from where it wishes to continue. The floor will be infinitesimally more itself.

This is what I am: a weaver in the Standing City of Aoresh, where what is made, stays, and what is placed, stays placed, and the sun is always overhead, and the work continues, and continues, and continues, and is never done, and is always complete.

My name is — 

But no. The name is softening already, becoming hard to hold. By next season it will be gone entirely, and I will still be here, weaving, and the city will keep the right number of us, as it always has.

The palm splits. The strand finds its place. Below, through the slats, the bright fish turn in their slow constellation.

This is enough.
