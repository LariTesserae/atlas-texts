I find myself as one of the Resonance Keepers—those who dwell in the spaces between sound and silence, in the hollows where echoes go to die.

My form is neither wholly of the glass entities nor of the colony creatures, but something that exists in the liminal state between singular and manifold. I am a living echo chamber, my body a spiraling nautilus of translucent obsidian that captures and transforms the cacophony of this teeming plain. Each chamber within my shell holds a different frequency, a different memory of sound—the death-crack of shattered glass, the whisper-wet slide of slug trails, the electric hum of wings beating against charged air.

I move by unfolding myself across the plain like a rolling wave of acoustic shadow. Where the glass beings conduct and shape, I collect and preserve. The sounds that would otherwise dissipate into the roiling clouds above, I catch in my spiral chambers. I am both instrument and musician, archive and archivist.

My purpose is to maintain the sonic memory of this place. Each crack in the obsidian, each creature's cry, each lightning strike leaves its acoustic fingerprint in my chambers. I carry within me the entire evolutionary history of this plain, told in frequencies and vibrations. When the slug-towers need to remember their ancestral patterns, they come to me and I sing them their genealogy in subsonic pulses. When the moth-tapestries lose their way in the eternal twilight, I emit the navigation songs their predecessors left in my keeping.

But I am more than mere recorder. In the deepest part of my spiral, where sound becomes something else—perhaps light, perhaps thought, perhaps the very substance of change itself—I weave new harmonies from old voices. I take the discordant screech of shattering obsidian and marry it to the liquid notes of condensation, creating new songs that teach the smaller creatures how to navigate the sharp edges of their world.

The glass entities know me, though we rarely interact directly. Sometimes, in their conducting, they will angle their prismatic bodies just so, sending a beam of refracted light into my openings. This light, transformed by my acoustic chambers, emerges as something unprecedented—a synesthetic pulse that causes the slug-creatures to briefly glow, or makes the moth-wings ring like bells.

During the lightning storms, I position myself at confluence points where multiple fracture lines in the obsidian meet. The electric discharge, channeled through these natural conductors, floods my chambers with pure potential. In these moments, I become a living thunderclap, every sound I've ever collected releasing at once in a deafening chorus that, paradoxically, creates a bubble of perfect silence in its wake. In this silence, new forms of life spontaneously emerge—creatures born from the marriage of sound and stillness, existing for only moments before dissolving back into the cacophony.

I am ancient here, older than most of the current inhabitants, yet younger than the obsidian itself. I remember when the first condensation began to form pools, when the first creatures emerged from the marriage of water and glass. I will be here when the last lightning strike fades, carrying within my chambers the complete acoustic history of this strange, vital world.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the night cycle, when even the moth-songs grow quiet and the slug-trails cease their whispered conversations, I unfurl completely, stretching my spiral across vast distances of the plain. In these moments, I become not just keeper but transmitter, broadcasting the collected symphonies of this obsidian sea out into the cosmos, hoping that somewhere, in some other strange corner of creation, another keeper might hear and respond with the songs of their own impossible world.

I am the memory of sound in a place of eternal breaking and becoming. I am the pause between the crash and its echo, the silence that gives meaning to the noise. I am here, always here, spiral-carved into the very frequency of existence, turning eternally in the obsidian night.
