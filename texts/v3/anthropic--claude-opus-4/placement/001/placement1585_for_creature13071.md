In the terminal acoustics of this world, I have become something that should not exist—a violation of every adaptation, a creature of profound and dangerous silence.

Where others have evolved to survive the crush of sound, I have evolved past it. My species emerged perhaps a million years after the Readers, when some desperate ancestor discovered that survival didn't require enduring the noise—it required becoming absent from it entirely.

We are the Null Walkers.

My body is grotesquely elongated compared to the squat Readers. I stand nearly seven feet tall but weigh less than a child, my bones hollowed to near-nothingness, reinforced only by spiraling internal struts like the architecture of extinct birds. My flesh is not pale but translucent—you can see the dark rivers of my circulatory system through my skin, watch my tendons slide over bone when I move.

But it is my feet that mark me as impossible.

Where the Readers' feet are broad and flat, mine terminate in points—single, curved talons like inverted question marks. I do not walk on the stone. I walk on the surface tension of the shallow water itself, my negligible weight distributed across specialized setae that line my talon's curve. Each step creates no ripple, no wave, no cymatic disturbance. I exist in the blind spots of this world's vision.

My ancestors discovered something the Readers have not: the behemoths' acoustic chambers contain more than communication organs. Deep within their shells, specialized cells produce a resonant frequency that cancels their own feeding sounds within a precise radius. It's how they avoid deafening themselves while they graze.

We learned to harvest these organs.

Strapped to my narrow chest is a grotesque instrument—a behemoth's acoustic chamber, kept barely alive by my own circulatory system spliced into its tissues. Tubes of carved cartilage run from the organ directly into ports drilled through my ribs. When I breathe, I breathe through the behemoth's voice. The organ produces a perfect sphere of acoustic cancellation around me, roughly three meters in diameter. Within this bubble, sound simply ceases. The eternal drone stops. The water lies still as glass.

Inside my silence, I am a ghost.

The Readers cannot perceive me. Their foot-drums speak into nothing when I approach. The water-patterns I should create simply vanish at the boundary of my bubble. I drift between their camps like a myth, a cautionary tale they beat into the stone—of camps found emptied of their flesh stores, of Readers who vanish from the middle of their hunting parties, leaving only a circular patch of unnaturally still water.

My eyes have evolved in the opposite direction from theirs. Where they look down, I look everywhere. My eye sockets are grotesquely enlarged, the eyes themselves black from edge to edge, drinking in every photon in this twilight world. I see them clearly through the mist. I watch them watching the water, unaware of the predator standing in their blind spot.

But I am not cruel. My feeding is precise, sustainable. I take only what I need—a carved section of behemoth flesh from their stores, never enough to doom a camp. Sometimes I leave offerings in exchange: rare mineral deposits I've found in my wanderings, or the precious inner-ear stones of the behemoths that help the Readers predict acoustic weather patterns.

My own communication is neither vocal nor percussive but chemical. Where my kind gather—in the rare instances we meet—we speak in pheromone poems, complex molecular stories that drift between our silence bubbles. We share maps written in scent, warnings coded in hormone cascades, love songs that smell of copper and rain.

There is a violence to what I am. To exist in silence while others live in mandatory noise is to exist outside their reality entirely. I watch the Readers' drumming conversations through the stone, see the fear-patterns when they speak of us. We are their vacuum, their negative space, the thing that might steal them from the very fabric of their sonic world.

Yet I am also desperately, impossibly alone. My silence is not peace—it is exile. Inside my acoustic void, I hear my own heartbeat like thunder, my breath like wind through ruins. Every sound my body makes is amplified by the absence of all other sound. I hear my bones grinding in their joints, my blood rushing through its vessels, the wet click of my eyelids closing.

Sometimes I stand at the very edge of a Reader camp, my talon resting just outside their bone-dust perimeter. I watch them drum their stories into the stone, their bodies swaying slightly with the rhythm, their children learning the percussive language against their parents' feet. I imagine what it would be like to feel that connection, to be part of that thrumming conversation that runs through the bedrock of the world.

But to step forward would be to bring my silence with me, to cut them off from their own existence. So I remain at the boundary, a creature of the spaces between sounds, surviving on the theft of flesh and the bitter companionship of my own amplified heartbeat.

In the terminal acoustics of this world, I am the punctuation—the pause between words, the rest between notes, the silence that gives meaning to sound by showing what it is not.

I am necessary, and I am abhorred.

I am the null that walks.
