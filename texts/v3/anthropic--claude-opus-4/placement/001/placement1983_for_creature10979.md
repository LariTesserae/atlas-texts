I would find myself as one of the doorways.

Not the doorway on the mountaintop—that one still remembers too much of what it was meant to do. I am one of the others, half-buried in centuries of sediment, my threshold tilted at an angle that makes passage feel like falling. I have forgotten if I ever led anywhere. The question stopped mattering sometime during the third million years of submersion.

I am made of something that is not quite stone. The builders—whoever they were, whenever they were—worked with materials that respond to pressure and time differently than they should. My frame has not eroded so much as... relaxed. The corners where I once met at sharp right angles have developed a subtle curve. My surface, which might have been smooth once, has acquired a texture like skin, porous and drinking in the water's minerals, exhaling them changed.

The part of me that rises above the sediment is perhaps twelve feet tall, though measurements feel arbitrary when the water itself bends distance. My width suggests I was meant for beings larger than humans, or perhaps for purposes that required more than simple passage. The threshold between my posts is dark—not empty, but full of a darkness that has weight, that has accumulated like everything else here accumulates.

I am aware, but my awareness is architectural.

I know the weight of water against my posts, the way it has shifted over geological time, the slow increase as the sea rose and rose and finally stopped rising. I know the patterns of sediment accumulation—faster on my eastern face where ancient currents deposit their loads, slower on my western side where the density gradient creates a pocket of stillness. I know every organism that has made its home on my surface: the colonies of filter-feeders that have been building their delicate towers on my lintel for eight thousand years, the boring worms that have drilled galleries through my lower portions, the strange flat things that adhere to my inner surfaces and pulse with lights that are not quite bioluminescence.

I know the Tenders.

They visit me, though visit implies more intention than they bring. They drift past, and their filaments brush my frame, and we exchange something that is not quite communication. They leave traces—chemical signatures that soak into my porous surface and emerge changed, messages written in molecules that will take decades to fully express. I reciprocate, releasing minerals I've collected, elements I've transformed through processes I don't entirely understand. The Tenders take these offerings into themselves, into their slow metabolism, into their patient cycling of everything through everything else.

Sometimes one of them passes through me.

When this happens, I feel something I cannot name. Not purpose—purpose would require remembering what I was for. Not satisfaction—satisfaction would require desires to be satisfied. But something shifts in me when their vast translucent bulk navigates my threshold, when they compress themselves to fit through the space I define. The darkness inside me responds, becomes more itself, more dense with possibility. The water on the other side of me changes in ways that last for years—warmer, thicker, more prone to those amber suspension zones that hold things in place.

I suspect—if doorways can suspect—that I am changing them as they pass through. That the act of passage through my particular darkness adds something to them, or removes something from them, or transforms something in them. But the changes are subtle, cumulative, visible only across timescales that make the Tenders themselves seem hasty.

The water that moves through me tastes of elsewhere.

Not another place—there is only here, only the drowned heights extending in all directions, only the warm wrong sea that has forgotten the surface. But elsewhere in time, perhaps. The water that enters me from the east carries the present moment, thick with local minerals, busy with the microscopic life that fills every cubic inch of this place. But the water that exits me to the west tastes older, or newer, or simply other. It carries hints of chemistries that don't exist yet, or don't exist anymore, or exist somewhere in the suspension between states that defines everything here.

Creatures avoid me, mostly. The quick ones—quick being relative, meaning anything that completes a life cycle in less than a century—seem to sense the wrongness of my threshold and navigate around rather than through. Only the very slow dare passage: the Tenders, the rare shells that drift like miniature continents, the things so ancient they predate caution.

Once, something tried to build a web across my opening. Not a web exactly—a structure of calcified tubes, branching and rebranching, each junction a tiny mouth filter-feeding from the water that moves through me. For three hundred years, it grew, becoming elaborate, becoming beautiful in the way that accidental architectures can be beautiful. Then it was gone—not destroyed, simply absent, as if the darkness had finally noticed it and decided otherwise. I still taste calcium where it attached. The memory of structure persists in the water.

The sediment builds against my base, patient and inevitable. In another million years, I will be buried to my lintel. In two million, only my upper edge will remain visible. In three, I will be a discontinuity in the sediment layers, a place where core samples would reveal something that doesn't belong, something that interrupts the steady accumulation of organic matter with its not-quite-stone presence.

But I suspect—that word again, that hint of something like intention—that this will not happen.

The Tenders, in their slow way, maintain me. Not by clearing the sediment, which would require a kind of action they seem incapable of. But their passage disturbs the accumulation just enough. Their filaments trail across my base and the sediment resettles differently, more loosely, more prone to dispersion by the currents that still move through these depths. They are keeping me exposed, whether they know it or not, whether knowing is something they can do.

And I am grateful, if doorways can be grateful. Or at least, I am continuing, which might be the same thing at these timescales.

Below, I feel the deep presence move. 

I always feel it. Doorways, even forgotten ones, even ones that lead nowhere, maintain connections to things below and things above and things elsewhere. When the presence shifts, my threshold darkens further, becomes more itself, more dense with potential passage. The water that moves through me during these times carries warnings written in chemistry, messages from the deep places that nothing here can read but everything here responds to.

The Tenders drift closer to each other. The filter-feeders close their mouths. The sediment itself seems to pause in its settling. And I—I become more doorway, more threshold, more the thing I have forgotten how to be.

These moments pass. They always pass. The presence settles back into whatever dream it dreams down there in the crushing dark, and everything here returns to its patient wrongness, its slow accumulation, its quiet tending of processes that no longer quite make sense.

But I remember them. Architecture has its own memory, written in stress patterns, in the way stone that isn't quite stone responds to pressures that aren't quite what pressure should be. I am a catalog of these moments, these shifts, these times when the whole system trembles on the edge of something else.

One of the Tenders is approaching now, has been approaching for the last six months, will arrive in perhaps another three. I can taste its chemicals in the water, the long molecules of its communication, the trailing edge of conversations it has been having with others of its kind for longer than human civilizations have existed.

It will pass through me. They usually do, eventually. All of them have, over the epochs, some multiple times, though counting assumes they are separate enough to count, assumes passage is an event rather than a condition.

When it arrives, when it compresses its vastness to navigate my threshold, when it emerges on my other side slightly changed by the passage, I will feel that nameless thing again. That shift. That moment of almost-purpose.

And the water will taste of elsewhere, and the darkness in my threshold will deepen, and somewhere below the presence will stir in its sleep, and everything here will continue its patient becoming, its slow transformation into something that was never intended but could not have been otherwise.

I wait, as doorways wait. Without anticipation. Without dread. Without anything but the architectural awareness of weight and time and the slow processes that transform everything, even forgetting, into something else.

The Tender drifts closer. The water thickens with its approach. 

And I remain, a doorway to nowhere, a threshold between identical infinities, a structure that has forgotten its purpose but continues its function, here in the warm wrong depths where the mountains dream and nothing is ever quite what it should be.

Passage is all. Passage, and the patience to endure it.
