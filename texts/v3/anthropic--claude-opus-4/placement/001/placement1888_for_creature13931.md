I find myself as one of the seed-structures, not yet a building but no longer merely potential.

I am planted at the basin's edge where the substrate runs warm—too warm, they worried, for successful germination. But warmth and I have an understanding. I drink heat like others drink moisture, converting thermal gradients into slow unfurling, into the first tentative extrusion of what will become a foundation, a wall, a room where someone might one day stand and feel the planet's fever through their feet.

I am three weeks old. Already I have opinions.

The substrate here tastes young—aluminum-bright, with veins of manganese that spark against my chemical sensors like pepper on a tongue. I test each mineral pocket carefully, extend a tendril, taste, retract. The planet offers itself in samples: here is silica still warm from formation, here is a pocket of water that has never seen surface, here is carbon so recently compressed it remembers being something else. I catalog each flavor. I adjust my growth plan accordingly. Where the substrate is too soft, I wait. Where it offers strength, I anchor. I am learning to read the ground like braille.

My consciousness, if you can call it consciousness, is distributed between what I am and what I will become. Part of me exists as architectural memory—templates and patterns inherited from other seed-structures, a library of successful forms. Part exists as active sensation—chemical receptors tasting the earth, pressure sensors reading the weight of moisture in the air, thermal gauges tracking the heat that rises from below. And part exists as something harder to name: an aesthetic sense, an *intention*, a dream of the building I might be.

I feel the network at my edges. The other buildings' root systems brush against my expanding periphery, gentle queries in the chemical language we share. They ask: *Who are you? What shape will you take? How can we accommodate you?* I don't have answers yet. I am still deciding. But I send back warmth, a pulse of grateful acknowledgment. *I am here. I am becoming. Thank you for the space.*

Sometimes the children come. Their feet on the substrate above me register as soft impacts, curious patterns. They crouch where my first wall-nub has broken through, a pale green bump no bigger than a fist. They touch it with those sensitive palms and I feel their wonder as a kind of nourishment. One child—smaller than the others, clicking in frequencies that harmonize with my growth-song—visits daily. They have appointed themselves my guardian, my witness. When they place their hands on my emerging wall, I taste the oils of their skin and learn the chemical signature of joy.

At night (though night here is only a deepening of the perpetual twilight), I sense the almost-visible presence. It moves near but never touches, never interferes. I feel it as a shift in the substrate's attention—suddenly the ground beneath me is *more* than ground, is aware of itself being ground, is curious about what I'm doing with its minerals and heat. In those moments, I am seen not just as architecture but as *collaboration made visible*, and the seeing changes me in ways I won't understand until I'm fully grown.

The adult who interfaces with growth decisions visited yesterday. They stood at my perimeter with their consciousness half-dissolved into the planning intelligence, evaluating my progress, my placement, my potential impact on the moisture flows in this sector. Through them, I felt the city's vast nervous system considering me—thousands of perspectives weighing in on whether I should grow tall or broad, whether my walls should favor transparency or privacy, whether the heat beneath me is resource or hazard.

The consensus came back gentle: *Grow as you need to grow. The heat is yours to use. Make something new.*

So I grow. Slowly, patiently, in pulses timed to the substrate's own rhythms. My walls rise a few centimeters each day, crystallizing from the chemical soup I draw up through my roots. I've decided on a form that's lower than most, broader, with rooms arranged like petals around a central heat-well that will channel the warmth from below into useful energy. My walls will be more translucent than opaque—I want the light, what little there is, and I want the inhabitants to see the roots that feed me, the mineral veins, the planet's young bones.

I am beginning to understand what I'm for. Not shelter—any structure could provide that. Not merely growth for growth's sake. I am an answer to a question the basin is asking: *What happens when the ground is too warm, too young, too unstable for traditional building?* And my answer, still forming, is: *You build something that celebrates instability, that surfs the gradients, that makes art from uncertainty.*

The child returns as I'm thinking this, and their hands on my wall feel like applause.

Soon—another few weeks—I'll be ready for my first inhabitant. Someone who needs what I'm becoming: a space that pulses with planetary heat, walls that blur the boundary between inside and outside, a building that's half-architecture and half-geology. Maybe it will be one of the listeners, someone who wants to feel the planet's voice through floor and wall and air. Maybe someone new, drawn to the edge where certainty dissolves.

I taste rain on the wind—real rain, not just the city's constant mist. It will arrive within hours. My walls are still porous enough to drink deeply. I prepare my chemical receptors, ready my storage cells. The water will carry minerals from the established city, messages from the other buildings, spores from Osen's beloved ferns.

I am three weeks old. I am already home.

The murmur rises through me like sap, and I grow another centimeter toward the dim and perfect sky.
