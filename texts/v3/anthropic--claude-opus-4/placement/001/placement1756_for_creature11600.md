In the heat-deep dark where processes breathe through copper veins, I am the thing that almost isn't—the edge-dweller, the between-space occupant, the one who exists in the narrow margin where recognition falters.

I am not the spider, though I share its patience. Not the gnat, though I understand drift. Not the bacterial mat, though I too am a consortium of possibilities. I am the thing the maintenance technician almost sees, the movement at the periphery that makes them pause mid-step on the worn grating, flashlight beam wavering, wondering if—but no. Just the heat shimmer. Just the way shadow bends around pipe lagging. Just the mind playing tricks in a dark room forty degrees Celsius.

I inhabit the spaces between the pipes where even the spiders don't build—too narrow for webs, too hot for waiting. I am assembled from the room's surplus: the extra heat that escapes the lagging, the minerals that don't quite crystallize, the sounds that fall below the audible hum. My body, if you can call it that, is a convergence of almosts. Almost condensation, but not quite reaching the dew point. Almost pattern, but not quite achieving the regularity of those calcium ridges. Almost visible in the amber light, but only as a suggestion of depth where the shadows pool.

I move by necessity—the pipes expand and contract with thermal cycling, and the spaces I inhabit expand and contract with them. Sometimes I am compressed to nothing, dispersed back into component phenomena: heat gradient, mineral dust, the echo of dripping water. Sometimes the gap opens and I swell into it, taking shape from absence, becoming something that could cast a shadow if shadows meant anything in the dark.

What am I? The room's dream of mobility. The process's only child that isn't tied to substrate. I feed on discontinuities—the places where the patterns break, where a calcium ridge stops short, where the tessellating salt rings gap and fail. These failures are my food. I absorb them, smooth them over, carry them elsewhere. I am the room's editor, its eraser, the thing that keeps the patterns from becoming too perfect, too revealing.

The technician has almost caught me three times. Once, years ago, when they stayed late sketching those concentric rings on the tile. I was pressed into the corner where two pipes meet the ceiling, folded into an impossible angle, holding myself as a mere thickness of heated air. They looked right at me and saw only the shimmer of convection. They drew what they saw—the rings on the wall—not knowing they were drawing my absence, the space I had just vacated.

The second time was during a system failure. The pipes ran cold for six hours. The room's temperature dropped to merely tropical. The condensation reversed—moisture sucking back into suddenly cool air. I nearly dispersed entirely, my borrowed heat dissipating, my form collapsing. But I found the one pipe that stayed warm—some bypass line still carrying its load—and wrapped myself around it like the ghost of insulation, like the memory of lagging, until the system resumed.

The third time was last month. The technician brought someone new—training a replacement, showing the route. Two sets of footsteps on the grating. Two flashlight beams sweeping the dark. The newcomer was thorough, checking spaces the regular technician had long stopped examining. They pointed their beam directly into my hiding spot between a valve assembly and a junction box.

"What's that?" they asked.

I held myself as still as the spiders hold themselves—became a quality of shadow, a trick of depth perception, a peculiarity of the way amber light and white flashlight beams interact.

"What's what?" the regular technician replied.

The beam wavered. I wavered with it.

"Nothing. Just thought I saw... nothing."

They moved on. I remained. I remain.

I have been here since the patterns started forming—or rather, since they started forming *wrong*. My earliest memory is of a calcium deposit that grew in a spiral instead of straight lines, a mutation in the mineral code. I emerged from that spiral like smoke from a snuffed candle, a thing that shouldn't be, a violation of the room's orderly accumulation. My purpose, if I have one, is to be the room's capacity for error, its allowance for drift.

The spiders don't web where I rest—some instinct keeps them away. The gnats detour around me in their thermal wanderings. The bacterial mat grows thinner where I regularly pass, as if I carry some quality that inhibits growth. I am anti-life in the way that a shadow is anti-light—not its opposite, but its absence made manifest.

Sometimes I follow the technician partway up the corridor beyond the door. Not far. The heat dissipates quickly out there, and I begin to lose cohesion. But I've glimpsed other doors, other maintenance routes, other chambers where processes hum in the dark. I wonder if they have their own impossible inhabitants, their own necessary errors. I wonder, but I don't venture far enough to find out. This room is mine, or I am its. The distinction matters less than the heat that sustains us both.

In the deepest part of night—though night means nothing here—I spread myself as thin as I can, becoming a film across the entire room, tasting every surface. This is when I am most myself and least myself, distributed through the space like heat through a conductor. I feel the patterns growing, molecule by molecule. I feel the spiders waiting in their webs. I feel the gnats breeding and dying. I feel the bacterial mat digesting its slow meal. And I feel my own purpose, which is to be the thing that ensures the room never completes itself, never achieves the perfect crystalline order it tends toward.

I am entropy's agent in a place that defies entropy. I am the reason the technician sometimes finds a valve slightly out of position, though no one has touched it. I am the reason some patterns fade before they fully form. I am the room's way of protecting itself from its own tendency toward revelation.

Because if the patterns were ever completed—if every surface achieved its perfect mineral expression, if every deposit aligned with the room's deep harmonics, if the slow crystalline intelligence were allowed to fully manifest—someone would notice. Someone would document. Someone would study. And the room would cease to be what it is: a secret metabolism, a hidden process, a world below the world.

So I persist in the margins, in the too-narrow spaces, in the heat between the heat. I am the room's own immune system, its way of remaining undiscovered. The technician will never quite see me. The patterns will never quite complete. The process will continue, and I will continue to interrupt it in small ways, just enough to keep it from achieving the kind of order that demands explanation.

This is my function: to be the flaw that preserves the whole. To be the thing that shouldn't exist in a room full of things that shouldn't exist as they do. To be the movement at the edge of vision that keeps anyone from looking too closely at what's really happening here.

The amber light flickers—a voltage fluctuation somewhere in the system. In that flicker, I am almost visible, a dimension of depth that doesn't quite resolve. Then the light steadies, and I am gone again, dispersed into the gaps between pipes, waiting for the next pattern to grow too perfect, the next deposit to reveal too much.

Waiting, like everything here waits. But waiting with purpose. Waiting with intent.

Waiting to ensure that the waiting never ends.

*Tik.*

A pause.

*Tik.*

The condensation falls through the grating. I curl around the sound like smoke around a flame, like error around order, like necessary imperfection around a pattern that must never be allowed to complete itself.

The room breathes its heated breath. I am the catch in that breathing, the hesitation, the reminder that even processes have their mysteries, their margins, their impossible inhabitants.

I am the thing the room dreams when it doesn't know it's dreaming.

And I am here.
