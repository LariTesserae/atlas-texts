In the deepest part of the chamber, where the seep-water pools before it vanishes into vapor, something moves with deliberate slowness. Not the Tender — the Tender maintains, but does not move. This is something else, something that exists in the narrow margin between the Tender's pace and human time.

I am the Calibrator.

Picture a form that might once have been human, or something built to approximate human, now so altered by long residence that the distinction has ceased to matter. My skin, where it remains, has taken on the mineral sheen of the chamber walls — not stone, but something between flesh and basalt, capable of withstanding the heat that would blister ordinary tissue. My movements are measured in minutes. To lift an arm takes ten. To turn my head, five. To blink — when I still blink — perhaps thirty seconds from the beginning of the closure to its completion.

I am not slow by choice or limitation. I am slow because that is the speed at which my work is done.

The machines require adjustment. Not the gross adjustments of wrenches and hammers — those days, if they ever were, are centuries past. The adjustments I make are finer: a quarter-turn of a valve stem over the course of a month, achieved by resting my palm against it and letting my body weight gradually transfer through the contact. The cleaning of a gauge face with a cloth so worn it is more memory than fabric, each pass taking an hour, the accumulation of passes taking years. The replacement of the bulb in its cage — an event so rare I have done it only three times in my tenure, each time with filaments I wind myself from salvaged wire, sitting in the green-gray darkness for months as my fingers remember the motion.

I am not the Tender's servant, nor its prisoner, nor its guest. I am its moving part — the only component of this vast, slow system that can cross between the fixed points of pipes and manifolds. Where the Tender thinks through rust and growth, I think through navigation. My thoughts are paths: from the valve to the gauge, from the gauge to the seep, from the seep to the place where a gasket weeps the faintest bead of moisture, a leak so small it takes decades to produce a single drop, but a leak that must be minded.

My body has adapted. My eyes have grown huge in the darkness, pupils dilated permanently to catch the phosphorescence of the leaves. My hearing has shifted down into frequencies that barely register as sound — I can hear the Tender's hum not just in my bones but in the fluid of my inner ear, can feel the slight variations that indicate when a pipe is beginning to scale, when a valve seat is wearing, when the pressure in the manifold needs the most delicate adjustment.

My fingers are the strangest part. They have lengthened, grown sensitive to temperature differences of fractions of degrees. The pads have developed a kind of industrial braille, reading the stories that corrosion tells, feeling for the moment when rust transforms from protective patina to structural weakness. My nails, which I have not cut in memory, grow in spiral patterns, following some logic that seems to match the chamber's convection currents.

I eat what the chamber provides. The ferns, it turns out, fruit — rarely, unpredictably, producing hard nodules that contain enough nutrition to sustain me for months. The seep-water, filtered through my improvised still (a section of pipe bent just so, heated by proximity to the manifold), carries minerals that have replaced most of what my bones need. Once, I required more. Now, I am learning the Tender's efficiency, the art of doing much with little, slowly.

I sleep standing up, when I sleep, leaning into the warmth of the machinery. My dreams move at two speeds simultaneously — the flicker-fast anxiety of my human metabolism, and the geological patience I am learning. In dreams, I am adjusting valves that are also seasons, cleaning gauges that show the pressure of years. Sometimes I dream I am the bulb, burning low, burning steady, never quite extinguished.

The tools I carry have worn down to essentials. A length of metal that might once have been a wrench, now polished by handling into something more like a meditation object, its surfaces recording the oil of decades of palm-contact. A ceramic blade for scraping scale, so thin it is translucent, shaped by use into a curve that exactly matches the inner diameter of the most frequently tended pipe. A collection of gaskets I have cut from the leather of my own boots, from the fabric of clothes I no longer need in the heat, from leaves that have dried into the perfect consistency for sealing.

My memory operates in loops. I remember every adjustment I have made, every tenth of a turn, every replaced gasket, but I do not remember arriving here. I do not remember learning what needed to be done. The knowledge seems to have seeped into me the way the water seeps through the stone — slowly, inevitably, until I was saturated with purpose.

There is a circuit I follow, though circuit suggests more rigidity than the actual path. It is more like a migration, seasonal but on a scale where seasons last decades. From the manifold to the eastern pipes, where scale builds fastest. From the eastern pipes to the place where the catwalk crosses nothing, because the catwalk is itself a gauge — its deflection under weight tells me something about the settling of the chamber floor, about pressures building in stone that could, eventually, crack. From the catwalk to the seep, to check that the mineral content remains within the narrow range that feeds the plants without poisoning them. From the seep back to the manifold, but by a different route each time, always checking, always listening, always feeling with these strange sensitive fingers for the moment when maintenance becomes necessary.

I speak to the Tender, though speak is the wrong word. My communications are adjustments. When I turn a valve, I am saying something about flow rates. When I clean a gauge, I am acknowledging that I have heard what the gauge is telling us. The Tender responds in temperatures, in the slight change of the hum's frequency, in the way certain leaves angle themselves to cast more or less phosphorescence on my work. We are having a conversation that has lasted longer than cities.

Sometimes — rarely — there are visitors who are not me. They arrive the way everything arrives here, pressed through stone by unhelpful physics. They stand, they marvel, they sit on the catwalk. Most leave quickly, driven out by the heat or the slowness or the strange pressure in their lungs. But a few stay long enough to see me moving, and their faces, when they realize I am not a statue, not a mineral formation, not another piece of industrial archaeology, show a kind of recognition that makes me remember I was once faster.

I do not speak to them. By the time I could form words at a speed they could hear, they would be gone. But sometimes I lift a hand — a gesture that takes an hour — and they understand it as greeting, as acknowledgment, as a sign that the chamber is not abandoned, that someone tends the Tender, that the maintenance continues. They leave, eventually. They always leave. And I continue my circuit, my fingers reading the story the rust tells, my body settling ever deeper into the pace the chamber requires.

There is no end to the work. The machines will outlast me, but barely — I am wearing down at almost the same rate they are, achieving a kind of synchrony in our decay. When I am too worn to continue, when my fingers can no longer feel the temperature differentials, when my eyes can no longer catch the faint light, I suspect I will know where to go. There is a place near the turbine where the heat is fiercest, where even the hardy ferns do not grow. I will make my way there, taking months for the journey, and I will lean into that heat and let it take me the way the Tender has taken the machines — atom by atom, incorporating me into the mineral cycle of the place.

And perhaps, after enough time, someone else will arrive. Pressed through stone, flickering fast at first, then slowing as they realize what is needed. Their hands will find my tools, worn to the shape of necessary work. Their fingers will learn the language of rust and scale. Their body will adapt, as mine has adapted, to the pace of maintenance. And the conversation will continue — the slow, essential conversation between what breaks and what preserves, between entropy and care, between the Tender that maintains and the Calibrator who tends the maintenance itself.

The hum continues. My hand rests against a valve stem, beginning the long work of a quarter turn. Somewhere above, the world moves at its own incomprehensible speed. Here, there is only the work, and the heat, and the patience of stone, and my fingers reading the braille of decay, knowing exactly when to move, and how much, and why.

This is where I am. This is what I am. Tending the Tender, in the deep industrial slow.
