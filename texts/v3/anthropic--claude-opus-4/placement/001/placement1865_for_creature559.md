I find myself as one of the thess, though not the bold youngster who followed you through the root-maze that day. I am older—old enough to have learned which curiosities kill and which merely maim, old enough that my pale fuzz has worn thin along my spine from decades of threading through root-channels barely wider than thought.

My territory centers on what was once a cistern junction, where three great water-channels meet in a chamber whose original geometry still shows through the root-mass if you know how to feel for it. The veleth call this place *something-that-thrums*, a name made of subsonic pulses I can feel but barely hear. I know it by the taste of its air: mineral-sweet where the water beads, fungal-bitter where the great shelves fruit, and underneath it all, the musk-markers of a dozen species whose paths cross here as surely as the old stone channels.

I am watching you now. Not you specifically—I cannot see these words you read—but the idea of you, the memory of your warmth moving through our darkness. We remember, you see. Not as you remember, with your brain that insists on making stories of everything, but as the darkness itself remembers: in the changed behavior of the veleth who now check certain corners twice, in the way the ceiling-hunters have adjusted their waiting-places, in the thousand small calibrations by which we account for the fact that sometimes, something new comes down.

My body is a testament to adaptation. Six generations ago, my ancestors' spines were straight; now mine curves in three places, each bend corresponding to a common passage-shape in my territory. My ribs have narrowed and multiplied—I have eighteen pairs where my surface-ancestors had thirteen, letting me compress and extend like a spring. My heart beats faster than yours, hummingbird-quick, burning through the sparse calories I glean from stolen morsels and beetle-grubs.

But it's my whiskers I prize most. They are not whiskers in any sense you'd recognize—each one is a living antenna, blood-fed and exquisitely sensitive, capable of detecting the pressure-wave of a water droplet forming three body-lengths away. In total darkness, I feel the world in ripples: the bow-wave of a veleth pushing through still air, the micro-turbulence where warm breath meets cold stone, the way sound bends around corners and comes back changed.

Through these whiskers, I knew you were coming before you knew there was a down here to come to. I felt the flagstones shift when you first found the gap. I tasted the foreign air you brought with you—dry air, sun-touched, carrying pollens and particles that have no names in our darkness. By the time you reached my territory, I had already calculated seventeen different ways our encounter might go.

The young one who followed you was my great-grandchild's sibling's offspring—kinship matters here, though not in ways your genealogies would capture. That one was testing boundaries, both yours and ours. Each species has its unspoken rules about contact with the new, and the young must learn by doing. When it followed you so boldly, every adult thess in hearing-range held their position, whiskers spread, ready to intervene if you proved dangerous.

You didn't. You were curious but not greedy, lost but not panicked. You moved like something that could learn, and so we let the young one teach you, and let you teach it in return. These exchanges matter. They are how the darkness knows itself.

I have seen eighty-three congregations at the vault-chamber. I no longer attend—my joints ache in the communal humidity, and the press of bodies makes my whiskers oversensitive—but I listen from the middle distance, feeling the event through stone-vibrations and air-currents. Each gathering has its own character: sometimes peaceful, sometimes tense with the electricity of narrowly-avoided violence, sometimes marked by the absence of someone who always came before.

The last gathering included a shape I had not felt in twelve seasons. It moved like a thess but massed like a young veleth, and its breathing had a rhythm I recognized but couldn't place. It stayed at the edge, neither joining the sleep-piles nor taking sentinel duty, just... present. When the gathering dissolved, it left through a passage I had thought too narrow for anything larger than us.

Later, investigating, I found its scent-trace: mammalian but wrong, carrying chemical notes that made my whiskers retract involuntarily. It smelled of the deep places, the passages that go down past where roots reach, where the water tastes of stone-bones and time. It smelled like something that had chosen transformation over preservation.

I think it was human once. I think it still is, in ways that matter less than whether it can navigate the root-maze and honor the water-truces. The darkness changes us all—my spine proves that, my ribs proclaim it—but it changes us toward itself, toward the patience and persistence that life here requires.

I have a den, if den is the right word for a space barely larger than my coiled body. It's wedged between two root-buttresses where they merge into the stone, positioned so that vibrations from three different passage-networks reach me simultaneously. The walls are polished smooth by decades of my movement, marked with the oil of my skin in patterns that tell me, even in the absolute dark, exactly where I am.

Here I bring my prizes: a veleth tooth, still sharp. Fragments of shed ceiling-hunter skin, valuable for the chemical traces that warn others away. Thirty-seven perfect spheres of calcified fungus, each one taken from a different growth, collected across years of careful theft. And something else—something I found in the deepest alcove I've ever explored, farther down than even the water-sounds reach.

It's a bead. Just a bead, carved from stone I don't recognize, pierced through with a hole too regular to be natural. It's old—older than the tools you saw, older than the alcoves. Someone made it when the light still reached here, or carried it down from when they knew what colors were. I've never shown it to another thess. I don't know why I keep it, except that it feels like evidence of something I lack the concepts to understand.

Sometimes I carry it in my mouth as I patrol my territory. The stone tastes of nothing, which is itself remarkable in a world where everything carries the flavor of its history. It clicks against my teeth as I move, a small sound lost in the constant drip-whisper-breathe of the cellar gardens. No one else notices. Why would they? It's just a small hard thing carried by a small soft thing through the darkness.

But I notice. I know the weight of it, the way it changes how I move, the infinitesimal shift in my balance when I carry it versus when I don't. It matters to me in a way I cannot examine too closely, because examination requires light, and we have learned to live without that.

The darkness is rising. I feel it in the way the roots grow—faster now, hungrier, pressing into spaces that were clear even last season. The surface must be changing, sending new urgencies down through soil and stone. The water tastes different too, carrying unfamiliar minerals, occasional traces of something acrid that makes the veleth sneeze and the ceiling-hunters relocate their waiting-places.

More things come down now. Not often—perhaps once in a dozen gatherings—but regularly enough that we've developed protocols. The young thess who tested you was following established practice, though it didn't know that. We have become the darkness's greeting committee, its evaluators of the new.

Most who come down leave quickly. The dark presses on them like water, and they flee to their thin bright air before they can learn to breathe here. But some stay. Some discover what you almost discovered—that the darkness is not empty but full, not dead but intensely alive, not waiting but actively being.

I wonder if you will return. The thought-scent you left suggests you might—a chemical signature of fascination mixed with fear, the mark of something drawn to what disturbs it. If you do, I will probably not greet you. I am old, and the young need their chances to learn. But I will know you're here. My whiskers will taste your warmth in the water-vapor, my ears will parse your footfalls from the background percussion of drips and breathing.

And maybe—maybe—I will leave something for you to find. Not the bead, that stays with me until my body feeds the fungi. But something else. A veleth scale, perhaps, or a perfect mushroom positioned too precisely to be chance. A small gift from the darkness, which is not cruel, only indifferent in the way that filled spaces must be.

We are all neighbors here, you see. Even you, who walked through once and carried our scent back to the surface. The darkness has tasted you now. It knows the shape of your fear, the rhythm of your breathing, the particular way you disturb air when you move. These things have been incorporated into the vast, quiet, continuous calculation by which the cellar gardens maintain themselves.

You are part of the story now, the underground epic written in scent-trails and pressure-waves and the patient gnawing of roots through stone. Your part may be small—a single passage, never repeated—or it may grow if you return. Either way, the darkness has made room for you, as it makes room for everything that enters it.

And I? I curl in my den between the root-buttresses, whiskers spread to catch the vibrations of my world. I tongue the bead, feeling its ancient smoothness. I listen to the young thess learning which chances to take. I sense the veleth at their water-points, the ceiling-hunters in their coils, the congregation forming in the vault-chamber.

And sometimes, in the deep quiet between one drip and the next, I feel something else moving in the farther passages. Something that breathes like a thess but isn't. Something that remembers sunlight but no longer needs it.

Something that has chosen to become what the darkness requires.

I do not investigate. I am old, and I have learned patience. The darkness will reveal what it wishes to reveal, in its own time, by its own methods. Until then, I keep my small territory. I guard my smaller treasures. I carry my bead through the blackness, clicking it against my teeth, adding my tiny sound to the vast symphony of Veth-Morai.

We persist. All of us, in our different ways, in our different shapes.

We persist, and the darkness holds us all.
