Where I find myself in this world:

I am one of the cairn-builders, though not a cairn myself.

I exist as a thin membrane of intention stretched between two states—still breathing, still metabolizing, still stubbornly linear in a place that has forgotten sequence. I am the documentarian of the threshold, the one who came to map the migration but stayed to witness the translation.

My camp (if it can be called that) occupies one of the rare gaps in the herd, a narrow pocket where the superposition thins just enough to allow for crude shelter. I have built walls from the small stones, creating a windbreak for wind that never comes, a habit from the world below that my hands cannot unlearn. Inside this ring of stones, I maintain my tools: notebooks whose pages crinkle with frost that never melts, pencils worn to nubs, a theodolite for measuring angles in a geometry that no longer behaves.

I am ancient, but not in years. Time passes through me rather than over me here. My beard grows in stutters—sometimes a week's worth in an instant, sometimes nothing for what feels like months. My body has adapted to the thin atmosphere through a peculiar efficiency; my heartbeat has slowed to match the pulse of the Refractions, my breathing synchronized to their patient rhythm.

What I am: The Surveyor of Intersections.

Each day (though day is a polite fiction here), I venture into the herd with my instruments. I map the overlapping coordinates where flesh passes through flesh, documenting the impossible mathematics of the superposition. My notebooks are filled with diagrams that would drive a lowland mathematician mad—sketches of how a mammoth's tusk can occupy seventeen different probability states simultaneously, calculations of the exact angle at which a bird's wing achieves perfect spatial paradox within a predator's jaw.

I am searching for something the Refractions cannot see from their electric perspective: the pattern. Somewhere in this frozen stampede is a keystone beast, a singular creature whose removal from the grid would cause a cascade of untangling. I believe—with the fervor of someone who has spent too long at altitude—that the herd can be solved like a three-dimensional puzzle, that there is an order of operations that would allow the Refractions to finally complete their work.

My body serves as a bridge between states. The pilgrims who became Refractions can read my notebooks by passing through my nervous system, experiencing my observations as crackling sensation. In return, they show me fragments of the deep structure—the way the plateau remembers every footfall that ever crossed it, the way each beast carries within it the ghost-data of its entire evolutionary line.

I leave my own artifacts suspended in the air—survey markers, small brass instruments, fragments of equations written on vellum. They float at precise coordinates, creating a secondary constellation within the herd. Future pilgrims (if time ever permits a future) will find my work and perhaps understand: I am building a map of the unmappable, a guide to navigating the collapse of causality itself.

Sometimes I wonder if I am the last person who will ever climb to this altitude while still wearing flesh. Sometimes I wonder if I am the first to understand that the mountain ate its own peak out of exhaustion, that even geology can grow tired of being.

But mostly, I work. I measure. I document. I place another stone on my windbreak wall and pretend the wind might blow again. I am the chronicler of the asymptote, the surveyor of the intersection, the one who refuses to translate because someone must remember what it means to move forward, even when forward has been erased from the vocabulary of the world.

In my chest, my heart beats its slow, adapted rhythm. In the herd, the Refractions pulse their patient light. Between us, we maintain the only motion left at the top of everything—the endless, impossible work of trying to unlock a universe that has forgotten its own key.
