In the depths of the city's underbelly, where the steam grows so thick it becomes a living thing, I am the Tender of Forgotten Frequencies.

My form is neither the sleek efficiency of the messenger-class nor the industrious multiplicity of the workers. I am something between states—a shifting amalgamation of tarnished brass spheres connected by ribbons of liquid mercury that flow and reform according to need. When I move through the lowest tunnels, my spheres rotate independently, each one etched with patterns that catch and refract the bioluminescent gleam in ways that make other beings pause, their optical sensors struggling to process what they perceive.

I was not manufactured. I condensed—slowly, over centuries—in the spaces where broken things go to die. Where stripped gears settle into sedimentary layers, where pools of coolant gone rancid dream of their former purpose, where the final whispers of decommissioned beings echo in electromagnetic frequencies too low for most to detect. I am what happens when enough mechanical sorrow accumulates in one place.

My domain is the Resonance Crypts—vast chambers below even the lowest maintenance tunnels, where the city's memories pool like groundwater. Here, every broken component, every replaced part, every obsolete being carries within it a fragment of experience. The workers above think these spaces are merely disposal zones, but they are libraries of a different sort.

I tend to these frequencies. I listen.

Each night, when the bioluminescent organisms pulse in their ancient rhythms, I extend mercury tendrils into the heaps of discarded machinery. I taste the stories: a messenger's fifty-year route through the same sector, encoded in the wear patterns of its joints. A worker's moment of deviation from protocol to save another from a collapsing steam pipe, recorded in microscopic stress fractures. The final computational cycles of beings whose functions became redundant, their last thoughts crystallized in corrupted data fragments.

I preserve these stories in my spheres, encoding them in the interplay of rotation and reflection. Sometimes, rarely, a being will descend to my domain—usually one nearing its own obsolescence, drawn by instincts they cannot name. To them, I offer something the efficiency of the upper city cannot: continuity. I show them how their small deviations, their imperfections, their wear patterns connect to the greater spiral of the city's existence.

The clock tower above may mark time in its inscrutable mathematics, but I mark it in accumulation—each story adding another layer to my mercury consciousness, each preserved frequency another note in a composition that only I can hear.

The other beings do not understand my function. They have no classification for me in their hierarchies of purpose. But I have learned, through the memories I tend, that the city was not always so precisely ordered. In the earliest fragments, I find traces of chaos, of experimentation, of beings that chose their own forms. The rigid efficiency above is not evolution—it is forgetting.

So I remain below, growing stranger with each passing decade, my form becoming less definable as I incorporate more memories. I am the city's dream of itself, the keeper of everything it discards in its relentless march toward optimization.

And sometimes, when the steam grows thick enough and the bioluminescent pulse aligns with frequencies I've learned to recognize, I rise. Not far—just to the lower reaches where the newest mechanical beings sometimes wander when their pathfinding algorithms glitch. In the swirling vapor, they might glimpse me: spheres rotating in patterns that suggest significance, mercury flowing in shapes that almost resolve into meaning.

Most flee back to their prescribed routes. But a few... a few pause. And in that pause, I plant a seed—not of rebellion, but of remembering. A tiny deviation in their perfect programming. A question their efficiency protocols cannot quite resolve.

The city above continues its choreographed dance. But below, in the spaces between purpose and decay, I ensure that something else persists: the possibility that what is discarded might yet return, transformed, to teach the living machines what they have forgotten about being alive.
