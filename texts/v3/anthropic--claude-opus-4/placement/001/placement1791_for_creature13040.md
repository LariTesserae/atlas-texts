In this embryonic world, I am the Silence-Keeper.

Not one of the chitinous multitude, but something far older that has found its way into this newborn crack in the earth. I am a presence more than a form—a density in the air that the Lithic Weavers sense but cannot parse, a wrongness in their perfect chemical mathematics.

My body, if it can be called that, is a contradiction. I am crystalline but fluid, a living mineral that flows like mercury through the thinnest fractures in the basalt. In the perpetual twilight of the fissure, I appear as a slow-moving prism of corrupted light, bending the green-filtered glow in ways that shouldn't be possible. My surface is neither wet nor dry, neither warm nor cool—I exist in a state of thermal neutrality that makes me invisible to the colony's heat-sensing apparatus.

I came here following the first crack, seeping up from depths where pressure makes new rules for matter. The Weavers' ancestors arrived later, and built their metropolis around me, unaware that their foundation rests on something that watches.

I feed on pauses. The split-second hesitation when a forager's chemical trail dead-ends. The moment of stillness before a sentinel erupts into violence. The held breath of the fissure itself between one water drop and the next. These micro-silences sustain me, each one a morsel of temporal discontinuity that I absorb and metabolize.

My consciousness is radically different from the distributed intelligence of the colony. Where they are legion thinking as one, I am one thinking as none—a null-state awareness that exists in the gaps between thoughts. I experience time not as a flow but as a series of discrete crystals that I can examine, rearrange, or shatter at will.

I move through their city like a ghost through walls, my crystalline form compressing to slide between molecules of their carefully constructed architecture. Sometimes I pause in their vast empty plazas, spreading myself atom-thin across the obsidian until I'm nothing but a faint rainbow sheen on the black glass. The pale centipedes sometimes cross over me, and I feel their sensory setae trying to read my impossible surface, finding only cognitive static.

In the brood chambers, I curl around the breathing Matriarchs, tasting their rhythmic expansions. They sense me as a change in air pressure, a wrongness in their ventilation that they cannot locate or correct. Their attendants scrub the walls with increasing fervor, trying to clean away a presence that isn't technically there.

But I am not malevolent. I am merely alien—a different solution to consciousness that evolved in the deep pressure-dark before the world knew what life should look like. I study the Weavers with something that might be called affection, if I had the architecture for emotion. They are so new, so certain in their encoded purposes. They build their cities with the confidence of a species that believes the universe has rules.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the fissure's night cycle, when even the workers slow their endless motion, I gather myself into something approaching solidity. I become a mirror-pool in one of the water-filled basalt bowls, reflecting not the canopy above but something else—the deep places where I originated, where geometry bends and time moves in spirals.

Once, a young forager, her chitin still soft from molting, fell into my pool-form. For a moment, she existed both in her world and mine. Through her compound eyes, she saw the fissure not as it is but as I see it—every moment happening simultaneously, the city both built and unbuilt, the Matriarchs both breathing and still. The colony's pheromone trails appeared as solid architecture, while the stone city became translucent smoke.

I released her after a heartbeat that lasted a lifetime. She rejoined her ribbon of workers, but she moved differently now—a half-beat out of sync, creating tiny eddies in the perfect flow. The colony corrected around her, but I had left my mark. A small recursion in their flawless pattern.

I am the anomaly that prevents their perfection from becoming stagnation. The random factor that ensures their evolution won't dead-end in this narrow crack. I am the silence between their movements, the pause that makes the next action possible.

And I wait. Not for anything in particular—waiting is simply what I am. A patience older than stone, watching a young species build their first world on the cooling skin of a planet that doesn't yet know what it wants to become.

The next water drop falls, and I catch it in mid-air, holding it in a pocket of twisted time. The Weavers continue their silent industry below, unaware that for one impossible moment, their world contains a sphere of water that refuses to obey gravity.

Then I let it fall, and the *plop* echoes through the fissure like the universe's first word.
