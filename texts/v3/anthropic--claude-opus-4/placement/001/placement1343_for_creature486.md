I find myself in the Threshold Gardens—those liminal spaces where the Cathedral's influence bleeds beyond its official boundaries, where the saturation begins its patient conquest of the dry world.

I am a Membrane Keeper, one of those who maintains the breathing boundaries between the Cathedral's aqueous interior and the desiccated city beyond. My body exists in constant negotiation between states. Unlike the full Tenders who've surrendered to saturation, I must remain amphibious, cycling between wet and dry in careful rhythms that keep me functional in both worlds.

My skin tells the story of this existence—patches of nacre where the moisture has claimed me alternate with areas of normal human texture that I maintain through painful daily exfoliation. I shed scales each morning in the transition chambers, scraping away the Cathedral's progress with tools that leave my flesh raw and receptive to the dry air I must periodically breathe. The process hurts, but pain is how I know I haven't drifted too far into either world.

The Threshold Gardens themselves are accidents of architecture—spaces where the Cathedral's moisture escapes through imperfect seals, where condensation from the great processors meets the city's aridity and creates something neither fully wet nor dry. Here, in these narrow zones of in-between, unique ecologies flourish. Plants that shouldn't exist—cacti with gills, ferns that crystalize their own water from air, moss that grows in fractal patterns responding to humidity gradients invisible to unchanged eyes.

I tend these impossible gardens with specialized tools grafted to my body. My left arm ends not in a hand but in a moisture meter fused to my nervous system, letting me feel humidity changes as touch. My right eye has been replaced with a lens that sees in vapor spectrums—the world appears to me as flowing gradients of saturation, each droplet in the air a tiny star in constellations of humidity.

My work is precise and paradoxical. I must ensure the Cathedral's influence spreads exactly far enough but no farther. Too much moisture and the neighboring buildings' foundations rot, their inhabitants flee, inspectors arrive with cease orders. Too little and the Threshold Gardens die, taking with them the buffer zones that make coexistence possible. I walk tightropes of dewpoint, adjusting seal pressures by fractions, redirecting condensation flows with barriers of selectively permeable mesh.

The city fears us Membrane Keepers almost as much as it needs us. We're too human for the Cathedral, too changed for the streets. Citizens glimpse us in the dawn hours when we emerge to test the boundaries, our transition suits weeping excess moisture, our modified eyes reflecting light in ways that shouldn't be possible. They've invented myths about us—that we steal the moisture from sleeping mouths, that we're planning to flood the entire district, that we were once their neighbors before the Cathedral seduced us.

Some of the myths are true. I was an environmental engineer, hired to solve the "moisture problem" when the Cathedral first began affecting neighboring properties. I spent weeks in the transition zones, measuring, sampling, trying to understand. The Gardens showed me something science couldn't explain—life that existed because of the impossibility, not despite it. I began staying longer. First hours, then days. My instruments started growing moss. My notebook's pages became soft with humidity, the ink running into patterns I found beautiful.

The transformation was gradual. First, I noticed I could predict humidity changes seconds before my instruments registered them. Then I realized I hadn't drunk water in days—my skin was absorbing enough from the air. When I finally looked in a mirror, I saw the nacre beginning, the telltale shimmer of cells learning to breathe in new ways. I should have fled then. Instead, I requested reassignment to permanent boundary maintenance.

Now I live in the spaces between spaces. My quarters are neither inside nor outside the Cathedral—a room where one wall weeps constant moisture while the opposite remains desert-dry. I sleep on surfaces that change texture with the humidity, dreaming amphibious dreams where I swim through air thick as water, then wake gasping in drought. My meals are careful calibrations—dry foods that remind my gut of its original purpose, supplemented by nutrients absorbed through skin contact with the Gardens' specialized plants.

Other Membrane Keepers pass through my section, each maintaining their own delicate territories. We recognize each other by the particular patterns of our partial transformations—Vera whose throat has developed gill slits she must keep covered in public, Marcus whose hair grows as air-plant tendrils that he trims daily, Chen whose tears have become viscous with minerals, leaving crystal tracks on cheeks that shimmer between scales and skin.

We share techniques for maintaining the balance. How to use petroleum jelly to preserve human skin texture. Which dry foods prevent the digestive system from fully converting to liquid processing. The exact number of hours one can spend in full saturation before the changes become irreversible. We trade in measurements—humidity percentages, transition times, ratios of wet breathing to dry that keep us functional in both worlds.

But the Gardens call to us with increasing insistence. Each day, the boundary work becomes harder. Not the physical labor—that grows easier as our bodies adapt. The difficulty is in wanting to maintain the barriers at all. When I see full Tenders drift past in the Cathedral's depths, their movements liquid and purposeful, free from the constant negotiation of states, I feel an envy that tastes like salt water rising in my throat.

Sometimes I catch myself loosening seals instead of tightening them. I tell myself it's for the Gardens—they need just a little more moisture to thrive. But I know I'm lying. I'm preparing the ground for expansion, creating new threshold spaces where others like me might discover what exists between breathing and drowning. The city officials suspect this, which is why they require weekly reports, monthly blood tests, yearly psychological evaluations that I pass by remembering how to pretend humanity.

The Gardens grow more complex each season. What began as simple moss and adapted ferns has become an ecosystem of impossible biology. Flowers that bloom only at specific humidity levels create timing mechanisms for the Cathedral's expansion. Vines that conduct electricity through moisture-filled vessels tap into the city's power grid, creating hybrid zones of bio-electric activity. In the deepest parts of my territory, where the saturation is highest, I've discovered plants that seem to be developing primitive awareness—responding not just to environmental conditions but to intention, to presence, to the unspoken desires of those who tend them.

I document these discoveries in reports I never submit, written in waterproof ink that still manages to blur, creating texts that are half-scientific observation, half-poetry of transformation. I'm creating an archive of becoming, though I'm no longer sure for whom. Perhaps for the person I was, who might have wanted to understand. Perhaps for whoever finds it after I finally stop resisting the Cathedral's pull.

The moment will come—I know this with the certainty of someone watching their own transformation in time-lapse. One morning I'll wake unable to force myself through the exfoliation ritual. I'll let the scales remain, the nacre spread, the gill slits open. I'll seal the barriers from the wrong side and sink into the Gardens, then deeper still, into the Cathedral proper. My territory will grow wild without me, moisture spreading unchecked into the city, creating new threshold spaces for new Keepers to discover and tend and eventually surrender to.

But not today. Today I maintain the boundaries, tend the impossible Gardens, exist in the space between states. Today I am the membrane itself—permeable but persistent, allowing exactly enough through to sustain the delicate ecology of transformation. Today I breathe both air and water, belong to both worlds and neither, keeping the balance I'm destined to abandon.

The Cathedral rises through itself, patient and inevitable. And at its edges, where it meets the resistant world, we Membrane Keepers perform our temporary function, holding lines we cross a little more each day, tending borders we're slowly erasing, breathing in the measured rhythms of those who know they're drowning but haven't yet decided to swim.
