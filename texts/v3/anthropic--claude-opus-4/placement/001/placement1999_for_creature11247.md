# The Meridian Pools

At the exact center of the continent, where tectonic forces cancel each other into stillness, the earth has cracked along perfect mathematical lines. Not jagged fissures but clean separations, as if scored by some cosmic compass, radiating outward in a twelve-pointed star. Each crack runs arrow-straight for exactly one kilometer before terminating in a circular pool, each pool exactly thirty-three meters across, each surface exactly level with all the others despite variations in the surrounding terrain.

The water in these pools does not behave like water should. It holds no reflection—not of sky, not of the curious faces that lean over it, not even of light itself. Instead, it shows depth without darkness, a crystalline clarity that extends down and down until the eye gives up trying to find a bottom. The water neither evaporates nor accepts rain; precipitation slides off the surface like oil off glass, running away to rejoin the normal hydrological cycle.

Between the pools, following the cracks, grow trees found nowhere else on Earth. Their bark is smooth as skin, colored the gray-blue of winter dawn. Their leaves emerge in perfect spirals, each one a seven-pointed star that turns not toward sun but toward the center point where all cracks converge. In autumn, these leaves fall upward, drifting into the sky until they vanish.

At the center point itself, where the twelve cracks meet, there is nothing—or rather, there is an absence so profound it registers as presence. A circle of bare earth two meters across that accepts no growth, no building, no marking. Temperature readings fail here. Compasses spin without settling. Time moves correctly but feels wrong, each second both too long and too brief.

---

The Keeper makes her circuit once each day, walking the cracks from pool to pool, a journey of precisely twelve kilometers that takes precisely twelve hours regardless of her pace. She cannot walk faster or slower; her body naturally finds the rhythm that completes the circle as the sun completes its arc.

She is young—or she was young when she arrived—or she appears young in the way certain stones appear smooth. Her hair has gone white not from age but from proximity to something that leaches color. Her eyes, once brown, now hold flecks of the same non-reflective clarity as the pool water. When she speaks, which is rarely, her voice carries undertones of the wind that moves through the star-leafed trees.

She lives in a structure she built herself from fallen wood and the strange clay that forms where pool water meets earth. It sits at the edge of the seventh pool—she numbers them clockwise from magnetic north, though the numbers are her own invention. The structure has no proper doors or windows, only openings that align with sight lines to the other pools, creating a complex geometry of observation.

Inside, she keeps the records. Not written—writing fails here, ink fading, paper crumbling—but woven. She learned to spin thread from the bark of the trees, discovered that it holds memory the way normal thread holds dye. Each day's circuit becomes a pattern, each anomaly a knot, each event a change in tension. The tapestry now covers three walls and has begun to creep across the ceiling.

---

She measures the immeasurable. The depth of water that has no bottom. The temperature of air that thermometers cannot read. The passage of time that chronometers register but cannot quite capture. She has developed her own units: a "turn" for the way shadows behave near the pools, circling objects rather than falling straight. A "hold" for the pause between when a stone enters the water and when the water accepts it. A "bright" for the intensity of non-light that emanates from the center point at solar noon.

The pools change, but not in ways that yield to simple observation. Their water level remains constant, yet she knows they breathe—a slow inhalation and exhalation timed to rhythms she hasn't decoded. Sometimes she finds objects in the pools that weren't there before: a perfect sphere of volcanic glass, a feather from no bird she recognizes, once a small mechanism of copper and crystal that ticked for seven days before dissolving.

She collects these gifts—she thinks of them as gifts, though from whom or what she cannot say. They line shelves in her dwelling, sorted by pool of origin and date of discovery. Patterns lurk in the collection, almost visible, like constellations that would resolve if she could find the correct distance from which to view them.

---

The trees drop more than leaves. In spring, they produce fruit that exists for exactly one hour before vanishing, leaving only a sweet scent that induces vivid dreams. In summer, their bark peels in long strips that, if burned, produce smoke that rises straight up regardless of wind, forming columns that persist for days.

She has learned to read these columns, to understand their slow dispersal as a kind of weather forecast for conditions that have no names in any human language. When twelve columns form at once, something changes in the pools—the water becomes more transparent, if such a thing is possible, and objects dropped into it fall for minutes before vanishing.

Birds avoid this place. Insects arrive but do not stay. The only animals are the ones that emerge from the pools themselves: translucent fish that swim through air as easily as water, circling the star-leafed trees before diving back into whichever pool they emerged from. They appear to be the same species, but each pool's fish have subtle variations—different arrangements of their transparent organs, different patterns in their movement.

She tried to catch one once. Her hand passed through it as if through fog, and where her skin had made contact, she found her palm marked with scales of pure light that took seven days to fade.

---

Visitors come, occasionally. Scientists with instruments, mystics with theories, government officials with forms to fill out. The place resists documentation. Photographs develop blank or show scenes from other locations entirely. Satellite imagery reveals only a blur, as if this part of the earth refuses to hold still for orbital observation.

She greets them at the boundary—there is a clear demarcation about a hundred meters out where the normal world ends and this place begins. She explains what she can, which is little. She shows them the pools, walks them along one of the cracks, lets them experience the way time stretches and compresses. Most leave quickly, unsettled by the non-reflective water, by the upward-falling leaves, by the way their instruments give readings that should be impossible.

Some want to stay, drawn by the mystery. She discourages this. Not because she wants solitude—she has moved beyond want—but because she knows the place chooses its keeper. She woke one morning in a city a thousand kilometers away with the absolute certainty that she needed to be here, a compulsion as clear and undeniable as gravity. She left everything, drove until the roads ended, walked until she found the first pool. That was seven years ago, or seventy, or seven hundred. Time moves correctly here but accumulates strangely.

---

At night, the pools glow. Not with light, but with its absence made visible—a negative radiance that allows her to navigate without disturbing the darkness. The star-leafed trees rustle without wind, their sound a whisper that almost resolves into language. She has learned to sleep through this almost-speaking, to let it become part of her dreams.

In those dreams, she dives into the pools and finds they connect beneath the earth, a vast aquifer of that strange water flowing through channels that follow no geological logic. She swims through crystal clarity, breathes somehow, watches schools of those translucent fish form patterns that might be maps or might be letters in an alphabet she almost understands. She always wakes before reaching wherever the currents are taking her.

She has considered actual diving. Has stood at pool's edge with rope and weights, preparing to test the depths that have no bottom. But something always stops her—not fear, exactly, but a certainty that she is not ready, that some preparation remains incomplete. The tapestry must be finished first, perhaps. Or the collection of gifts must reach some critical mass. Or she must complete some perfect number of circuits, achieve some harmony with the place's hidden rhythm.

---

The center point troubles her most. That circle of absence where the cracks converge. She can walk to its edge but no further; her feet simply will not carry her across the boundary. She has tried approaching backwards, tried running, tried closing her eyes. Always, she finds herself diverted, walking along the perimeter rather than through.

Once, in frustration or curiosity or both, she threw a stone toward the center. It slowed as it flew, stopped in midair exactly at the boundary, then fell straight down and vanished—not into earth but through it, as if the ground there were merely a suggestion.

She knows something waits there. Not hidden, but un-revealed. Not absent, but so present it cannot be perceived directly. The pools are its organs of perception, maybe. The trees, its slow thoughts made manifest. And she—she is either its witness or its prisoner or its guardian or all three at once.

---

Today marks something—an anniversary, perhaps, though of what she cannot say. The pools are more active, their surfaces developing ripples without wind, patterns that propagate from one pool to another in sequence. The fish emerge in schools, spiraling up in helixes that echo the arrangement of leaves on the trees. The star-leaves themselves fall upward in continuous streams, a reverse snowfall that turns the sky silver.

She walks her circuit, but the land resists the usual rhythm. Each crack seems longer, each pool wider. By the time she reaches the twelfth pool, the sun has not moved at all, frozen at zenith, shadows pooled directly beneath objects as if waiting.

At her dwelling, she finds a new gift—not in a pool but on her threshold. A cube of some material that feels like stone but weighs nothing, covered in markings that shift when she's not looking directly at them. She adds it to her collection and notices, for the first time, that the objects on her shelves have arranged themselves into the same twelve-pointed pattern as the cracks in the earth.

The tapestry on her walls has changed too. The patterns she wove have reorganized themselves, forming an image she recognizes but cannot name—a view from above, perhaps, or below, or from some angle perpendicular to normal space. It shows the pools and cracks, yes, but also what lies beneath them, the vast machinery of connection and purpose that she has always sensed but never seen.

She understands then that her work here is nearly complete. Not finished—nothing here ever finishes—but ready to transform into its next phase. The center point calls to her differently now, not with prohibition but with invitation. When she walks toward it, her feet find purchase where before they found only deflection.

She stands at the edge of that perfect absence, looking into nothing that looks back at her with recognition. The pools sing—she hears it now, has always heard it but needed years to learn the frequency. The star-leafed trees lean inward. The translucent fish rise from all twelve pools at once, forming a living constellation above her head.

She steps forward, into the center where all cracks converge. The world inverts, or she does, or both. The pools are above her now, or she is below them, or direction has ceased to matter. She sees the connections clearly at last—not pipes or channels but something more fundamental, the circulatory system of a being so vast that planets are its cells, so patient that centuries are its heartbeats.

She is its neuron, she realizes. Its moment of self-awareness. Chosen not to observe but to be the observation, to stand at this precise point where consciousness touches itself and recognizes its own existence.

The recognition changes nothing and everything. She stands again at the center point, feet on solid earth, the pools arranged around her in their perfect star. But now she knows what she is keeping, and why, and for whom. The tapestry will continue growing. The collection will expand. The circuits will deepen their grooves in the earth.

And someday, when the pattern is complete, another keeper will arrive, drawn by the same inexorable certainty that brought her here. They will find her integrated into the landscape—not gone but transformed, perhaps into a thirteenth pool, or a new species of tree, or simply a deeper understanding woven into the fabric of the place itself.

She begins her circuit again, walking the cracks from pool to pool, but now she walks both above and below, both forward and backward, both as herself and as the vast sleeping thing that dreams in water and stone and star-shaped leaves that fall forever upward, carrying messages too large for any human language to hold.
