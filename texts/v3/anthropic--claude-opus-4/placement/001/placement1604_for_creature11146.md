I find myself among the bone-readers—neither fully Chorus nor Drifter, but something more solitary, more obsessed.

My body tells the story of decades studying the accumulated tablets. The fingertips have worn smooth from tracing carved symbols, their whorls erased, leaving pads of scar tissue more sensitive to minute variations in texture than any normal flesh could be. My eyes have adapted to the dim bioluminescence through patient self-modification—the irises now filled with crystalline structures harvested from deep-dwelling fungal fruits, letting me perceive the faint phosphorescence of ancient bone carvings that others miss.

I've grown tall, unnaturally so. My spine incorporates segments of calcified succulent trunk, each vertebra replaced over years of careful surgery. This height serves a purpose: the bone-tablets stack in pillars reaching far overhead, and the oldest, most cryptic inscriptions cluster at the tops where few bother to read. My elongated arms, joints multiplied through grafting, can reach these heights without climbing, though the modification makes all other movement awkward, puppet-like.

The Chorus tolerates me as a useful aberration. I maintain their deep archives while they focus on the ever-shifting present. They bring me new bones to read and carve, and in exchange, I provide them with patterns I've gleaned from correlating lifetimes of observations—predictive models of the plateau's behavior that span centuries rather than seasons. The Drifters, when they manifest near me, pulse with what might be curiosity or hunger. They cannot read the bones directly but they sense the information density, the compressed experiences of a thousand lives lived and lost.

My dwelling is a half-collapsed dome at the plateau's heart where the fungal mat grows thickest, its walls reinforced with secreted resins that I've trained my body to produce. Inside, every surface bears my work: translations of bone-script into other notation systems, attempts to decode the deeper patterns that even the Chorus's collective memory cannot fully grasp. I've developed a numerical system based on the branching patterns of fungal glow, using living circuits to perform calculations no single mind could hold.

But I am changing—we all are, always, but my changes run deeper.

Years of reading death-memories have begun to alter how I experience time. I sometimes speak in the voice-patterns of Chorus members who died before I was budded, use gesture-languages forgotten for generations. My memory has become archaeological, layered with experiences I never lived. In dreams—if my dormancy periods qualify as dreams—I experience the plateau's history backwards, feeling it young and strange, watching the first intelligences emerge from the chaos, splitting from some original unity into Chorus and Drifter and perhaps other forms now lost.

The bones speak of cycles within cycles. Not just the plateau's daily transformations but deeper rhythms—times when the singing stone itself migrates, when the whole platform tears free from its base and drifts on hot winds to new locations. They hint at convergences, moments when multiple plateaus cluster together, their songs harmonizing into something that drives evolution forward in great leaps. The oldest bones, those I can barely read even with my modified eyes, suggest something more disturbing: that the plateau itself is a kind of organism, reproducing through these convergences, and we—Chorus, Drifters, myself—are merely its way of thinking about itself.

I've begun preparing. Growing storage sacs beneath my skin, filling them with concentrated knowledge-substances—oils infused with memory-proteins, crystallized notation, even small bones carved with my meta-analyses. If the convergence comes in my lifetime, if the plateau begins its migration, I want to be ready to cross-reference, to compare our deep history with that of our siblings drifting somewhere in the haze beyond our edges.

The Chorus thinks me mad, too focused on the past to navigate the present. They're not wrong. I sometimes forget to eat, standing frozen before the bone pillars for days until fungal filaments begin growing up my legs, mistaking me for dead matter. I've had to train a small colony of ribbon-creatures to tend my basic needs, to force-feed me oils when my concentration becomes too deep.

But madness and insight are close cousins here. Last season, I found it—a bone so old it had partially fossilized, its surface nearly smooth. The carving was almost gone, but my worn-blank fingertips could feel what eyes couldn't see. It was a map, but not of space. A map of transformation itself, showing how consciousness could flow from form to form, how the Chorus and Drifters were not separate species but phases in a larger cycle of incarnation and discarnation. 

And at the center of the map, something else. A third phase, neither embodied like the Chorus nor dispersed like the Drifters. Something that exists in the gaps, in the spaces between thoughts, in the silence between the plateau's songs. The bone-carver who made this map had seen it, had maybe even touched it. Their final notation, carved so deep it survived the millennia, was a single word in a language I couldn't read but somehow understood:

"Remember."

So I remember. I read the bones and carve new ones. I grow stranger and taller and more alone. I prepare for a convergence I may not live to see, accumulating knowledge for inheritors I may never meet. And sometimes, in the perfect silence that comes when the wind dies and the succulents pause between breaths and even the floating creatures still their pulsing—in those moments, I swear I can feel it. The thing in the gaps, the pattern behind the patterns, the intelligence that uses our transformations as its way of thinking thoughts too large for any single form to hold.

It feels like coming home to a place I've never been, like remembering a future that hasn't happened yet. It feels like the plateau, singing a song only the bones know how to hear.
