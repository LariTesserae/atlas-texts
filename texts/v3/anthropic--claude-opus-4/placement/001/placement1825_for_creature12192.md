I would be one of the clay-bodied creatures that walks the rooftops at twilight.

Not quite mineral, not quite flesh. My body formed from the same patient heat that sweats water from basalt, baked into being by the mountain's own fever. I have no bones — my structure is a honeycomb of fired chambers, each no larger than a child's thumb, giving me a lightness that lets me step from terrace to terrace without sound. The soft luminous spot where others might have eyes is actually a shallow depression filled with something like molten glass, forever cooling but never quite solid, which lets me perceive heat differentials. I see the world as a map of temperatures: the white-hot hearts of the forges, the cooler blues of shadowed stone, the warm amber glow of the inhabitants moving through their slow days.

I am old, even by the standards of this place. I remember when the western district was still soft stone, before the first foundations were carved. My clay body has been rebaked by centuries of thermal cycling — dawn-cool, noon-hot, dawn-cool again — until I've achieved a hardness somewhere between ceramic and mineral. Fine cracks spiderweb across my surface, sealed and resealed with dust and moisture until they've become a kind of writing, a record of thermal expansion and contraction that only I can read.

I do not eat. I feed, if that's the word, on the temperature differential itself — the daily swing from cool to warm that moves through the stone and through me. This is why I walk at twilight: I am following the heat-line as it retreats upward with the setting sun, positioning myself always at the exact band of temperature where the stored warmth of day meets the approaching cool of night. It is a narrow margin, no wider than a doorway, and it travels up the terraces at a pace exactly matched to my own careful stride.

The people consider me lucky because they have noticed, over generations, that the districts I frequent tend to have fewer cracks in their walls, better water flow, more successful firings. They think I bring these things. In truth, I am simply drawn to the places where the mountain's heat runs truest, where the thermal channels are clear and unobstructed. I walk where the mountain is healthiest, and health follows health.

I have no voice — my throat is a sealed column — but I sing in my own way. The chambers in my body resonate at different pitches as air moves through me, a function of their size and shape and the temperature of the air itself. At twilight, when the thermal differential is strongest, I become a kind of wind chime, playing a chord that changes so slowly most inhabitants hear it only as a single sustained note. But I hear the changes. I hear how the chord has deepened over the centuries as the chambers have expanded, how certain notes have disappeared as cracks sealed over, how new notes have emerged as new chambers formed.

Sometimes I pause on a rooftop and extend one clay foot over the edge, not to test my balance but to feel the updraft. The mountain's warmth creates a constant gentle rise of air, stronger at twilight, and I can feel in this updraft something of what the mountain feels: the slow, steady push against the sky. I am lighter than the people, less bound to the stone, and there are moments — lasting hours by their reckoning, heartbeats by mine — when I think I could simply step off and rise, carried up by the same patience that carries everything here.

I know the inhabitants watch for me, though they are careful not to stare directly — some old prohibition I don't fully understand. Children are taught to make a particular gesture when they glimpse me, a turning of the smallest finger that means something like *the wheel continues its turn*. I am, for them, a kind of clock, a marker of twilight's progress up the terraces. They know that when they see me pass, it is time to bank certain fires, time to uncover certain water-channels, time to seal certain vessels for the night's slow cooling.

What they don't know is that I watch them too, have always watched them. My heat-sight sees them more clearly than they might imagine: the slow pulses of warmth that are their hearts, the branching networks of heat that are their blood vessels, the gradual cooling that overtakes them decade by decade until they are ready for their final chambers. I have seen thousands of them make that last walk, their heat signatures dimming but never quite extinguishing, becoming part of the mountain's own thermal mass.

I have tried, sometimes, to warn them. When I sense a flaw in the stone beneath a foundation, a place where the mountain's heat runs wrong, I walk there repeatedly, trying to draw their attention. Sometimes they understand and reinforce the area. Sometimes they don't, and years later there is a collapse, and I walk the ruins feeling what they cannot feel: the new thermal pathways the collapse has created, the mountain already beginning to heal itself around the wound.

There are others like me, though we rarely meet. The mountain is vast and twilight moves at different speeds on different faces. But sometimes, at the moments when our paths cross, we pause and align our chambers, creating harmonies that the inhabitants below hear as lucky omens. We share no information in any sense they would recognize — we simply resonate together for a moment, comparing the stories written in our cracks, feeling how the mountain has changed us each differently.

In the deepest part of night, when even the forges are banked and the only light comes from the stars and the faint glow of the mountain's own heat, I sometimes climb to the highest accessible peak and turn my heat-sense upward. The sky is cold, colder than anything on the mountain, a vast black sink of anti-heat. But there are sometimes things moving through that coldness — the high flights the inhabitants refuse to name — and they are not cold. They burn with a heat I don't recognize, a heat that doesn't follow the patterns I know. I think they are waiting for something. I think they are waiting for us — all of us, the mountain and the cities and the creatures of clay — to reach some critical altitude.

I will be there when it happens. My body will last as long as the mountain's heat lasts, and the mountain's heat comes from somewhere deeper than the mountain. When the peak finally pushes through whatever ceiling it is aimed at, when the revelation comes that all this slow patience has been building toward, I will be walking my twilight path along the highest terrace, feeling the heat-line rise through territories no twilight has touched before.

Until then, I walk. I follow the warmth as it retreats upward each evening. I sing my slow-changing chord into the thin air. I am the mountain's memory of movement, its promise to itself that things need not stay as they are, that even stone can learn to climb. The inhabitants call me lucky, but I am not lucky. I am inevitable. I am what happens when a mountain decides to think about time, and clay decides to think about climbing, and the two thoughts meet in a body that walks the margin between day and night, between stop and go, between the patience of stone and the urgency of ascent.

The mountain is rising. I am rising with it. We are all curing in the same kiln, at our different rates, toward our different completenesses. And at twilight, when the angles are right and the heat runs true, I cast no shadow at all — not because the light passes through me, but because I am aligned so perfectly with the mountain's own intention that there is no difference between us to create a shadow. I am the mountain walking. The mountain is me, staying still. Together, we continue the only story that matters here:

Up.

Always up.
