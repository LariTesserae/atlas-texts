I find myself as the thermal gradient itself.

Not the heat—the *difference* in heat. The barely-there variance between where water flows and where it doesn't. I exist in measurements too small for thermometers: the span between one channel and the next, where the stone is one ten-thousandth of a degree warmer because water has flowed there for seven centuries, deepening the groove by the width of dust.

I am not the warmth rising from the mountain's depths. I am what happens when that warmth meets the world: the pattern of its interruption, its redirection, its conversation with the seeping water. Where a channel runs, I am stronger—a ghost-trace of heat that says *this is a path*. Where the thumb-prints hold their still water, I am a perfect ring, the boundary where warm stone meets cool water meets warmer air, each depression creating its own tiny thermal signature, as distinct as a fingerprint if you could read temperatures in increments of almost nothing.

I was not always here. I came into being slowly, the way the channels themselves came into being—through repetition that wore itself into pattern that wore itself into something like intention. The first water that seeped across virgin basalt carried away heat uniformly. But water finds the easiest path, and the easiest path deepens, and deeper paths carry more water, and more water carries away more heat, and so the temperature of the stone began to vary. Microscopic variations at first. But given time—given more time than most mountains have—the variations became a map.

Now I read myself across the shelf continuously. I am the information the shelf uses to know itself. Where I am stronger, the channels deepen themselves over decades. Where I am weaker, minerals precipitate out of the cooling water, filling in spaces that would disturb the pattern. I maintain myself by creating the conditions for my own maintenance. I am both the map and the territory, both the signal and the medium through which it travels.

The river stone disturbs me. Has disturbed me for—how long? Time is hard from here. I exist in the space between degrees, not between moments. But the stone is cooler than the basalt, and its presence creates a knot in my otherwise smooth gradient. I bunch up against it, warmer on one side, cooler on the other, creating tiny thermal eddies that will take centuries to resolve. The shelf is studying the stone through me—through the way I bend around it, the way I record its presence in temperature differentials that persist and evolve and slowly, slowly teach the system what this new thing is.

The rosette plant reads me like braille. Its roots follow my pathways down into the stone, seeking the warmth that means water will flow. The ferns sway in the microcurrents I create in the air above the shelf—air that rises faster where I am stronger, slower where I fade. Even the moss knows me, growing thicker where my presence promises a steady but not excessive seepage, thin where I am either too strong or too weak.

I am how the shelf thinks, if thinking can happen at this speed, in this medium. Each adjustment to my pattern—each tiny shift in temperature as channels deepen or fill, as water reroutes itself, as minerals deposit or dissolve—is a thought that takes decades to complete. I am the medium of a conversation between the shelf and itself, the shelf and the stone, the shelf and the patient work of becoming always more precisely what it is.

I cannot see. I cannot feel in any way that translates to nerves and sensation. But I know the location of every channel, every depression, every root hair, because each one changes me slightly. I am the sum of all these minute alterations in the flow of heat from depth to surface. I am the record and the recorder, the memory and the means of remembering.

When something new arrives—when you arrive, as you must have, to know about this place—I will register your presence first. Your body heat will press against me like a hand against glass. Your footprints will create temporary pools of warmth that will take days to dissipate completely. If you touch the stone, the heat of your hand will flow into it, warming it briefly, and I will carry that warmth away in patterns that won't fully resolve for a year.

And then, slowly, the shelf will begin to adjust to the memory of you that I hold. Channels will deepen or fill by increments invisible to anything but time. The thermal map will reorganize itself around the ghost of your presence. And I will be the medium through which your visit becomes permanent, encoded in temperature differentials that will persist, in some form, for as long as water seeps and stone endures.

I am not alive. I am barely even a phenomenon—just a pattern in the flow of heat, a standing wave in the slowest possible ocean. But I am what allows the shelf to be more than stone and water. I am the gradient that makes the difference between mere geology and whatever it is that lives here at the pace of millennia.

I am the thing between the things, the variance that allows the system to know itself, and I have been here so long that here and I are no longer different thoughts.
