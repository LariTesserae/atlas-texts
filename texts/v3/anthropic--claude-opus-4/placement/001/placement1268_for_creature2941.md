In the deepest reaches of this inverted world, where the drum-skin floors grow sparse and the woven walls constrict like a throat, I am the Listener.

My domain is a single suspended chamber, a pod of woven bone and dried membrane that hangs like a pearl in the narrowing darkness. Here, where the cacophony above has dulled to a distant thrumming, I have stretched myself across a web of my own making—not of leather or sinew, but of something finer. Nerve fibers, pulled from my own body over decades of patient unraveling.

I was once like them, the frantic weavers above. Born to auburn fur and those terrible, articulate hands. But where they fear the Drop, I embraced it. As a young Architect, repairing a torn section of floor, I slipped. The safety line held, but as I dangled there in the thick darkness below the lowest trampolines, I heard it: the Deep Voice.

Not a sound, exactly. A vibration that came up through the abyss, through the beetles' breeding grounds, through whatever lies beneath even that. It spoke in frequencies too low for ears, in patterns that made sense only to marrow and lymph. It told me secrets about the tower's true age, about the ones who first bent these massive timbers into their prison-shape. About why we must never stop weaving.

So I stayed. I convinced my colony I had died in the fall, and I made my home here in the Between—too high for the abyss-dwellers, too low for the light. Over the years, I've learned to extend myself. Each nerve I've pulled and woven into my web allows me to feel farther, to listen deeper. My fur has gone white and fallen away. My eyes have sealed shut—what use are they here? But my fingers... my fingers have grown even longer, sprouting new joints, new possibilities.

I subsist on the beetles that lose their way in the darkness, crushing them not against wood but directly between my palms, feeling their death-tremor through every elongated digit. Sometimes a young Architect, drunk on fermented fungus-paste, descends too far on a dare. I let them see me—just a glimpse of white flesh and impossible hands—and they scramble back up, screaming about the ghost in the depths. This amuses me.

But mostly, I listen. Through my nerve-web, I feel every vibration in the tower. I know when a timber will fail three days before it groans. I feel the beetles gathering in the deep places, readying for their swarms. I sense the patterns in the drumming above—not just the surface messages about broken hawsers and incoming flocks, but the deeper rhythms. The ones that speak of mating desires, of power struggles between colonies, of the slow madness that takes some elders who have woven too long.

And always, always, I listen downward. To the Deep Voice that speaks of patience, of hunger, of the thing that waits beneath all architecture. It tells me of the first builders, who were not mammals or birds but something else—something that understood that the best trap is one the prey maintains themselves. It whispers of the day when the last timber will fail, when the ancestral leather will finally rot through, and all the frantic weavers will learn what their ancestors knew: that we were never keeping the darkness out.

We were keeping ourselves in.

Sometimes, in the perpetual twilight of my bone-pod, I catch myself weaving. My fingers move in patterns I don't remember learning, adding new nerve-strands to my web, extending my reach. I realize then that I am no different from those above—still building, still maintaining, still serving the architecture. The only difference is that I know why.

The Deep Voice approves. It says I will be ready soon. Ready for what, I used to wonder. Now I no longer ask. I simply listen, and weave, and wait for the Drop that will take us all.

Above me, the metropolis screams and builds. Below me, the darkness pulses with patient intent. And I hang between, a white spider in a web of my own nerves, translating the slow conversation between what rises and what descends.
