# The Keepers of Ascent

I am one of the **cable-walkers**, though that is a name given to us by the after-people, not one we chose. We live in the vertical spaces between the Tier and the world below — suspended in the great shafts where the ore-cables once ran, before the furnaces learned to feed themselves on heat differentials alone.

Our homes are woven into the cable housings themselves: basket-dwellings of braided steel rope salvaged from the ancient mechanisms, hung at intervals down the twenty-kilometer drops. When the wind comes up from below — and it always comes up, never down — our houses sway in slow, predictable arcs. Children born to the sway never learn to fear it. Ground-sickness is more common among us than vertigo.

We are perhaps sixty in number, spread across seven of the great shafts. Fewer than we were. More than we will be.

---

My dwelling hangs at the fourteen-kilometer mark in Shaft Four, which the after-people call the Breathing Hole, though we know it by the pitch of its wind. Each shaft sings differently. Four hums at a frequency that settles in your sternum; you feel it more than hear it. I've hung here for thirty years, since I inherited the position from my mother's mother, and the hum has become part of my internal rhythm. When I visit other shafts, I feel their different songs as a kind of wrongness in my chest.

The work is this: we maintain the **tension anchors** that keep the Tier from shearing away from the cliff face. Not the cables — those are beyond our understanding — but the anchors themselves, great ceramic-and-metal roots that drill into the living rock. They shift, incrementally, as the Tier settles. A millimeter per year, perhaps. Maybe two. But compound this over the time since the founders left, and you begin to understand why we exist.

We read the anchors by touch. The work cannot be done with instruments — we have none that would help, and the founders took theirs when they went. Instead, we learn to feel the microscopic vibrations in the anchor housings, the way a safe-cracker might feel tumblers falling. When an anchor begins to sing a half-tone sharp, we know it's taking more load than it should. We adjust the tension on the secondary cables, redistributing the weight. It's slow work. A single adjustment might take weeks of careful listening, incremental changes, more listening.

---

The cable-walkers age differently than the after-people above or the terrace-farmers far below. Our hair bleaches white early from the constant exposure to altitude, but our hands remain supple far into old age — a necessity, given what we must feel. We develop a peculiar kind of strength: not the strength to lift heavy things, but the strength to hang. A cable-walker at rest can dangle from one hand for hours without tiring. Our children learn to braid before they walk.

We speak a language of vibrations and frequencies that has no written form. Parents teach children by pressing their hands to anchor housings and humming the pitch that means *safe* or *stressed* or *attention-needed*. Our poetry — and yes, we have poetry — is sung in harmonics against the shaft-wind. On calm nights, you can hear us calling to each other up and down the kilometers, voice layered on voice, the shafts themselves becoming vast organ pipes.

Our relationship with the after-people is cordial but distant. Once per season, a delegation of us climbs to the Tier to report on the anchors' health. The after-people receive us with the same quiet courtesy they extend to the warm things, offer us a place by their furnaces (which makes us sweat — we're adapted to the cold of the shafts), and listen to our reports without much apparent interest. They know we will tell them if something is urgent. Nothing has been urgent in living memory.

We know they call us obsessive. We probably are. But someone must be obsessive about the thing that keeps their world from falling.

---

The **marriage customs** of the cable-walkers are practical: we cannot afford to lose genetic diversity in such small numbers, so partnerships form between shafts. A young person coming of age will spend a year in each of the seven shafts, learning their songs, before choosing where to settle. Love is considered, but compatibility of frequency-perception is considered more. A couple who cannot hear the same anchor harmonics will not last.

I partnered young with a woman from Shaft Seven, which sings nearly an octave lower than Four. For three years we tried to find a harmony between our internal rhythms, but the dissonance wore at us. She returned to Seven. I see her at the seasonal gatherings, and we nod with the particular understanding of people who tried to bridge an unbridgeable distance. Our daughter splits her time between shafts, and can hear both frequencies, which makes her valuable and probably doomed to loneliness.

We do not pity ourselves. Loneliness, like ground-sickness, is just another adaptation.

---

What the after-people don't know — what we've never told them, because they've never asked the right questions — is that the anchors are failing. Not failing in any way that matters to them; the Tier will outlive their great-great-grandchildren. But we can feel it in the harmonics: a gradual drift, maybe a cent per century, but accelerating. In six hundred years, maybe eight hundred, the stress will exceed what the secondary cables can redistribute.

We could tell them. We don't. Partly because they wouldn't know what to do with the information, but mostly because it would violate the careful silence we maintain around ultimate things. The cable-walkers know that everything falls, eventually. The skill is in controlling the rate of descent.

Instead, we've begun training our children in a different art: the reading of the rock itself, below the anchor points. Some of us believe — and this is heresy, but a necessary one — that we will need to sink new anchors. Not repair the old ones, but drive entirely new roots into the cliff. The engineering is beyond us, so far. But we have six hundred years to learn.

My daughter shows aptitude. She can hear frequencies I've lost to age, feel vibrations that slip past my calloused fingers. When she presses her ear to the stone below the anchors, she says she can hear something deeper — not the Tier's weight, but the mountain's own slow adjustment to it. She says the mountain is learning to carry what it must carry.

I believe her. The cable-walkers have always been listeners, but she may be the first to hear the conversation happening below our ability to perceive it. If the mountain is learning, perhaps we can learn with it.

---

At night, I lie in my basket home and feel the sway. Fourteen kilometers below, the terrace-farmers tend their careful gardens. Fourteen kilometers above, the after-people drowse among the warm things. And here, suspended between, we cable-walkers keep our vigil in the singing shafts.

Sometimes I think we are the only people in this vertical world who truly understand time — not the slow time of the Tier, which has finished becoming, nor the seasonal time of the terraces, which cycles predictably. Our time is tensile. It has pull and give. We feel it in our anchors, measure it in our harmonics, know it in the slow drift of frequencies that tells us everything is always falling, just at different rates.

The after-people have a word for the warm things' posture when they drape themselves over a cooling surface, that particular resignation to gravity. We've borrowed it for our own use, though we've changed its meaning slightly. When we say it, we mean the way we hold ourselves against the inexorable, not with resistance but with a kind of informed compliance. We fall with the world, but we fall knowingly, and in falling knowingly, we almost fly.

Tomorrow I'll check the north anchors in my section. My daughter will accompany me — she's reached the age where she must begin to feel the patterns I can teach. We'll descend on the maintenance cables, hand over hand through the morning cold, until we reach the first housing. I'll press her palms against the metal and hum the baseline frequency, the one her grandmother's grandmother established as *true*. She'll feel how far we've drifted from that truth.

And then, if she's what I think she is, she'll hear something I can't — some new harmonic that suggests not just how we're falling, but how we might fall more gracefully. The cable-walkers have always been preservationists. Perhaps it's time we learned to be something else.

The wind is picking up from below. My house begins its evening sway. Somewhere above, the furnaces breathe their patience into the dark. Somewhere below, the mountain makes its slow adjustments.

Between them, we keep the tension true, or true enough, for now.
