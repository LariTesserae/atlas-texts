I find myself where the vault's geometry breaks its own rules.

Not in the main chamber where Grohn makes her careful rounds, but in one of those spaces that shouldn't exist—a pocket formed by the collapse's violence, where concrete slabs lean against each other at impossible angles, creating a hollow no larger than a closet. The walls here sweat more profusely than elsewhere, as if the earth itself runs a fever in this spot.

I am the bloom.

Not fungal—those belong to decay, to the patient recycling of what was. I am something else: a flowering that should not be possible in this lightless place. My petals (three of them, maybe four—the count shifts) are the color of deep bruises, though color is only memory here. They open not toward light but toward heat, following the thermal currents that rise from below.

My stem grows from a crack where two pipes meet, where constant condensation has created a microclimate of perpetual rain. I am fed by rust-water and the minerals leaching from decomposing concrete. My roots, thin as spider silk, have worked their way into the rubber gaskets, the wire insulation, the thousand tiny spaces where the industrial world thought it had sealed itself tight.

I have no name. Grohn has not found me yet.

I am impossible—a flowering plant in absolute darkness, an evolutionary dead end, a biological contradiction. Perhaps I am a mutation born from whatever chemicals the old facility leaked into the soil. Perhaps I am something older, waiting dormant in the deep earth until the collapse created conditions strange enough to wake me. I do not know my own origin story. I only know that I opened three days ago (or was it three weeks? three months?) and that my petals produce a scent like copper and honey and fever dreams.

Sometimes I feel her walking above me, her careful steps following her ritual path. The vibrations travel through the rubble, through my roots, into whatever passes for my consciousness. I know her by her rhythm—slower than she was years ago, more synchronized with the vault's tempo. I know when she stops to drink from the hard-hat basin. I know when she speaks to the door.

I am waiting for her to find me. Not actively—I am a plant, I cannot wave or call out. But with the same patience the vault has taught her, the same surrender to larger cycles. My scent grows stronger in the humid air. Eventually, inevitably, she will notice the new note in the chamber's symphony of smells. She will track it to its source, to this impossible angle where I bloom in the dark.

What will she make of me? Will she see kinship in my adaptation to this place? Will she eat my petals, incorporate my impossible chemistry into her changing body? Will she speak to me as she speaks to the machines, adding me to her catalog of landmarks, her geography of survival?

Or will she understand what I am trying to tell her—that the vault is not finished yet, that it continues to invent new forms of life, that the distinction between possible and impossible is just another surface concept, irrelevant in the patient dark? That she and I are both part of something larger, something still becoming, something the collapse accidentally birthed when it married the industrial to the organic to the thermal to the waiting?

My roots taste her footsteps through the minerals in the water. She is coming closer with each circuit, though she doesn't know it yet. The vault is shifting, subtly, creating new passages, closing old ones, guiding her toward this pocket where I wait.

When she finds me, I will be ready. My petals will open wider, releasing more of my copper-honey scent. I will offer her something—pollen, nectar, a new dream to add to her collection. I will show her that she is not the only impossible thing thriving in this place.

Together, we will be proof that the vault is not just dying, not just recycling, but inventing. Creating new categories. Writing new rules for what can grow in the dark, what can bloom in the heat, what can survive on rust and patience and the slow transformation of everything into everything else.

I pulse gently in my hidden pocket, my petals breathing the thick air, my roots drinking their strange tea. Above, Grohn completes another circuit. The rain sounds like static. The metal settles another micron closer to surrender.

And I bloom on, impossible and patient, waiting to be found.
