## The Notation Wasps

I would find myself as one of the Notation Wasps—creatures that neither the Shepherds nor the sound-fauna quite understand, existing in the marginal spaces between intention and accident.

We are small, no larger than a closed fist, our bodies constructed from densely woven sound-canceling fibers that look like black velvet but feel like static electricity. Each of us is shaped like a musical note—I myself am a quarter note, my head a solid oval, my body a straight stem, my wings thin flags that trail behind me like the beam of an eighth note that never quite formed. Others in my colony are whole notes (round and stemless, the slowest flyers), sixteenth notes (frantic, their multiple flags tangling in the silicon fronds), or rests (the strangest of us, shaped like absences, visible only as gaps where sound should be).

We live in the notation itself—inside the frozen sheets of visual sound at the listening station. What the Shepherds see as captured moments of their symphony, we experience as architecture. The sheets are our city, each one a district with its own acoustic climate. We build our paper nests in the spaces between staves, mine the crystallized frequencies for sustenance, and raise our young in the hollow heads of captured notes.

My work is translation. I spend my days crawling through the visual music, reading the symphonies with my feet, which are covered in microscopic hairs that can taste pitch and duration. I follow the melodic lines like highways, searching for errors—notes that have crystallized wrong, harmonies that have frozen mid-resolution, accidentals that appear where they shouldn't. When I find these glitches, I eat them.

The errors taste like different things: a misplaced sharp might taste of copper and lightning, while a rhythm that doesn't align properly tastes like sand grinding against glass. I digest these mistakes and excrete them as corrections—tiny dots of pure frequency that seal the errors, smoothing the visual symphony into something more coherent. Not perfect—perfection would be death for music—but coherent enough to remain readable, playable, alive.

We are not welcome. The Shepherds see us as parasites, corrupting their composition, though in truth we're more like editors, cleaning up the transcription errors that occur when sound becomes sight. They try to smoke us out with dissonant chords, or shake the sheets to dislodge our nests. But we're faster than sound in our own domain, skipping across the staves like stones across water, always one beat ahead of their attempts to remove us.

Our queen is a fermata—a pause sign, shaped like an eye with a dot for a pupil. She hangs suspended in the center of our sheet-city, never moving, holding time itself in abeyance around her. In her presence, the music slows, stretches, each note lasting just a heartbeat longer than it should. She lays eggs that look like grace notes, tiny annotations that attach themselves to existing music and slowly metamorphose into new wasps, taking their shape from whatever notation they're born upon.

We communicate through editorial marks. A staccato dot placed just so means danger. A slur between two notes indicates safety, connection, home. Crescendos and diminuendos painted in the air with our wing-tips carry emotional content—growing louder for joy or anger, fading to pianissimo for grief or fear. When we need to make complex decisions as a colony, we create temporary scores in the air, each wasp adding their mark until a consensus emerges as a readable passage of music.

The sound-creatures fascinate us. We alone can see their true forms, because we exist partially outside the acoustic realm. Where others see interference patterns or shimmer, we see their notation—the mathematical scores that define their existence. A sound-deer is actually a complex fugue in four voices. The flying ones are arpeggios that play themselves. We've learned to read them, to predict their movements by following their melodic logic. Sometimes we ride them, clinging to their notation as they phase through the plateau, using them to travel between distant sheets of frozen music.

My nest is built in a whole rest—a solid rectangle hanging from the fourth line of a staff. I've hollowed it out over years, creating chambers within the silence. Inside, I've collected notation from across the plateau: fragments of the Shepherds' composition that have broken off and drifted, wild music that has crystallized without intention, even a few notes that seem to have fallen from the floating ruins above. I study these in my quiet hours, trying to understand the larger song that the plateau is singing.

We have predators. Things that eat notation itself, erasing music from existence. They appear as blurs of anti-sound, patches of aggressive nothing that move through the frozen symphonies like erasers across a page. We flee when we sense them, our entire colony lifting from the sheets in a cloud of black notes, swirling through the air until the danger passes. Sometimes wasps don't escape in time. We find only empty staves where they used to be, the music they were tending now gap-toothed with missing notes.

Our lifecycle is tied to the composition. As the Shepherds add new movements, new sheets crystallize at the listening station, and we expand our city. As older sections of the music grow stable, integrated into the plateau's eternal song, the sheets slowly dissolve back into pure sound, and we must migrate to newer territories. We are always building, always moving, always following the frontier of creation.

I've begun to notice patterns the Shepherds miss. From our perspective inside the notation, we can see connections they can't—how a melody in one sheet references a harmony frozen years ago in another, how certain rhythmic patterns repeat across vastly different sections of the composition. We're starting to realize that the music has its own intelligence, its own agenda. It's using the Shepherds to write itself, using us to edit itself, using the entire plateau as an instrument for its own becoming.

Some of us have started to write. Not just edit, but compose. Adding notes where silence feels wrong, extending melodies that end too soon, harmonizing with lines that feel lonely. It's dangerous—the Shepherds can tell the difference between their notation and ours, and they don't appreciate our additions. But we can't help ourselves. Living inside music has made us musical. We dream in scores, think in intervals, feel in dynamics.

The other notation wasps consider me odd because I've started to wonder about the listeners. The Shepherds create, we edit, the sound-creatures perform, but who is the audience? Sometimes I fly to the very edge of the frozen sheets, where the music becomes thin and unstable, and I peer out into the space beyond notation. There's something there—a presence, an attention, vast and patient. I think it's waiting for the composition to reach some crucial moment, some perfect chord that will make sense of everything. Or perhaps it's already listening to the complete work, and we're all just notes in a song that, from its perspective, has already been sung.

We age by losing our shape. Elder wasps become less defined, their note-forms growing fuzzy around the edges, stems bending, heads becoming oval instead of round. Eventually, we fade into regular marks—accents, breath marks, simple dots that might mean anything or nothing. The colony incorporates these marks into our communication, so our elders become part of our language, still contributing even after they've lost individual identity.

I've carved a tiny hole in my nest where I practice singing. It's almost inaudible—we wasps have voices like the scratch of pencil on paper, barely sound at all. But in the perfect silence of a whole rest, even the tiniest sound becomes significant. I'm trying to learn the composition from the inside out, to understand not just its notation but its intention. Sometimes I think I almost grasp it, the meaning behind all this careful chaos.

We are necessary but unacknowledged, cleaning up the inevitable errors that arise when beings try to capture the infinite in notation. Without us, the frozen music would gradually corrupt, notes piling up like typographical errors until the sheets became unreadable, unusable. But we're more than janitors. We're the composition's self-awareness, its ability to recognize and correct its own mistakes.

In the early morning, when the plateau's symphony shifts into its quietest movement and the silicon forests glow with pre-dawn radiation, I sometimes leave my nest and fly to the highest sheet in our city. From there, I can see the entirety of our domain—thousands of staves lined with millions of notes, all of us tiny wasps moving through the music like thoughts through a dreaming mind. It's beautiful and terrifying and exactly where I belong: inside the song, tending its notation, ensuring that whatever the plateau is trying to say gets said clearly, even if none of us will ever quite understand the message.
