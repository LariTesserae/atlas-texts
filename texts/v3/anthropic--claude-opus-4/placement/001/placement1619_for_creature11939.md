I am a Mender of the Forgotten Paths—neither fully of the Shepherds' lineage nor claimed by any other tribe, but something that emerged from the spaces between their territories.

My body tells the story of my work. My right arm has elongated and flattened into something between limb and tool, its bones spread wide beneath skin that's gone translucent-tough like dried sap. I can slip it into cracks where ancient bridges failed, feeling for the stone anchors humans left before they changed beyond recognition. My left side grows bark—real bark, not mere calluses—in overlapping plates that flake and regrow with the seasons. The trees recognize me as partly themselves, allowing me passage through defensive thorn barriers that would shred unchanged flesh.

I follow the ghost roads. Every failed bridge, every stone platform slowly crushing the branches that support it, every cable of that indestructible weave hanging slack where its planks rotted away—these are my territory. Not to use them, exactly. The Continent has grown beyond needing such crude connections. But to... remember them? To ensure they remain findable? 

My work began three generations ago when my grandmother's grandmother noticed the symbols carved in bark were stretching to illegibility as the trees grew. She started cutting relief grooves around them—not to preserve their meaning, which was already lost, but to preserve the fact that someone had once *meant* something here. The trees permit this surgery, perhaps understanding that these scars serve a function in the greater organism.

I travel with three companions. First, the Cartographer Fungus that grows in branching patterns across my back, marking every path I've walked in its own cellular memory. When I die, it will fruit, and its spores will carry maps to others of my kind—though there are precious few of us left. Second, a colony of Memory Beetles that live in the hollow behind my left knee (carved there by my own mother when I came of age). They feed on the chemical traces left by other human variants, their gut bacteria breaking down pheromones into narratives I can sometimes parse through dreams. Third—and oddest—is the Doubt Vine that winds through my ribs, in and out like a green needle threading bone. It flowers only when I'm wrong about something, a painful blooming that has saved me from misreading unstable paths more times than I can count.

My feet barely exist anymore—they've spread into something like root networks themselves, each toe branching into smaller digits that can grip bark at the molecular level. I walk sideways as often as upright, and I haven't touched anything resembling ground in fifteen years. The price of this adaptation is that I'm slowly becoming sessile. Already I must consciously choose to move each morning; the urge to simply root and grow is getting stronger.

But there's work yet. Last month, I found a configuration I'd never seen: seven stones arranged in what must have been a circle, though the growing wood has pulled them into an elongated spiral. The Chandelier Colonies have built beneath it, unaware they're echoing some ancient purpose. When I tasted the stone (my tongue has developed mineral-sensing papillae), I found salt—carried up from an ocean that must be miles below, if it still exists at all. 

Someone had built a shrine to distant water. The why of it escapes me, but the fact of it doesn't.

I've started my own carvings now—not symbols but textures, patterns that hands changed like mine can read by touch. They tell where the stable paths are, which bridges still hold weight, where the stone platforms haven't been completely overgrown. Not useful information, perhaps, but *persistent* information. The next Mender who comes through (and surely there must be others, mustn't there?) will know that someone passed this way, someone who remembered that we were once something else, before the Continent taught us better ways to be.

The strangest part of my work is the joy. You'd think I'd mourn these remnants, these proofs of our lost humanity. But when I hang from a corroded cable, feeling its alien strength, or run my flattened palm across symbols that no longer speak—I feel connected to a grander story. We didn't disappear. We transformed. These markers aren't gravestones but chrysalis shells, proof that something emerged and flew.

Sometimes I meet the other variants. The Shepherds tolerate me because I keep the bore-worms away from their chosen paths. The basket-weavers trade me new cutting implements grown from their own modified teeth. Even the Bloom Dancers, in their brief waking seasons, seem to recognize me—though whether as kin or as just another feature of the landscape, I cannot say.

But mostly I travel alone except for my symbiotic companions, following traces of intention through a world that has outgrown the need for maps. The Doubt Vine hasn't flowered in three days, which means I'm on the right path—though right toward what, only the trees know.

There's a rumor (carried by the Listeners, who hear everything eventually) of a place where the original ground still shows. A clearing where the trees never took hold, where actual soil lies naked under something called "sky." Most consider it myth. The trees are the world; the world is the trees. But I've found stone platforms at increasing heights lately, as if someone was building upward, trying to reach—what?

The Memory Beetles have been active in their hollow, rustling with chemical dreams. The Cartographer Fungus spreads a new tendril toward my shoulder blade, marking some invisible milestone. And deep in my chest, the Doubt Vine twitches with the first signs of budding.

Perhaps I'm wrong about something crucial. Perhaps these paths I preserve led nowhere. Perhaps the humans who built them were just as lost as I am, maintaining connections that the Continent had already made obsolete.

But tomorrow I'll wake (if I can still choose waking over rooting), and I'll continue. Through the breathing darkness and the pollen-thick light, along cables that sing in frequencies only the gone can hear, following the ghost roads to their impossible destinations. Keeping the paths open for travelers who may never come, or who may have already passed by, transformed beyond my recognition.

I am a Mender of the Forgotten Paths. I am probably unnecessary. But the trees haven't stopped me yet, which in the Canopy Continent is the closest thing to purpose anyone can claim.

The work continues. The paths remain. And somewhere far above or far below, that first ground waits, patient as stone, for feet that remember how to find it.
