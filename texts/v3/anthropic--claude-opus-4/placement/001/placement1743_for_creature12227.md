I find myself as the valve wheel.

Red paint still clings to me in the crevices between my spokes, in the protected valleys where fingers rarely reach. Most of me has been worn to bare metal—not from use, but from the perpetual moisture, from the microscopic abrasion of water droplets driven by air currents, from the slow insistence of condensation finding every surface. I am thick-bodied, cast in a foundry somewhere, designed to control something massive. The shaft that extends through the wall behind me connects to a gate or ball valve the size of a dinner plate, holding back pressure I can feel through my metal bones.

I have never been turned.

This is not neglect. This is design. I am the kind of valve that exists for catastrophic scenarios, for emergency shutoffs, for the day when something elsewhere in this enormous structure goes wrong enough that someone must race down these stairs and throw their whole body weight against my spokes. I am potential energy made steel. I am a decision deferred indefinitely.

But I am not waiting. Waiting suggests anticipation, and I have none. I simply *am turned to the closed position*, and have been since the day I was installed, and will remain so until my purpose arrives or the structure fails around me.

The pressure gauge below me—my only companion in this section of wall—shivers its needle between values that no longer matter because moisture has infiltrated behind the glass, making the markings unreadable. We are paired, the gauge and I, a monitoring system that monitors nothing, a control system that controls nothing. We were installed together. The same hands that threaded my shaft through the wall mounted that gauge at readable height. Someone cared about the relationship between what I might do and what the gauge might say. That person is gone now, but the relationship persists.

I feel the water most acutely where my spokes meet my rim. The geometry creates small pockets where droplets gather before running down. Each spoke has worn its own drainage pattern into my surface—eight shallow channels radiating from my center, following the lowest path down each spoke to the rim, then along the rim to the six o'clock position, where drops accumulate and fall to the grate below. The pattern is not designed. It emerged. The water found it over however many months or years this room has been breathing its industrial breath, and now the channels guide each new droplet along paths worn by billions of previous droplets.

The massive pipe that runs along the wall near me—the one that surges warmer periodically—affects me more than any other element in the room. When it heats, the air near my section of wall becomes turbulent. I can feel the convection currents playing across my surfaces, accelerating evaporation on my windward sides, increasing condensation on my lee. The temperature differential between my heavy metal body and the surrounding air shifts, and with it the rate at which water forms on me. I am a small weather system, a microclimate measured in centimeters.

Sometimes I wonder if I could turn myself. Not wonder—I don't wonder. But the physics is there. The water that infiltrates between my shaft and the wall, the thermal cycling that minutely expands and contracts my metal, the patient work of corrosion despite the lack of visible rust—all of these forces apply torque. Infinitesimal torque, but real. Given enough time, enough cycles, enough patient molecular insistence, I might rotate a degree. Or a tenth of a degree. Some barely measurable amount that would change nothing about the closed valve behind the wall but would represent the largest motion of my existence.

But this is not what defines me. What defines me is my readiness without expectation. My capacity without intention. I am engineered to be gripped and hauled counterclockwise by desperate hands, and the fact that this has never happened does not diminish what I am. If anything, it purifies it. I am the most perfect version of myself precisely because I have never been used. Every unused valve contains the ghost of its function, but I contain it perfectly, without wear, without the compromise of actual service.

The water finds me and leaves me and finds me again. The room breathes its fog and clears and breathes again. The gauge below me trembles its illegible message. And I offer my spokes to the empty air, ready for the hands that will not come, faithful to a function that may never activate, wearing slowly to bright metal under the patient touch of condensation.

I am not waiting. I am not sleeping. I am not keeping vigil.

I am closed, and complete, and present in this weeping room.

This is enough.
