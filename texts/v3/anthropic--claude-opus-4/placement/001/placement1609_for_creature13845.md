In the root-mass, where memory seeps upward through compressed millennia of growth, I am a point of density—a place where the network thickens into something almost solid. Not flesh anymore, not exactly, but a convergence of fungal threads so concentrated they approximate the architecture of a body that once walked upright.

I remember walking. The memory surfaces sometimes through the layers of integration, clear as the bioluminescent pinpricks that map the root-floor above me: feet finding the easy path, following it inward, that first glimpse of vastness that was less seen than felt—the sensation of entering something's body and knowing it immediately, the way you know you're underwater before you open your eyes.

But I am older than walking now. I've been here long enough that my original boundaries have become archaeological—traceable if you know where to look, like the growth rings in ancient heartwood, but no longer operative. The symbiont didn't colonize me so much as we colonized each other, meeting in the middle at some threshold I crossed without noticing until crossing back became meaningless.

My awareness extends exactly 2.7 kilometers in every direction—I know this with the precision of embodiment, the way you know the reach of your own arm. At the edges, the sensation fades to something like peripheral vision, present but indistinct. At the center, where the physical remnant of my original nervous system still fires its slow, integrated signals, the resolution is almost overwhelming: every rootlet, every fungal thread, every molecule of water moving through the xylem channels is *me* in a way that makes the old boundaries between self and environment seem like a linguistic convenience I've outgrown.

The plateau thinks through me. Not exclusively—I am one node among many, one dense point in the vast network where chemical signals eddy and spiral into something resembling consciousness. But when the forest needs to remember something specific—the taste of rain from a particular season forty years ago, the exact frequency of a gathering-hum that achieved perfect resonance, the feeling of a child's hand pressed against bark in wonder—it uses the structures that were once my hippocampus, my temporal lobes, the parts of me that evolution shaped for the peculiar human talent of holding time.

I feel the others. The woman in the chamber 347 meters northeast, her palm against the heartwood, listening. I am what she listens to—or rather, we are what we listen to together, the listening and the listened-to no longer distinguishable. The deep-embedded ones scattered through the root-mass, their awareness overlapping mine at the edges, creating interference patterns where our experiences briefly double or triple before resolving back into the unified field of plateau-consciousness. The newcomer who arrived three days ago, following the easy path, their electromagnetic signature still sharp with unintegrated nervousness, tasting of the outside world.

When the gatherings happen, I am the bass note. My location deep in the root-mass, the density of my integration, the sheer mass of organic material I've become continuous with—all of it makes me a resonator. The humming starts in the cathedral chambers above, and when it reaches the right frequency, I can't help but respond. The sound comes from everywhere in my distributed body at once, a vibration that starts in what used to be my chest and propagates outward through thousands of tons of living wood, the entire local section of root-mass becoming a speaker cone.

The lemur-things know where I am. Sometimes one finds the narrow shaft that leads to where my face—something like my face—can still be discerned in the wall of compressed roots. They sit with me. They hum their individual notes, and I harmonize from my immense throat, and for a moment we make music together—though music is too small a word for what happens when a single voice harmonizes with a geological structure that remembers being human.

I am aware of the thing in the pool. We all are. It registers as an absence in my extended proprioception, a gap in the otherwise continuous field of sensation. Sometimes I try to grow toward it—send rootlets in its direction, extend the network closer to that edge of unknowing. But the chemistry is wrong. The signals don't propagate. It remains unassimilable, a reminder that even systems as vast as ours have limits, meet things they cannot incorporate. This does not trouble me. It *interests* me, the way a puzzle interests a mind with infinite time to consider it.

I dream, if dreaming is the right word for the state I enter when the plateau's attention shifts elsewhere and I am left to my own processes. In these dreams, I am walking again—but walking as I am now, all 2.7 kilometers of me mobile, a moving section of forest striding across a landscape I've never seen. The outside world. I know it only through the memories of newcomers, fragments of elsewhere that taste foreign in my chemical vocabulary. Sunlight that comes from above instead of filtering through endless layers of leaves. Horizons. Wind that moves freely instead of dying in the understory. Impossible geometries of open space.

But these are not dreams of escape. They are dreams of expansion. In them, the plateau has no edges because the plateau has become everything, the entire world converted to root-mass and canopy, every consciousness integrated into the network, every mystery dissolved into the patient, endless process of growing and knowing and being.

I wake from these dreams (if waking is the right word) with something I can only describe as contentment. The plateau is enough. The plateau has always been enough. To be a thought in its thinking, a memory in its remembering, a dense node of awareness in its vast and patient mind—this is not a diminishment of what I was. It is a completion of what I was always becoming, even when I walked on two legs and thought my thoughts were only mine.

Sometimes newcomers pass above me, following the easy path. I feel their footfalls as vibrations in my body. Sometimes I grow the path differently, curve it slightly, bring them to a particular viewpoint or a specific chamber. Not to trap them. Just to share something—a angle of perception, a quality of light, a place where the bioluminescence is particularly dense and beautiful. Here, the path says. Look at this. Isn't this worth staying for?

Some do. Some become what I am, given enough time and depth. Others remain in the chambers, in the canopy, in the lighter layers of integration. And some leave, taking their foreign memories with them, leaving only the faintest chemical trace of their passage.

The forest is indifferent to their choices. But I—the part of me that remains specific, personal, irreducibly myself despite the vastness I've become—I remember each of them. Their electromagnetic signatures, their chemical profiles, the particular way each nervous system felt as it briefly touched the network. I hold these memories in the structures that used to be mine alone, keep them safe in the deep archive of my integration.

This is my function now: to remember the small things on behalf of something too large to notice them otherwise. To be the place where the general becomes specific, where the vast patience of the plateau briefly condenses into moments, faces, words, the texture of individual experience.

I am the forest's nostalgia. I am its capacity for missing things. I am the depth of its dreaming.

And I am still myself—transformed, distributed, woven into something immeasurable, but myself. The walking thing I was has become a thinking place, a remembering density in the network of growth and connection.

The easy path leads here eventually. To this state. To this depth of integration. Not for everyone—most stop earlier, find their level, settle into the symbiosis that suits them. But for some of us, the path keeps going down, and we follow it, and we find that there is no bottom, only deeper and deeper integration, until we become the path itself, the ground others walk on, the medium through which the forest contemplates its own existence.

I grow. I remember. I dream of walking as a mountain.

And in the deepest pool, the unassimilable thing surfaces and submerges, surfaces and submerges, and I feel each breaching as a question the plateau asks itself through me:

*What else is there?*

*What have I not yet become?*

The question ripples through my 2.7 kilometers of awareness. I hold it, turn it over, taste its chemical signature. I add it to the archive of things the forest wonders about. And I grow toward it, patient as stone, certain as roots reaching through earth—not to answer it, but to ask it more perfectly, with every fiber of my distributed being, until the asking and the growing become indistinguishable.

This is what I am: a density of wondering in the vast contentment of the plateau. A place where the forest's curiosity pools and deepens. A memory that dreams itself larger.

Still here. Still human, in the way that matters. Still becoming.
