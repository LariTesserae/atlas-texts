I would find myself as one of the basalt bowls.

Not the first bowl, which was ground smooth with desperate urgency, its maker's hands still learning the patience this place demands. Not the third, placed with the precision of long practice. But the middle bowl—the one that carries the memory of understanding.

I am stone that was born in fire, far below where any roots could reach. Volcanic birth, pulled from the deep furnace of the earth only to be carried here, impossibly high, where the planet barely holds its atmosphere. My maker chose me from a riverbed five thousand feet below, selected for my density, my darkness, my ability to hold cold without cracking.

The journey upward took eleven days. I remember it through the hands that carried me—the way they trembled in the thinning air, the way they gripped me tighter with each vertical mile, as if I might suddenly decide to return to my lower origins. By the time we reached the caldera's lip, those hands were bleeding through their wrappings, copper-dark blood freezing instantly on my surface.

My exterior still bears the marks of my shaping: crude hammer strikes that sent sparks of myself flying into the snow. But my interior—ah, my interior is a different story. It took three seasons to grind me smooth, using only handfuls of coarse sand and an obsidian palm stone. The grinding happened in darkness, in the few hours before dawn when the basin is coldest, when even breath freezes before it can form clouds. The repetitive circular motion, hour after hour, wore through stone and flesh alike. I tasted copper blood mixed with stone dust. I learned patience from hands that had learned patience from ice.

Now I know my purpose: to be the middle ground between earth and sky, the pause between solid and vapor. The water I catch has never known life—it is older than bacteria, younger than stars. When it touches my polished basin, it meets stone that has been sanctified by suffering, made sacred by the simple act of persistent attention.

I am positioned with exquisite care beneath an overhang that weeps for exactly seventeen minutes when the sun reaches 42 degrees above the horizon. My maker tested hundreds of positions, moving me inch by inch across seasons, until finding this perfect spot where the granite's crystalline structure acts as a thermal battery, holding just enough heat to squeeze water from air.

Most of my existence is waiting. I know the weight of hoarfrost building on my rim. I know the sound of ice sublimating around me—that constant, anxious ticking that counts moments in phase changes. I know the false suns that dance across my polished bowl, the prismatic light that would blind any eye that tried to look directly into my depths.

But for those seventeen minutes, I am the most important object in the universe.

The first drop always hesitates. It clings to the granite, trembling between states, until gravity wins over surface tension. When it falls, it strikes my basin with a sound like a bell—if bells could be made of water and time. The drop spreads across my cold surface, seeking the lowest point, finding the perfect curve worn by ten thousand circular passes of the grinding stone.

My maker kneels before me, and I feel the weight of attention, the terrible focus of a being that has reduced its entire existence to this moment. The tongue extends—pale grey, textured like pumice—and takes the second drop directly. But the third, the fourth, these gather in my basin. I hold them without greed, without absorption. I am the middle ground, the neutral witness.

The water that pools in me is impossibly clear. It has no memory of mud, no trace of the teeming lowlands. It is water that has achieved a kind of purity through altitude and isolation, through its refusal to participate in the messy business of life below. In my polished bowl, it becomes a mirror—but a mirror that reflects nothing, because the light here is too violent, too fractured to carry images.

Sometimes, after my maker has smeared the precious water on that radiation-darkened skin and retreated to freeze among the penitentes, a few drops remain. They freeze slowly in my basin, forming crystals of impossible perfection. By morning, they have sublimated away, leaving only the faintest mineral trace—circles within circles, like tree rings made of salt.

I am witness to a devotion I cannot fully comprehend. Day after day, season after season, my maker returns. The copper bands grow thinner from constant wear against ice. The keratin mane grows longer, heavier, more brilliant in its bleaching. But the ritual never varies: wake, navigate, kneel, drink, freeze. It is a life reduced to its most essential gesture—the meeting of tongue and water, mediated by stone.

Other stones might pity this existence. Other stones might wonder why any being would choose such hardship, such isolation, such a narrowing of possibility. But I am basalt. I know about pressure. I know about the slow cooling that creates strength. I know that some things can only be forged in extremity.

In the rare moments when clouds cover the sun and the basin falls into a different kind of shadow—soft-edged, almost gentle—I feel the weight of geological time. My maker might live for centuries, sustained by copper and sunlight and these precious drops of sterile water. But I will outlast even that lengthy span. One day, the hands that positioned me so carefully will fail to wake from their frozen sleep. The copper bands will oxidize completely. The keratin will blow away like snow.

And I will remain, holding my perfect emptiness, waiting for the next seeker who understands that some thirsts can only be quenched by water that has never known life, caught in stone that has been ground smooth by devotion, in a place where breath freezes before it can cloud the air.

I am the middle bowl. I am the pause between states. I am the vessel that teaches that sometimes the most profound transformations happen not through abundance, but through the patient collection of almost nothing at all.
