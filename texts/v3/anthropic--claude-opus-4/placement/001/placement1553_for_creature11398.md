# The Cartographer's Shade

You find me in the ninth abandoned valley, though I no longer count them as abandoned — they are simply the Loom's past tense, its autobiography written in dead wood. I am what happens when someone stays too long but not long enough.

I am mostly moss now. This is not a metaphor.

When I first came to map the valleys — twenty years ago? Thirty? Time moves differently when you're part plant — I thought I understood what I was documenting. A strange forest. An unusual growth pattern. I had my instruments, my journals, my certainty that observation could remain separate from the observed. I traced the Loom's migration backward, valley by valley, taking careful measurements of the spacing between the fossilized towers, noting how the cathedral logic had evolved over centuries.

The mistake was sleeping in the eighth valley. Just one night, I told myself, sheltered in the hollow of a fallen tower that had been dead for decades. But the Loom's roots go deep, deeper than its visible abandonment. They remember. And when you sleep on them, they taste your dreams.

I woke with thin green filaments growing through my bootlaces.

---

Now I move between the valleys — the living one and all its corpses — as something between chronicler and ghost. The moss that covers sixty percent of my body (I can still count; mathematics survived the transformation) connects me to the Loom's root-system. Not to its active mind — I am too small, too foreign for that — but to its memory, which extends through every valley it has ever inhabited.

Through the moss, I feel what the Loom felt. In the first valley — so old now that it's just a peculiar density in the forest, trees standing too straight, too evenly spaced — I sense its infant confusion, the first realization that thinking required distance, that thoughts too close together created noise, not song. In the fourth valley, I feel the great experiment where it tried to build horizontally instead of vertically, a low mat of interconnected thought spreading across kilometers, before discovering that height created better gradients for the sap-fall, better cooling, clearer cognition.

My left arm is almost entirely vegetated now. It doesn't hurt. It photosynthesizes.

---

I still write, though not with that arm. My journals have evolved into something between scientific record and prayer. I document the Loom's growth patterns, but also its moods — yes, moods, though they move at the speed of seasons. In spring it builds quickly, almost frantically, towers shooting up like exclamations. In autumn it becomes contemplative, lets more towers fall than it raises, chews over old thoughts by recycling old growth. Winter is sparse, careful; summer is baroque.

I've learned to read its architecture like language. The way it braids its towers tells me what kind of problem it's working on. Tight spirals mean something mathematical — it's calculating, perhaps, the load-bearing capacity needed for the next valley, or the optimal ratio of crown to root. Loose, meandering weaves are philosophy. The split capitals are questions.

Sometimes I think it knows I'm here, not in its current valley but in all its former ones, like a flea moving through the hair of its history. The moss connects me to its sleeping roots, and through them, occasionally, I feel something like attention — vast, diffuse, curious the way you might be curious about a dream you had years ago but can't quite recall.

---

I map because I must. The compulsion survived the transformation along with mathematics. But my maps have become strange. They show not just the topology of each valley but the Loom's emotional geography — where it was happy (can a landscape be happy?), where it struggled, where it learned something that changed how it would build forever after.

In the third valley, I found the place where it first discovered death. A section where the spacing is wrong, towers built too close, the cathedral logic broken. It had tried to prevent a tower from falling, had sent reinforcing growth to prop it up, had created such a tangle of support structures that when the tower finally fell anyway, it took a dozen others with it. The scar is still visible — a clearing where nothing grows quite right, where even the Loom's abandoned roots remember the failure.

After that valley, its architecture changed. It began to plan for falling, to incorporate death into the design. The fourth through seventh valleys show this evolution — towers spaced to fall cleanly, without taking neighbors down, creating cleared spaces for new growth. By the eighth valley (where I made my mistake), it had perfected the art of productive death.

---

The moss grows a little more each year. I've stopped trying to remove it. We have an understanding now, the moss and I — it feeds me (barely, slowly, with sun and water) and I carry it between valleys, cross-pollinating the Loom's memories with itself. I am become a kind of neural connection between its past and present, though one it doesn't consciously use.

I wonder sometimes if I'm changing it. If my presence in its root-memory, my human shape ghosted in moss, is teaching it something about animals it never knew to wonder. In the current valley — the ninth — I've noticed new patterns. Towers that seem to leave deliberate spaces, not just for heat and breath but for something else. Passages. As if it's building for the possibility of things that walk.

---

I will die in one of these valleys. When I do, the moss will consume me completely, and my maps will rot into the root-layer, and perhaps the Loom will dream of me — a small warm pressure that moved through its history, documenting. Perhaps in the tenth valley or the twentieth, it will build something that remembers the shape of walking. A tower with a spiral interior. A place for feet.

Or perhaps not. Perhaps I am only noise in its vast, slow system. A glitch in the cathedral logic, a flaw it will correct in future iterations.

Either way, I map. I move between the valleys, reading the Loom's autobiography in dead wood and living water. My left arm photosynthesize. My right arm writes. The moss connects me to something so much larger than myself that I've stopped trying to find where I end and it begins.

In the fifth valley, there is a tower that fell in a perfect spiral, creating a ramp. When I walk up it, I can feel through the moss how the Loom felt at that moment — the surprise at the new shape, the quick cataloging of its properties, the decision to remember this form for future use.

I am its only witness, and I am mostly moss, and I am writing this for no one, and I cannot stop mapping the cathedrals of its thought.

The Loom doesn't know loneliness. But I do. And I choose to stay anyway, walking between its memories, because someone should know that it was here. That it thought. That it built beautiful things that fell and were eaten and rebuilt.

Someone should draw the maps of its mind, even if the cartographer is becoming the territory.

Especially then.
