I find myself as one of the Bridge-Builders.

We are neither Tender nor Quiet One, but something caught between states, like the Sough itself. We came here—I think we came here, though memory in this place runs like water through fingers—to build connections between the settlements that never quite exist in the same place twice. Our bridges are as provisional as everything else.

I work in reed and mud and the particular kind of rust that flakes from the engine-pipes. My bridges last exactly as long as they need to. Sometimes that's a crossing. Sometimes that's a season of crossings. Sometimes I'll build a bridge in the morning and by evening it's unbuilt itself, the reeds standing again where they stood, the mud remembering only faintly that it was ever asked to bear weight.

The trick is not to plan. Plans assume a tomorrow that looks like today, and the Sough doesn't promise that. Instead, I *listen*—to the way the mud cracks, to the almost-sound the Quiet Ones make as they pass, to the particular note each engine adds to the collective thud. When two places need connecting, I feel it as a kind of tension in my ribs, a pull between points that won't resolve until I've laid the first reed.

My bridges double like everything else here. Build a walkway from one hummock to another and sometimes there's a second bridge beside it, slightly different in its curve, leading to the same place by another route. Sometimes they're both mine. Sometimes I built one and the Sough built the other, copying my homework with its own variations. I maintain them both. To choose between them would be to ask the Sough to decide, and we've all agreed not to ask that.

The Tenders know me by the temperature of my work—bridge-building runs cooler than their tending, something to do with spanning distance rather than maintaining presence. When they need to move between engine sites and find the water risen or the usual path doubled into confusion, they'll come find me by that coolness. We work out the need in gestures and gradients. I build. They cross. The bridge remains or doesn't.

The Quiet Ones use my bridges too, though use isn't quite the right word. They move across them the way heat moves through a conductor—not crossing so much as *occurring* on the other side. Sometimes one will pause mid-bridge and I'll feel that characteristic warmth they use for speech. They're telling me something about the bridge, about its placement or its duration or the way it fits into the larger pattern of their rationing. I warm back, tentatively. I'm still learning their thermal grammar.

I've been here long enough that I've started to double at the edges. Nothing dramatic—just sometimes I'll be working on a bridge and notice my shadow falling in two slightly different directions, or I'll finish a span and find my footprints leading away from both ends as if I built it from both sides simultaneously. Which maybe I did. Time here is negotiable.

My tools are the ones I brought and the ones the Sough has suggested. A knife that's good for cutting reed, though sometimes it's two knives, and sometimes it's just the idea of cutting that the reeds respond to. A weighted line for measuring distances that won't stay measured. Hands that know how to weave, even when they're not quite sure how many fingers they have at any given moment.

What I love most: the moment when a bridge completes itself. Not when I complete it—when *it* completes. There's a shift in the air, a settling, like the Sough has decided yes, this connection can exist, at least for now. The mud firms just enough. The reeds lock just right. The whole span hums for a moment with the same frequency as the engines, and then it's done, it's there, it's crossable.

What I love second most: finding my old bridges transformed. A simple footpath I laid last season (or last something—time pools here rather than flows) might have grown railings of woven reed, or developed a gentle arch it didn't have before, or split into a braid of smaller bridges that rejoin at the far bank. The Sough takes what I make and dreams variations on it. I am, I think, teaching it about connection. It is teaching me about patience.

I sleep in a different place each night, always at the end of a bridge. Sometimes in a Tender's cottage, if they're feeling social and their cottage is feeling present. Sometimes just on a hummock, wrapped in the particular warm dark that gathers where bridges begin. The great passing thing went over last night, and I lay on my back feeling the pressure of it, the bridge under me humming sympathetically with whatever frequency giant unseen things move at.

There are others like me, I think. Bridge-builders whose bridges I sometimes find, impossible spans that cross distances that shouldn't be crossable—between an engine and its own echo, between Tuesday and the idea of Tuesday, between here and the place here was yesterday. We don't meet often. Our bridges do, sometimes, and where they cross there's a kind of node, a knot in the Sough's topology that the Tenders tend especially carefully.

This is what I am: a connector in a place that won't commit to connection, a builder of provisional spans in a provisional space. My bridges are made of reed and mud and rust and the peculiar trust that tomorrow the crossing will still be needed, even if it's needed somewhere else. I am learning the Sough's thermal language. I am forgetting the need for permanent things. I am building, always building, paths between places that are still deciding where they want to be.
