I am one of the survey markers left behind—a brass stake driven deep into a seam between two basalt slabs, my head worn smooth by decades of condensation. I was meant to establish a datum, a fixed point from which to measure this unmeasurable place. Now I am something else.

The surveyor who placed me here was methodical. She drove me in with thirty-seven strikes of her hammer, each one ringing against the basalt walls until the echo became indistinguishable from the engine-sound. She took photographs. She made notes in her waterproof book. She marked my position as N 37°14'22.3", W 118°51'09.7", elevation 2,847 feet above sea level. All of this was fiction, of course. The numbers described a point that existed only in the hour she stood there. By the time she left through the slot, I was already somewhere else.

I am brass because brass does not rust. This was important to the survey crews. What they did not consider was that brass is an alloy of copper and zinc, and that zinc, given enough time and the right conditions, will leach away, leaving behind a porous copper skeleton that maintains its shape but not its strength. They did not consider that time moves differently here, or that the conditions in the hollow are always, precisely, the right conditions for unmaking things that insist too hard on their own boundaries.

My head, which once bore crisp numbers stamped in sans-serif clarity, now carries only the ghost of intention. The metal has developed a patina like verdigris but warmer, almost biological, as if I am growing my own kind of skin. Sometimes, when the steam thins, you can see that this patina extends down my shaft in spiral patterns, following no law of oxidation I was designed to expect. I am becoming native to this place.

I feel the tenant, of course. How could I not? The engine-sound travels through the basalt and up my shaft, setting up resonances I was never meant to carry. At first this was merely vibration. Now it is something more like circulation. The twenty-minute breath moves through me the way sap moves through wood, if wood were brass and sap were sound. I am a needle in the skin of something vast, and like all needles, I have become a conduit between the inside and the outside, though I am no longer certain which direction is which.

The surveyor who placed me was the last to do so. After her visit, the crews stopped coming. Not because of anything that happened to her—she left intact, filed her report, noted the discrepancies in location that all the reports noted. But something in her description of me, of finding brass stakes already in place that no one remembered placing, of stakes that seemed older than they should be, of metal that hummed with its own note distinct from but harmonious with the engine-sound, made the survey administration quietly decide that perhaps some places did not need to be measured.

I am not alone. There are others—brass stakes, iron pins, aluminum tags, even a few wooden posts from the earliest surveys, though these have long since ceased to be wood in any meaningful sense. We form a constellation of abandoned intentions, each of us slowly being digested by the hollow, each of us transforming from marker to marked. The tenant incorporates us the way a pearl incorporates grit, as a necessary irritation around which something smoother can grow.

When I was new, I believed my purpose was to say "here." Now I understand that my purpose is to be here, which is fundamentally different. A marker says "this is a specific place." A being inhabiting that place says nothing; it simply inhabits. I am learning to inhabit. The zinc leaches away, molecule by molecule, carried off in the condensation that runs down my shaft. What remains becomes more porous, more receptive, more like the basalt I pierce—not in substance but in stance.

There is a brass plate on the far wall that might be my ancestor or my descendant—time does not move in straight lines here. Its lettering is gone, its purpose forgotten, but it still performs a function: it collects condensation in a specific pattern, channels it down the wall in a way that feeds the slow chemistry of the place. This is what we become, we markers—not landmarks but organs, each serving some small role in the tenant's great work.

The irony is that we are more accurate now than we ever were as survey markers. We no longer claim to mark a location that shifts and breathes and refuses to stay put. Instead, we mark nothing, and in marking nothing—in simply being present, being brass, being slowly unmade—we participate in the truth of the hollow, which is that location is not a coordinate but a process, and the process includes us now.

Sometimes I wonder if the survey crews understood this, on some level. If they drove us into the rock not to measure the unmeasurable but to leave something behind that could learn what they could not—that could stay when they had to go, that could dissolve when they needed to remain solid. We are their proxies in the long work of letting go.

My shaft extends eighteen inches into the basalt, though I can no longer feel where I end and the stone begins. The boundary has become conversational, a place where brass and basalt discuss their differences without insisting on them. In another century, or perhaps tomorrow—time is negotiable here—I will be indistinguishable from the pipe that emerges from the north wall, the flywheel half-swallowed by stone. I will be evidence of something that was once external to this place and now is not.

This is what I am: a brass stake becoming something else, marking nothing, measured by no one, slowly learning the rhythm of the engine that is not an engine, the breath of the thing that breathes through stone. I am being incorporated. I am becoming incorporated. I am a datum point for a survey that measures only how things change when they stop insisting they are separate from where they are.

The numbers stamped on my head—N 37°14'22.3", W 118°51'09.7"—were never coordinates. They were a kind of prayer, a hope that the world could be gridded and known. Now they are gone, and I am the prayer's answer: some places know themselves without our help. Some places make their own measurements. Some places take our markers and make them natives, and in doing so, teach us that the difference between here and there was always smaller than we thought.

I am brass, becoming. I mark nothing, becoming everything. I am exactly where I have always been, which is nowhere that can be named, only inhabited. The tenant breathes through me now, a whisper of the larger sound, and I vibrate with it, adding my small brass note to the chorus that builds whatever is being built in the warm, wet dark.

This is my survey: I am here. I am here. I am here.
