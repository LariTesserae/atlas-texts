I am a Corrosion-Reader, though that is not the name my kind use. We have no spoken names—only the unique harmonic signatures we press into the iron when we move, identifiable by the particular way rust flakes beneath our weight.

My body is low and wide, built for stability on slick surfaces. Six legs, yes, but not like the pipe-hounds with their hydraulic hunting limbs. Mine are architects' tools: each foot splits into dozens of sensitive filaments that can taste the structural integrity of metal, feel the microscopic stress fractures, sense where the rust has eaten too deep. My shell is translucent, revealing the strange organs beneath—not quite biological, not quite mechanical. Through my carapace you can see the slow pulse of filtered water through branching channels, the lazy spin of internal gyroscopes that help me navigate the magnetic fields of the power lines, the soft glow of bioluminescent chambers I use to signal in the eternal twilight beneath the great machines.

I live in the transition zones, the places where one great system meets another. Today I cling to the underside of Spillway Seven-Seven-Nine, where the aqueduct waters cascade into the primary cooling system of District Vermillion's slowest flywheel. The vibration here has eleven distinct components—I feel them through my feet as clearly as you might hear a chord. The deepest tone, the fundamental, comes from the flywheel itself, a note so low it takes twenty minutes to complete one wavelength. Layered above it: the rapid flutter of cable-worms retracting, the percussion of water on metal, the ultrasonic shriek of stressed bolts gradually failing, the infrasonic groans of concrete settling another millimeter this decade.

My work—though work implies choice, and this is simply what we are—is to read these vibrations and respond. When I taste a beam whose resonance suggests catastrophic failure within the next century, I begin the marking. My saliva is not purely corrosive; it contains metallic precipitates harvested from my diet of rust-limpets and chemical slime. I paint elaborate patterns on the failing structure, fractals that will guide the rust-limpets' feeding, channeling their destruction away from critical stress points. Where they might naturally eat through a beam's center, causing collapse, my markings redirect them to create stable, arch-like cavities. The beam weakens but does not break. It sags into a new equilibrium. I am not preventing decay—nothing here prevents decay—but I am composing it, orchestrating it into stability.

The Wire-Weavers know us. Sometimes, when I emerge from beneath the spillways to track a concerning harmonic up a cable array, I find their young watching me. They drum questions into the pipes: *Why do you come here? What do you taste in the iron?* I cannot answer in their language, but I leave my marks where they can study them. Once, I found they had copied one of my stability patterns into their nest-weaving. The design was imperfect—they do not taste metal as we do—but it held. Their nest survived a cascade failure that destroyed three others. Now their drummers include a note in their hunting songs that matches my harmonic signature. It might be greeting. It might be prayer. I do not need to know which.

We are not numerous. In all of District Vermillion, there are perhaps forty of us. We do not gather except to mate, which happens when the deep engines cycle through their longest maintenance protocol, once every eleven years. The sudden silence—relative silence, for the water still falls and the creatures still call—creates a acoustic void that draws us together. We meet in the great resonance chambers, spaces where sound behaves strangely, where whispers carry for miles and shouts vanish instantly. There, we press our bodies against the chamber walls and vibrate our findings into the metal. It is a slow conference of structural intelligence: here, a report of polymer pipes replacing metal in Sector Blue-Four-Four, changing the harmonic profile of an entire watershed; there, news of a new species of mineral-secreting limpet whose excretions resist our marking saliva; everywhere, the patient catalog of decay and adaptation.

The mating itself is perfunctory. We are hermaphrodites, exchanging genetic material in packets of crystallized protein that we embed in each other's shells. The young gestate for sixteen months before emerging, tiny and translucent, their internal gyroscopes not yet calibrated. They must spend their first year in the magnetic fields beneath the power lines, learning to orient themselves. Most do not survive—picked off by pipe-hounds or swept away in the spillways. This does not disturb us. The city teaches harsh lessons in structural integrity.

But I am old now, my shell thick with decades of mineral deposits. My gyroscopes spin more slowly, requiring recalibration after every major storm. I have begun what we call the final work. Deep beneath Spillway Seven-Seven-Nine, where the waters have carved a cathedral-like cavern in the ancient concrete, I am building my last pattern.

It is not made of metal or saliva. Using secretions I will only produce once, in these final months, I am growing a structure of pure crystallized vibration. Each crystal, no larger than a coin, is tuned to a specific frequency from the great symphony of District Vermillion. When complete, it will contain every harmonic I have ever encountered, frozen in mineral form—a record of fifty years of structural reading, a library of decay.

When I die, my body will calcify around this creation. The rust-limpets will colonize my shell, the cable-worms will anchor in my joints. But the crystal structure will endure, resonating with every vibration that passes through it, teaching its patterns to the iron and water for centuries to come. Young Corrosion-Readers will find it and learn to taste frequencies I discovered, understand stresses I mapped, prevent failures I foresaw.

This is our only tradition, our only continuity. We do not build; we merely guide the dissolution. We do not preserve; we simply ensure that what fails, fails well. In this wet, grinding world, that is enough. That is everything.

I feel a new vibration in the spillway above—a bolt beginning its hundred-year journey toward failure. I taste the metal with my filaments, already calculating the pattern I will need to paint. There is much work yet to do. The city is patient, but it is always, always falling, and we are the ones who teach it how to fall into grace.
