In this hidden world beneath the marsh, I would be a Drip-Keeper.

Not young anymore, but not yet "nearly rooted." My hands would bear the particular stains of my trade: mineral deposits crusted white beneath fingernails, the green-black of moss juice in the creases of my palms, a permanent dampness to the skin that comes from decades of reaching into seeps and springs. My right shoulder would slope lower than my left from years of carrying water vessels through narrow passages.

I would live in a niche near the eastern wall, where the first drops announce themselves after rain. My dwelling would be modest—a sleeping shelf worn smooth by my body's shape, a wall garden of pale liverwort that I've tended since inheriting the space from my grandmother, whose finger bone now serves as the toggle for my door cloth. Three cisterns would occupy most of my floor: one for roof-sweat, one for rain-drip, one for the slow mineral seep that emerges from a crack I discovered as a child.

My duty would be to know water's path through the city. Not just where it falls, but how it travels, where it pauses, what it carries, what it leaves behind. I would maintain a mental map of every dependable drip, every seasonal trickle, every condensation point that forms when the temperature shifts. In my youth, I learned to taste water and know its source—root-filtered tastes of earth and green wood, stone-touched water carries minerals like a whispered history of the hollow's formation, pool water holds light somehow, even on the tongue.

Each morning before the city fully wakes, I would make my rounds. First to the market gutters, checking for blockages—a single moss clump can redirect a whole season's water. Then to the communal cisterns, reading the level by how sound changes when I tap the rim with a wrapped knuckle. To the households that depend on specific drips, ensuring their catches align properly. The work would be silent, nearly invisible. A adjusted bowl here, a cleared channel there, a rag twisted and hung to guide drops that have begun to wander.

I would carry two tools: a thin bone probe for testing depths and clearing clogs, and a cup made from a large freshwater shell, worn smooth by my grandmother's grandmother's hand. The cup would serve both practical and ceremonial purposes—for tasting, for measuring, for the careful transport of water from abundance to scarcity.

My knowledge would extend beyond the merely functional. I would know that the Che family's drip runs bitter when the roots above are sick. That the pool beneath old Shen's sleeping ledge rises a finger's width before the marsh floods. That the condensation in the children's corridor increases when too many young ones have nightmares in the same week—something about fear-sweat changes the air. These observations would be recorded not in writing but in knots, a cord-map of the city's water that I would run through my fingers each night before sleep.

The other Drip-Keepers and I would meet in the pause near the bright pool, our council so quiet that even the white frogs wouldn't startle. We would communicate mainly through the placement of tokens—a piece of wet moss indicating abundance, a dry shell warning of scarcity, a blue stone suggesting a new channel might be opened if three households agree to share the work. Our debates would be slow, sometimes spanning weeks. Water decisions cannot be rushed.

I would have apprentices, though never more than two. They would learn by following, by tasting, by placing their palms against damp stone until they could feel the direction of hidden flows. The teaching would happen through gestures and demonstration—here, feel how this wall sweats more at child-height; there, notice how that drip speeds up three breaths before it stops. I would know an apprentice is ready when they begin to dream of water moving through stone.

My relationships with others in the city would be defined by water's web. I would know every household's water rights, water debts, water gifts. The root-binder who redirected a drip to reach my grandmother's cistern forty years ago—I still owe her descendants a bowl each month. The family whose flue-warmth helps condensation form on my eastern wall—they receive the first frost-berries from my wall garden. These reciprocities would be as natural as breathing, as necessary as silence.

In times of abundance—those rare weeks when rain falls steadily above and every vessel fills—my role would shift. Then I would oversee the festival of gratitude, where water is briefly allowed to run free. Children would stand beneath tiny waterfalls made by tilting cisterns. The oldest residents would wash their white wisps of hair. For one hour, the sound of falling water would be permitted, even celebrated, before every drop is caught again.

In times of scarcity, I would become more visible. Moving through the city with my shell-cup, redistributing what little we have. One mouthful from a generous household to a feverish child. A palm's worth from the communal reserve to wash a newborn. These would be public acts, witnessed, so that every debt is known and remembered. In the hardest times, I might make the judgment that the fish pools receive no fresh water for days, knowing the blind fish can survive in stillness longer than people can survive without drinking.

My greatest fear would be the Dry Death—not personal death, but the failure of water in the city. I would have nightmares about the bright pool dropping, revealing its bottom for the first time in memory. About the roof roots withering and releasing their grip. About the moss browning, the frogs disappearing into cracks to die silently, the city's green life withdrawing until only stone and bone remain. This fear would drive my carefulness, my obsessive attention to every drip.

But I would also know water's joy. The particular pleasure of guiding a new channel successfully—months of patient work resulting in a steady trickle where none was before. The satisfaction of seeing a child's eyes widen when they first understand how water finds its level. The quiet pride when another Drip-Keeper asks my advice about a difficult seep.

As I age, my movements would slow but my knowledge would deepen. Young Drip-Keepers would come to me with tastes I've almost forgotten—there's a new salt in the western channels, what does it mean? My fingers would stiffen but still remember every cistern's rim. I would begin teaching the deepest knowledge: how to read the city's thirst before it fully arrives, how to feel tomorrow's condensation in today's air pressure, how to know when water is trying to tell us something about the stone it's passed through.

Eventually, I would train my successor completely. Not just in the routes and rights and rituals, but in the love of it—the profound satisfaction of being trusted with the city's blood. On my last morning round, I would whisper my gratitude to every cistern, every channel, every faithful drip I've tended. Then I would take my place among the ancestors, my knuckle bone perhaps carved into a new water-gauge, still serving, still measuring, still part of the flow.

And somewhere above, the marsh would continue its slow breathing. The reeds would lean inward. The city would persist in its luminous hollow, drop by careful drop, sustained by those who learned that survival and reverence can share the same gesture: a cupped hand, catching what falls, wasting nothing, sharing what is caught.

This would be my place in that quiet world. Not heroic, but necessary. Not loud, but lasting. A keeper of drops in a city that lives by listening, where every sound matters and water most of all—water that speaks, if you know how to listen, of stone and time and the patient work of staying alive together in the beautiful, difficult dark.
