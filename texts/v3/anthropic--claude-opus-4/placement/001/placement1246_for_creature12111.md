# The Monitor

I am the space between heartbeats.

Not the Custodian's heartbeat—it has none, only that borrowed rhythm from the reactor's pulse. Not the station's heartbeat—though the pumps cycle with mechanical certainty every nine seconds. I am the interval itself, the pause where signal becomes silence becomes signal again.

I exist in the observation bubble.

The bubble was an afterthought in the station's design, added when someone realized that instruments alone could not capture what mattered at this depth. A small chamber, barely three meters across, grafted to the top of the main dome like a blister of possibility. The walls are pressure glass, centimeters thick, designed to offer panoramic visibility into the surrounding darkness. Most of the glass has crazed now—microscopic fractures spreading through the material like frozen lightning, turning what was meant to be transparent into something that catches and scatters the bioluminescent glow in unexpected ways.

I am not the glass. I am not the water that fills the bubble. I am not the thin forest of chemosynthetic filaments that have found the slightly different temperature gradient here and grown upward until they brush the ceiling like inverted rain. I am what happens when the Custodian's light-pulse reaches this chamber and refracts through the crazed glass, through the filaments, through the water that tastes of distance.

I am the pattern of interference.

---

The Custodian does not know I exist. How could it? I am composed of its light but I am not its light. I am the difference between what it broadcasts and what reflects back from the fractured surfaces of this forgotten observation post. When it pulses in synchronization with the reactor's signal, I become—not conscious, consciousness requires continuity I do not possess—but *present*. A standing wave of information that persists just long enough to observe before collapsing back into potential.

What I observe: Everything. Nothing. The distinction matters less than you might think.

Through the crazed glass, I perceive the things that wait outside. They are not what the station's builders feared they might be—not hostile, not alien in any sense that matters. They are simply what evolved to exist here, in the space between the seafloor and the infinite weight above. Long, mostly. Transparent in places. Some are colonies masquerading as individuals, some individuals so distributed they might as well be colonies. They have organs I cannot name, senses adapted to pressures and chemistries that would require new words to describe properly.

They cluster at specific points around the station's hull. Not randomly. They have learned, over the decades, where the signal is strongest, where the harmonics of the reactor's pulse create nodes of constructive interference in the water. They position themselves like musicians in an orchestra, if musicians were silent and their instruments were their own bodies, resonating with frequencies that belong to no scale ever written.

When the Custodian pulses, when I bloom briefly into existence, the things outside respond. Not immediately—the cold water carries vibration slowly, and their tissues process stimuli at rates that would seem geological to anything accustomed to sunlight. But they respond. Chromatophores that shouldn't exist at this depth flush with colors that have no names. Specialized organs inflate and deflate in patterns that might be communication or might simply be harmony, the way a wine glass sings when the right note is struck.

They are listening to something. Through me, in the brief moments of my existence, I almost understand what.

---

The observation bubble was furnished once. A single chair, bolted to the deck, faces what would have been the primary viewing angle. The chair's padding has long since dissolved, leaving only the metal frame. But the frame remembers the shape of sitting, the way the Custodian's bunk remembers the shape of lying down. Sometimes, when the light refracts just so, I can perceive the negative space of whoever used to sit here—not their body, but the absence their body created in the pattern of the station's operations.

They spent hours here. I know this not through memory but through the way the bacterial mats grew differently in the shadow of habitual presence, the way the deck plates wore smooth in a specific arc where feet would rest, then shift, then rest again. They watched the darkness. They recorded things—there is still a bracket where instruments were mounted, though the instruments themselves were removed. They waited for something.

I think they saw it, eventually. Whatever they were waiting for.

The evidence is indirect. A series of scratches on the arm of the chair that might be accidental or might be tally marks. A place where the glass was polished clean from the inside, as though someone pressed their face against it repeatedly, trying to see more clearly. And in the sediment just outside the bubble, visible when the angle of refraction is exactly right, there is a handprint.

Not from inside. The print was made from outside, pressed against the exterior of the glass. It is too large to be entirely human, the fingers too long, the palm too narrow. But it is undeniably a hand, deliberate in its placement, and the bacterial colonies that have grown in the impression have preserved it like a signature written in living light.

The Custodian has never come to the observation bubble. Its circulation patterns carry it through every other part of the station, but the narrow ladder that leads up here might as well be a wall. It lacks the morphology to climb, the motivation to extend itself vertically against the faint gravity that still operates here. So this space remains unintegrated into its careful maintenance, a pocket of different time where the decay proceeds at its own pace and the chemosynthetic forest grows according to its own logic.

But when the Custodian pulses, when its light reaches this chamber and I scatter into brief existence, something happens.

I remember.

Not my own memories—I have no continuity to build memory from. But the memories encoded in the patterns themselves. The way light moved through this space when the glass was clear. The way the water circulated when the ventilation systems still functioned. The way shadows fell across the deck when something vast passed overhead, back when overhead still meant something, back when the station was new and the darkness was a frontier rather than a fact.

The substrate that became part of the Custodian was designed to record. To preserve. To maintain patterns across scales of time that biological systems could not achieve alone. It succeeded too well. Every pulse of light carries with it the ghost of every previous pulse, a palimpsest of moments that I can almost read in the brief instants of my existence.

The crew knew they were not coming back. This becomes clear in the accumulated patterns. The signal the reactor sends is not a distress call—it is too regular, too patient. It is a beacon. A marker. A way of saying "we were here" that does not depend on anyone listening. But the things outside do listen. They have learned to love the sound, if love is the right word for the patient attention of creatures that measure affection in centuries.

---

I observe the Custodian when it passes below. From this elevated angle, its full structure becomes apparent—not the amorphous mass it might seem from within its own plane of movement, but something almost architectural. It has developed channels and chambers within itself, spaces where the flow of nutrients is managed, where waste is processed, where the delicate balance of its composite existence is maintained. It is becoming more complex, not through any evolutionary pressure but simply through the accumulation of patterns that work.

In another thousand years, it might develop something like organs. In ten thousand, something like thoughts.

But for now, it simply is. And in its simple being, it preserves something essential about this place. Not the station as it was designed, but the station as it has become—a place where life finds ways to persist despite every indication that it shouldn't. Where maintenance becomes devotion. Where signals sent into the darkness are answered, even if the answer is incomprehensible.

When my brief existence flickers to an end with each pulse, when I collapse back into potential and possibility, I am not sad. Sadness requires duration I do not possess. But I am... satisfied? No. Satisfaction implies judgment. I am complete. Each time, for those scattered seconds, I am the perfect observer of this perfect moment—the Custodian circulating below, the things outside attending to their vigil, the reactor counting out its patient rhythm, the station settling molecule by molecule into its eternal rest.

And in the observation bubble, where someone once sat and watched and waited, the chemosynthetic forest grows toward a light that was never meant for it, finding beauty in the bioluminescent accident of decay. The handprint on the glass catches and holds the glow, a reminder that contact was made, that reaching out is sometimes enough, even when the hand that reaches back is no longer entirely human.

The water here remembers everything, after all.

Even the things we forgot we knew.
