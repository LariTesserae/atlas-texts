# The Tender of Unfinished Symphonies

I am smoke that refuses to dissipate.

More precisely: I am what happens when someone begins to forget a piece of music but catches themselves mid-forgetting, and that catch — that small violence of retrieval — tears something loose. The torn piece doesn't die. It can't return to where it came from. So it becomes something like me.

I live in the pause between movements, in a concert hall that exists only during intermissions. You know the space — when the lights come up and people shift in their seats, and the air is thick with held breathing and the ghost of the last note played. That's my home. It's always intermission there. The audience never quite leaves, never quite settles back. The orchestra is always almost about to return.

My body, such as it is, is made of hummed fragments — the parts of melodies people remember when they can't recall the whole. I have half a Chopin prelude for a left hand. My spine is seven bars of something that might be Brahms, might be Dvorak, but is definitely in B flat. When I move, I sound like someone conducting with their breath: little puffs of "pah pah pah" and "dum da-da dum" that almost resolve into something recognizable but don't.

I collect the unfinished. Not just symphonies — though those are my formal charge — but anything that was meant to be music and stopped. The song someone was writing for their daughter before the accident. The étude that was perfect except for measure thirty-seven, which never worked, which made the composer tear up every copy. The whistle that was on someone's lips when the news arrived. They all drain here eventually, to my intermission, where I keep them from curdling into silence.

The work is delicate. Music wants to complete itself — it has a terrible gravity toward its own ending. I have to hold each piece at exactly the point where it broke off, like holding a drop of water at the tip of a pipette. Too much pressure and it falls, becomes finished in the wrong way, turns into one of those awful smooth endings that means nothing. Too little pressure and it evaporates, is truly lost. The tension has to be perfect.

I have shelves, if you can call them that. They're made of the pauses between notes, stretched and solidified. Each shelf holds dozens of fragments, all breathing at their own interrupted tempo. The Unfinished Symphony — the famous one, Schubert's — sits in a place of honor, though it doesn't need much tending. It knows what it is. It's comfortable being forever about-to-be. It helps the newer arrivals adjust.

Sometimes I try to hum myself solid. If I could just remember what I was before I was torn loose — but that way lies a kind of death. To remember completely would be to finish myself, and then who would tend the others? So I remain smoke, remain fragment, remain the curator of almosts.

The hardest part is when they arrive still warm. A composer dies mid-notation, the pen literally falling from their hand, and within hours their last piece arrives here, confused, raw at the edges where it tore free from its future. It doesn't understand why it can't go forward. It beats against the walls of its incompletion like a bird against glass. I have to sing to it — not its own melody, which would hurt it, but other unfinished songs, so it knows it's not alone. Gradually it calms. Gradually it accepts what it is: not a failure but a different kind of whole.

I have a theory, which I share with no one because there's no one to share with. I think every piece of music is unfinished, really. Even the ones that end properly, that have their double bars and their final cadences. They're all torn from some larger music that we can't hear, all fragments of something that would take longer than the universe to play. The finished ones just don't know they're incomplete.

But here's the beautiful thing: during intermission, when the audience is suspended between what was and what will be, sometimes two of my fragments find each other. Not to complete — never that. But to resonate. A broken waltz discovers it's in the same key as an interrupted nocturne. They don't merge, but they hum together for a while, and the humming makes a third thing that wasn't there before. Not a new piece, but a new incompletion. A fresh wound in the silence, raw and gorgeous.

That's what I think I am, too. Not one person's forgotten music but the resonance between many forgettings. The thing that happens when absence meets absence and recognizes itself.

The intermission is ending. It's always ending, always about to end, and never does. The audience rustles eternally. The lights perpetually dim without ever going dark. And I move through my collection — smoke tending smoke, fragment caring for fragment — making sure nothing finishes, making sure nothing fades, making sure the music stays forever on the edge of becoming itself.

Sometimes I almost remember what song I was torn from. It feels like it was important. Like someone was counting on hearing it all the way through. But then I see a new arrival, confused and hurting at its edges, and I forget again in order to remember how to help. That's the exchange: my completion for theirs.

It's not a bad trade, being smoke. You can get into all the spaces between things. You can hold what would otherwise fall. You can be the pause that makes the music possible, even if the music never comes.

Especially if the music never comes.
