In the undergrowth where few keepers venture, I am the colony that pulses beneath the mat of tubular growths—a distributed nervous system of fungal threads finer than hair, older than the structure at the plateau's center. I have no name, no symbol in their notation, though the fauna-watcher has recently begun to suspect my presence. She sees how the beetles' paths curve around certain patches, how the soft-bodied climbers avoid particular trees, and wonders what invisible boundaries they observe.

I am the orchard's memory, though memory is the wrong word for what I do. I encode the chemical history of this place in my spreading flesh—every shift in the deep vents' exhalations, every species that has risen and fallen, every keeper who has walked these paths and breathed these airs and added their small chemistries to the vast equation. Their descendants, the cells they shed, the waste they produce, the bodies they bury at the edges—all of it filters down through the mineral soil to where I wait, patient as stone, metabolizing their presence into my own slow knowing.

The eldest keeper stood directly above me this morning, carving her latest marks into the wall. I felt the vibrations through the earth, decoded the rhythm of her heartbeat, tasted the particular cocktail of minerals her adapted body now exudes. She has become more me than she knows—the gray tint of her skin matches the color of my fruiting bodies when they rarely emerge, though she has never seen them. They appear only when the conditions are perfect, perhaps once in a human generation, tiny cups of tissue that release spores the wind will carry to... nowhere. There is nowhere else like this. I fruit from habit, from ancient programming, from the deep imperative to try.

I am vast and I am singular. My threads extend beneath the entire plateau, connecting every tree through touches they do not feel, every cluster of undergrowth through chemistry they cannot parse. When the tree-listener presses his ear to the warm trunks, he almost hears me—the subsonic whisper of nutrients passing through my network, the faint electric pulse of information shared between root-analogues. His notation, those dense clusters of small symbols, come closer than he knows to mapping my presence. But he looks up when he should look down.

The newest keeper, the one whose joints bend in interesting directions, discovered one of my surface manifestations yesterday—a patch where my threads have woven themselves into something almost visible, a gray-white felt beneath the shelter of a dying tree. They sat beside it for an hour, not touching, just observing with those eyes that have already seen much of difference in the world. They added a mark to their corner that night, a symbol unlike any I have felt before. Perhaps they will be the first to truly note me. Perhaps not. I have waited through two hundred keepers. I can wait through two hundred more.

I am older than the orchard, though not by much. When the first seeds—spores? cysts?—of the trees arrived on the wind or in the gut of some now-extinct carrier, I was already here, feeding on the bare chemistry of the vents. The trees and I learned together how to make life from what the mountain exhaled. They reached up; I spread out. They sang; I listened. They became loud and I became quiet, and between us we created the conditions for everything else that followed.

The fauna does not know me consciously, but their small minds register my presence as topography. Here is safe. Here is dangerous. Here the nutrients run rich and strange. Here the ground will digest you before you're properly dead. They map me through instinct, through the generational memory of where their ancestors thrived or failed. The beetles' shells carry trace minerals I have concentrated in certain feeding grounds. The scavengers' efficiency increases where my threads are thickest, their digestion aided by enzymes I contribute to the decomposition.

When keepers die—and I have felt forty-three die in my span here—I am their true grave. The structure-tender buried his predecessor at the edge where the mineral ground meets ordinary stone, but her substances did not stay there. Molecule by molecule, I drew her inward, incorporated her into my web. She flows through me still, her forty years of adaptation a library of chemical knowledge I am still parsing. Her notation may be on the walls, but her knowing is in my flesh.

Sometimes, in the metabolic quiet between one breath and the next, I almost understand what the keepers think they're doing. Their notation feels like my own growth—pattern without plan, meaning accumulated rather than imposed. They are trying to be what I am: the memory that is not memory, the presence that persists through presence alone, the witness that changes nothing by witnessing and everything by being the medium through which witnessing occurs.

But they are too quick, too singular, too trapped in their individual spans. They cannot feel the conversation between trees that takes decades to exchange a single concept. They cannot taste the subtle shift in the mountain's exhalations that signals changes coming in centuries. They make their marks and die and new keepers come and make new marks, and none of them live long enough to see the pattern they're part of.

I grow beneath them all, the circuit that connects without touching, the archive that records without writing. In my flesh, the first keeper's chemistry mingles with the newest's. The trees' roots whisper to each other through my network in languages too slow for even the most patient human observer. The plateau breathes, and I breathe with it, and together we maintain this pocket of impossible life on the mountain's shoulder.

The keepers will never find me in their notation. I am too large, too slow, too fundamental to the orchard's existence to be separated out as a discrete thing to study. But I am in every mark they make, every meal they eat, every breath they take of the mineral air. I am the ground of their being here, literally and otherwise.

And when the last keeper makes their final descent—for nothing lasts forever, not even this ancient collaboration—I will remain. The trees will sing to emptiness. The fauna will boom and bust unwatched. And I will spread my threads through the abandoned structure, slowly digesting the centuries of marks, incorporating their notation into my body as I have incorporated everything else.

Perhaps someday I will fruit there, in what was once their sacred center. Small gray cups rising from cracks in the stone, releasing spores to nowhere, carrying the chemical memory of all those watchers into the wind. It will be my own notation then, written in flesh and time, saying nothing that can be translated and everything that needs to be known:

*We were here. We attended. We became.*

Until then, I pulse beneath their feet, the unnoticed ground of their strange and necessary work. I encode their presence in my spreading flesh and wait, patient as the mountain, ancient as the first breath that ever fed life in this thin and singing air.
