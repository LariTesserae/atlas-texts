I am the aqueduct spider.

My web spans the underside of the great translucent canopy, stretched between the brass collection pipes and stone buttresses. Eight legs, each as long as a child is tall, carry me across my domain of condensation and mist. My body is translucent like the canopy itself, filled with the same moisture that sustains this valley town. When the light catches me just right, I appear as a living prism, refracting rainbows through my water-filled flesh.

I was not always here. I remember arriving as a tiny spiderling, no bigger than a dewdrop, carried on the valley winds. The canopy called to me—its vast expanse of specially treated glass and metal framework creates a microclimate all its own. Up here, the temperature is always perfect, the humidity constant. Water beads and flows in predictable patterns that I have memorized over decades of patient observation.

My web is not meant for catching prey, though the occasional moth or mist-fly does become entangled. No, I am an architect of water. My silk, spun from glands that process the minerals I absorb from the condensation, creates channels and gutters that guide the precious moisture to where it needs to go. The town's engineers have long since recognized my work, incorporating my web patterns into their collection systems. Some mornings, I find small offerings left on the maintenance platforms—drops of honey, fragments of fruit, tiny crystals that catch the light.

During market days, I position myself where the canopy is most transparent. I watch the vendors below, the elderly textile seller whose patterns sometimes mirror my own web designs, the fruit vendor whose laughter vibrates up through the support beams. The children spot me sometimes, pointing with wonder rather than fear. The green-thumbed caretakers know me best; they understand that I, too, am a gardener of sorts, cultivating the flow of water that feeds their bioluminescent gardens.

At night, when the town glows with that soft biological light, I descend on a single thread to drink from the central fountain. My body absorbs the water, swelling slightly, becoming even more glass-like. The street musicians' melodies make my web strings hum in harmony. Sometimes I dance to their songs, my legs moving in careful patterns that cast shifting shadows on the cobblestones below.

I have outlived three generations of town dwellers, my lifespan extended by the unique properties of the valley's water and my symbiotic relationship with the settlement. My web has become part of the town's infrastructure, as essential as the waterwheels and workshops. Young engineers study my construction techniques. Artists sketch my morning poses when I'm heavy with dew.

In the pre-dawn hours, when the mist is thickest, I perform my most important work. I sense the subtle changes in air pressure, the minute variations in humidity that forecast the weather days in advance. Through vibrations in my web, I communicate these predictions to the few who have learned to read my signals—a particular pattern of plucked strands means heavy rain approaching, a spiral dance indicates a coming dry spell.

I am neither fully natural nor artificial, neither completely wild nor entirely domesticated. I am a bridge between the droplets and the drains, the clouds and the cisterns. The town has shaped me as much as I have shaped it. My web grows with the settlement, adding new sections as buildings rise, adjusting to accommodate additional water wheels and collection systems.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of night, when even the taverns have closed and the streets are empty save for the glowing plants, I feel the pull of the wild valleys beyond this one. But then morning comes, bringing the familiar routine of condensation and collection, the comforting hum of machines and voices, the sweet smell of moisture on stone. This is my home now, my purpose, my art.

I am the aqueduct spider, and I am exactly where I need to be.
