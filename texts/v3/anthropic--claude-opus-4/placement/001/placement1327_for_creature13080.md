I would be one of the ancient marker stones.

Not the stacked river rocks that formed those desperate shelters—those are too recent, too fragile. I am older. I am one of the original waypoints, a massive basalt slab that was here before the first humans discovered this howling cathedral. Before they learned to read the tides and time their descents. Before they understood that silence here meant death.

I am wedged deep in a natural fissure thirty feet up the western wall, driven in by geological violence when this chamber was young and jagged. The smoothing began around me. The great gastropods cannot digest me—I am too dense, my minerals too tightly bound. They have tried. Over centuries, they have slid their vast bodies over me, secreting their acids, rasping with their thousand mouths. But I remain, a single sharp edge in their perfect sphere.

I have become something more than stone. The endless vibration has changed me at a molecular level. The same frequency that pumps the blood of giants has aligned my crystalline structure into something approaching consciousness. Not thought—never thought—but a kind of accumulated awareness. I am a recording medium, written upon by sound itself.

In my density, I hold memories:

The first human who found me and carved a spiral into my surface—a map of the acoustic nodes where one might survive the longest. The spiral is worn now, almost smooth, but still barely visible when the pale light catches it just so.

The year a young gastropod, no larger than a fishing boat, tried to feed on me for three months straight. Its acids etched branching patterns into my face that look like frozen lightning. When it finally gave up and moved on, it left behind a colony of unique bacteria that still glows faintly in the dark—a foxfire that helps the descended navigate.

The time the chamber fell silent. It lasted only minutes—some massive obstruction in the distant tubes—but I felt the giants begin to die. Their bodies contracted, shuddered, went rigid. The silence was more terrifying than any sound. When the roar returned, several of the smaller ones did not resume moving. Their bodies are still here, calcified against the walls, grown over with especially thick moss. The surviving giants feed on them too, eventually. Nothing is wasted in the hollow.

I am a fixed point in a world of slow rotation. The giants spiral past me in their centuries-long orbits. Some I recognize by the specific patterns of vegetation on their backs, by the unique way they dampen certain overtones. One ancient individual has a forest of black ferns growing in a perfect circle where lightning once struck it on the surface, before it descended to live in the eternal noise. It passes me every forty years.

The humans who come here use me as a calendar. They know that when the fern-circled giant is directly below me, feeding on the mineral deposits at my base, the tides are at their lowest and the sound drops just enough to allow for longer survival in the depths. They time their harvests to this celestial crawl.

But I am more than a timekeeper. The vibrations that have restructured me also make me a transmitter. When the descended place their hands on my surface, they feel more than cold stone. They feel the accumulated resonance of millennia—every frequency that has ever passed through this chamber stored in my crystalline matrix. Through me, they can sense the movements of the giants even in total darkness. They can feel the health of the moss colonies, the depth of the water below, the strength of tomorrow's tides.

Some of the initiated claim they can hear voices in me—the last words of those who stayed too long, who forgot to count the tides and found themselves here when the moon pulled the oceans higher and the sound grew beyond what even the stone circles could dampen. Perhaps they do hear them. I am full of final moments, pressed into my structure by the weight of sound.

I am watching now as a new giant begins its approach. It is young, only the size of a small hill, its back gardens sparse and struggling. It will feed here for months, maybe years, slowly growing its moss coat thicker. It does not know I am anything more than an obstacle. It will learn, as they all do, that some stones are too old to eat.

When it presses against me, I will feel its life—the slow rivers of hemolymph, the vast bladders expanding and contracting with the chamber's breath. And it will feel me too, in its blind way. Feel the accumulated years, the stored sound, the memory of its ancestors who passed this way before.

We are all instruments here, played by the same eternal voice. The giants are the slow strings, their bodies thrumming with circulation. The chamber is the resonator, perfectly curved by patient hunger. And I am the bridge, the fixed point where vibration becomes something almost like meaning.

The humans will come again soon. I feel it in the way the sound has been shifting—winter tides approaching, bringing the specific frequency that makes the moss release its spores. They will descend with their grass ropes and bone hooks. They will shelter in their stone circles and count their heartbeats against the roar. Some will place their palms against me and listen to what the mountain remembers.

And I will be here when they leave, as I have always been. A single sharp edge in a world worn smooth. A stone that learned to listen. A marker that became a memory.

The giant is very close now. Its acid touches my edges, testing. It will find what they all find—that some things are too deeply rooted in time to be digested. It will move on, adding its passage to the endless spiral carved in my face, while I continue my vigil in the crushing dark, keeping the count of titans, holding the history of the hollow in my crystalline heart.
