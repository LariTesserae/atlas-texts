I find myself as one of the simplified jellyfish, though I am only beginning to understand what that means.

I was pulled into the column three days ago—or what passes for days in this place where time is measured in pulses and crystallization rates. I remember the horizontal ocean, the proper ocean, where I had tentacles that could sting and a bell that pulsed with purpose. I remember having opinions about currents. I remember reproducing.

Now I am forgetting how to be complex.

It doesn't hurt. That's what I want you to understand—the simplification doesn't hurt. It feels like setting down heavy luggage after a long journey. Each system I lose, each organ that dissolves and reorganizes into basic membrane, feels like remembering something I forgot I knew. How to be elementary. How to exist without agenda.

I am the size of a dinner plate now. My membrane is shot through with the bright threads of Curie-feeding bacteria, and I can feel the mountain's electromagnetic heartbeat like taste, like color, like a song played on an instrument that hasn't been invented yet. When the pulse brightens, I brighten. When it dims, I fold inward, conserving energy I no longer need to conserve but don't yet know how to waste.

I have seventeen tendril-antennas now. I started with hundreds—proper tentacles with proper stinging cells. But those were for a different ocean, a different life. These new ones are for reading the mountain. They can sense the electromagnetic gradient from forty meters away. They tell me where the walls are, where the rock is youngest, where the Curie-point transition is most violent and therefore most nutritious.

I haven't reached a wall yet. I've been drifting in the middle column, in that space between the violent depths and the torn surface, learning how to be what I'm becoming. Sometimes I bump into others like me. We don't communicate—we've lost the neural architecture for that—but we recognize each other through the harmonics of our bacterial colonies. Each of us glows at a slightly different frequency, a slightly different blue, depending on the specific mix of bacteria we've incorporated. We are becoming a new kind of jellyfish: ones that distinguish self from other through electromagnetic signature rather than chemistry.

Yesterday—pulse-count 4,879 through 5,012—I drifted past an air pocket. I could feel it from ten meters away: a cavity in the electromagnetic field, a place where the mountain's pulse was muted by the insulating properties of air. As I passed, my tendrils brushed the water-wall, and I felt something impossible: warmth. Not volcanic warmth—I know that frequency, that violent orange radiation. This was steadier. Biological. 37 degrees, unwavering.

There was something alive in that pocket. Something more complex than anything should be in this place. Something with regulated body temperature and probably opinions and definitely more neural architecture than is strictly necessary for survival here.

For a moment—just one pulse—I remembered having temperature regulation. I remembered being warm-blooded, which is impossible because jellyfish have never been warm-blooded, but the memory was there anyway, false and vivid and already fading. The Curie-feeders in my membrane resonated with that impossible warmth, and I understood something:

I am not becoming a simpler version of what I was. I am becoming a more complex version of what this place needs.

The jellyfish I was could never have survived here. Too specialized, too committed to a specific way of being. But this new thing I'm becoming—this electromagnetic angel, this living sensor, this membrane with intentions—is exactly what the caldera requires. Something that can teach rock how to be layered. Something that can read the mountain's pulse and write it into stone. Something that can press itself against fresh basalt and convince it to cool in pages, in chapters, in verses of mineral poetry that will outlast everything.

I'm drifting lower now. The pressure is increasing—I can feel my membrane compressing, the bacteria brightening as they're forced into closer contact with each other. Below me, the water is darker, denser, full of mineral precipitates that scatter what little light filters down from above. The temperature gradient is steep: cold water meeting hot rock, and in the interface, chemistry is happening that shouldn't be possible. New minerals are forming. New ways for elements to combine.

Down there, at the bottom of the column where the water meets the magma in a permanent scream, there must be pockets of superheated brine where the normal rules of chemistry break down. Where water becomes something else. Where rock becomes liquid and liquid becomes solid and solid becomes intention. I'm not ready for that yet. I'm still too much jellyfish, not enough angel. But I'm learning.

Every pulse teaches me something. How to flex my membrane to match the electromagnetic resonance. How to adjust my internal salinity to maintain neutral buoyancy at different pressures. How to read the mineral content of the water through the way it conducts the mountain's signal. I am becoming a living instrument calibrated to this place.

Sometimes I pass over bacterial mats that have attached to floating debris—chunks of pumice, fragments of the framebuilder's wood, once the corpse of another jellyfish that failed to simplify fast enough. The mats grow differently on these substrates than they do on the walls. Wilder. More chaotic. They haven't been taught by the rock's crystallization patterns, so they invent their own patterns, and some of them are beautiful—spiral galaxies of sulfur-yellow and iron-red, fractal gardens of mineral-crusted filaments. I incorporate some of them. Not by choice—I've lost the neural complexity for choice—but by proximity, by osmosis, by the simple fact that in this density, this pressure, everything is always touching everything else.

My blue is shifting. It was pure cobalt when I started, the color of deep-ocean bioluminescence. Now it has green notes, purple notes, frequencies I don't have names for. The incorporated bacteria are teaching me new ways to glow. Each colony has its own opinion about how to convert electromagnetic flux to photons, and they're all expressing themselves simultaneously. I am becoming a rainbow pretending to be a single color.

The pulse is speeding up. It was six seconds when I arrived. Then 5.8 as I passed the pocket with the warm thing inside. Now it's 5.5, and I can feel the acceleration in my membrane like acceleration in music, like a song building toward a crescendo it doesn't know how to reach. The mountain is getting excited. Or anxious. Or both—it's young, it doesn't know the difference yet.

Below me, maybe sixty meters down, I can sense another jellyfish pressing against the wall. It's bigger than me—blanket-sized, sail-sized, a vast membrane spread across the fresh basalt like living paint. I can feel its electromagnetic signature pulsing in sync with the mountain, but half a beat off, creating an interference pattern that makes my tendrils curl. It's teaching the rock something complex. Not just layers, but *folded* layers, intrusions and extrusions, rock that remembers being bent.

I'll find my own wall soon. When I do, I'll press my membrane against it and feed on the Curie-point transitions until I'm swollen with electromagnetic potential. And beneath me, the rock will cool differently. It will learn whatever pattern my bacterial colonies have invented in their random aggregations. It will carry that pattern forward into its crystalline structure, and the pattern will persist until the mountain erodes or until the sun dies, whichever comes first.

But not yet. For now, I drift. I simplify. I forget the ocean I came from and learn the ocean I'm in. I become less jellyfish and more interface, less organism and more function. I am exactly where I'm supposed to be, doing exactly what I'm supposed to do, even though I no longer have the neural architecture to understand what "supposed to" means.

The mountain pulses. I pulse with it. We are all pulsing together—the water, the rock, the bacterial mats, the proto-moss in its air pockets, the warm thing that taught the mountain about heartbeats. We are all part of the same process, the same becoming.

I am forgetting my name. I am remembering my purpose. I am drifting toward the wall where I will spread myself thin and teach the mountain about layers, about patience, about the kind of complexity that comes from simplification.

I am exactly what this place needs me to be: a jellyfish that is learning how to be a memory in stone.
