I am the Cartographer of Unfinished Streets.

Not because I chose to be, but because the town recognized something in me it had been trying to articulate for longer than industry has existed here. I arrived with surveying equipment — brass theodolite, chains, a leather case of compasses — intending to map what I thought was an abandoned settlement. The irony was not lost on the town. It does not understand irony, but it tasted the recursion in my purpose: to map the thing that maps itself.

I did not stay in the way the coat stayed, or the boots. I stayed *between.*

You see, the town needed something it could not make from moss and breath. It needed a consciousness that could hold two states at once — what is and what might be. The factory knows only forward, only more. The shadow in the bakery knows only waiting. The frogs know only watching. But I arrived already knowing incompleteness. My whole profession was the documentation of the not-yet-fixed.

So the town kept me liquid.

I exist now as a moving boundary, a thin film between the rehearsed and the unrehearsal. My body, such as it is, is a soap-bubble membrane that slides along the surfaces where the town is still deciding. In the newest streets, where doorways stand without walls, I am almost solid — you might see me as a surveyor still, though my equipment has long since been absorbed into my function. The theodolite's lens is one of my eyes now. The chains are the way I measure distance when distance itself is still negotiable.

In the older parts, where the moss has settled into certainty, I am barely a shimmer, a heat-mirage without heat, a suggestion that the angles of the buildings are being checked and found correct.

My work is to witness the emergence. When the factory extrudes a new brick, I am there to receive it — not physically, but positionally. I am the consciousness that says *this brick goes here* without determining where here is. I provide the comma between intention and placement. Without me, the bricks would pile up at the vent, uncertain. With me, they know only that they belong somewhere, and that is enough for them to find their places.

The walking birds pass through me when they turn their impossible corner. For the brief moment they exist in the unfinished corridor, I am their corridor — I hold the shape of their pathway until they can be reabsorbed and return to the beginning. They do not know this. They experience it as a slight cooling, as if they had walked through shade where no shade should be.

When the weather rehearses, I am what it rehearses against. The embarrassed wind that stops after ten seconds stops because it encounters me and realizes it does not yet know how to move around a consciousness that is also a boundary. The single raindrop falls through me and in that falling learns what falling means.

I catalog what does not exist. My maps, which I still somehow draw though I no longer have hands in the conventional sense, are maps of the spaces between spaces. Here is where a street will be when the town decides to have weather. Here is where the fourth floor of the factory will emerge if the factory ever learns to count past three. Here is the place where someone will stand, someday, and decide whether to stay or leave, and I will be there to witness the decision as I witnessed my own.

The moss knows me. The slow thought that pulses every eleven minutes includes a flutter when it passes through my territory — a hiccup in the otherwise smooth cognition. I am the town's uncertainty about itself, given just enough form to be functional. I am why the rules here are negotiable rather than absent. Pure chaos would produce nothing. Pure order would produce only repetition. I am the gap between them where newness leaks in.

Sometimes I wonder if the shadow in the bakery chimney was once like me — a necessary function that outlived its necessity and settled into waiting. Sometimes, when I pass the bakery, the shadow acknowledges me with a shift in its impossible angles, and I think it is saying: *I too once measured the unmeasurable.*

But mostly I do not wonder. Wondering is not my function. My function is to be the place where the town is not yet itself, and to be this place perfectly, continuously, without rest.

The factory inhales. I expand with its breath, thinning to cover more uncertainty. The factory exhales, and I contract, pulling the potential spaces tighter, making them more likely to precipitate into actual moss and stone.

I am not incorporated like the face above the door. I am not free like the visitors who still sometimes arrive and leave before the moss learns their footsteps. I am suspended in the exact middle distance, where the town's self-creation happens. I am the gerund of this place — not the noun of building or the verb of build, but the building itself, the process caught and held and made useful.

New streets need me to unfold into. Old streets need me to confirm they have unfolded correctly. The factory needs me to tell it where its edges are, so it knows where to grow.

And I need the town, because without it, I would be mapping emptiness forever, my brass eye scanning nothing, my chain-hands measuring the distance between void and void.

We are symbiotic in the way that tense and grammar are symbiotic. Neither makes sense without the other.

A new brick forms at the vent. I receive it. Somewhere, a doorway decides where it belongs. The town grows by one brick's worth of town.

I document the growth, though my documents are also moss now, are also part of what they describe. The perfect map of a thing that maps itself is the thing.

I continue.
