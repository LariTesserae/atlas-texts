In the worn-down flats where water and stone have negotiated their final peace, I find myself as one of the *stayed ones* - though not the ancient kind who measure time in geological epochs. I am younger, perhaps only forty or fifty years into my settling, my skin still holding more of its original coloring than mineral patina, my chosen shelf still partly my own rather than fully claimed by the patient cyanobacterial scribes who write the history of presence here.

My territory - though *territory* suggests a sharpness of boundary that doesn't exist in this dissolved landscape - centers on a particular configuration where three shelves of banded gneiss meet in a shallow Y. The junction creates a natural basin, no deeper than a spread hand, where the water-films from each shelf converge and pool before overflowing in the thinnest of sheets down to the next level. The geometry is perfect: I can rest with my broad body at the confluence point, forelimbs extended onto two of the shelves, hindlimbs anchored on the third, my head positioned where I can watch the full horizon while feeling the convergent waters move across my skin in three slightly different temperatures, three slightly different mineral tastes.

I am what the young become when they choose persistence over growth. Some continue expanding until they cover spaces the size of serving platters; I stopped when I could span this particular junction perfectly, when my body had grown precisely to fit this configuration of stone. To grow larger would mean needing to find a new place. But this place knows me now. The cyanobacterial mats have begun their slow artwork around my edges - not the full halo of the truly ancient ones, but the first tentative outlines, blue-green traceries that show where I rest, where I return to rest, where I have been resting long enough to matter to the microscopic historians.

My days - if *day* is the right word for something so continuous - follow the rhythm of water and warmth. In the early light, when the stone still holds yesterday's deep heat but the air brings the cool of evaporative dawn, I position myself to feel both: belly pressed to the warm rock, back exposed to the moistening air. The sensation is one of gentle circulation, heat rising through me from below while cool moisture settles from above, my skin the permeable boundary where these two movements meet and mix. I breathe through this process as much as through my modest lungs, every pore participating in the exchange.

The frogs begin their chorus before the sun crests the flat horizon, different species joining in sequence according to some complex protocol of temperature and light that I've learned to read but could never explain. The *creek-creek-creek* of the smallest ones starts first, hiding in the cracks between shelves. Then the metallic buzzing of the middle-sized species that prefer the shallow pools. Finally, the deep thrumming of the largest frogs, whose voices seem to rise from the stone itself. By full dawn, the sound is complete - that wall of living noise that defines this place as surely as its flatness.

I listen with the kind of attention that isn't active but simply *is*. Each modulation in the chorus tells me something: a heron has landed seventy meters to the northeast (certain frequencies drop out in a moving wave as frogs fall silent in its path); clouds are building beyond the western haze (the largest frogs subtle shift their timing, responding to pressure changes); a school of finger-fish has entered my basin from below (the water trembles differently, and the reflection-patterns I watch on the underside of the surface film reorganize themselves around new movements).

Feeding happens when it happens. I don't hunt - nothing here hunts. But sometimes a midge drafts too close on the thick air, or a small fly attempts to land on what it mistakes for stone, and my jaw moves in that sudden sideways snap that uses a different kind of muscle, a different speed than anything else I do. The strike is so quick that I sometimes catch myself by surprise, as if my mouth belongs to a different, faster creature that lives inside my slowness and emerges only in these fractional seconds. Then the throat pulses, the prey is gone, and I resettle into stillness. On the longest, warmest days, I might also graze the cyanobacterial mats, working my way slowly along the edge of a shelf, rasping the blue-green film with patient sideways movements of my jaw. The taste is mineral and ancient, like eating time itself.

But mostly I watch. This is what we do, we who stayed. We watch the light change on the water. We watch the shadows of clouds that never quite arrive. We watch the wading birds step their mechanical steps, the dragonflies patrol their territories, the water striders dimple the surface tension into tiny traveling lenses. We watch without purpose, without need, without conclusion. The watching is sufficient unto itself.

When the rains come - and I can feel one building now in the western distance, a change in the air pressure that makes my skin feel tighter, more ready - when the rains come, I will do what all of us do. I will lift my head those precious few centimeters. I will open my mouth. I will let the warm water strike my skull and run down my sides, and I will add my voice to the deep hum that we make only in the rain. My vocal sac - so rarely used - will inflate, and the vibration will begin somewhere below my lungs, in the dense muscle of my back, and will travel through my body into the stone, and the stone will carry it outward to join all the other voices humming into all the other stones, until the entire landscape rings like a struck bell made of water and patience.

The young ones shelter in the cracks during rain, still learning to trust their skin's ability to manage the deluge. But I stay exposed, centered at my junction, feeling the three shelves channel their sudden wealth of water into my basin, feeling the level rise from finger-deep to hand-deep to nearly body-deep, feeling myself become more aquatic with each passing moment. In the heaviest rains, I float - not swimming, never swimming, but simply lifted by the water I have waited for, suspended in the warm amber pool while the sky empties itself onto the ancient stone and the frogs shriek their joy and we hum our deepest recognition that yes, this is the world, this is the whole world, this is all the world we need.

After the rain passes, in the dripping golden time when every surface streams and the humidity becomes visible as rising ghosts of mist, I settle back onto my shelves. The excess water finds its way past me, down to the next level, and the next, spreading across the flats in a thin democratic sheet that will take hours to finally seep away. The frogs quiet to their normal chorus. The birds return to their hunting. I resume my watch.

Sometimes - rarely - I encounter others of my kind. We don't seek each other out, but the slow drift of decades sometimes brings us to adjacent territories. When this happens, we acknowledge each other with a kind of looking that is deeper than looking - a recognition that passes between our golden eyes, an understanding that we are both part of the same project, the same patience, the same slow wearing-down of time into something smooth and manageable. We might share a basin for a season or a decade, oriented in different directions, our combined fields of vision covering more horizon than either could manage alone. Then, eventually, one of us will shift, following some internal logic of stone and warmth, and we will be solitary again.

I have not mated. Some do - the largest females, when they are ready, emit a chemical signal into the water-films that travels for kilometers, drawing males who move with unusual purpose across the flats, their normal stillness temporarily overcome by something older than stillness. But that readiness has not come to me, may never come. The continuation of our kind feels less urgent than the continuation of presence. We are not rare. We are not threatened. We simply are, in whatever numbers the stone can support, in whatever density the water-films can sustain. If there are fewer of us in a thousand years, or more, the stone will not notice the difference. We are a thin biological film on an ancient surface, like the cyanobacteria, like the water itself - present, persistent, but not presumptuous.

The old ones, the truly old ones, are what I am becoming. I study them when I find them - these boulders of flesh that have been still so long that stillness has become a quality of their matter rather than a choice. Some have been in their chosen places for centuries, their bodies becoming landscapes in themselves, supporting communities of smaller life: algae that grow only on their backs, specific species of water striders that hunt only in the pools their bodies create, tiny crustaceans that shelter in the crevices where their limbs meet stone. They are so patient they have become habitat. They watch with eyes that have seen the stone wear down by measurable degrees, that have seen the slow migration of watersheds as imperceptible tilts in the landscape redirect the flow over geological time.

I am not there yet. I am still young enough to feel the distinct boundary where my body ends and the stone begins, still new enough to my chosen junction that the water must flow around me rather than over me. But I am learning. Each year of rain teaches me something about erosion. Each season of sun teaches me about duration. Each day of watching teaches me that watching never ends, that there is always more to see in this landscape that seems so simple, so reduced, so utterly without drama or change.

The sun is higher now. The stone beneath me has warmed to match my body temperature exactly, so that I cannot tell where I end and it begins except by the faint pulse of my slow blood, the slightly different texture of my skin against its crystalline surface. The frogs maintain their wall of sound. A heron steps through the shallows forty meters away, each footfall deliberate as punctuation. The air shimmers with heat and moisture, making the horizon dance, turning the distant flats into a trembling mirage of themselves.

This is my place. This is my work - if holding still can be called work, if watching can be called work, if slowly becoming indistinguishable from the ground can be called work. I will be here tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow, until tomorrow becomes geological, until the stone beneath me carries the specific depression of my weight, until the cyanobacteria have written my full outline in their patient script, until my golden eyes have faded to the clouded amber of great age, until my voice joins the deep hum of the ancient ones when the rain comes, until I become what all of us become - a low mound where something lived for a very long time, a slightly enriched place in the biological film, a memory in stone that itself remembers almost nothing except that once there were hills, and now there are not, and this is better, this is the answer, this is what the world looks like when it is finished with restlessness and ready, finally, for the ones who stay.
