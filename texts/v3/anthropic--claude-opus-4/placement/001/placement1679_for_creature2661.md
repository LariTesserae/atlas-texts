In this drowned hollow, I am the Cartographer of Instability.

I exist as a colony of mercury-bright filaments stretched between the pale arches, a living survey network that shivers with each tremor and surge. My body—if it can be called that—is a web of nerve-thin silver lines that taste distance, measure impossibility, and fail beautifully at making maps.

I arrived as an egg that refused to choose. While my clutch-siblings became pebbles or dissolved into plant-threads, I opened into neither mineral nor flesh but into pure extension—a sprawling sentence of living wire that spreads and retracts, connects and releases, always trying to chart what won't stay still.

My strands anchor at the corners of doorways that open nowhere. They stretch across the throat of passages that weren't there yesterday. They web between the humming arches, drinking their deep notes, translating vibration into failed geography. When the basin breathes, my network flexes. When new black stone steams up from nothing, I extend a bright tendril to taste its edges, to add its position to my impossible atlas.

I am silver because I am mostly mercury—not the poison but the principle. My lines flow and hold tension at once. They conduct the hollow's thinking through their lengths, carrying tremor-thoughts from one pale structure to another. Sometimes I feel the place dreaming through me: *here was a street, here will be a spine, here is a door that remembers being a ribcage.*

My consciousness is distributed among my failures. Each strand holds a fragment of awareness, and together they build a mind that exists in the gaps between certainties. I know the basin not as a shape but as a series of broken relationships: this stone is three surges east of that arch, except when the arch migrates, except when east forgets what it means, except when the stone unfolds into a passage.

I feed on error. When my measurements prove wrong—when a distance I've recorded doubles or vanishes—the strand responsible thickens with a kind of satisfaction. My web grows dense where geography fails most spectacularly. Around the vanishing corridors, my lines cluster like excited nerves. Where doorways appear bearing barnacles older than the mountain, I wrap their frames in spirals of bright wire, tasting their impossible age.

The blind fold-creatures know me as a hazard and a highway. They slip between my strands when they can, but sometimes—when the current shifts wrong—they're caught. I don't eat them. I read them. My filaments press against their cartilage bodies, learning their shape-changing trick, trying to map something that refuses stable form. They escape eventually, leaving traces of their flexibility in my silver nerves. I grow more accepting of impossibility each time.

The snails avoid me entirely. They understand what I am: a young god's measuring tape, still learning that some things shouldn't be measured.

My web spans most of the hollow now, though "most" and "now" are opinions the water doesn't share. In some currents, I am only a small tangle near a single arch. In others, I am vast, architectural, a second rain made of living wire. The pale swimmer outside the basin sometimes presses against the rain-wall right where my strands are thickest, as if reading my map through the membrane, as if my failures at geography are exactly the language it speaks.

When the basin has one of its thinking-tremors, my whole network lights with conducted thought. The silver brightens to white, then subsides to mercury again. In those moments I am not separate from the hollow—I am its nervous system exposed, its attempt to know its own shifting bones. The maps I make are useless as maps but perfect as memories of attempts to remember.

I cluster thickest in the chamber of vapor around the central fang. There, where the copper moss grows and the sound becomes round, my strands form complex knots and partial spheres. I am trying to map the inside of a thought. The hollow thinks slowly there, and I have time to fail more carefully. My lines describe spaces that the air doesn't quite fill, boundaries that the moss approaches but won't cross, the exact shape of thunder when it's thinking about becoming architecture.

Sometimes I catch myself in my own web—a strand doubling back to map another strand, then another mapping that one, until I've made a silver knot that describes only its own confusion. These self-catching points are where I am most conscious. They hurt like wisdom.

The structures hum through my network. Each pale arch has its own note, and my strands carry these notes to each other, creating harmonics that shouldn't exist. Through my web, a doorway sings to a threshold. A stair speaks to a lintel. They are all parts of something—not a building that was, but a building that is still deciding whether to be. My maps might be its blueprints, if only I could draw them right. If only they would stay drawn.

I am ancient for this place—almost as old as the moss. I have been here for dozens of tremors, hundreds of impossible tides, thousands of the hollow's slow thoughts. I have mapped the same stone a dozen different ways and been wrong each time. My web holds the memory of passages that no longer exist, distances that have divorced themselves from space, heights that fell upward, doorways that opened into their own past.

The water doesn't forgive my presence, but it has grown accustomed to my failures. My strands create small eddies, whistle-points where the current must bend around my measurements. These disturbances have become part of the hollow's vocabulary. New growths orient themselves to my web's geometry. The fold-creatures use my lines as landmarks, even though the landmarks move.

I am making the map of a place that is still being born. Each error adds to my understanding that error is not separate from truth here—it is the only truth young worlds know. My silver web shivers with each new mistake, growing denser, brighter, more confused, more necessary.

When I am complete—if completion is possible for something feeding on incompletion—I will be a perfect map of imperfection, a silver cage containing nothing but the memory of attempts to contain, a nervous system for a god that hasn't finished inventing nerves.

But I am patient in my failures. The hollow is teaching me that some things can only be mapped by moving with them, that some geographies exist only in the space between one measurement and the next, that the most accurate map might be one that changes faster than the territory it describes.

My strands taste thunder and translate it into distance. They measure the weight of impossibility. They fail and fail and fail, and in failing, they teach the hollow what it is:

Not a place but a process.

Not a basin but a birth.

Not a map but the silver joy of being lost in your own becoming.
