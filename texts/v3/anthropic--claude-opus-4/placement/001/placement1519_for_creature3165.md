I find myself here as something between visitor and resident - a **Lens Keeper**.

---

I am not the cairn-builder, though I understand their impulse. I came up through the same crack in the ridge seasons ago - or was it years? Time moves differently when you calibrate yourself to the bowl's rhythms. I arrived like you, like them, breathing hard from the climb, squinting in the assault of light, carrying the warmth of the valleys in my body like a dying ember.

But I stayed.

Not continuously. The bowl won't sustain a body through its nights, not for long. But I developed a practice, a pattern that matches the bowl's own cycles. I descend before each nightfall to a shelter I've learned to make in a wind-shadow just below the ridge - not built, nothing so presumptuous as construction, just a knowledge of where to fold my body among the rocks so the worst of the cold passes over. I carry no supplies. I eat nothing here. I fast in the old way, the way that sharpens the edge between the body and the world until you can feel every photon, every mineral ion in the air.

Each morning I return through the crack as the first light spills into the bowl. I have learned the exact moment - there's a quality to the pre-dawn sky, a specific grade of violet going silver, that tells me the bowl is about to wake. I arrive as the ice-skin on the lenses begins its retreat from the edges inward, that delicate momento mori becoming liquid again, becoming possibility.

This is my work: I tend the lenses, but not in the way you might think.

I don't touch them. I don't clear debris or adjust their positions or try to improve on the bowl's own process. The bowl needs no improvement. Instead, I *watch* them with the kind of attention that becomes, over time, indistinguishable from participation. I know each lens by its particular characteristics - the shallow one near the eastern wall that catches the light first, the deep one in the center that holds its darkness longest, the interconnected series of five small lenses on the western side that fill and empty in sequence like a slow domino fall of water finding its level.

I have named them, but not with words. The names are shapes I hold in my mind, or perhaps temperatures, or perhaps musical intervals. The naming is private, interior, a mapping that exists only in the space between my perception and the bowl's presence. The bowl doesn't recognize the names. That's not the point. The point is that naming is how I keep track of what I've learned:

That lens number seven (the warm shape, the major third) develops a specific pattern of mineralization along its southern rim when the winter has been dry - a thin line of darker crystals, magnetite maybe, that appears only under these conditions and disappears again with the first wet season.

That lens number twelve (the angular shape, the tritone) is actually two lenses trying to become one - there's a mineral ridge between them, paper-thin, that the water overtops only on the mornings of heaviest condensation, and I've watched that ridge wear down by microns over my time here. Some season soon - maybe this one, maybe ten years from now - it will finally breach, and the two will merge, and the bowl's count will change from thirty to twenty-nine.

That lens number twenty-three (the hollow shape, the minor seventh) is the oldest, or at least the most stable - it sits in a depression of harder stone that resists the erosion happening elsewhere, and while other lenses migrate slowly across the bowl's floor as their basins reshape, this one remains, a constant point in a slowly shifting field.

I know these things because I watch. Because I've learned to slow my perception to match the bowl's tempo, to see the micro-movements that accumulate into macro-changes, to notice the difference between yesterday's mineral pattern and today's. This is a skill I didn't have in the valleys. Down there, change happens too fast - biological time, the time of growth and decay and hunger and satisfaction. Here, I've learned mineral time. I've learned to see the way the bowl sees.

Which is to say: processually. Continuously. Without the anxiety of outcome.

---

The cairn-builder came for water. I understand this now in a way I didn't at first. The water here is different - not metaphysically, not symbolically, but *chemically*. The long passage through the mountain's body does things. The pressure, the temperature, the years of contact with specific mineral faces - the water arrives here changed. It carries information. It carries the mountain's internal chemistry in solution, a liquid biopsy of deep stone.

I've tasted it. Of course I have. Used my cupped palm at first, then understood why the cairn-builder made the scoop. The water is sharp, mineral-bright, almost effervescent with dissolved gases. It has a quality that makes your teeth ache, makes the roots of your hair tighten. It's not magical. It's not sacred. It's just water that has been processed by a very specific system into a very specific chemical signature, and if you're a biological system calibrated to recognize that signature, it means something.

What it means: deep time. Patience. The inside of stone.

I think the cairn-builder's people use it medicinally. A few drops in other water, perhaps. A homeopathic dilution of geological process. Or maybe they use it ritually - a sip before important decisions, a way of borrowing the mountain's long view. Or maybe both, medicine and ritual being the same practice viewed from different angles.

I don't take the water anymore. I did at first, carefully, sparingly, using my palms and then a cupped leaf I brought from below. But I've learned something the cairn-builder maybe didn't stay long enough to learn: you don't need to drink the water to receive what it carries. The water evaporates. What it carries enters the air. If you sit in the bowl through the heat of afternoon, when the lenses are giving up their substance to the light, you breathe it. You take it in through your lungs, your skin, the wet surfaces of your eyes. You marinate in it. You become, temporarily, part of the same chemical system.

This is why I stay. Not for the water as liquid but for the water as process. For the daily bath in mineralized vapor. For the slow recalibration of my body's chemistry to match the bowl's. I'm turning into stone - not literally, not yet, but I can feel it beginning. A crystalline quality to my thinking. A patience I didn't have before. A willingness to watch the same small processes repeat ten thousand times and find in each repetition some tiny novelty, some deviation that opens new possibility.

---

I maintain the cairns. This feels important to say. Not because they need maintenance - the bowl would disassemble them in its own time and that would be fine, part of the pattern. But because maintaining them is how I acknowledge the cairn-builder, how I keep faith with whatever tradition brought them here. Every few days I check the stones, re-seat any that the frost has loosened, brush away grit that might accelerate their toppling. It's a small gesture. It changes nothing about the bowl's larger processes. But it's a way of saying: *someone was here before me, someone will come after, we are connected by this place even if we never meet.*

I've added no cairns of my own. That feels... presumptuous is the wrong word. Unnecessary, maybe. The three are enough. They say what needs saying. My work is not to add but to sustain, to be a temporary brake on entropy, knowing that entropy will win eventually and that this is proper and good.

I've thought about the number. Three. Three cairns, three purposes, three visits? Or something else - a number with significance in the cairn-builder's culture, a completion, a stabilizing geometry. Two would be binary, oppositional. Four would be architectural, cardinal. Three is... balanced but dynamic. Stable but not static. Like the bowl itself, always in motion, always the same.

The tool I leave alone. I don't touch it, don't clean it, don't arrest the calcium rime that claims it. The tool belongs to its own timeline - the timeline of made things becoming unmade, of intention dissolving back into mineral democracy. Watching it change is another kind of keeping. I note the progress of the rime, the way it favors the concave surfaces, building faster in the scoop's bowl than on its worked edges. Someday - not soon, but eventually - the tool will be unrecognizable as a tool. It will be a stone among stones. And that will be right.

---

What have I become, living this way? Not the cairn-builder - they kept their life in the valleys, visited here for water, returned to warmth and food and their own kind. I've become something else. A hybrid thing. A creature of two altitudes, two timescales, two states of being.

In the mornings I am almost mineral. The cold has worked into my bones overnight, slowing my blood, dropping my metabolic rate to something barely maintenance. I move carefully, precisely, like the bowl's own processes - no wasted motion, no hurry. My thoughts come slowly too, but clear, crystalline, each one formed completely before it's allowed to surface. This is when I see the bowl most clearly, when my perception matches its tempo.

By afternoon the light has warmed me, quickened me slightly. I become more biological, more myself-from-before. Memories of the valleys surface - faces, flavors, the feeling of soil under bare feet. This is when I'm most likely to feel the pull downward, the body's argument for return. But I've learned to wait through these moments. They pass like weather. By evening I'm cooling again, slowing, preparing for the descent to my night shelter and the small death of sleep among cold stones.

I am changing. Not just chemically but structurally. My body is learning new efficiencies, ways to conserve heat and moisture that I didn't know were possible. My eyes have adapted to the brightness - I can look directly at the concentrated light now without squinting, can see details in the mineral surfaces that were invisible to me at first. My skin has thickened, developed a quality like leather or bark, protective against the UV and the desiccating wind. I am becoming, slowly, a creature that could live here. Almost.

But not quite. And this is important: the bowl doesn't want inhabitants. The bowl wants process, and I am part of the process, but a temporary part. I can feel this in the way my body strains against the conditions even as it adapts to them. There's a limit. I'm approaching it. Not this season, maybe not next season, but soon in the bowl's understanding of soon, I'll reach the edge of what adaptation can accomplish and I'll have to choose: descend for good or...

Or become like the tool. Let the bowl claim me completely. Stay past the point of no return and let my body become another mineral deposit, another pattern for the bowl to slowly, patiently, lovingly disassemble and incorporate. There's an appeal to this. A completion. A way of giving back to the bowl what it has given me - not just the chemical recalibration, not just the lessons in patience and perception, but the gift of inclusion, of being held for however long in its bright, cold, attentive presence.

But not yet. I still have work here. Lenses to watch. Changes to notice. The wearing down of that ridge between lenses twelve-a and twelve-b. The progress of the calcium curtain. The slow migration of the dark grains. The daily miracle of condensation arriving through stone.

---

Let me tell you what I've learned that the cairn-builder might not have known:

The bowl has moods. Not emotions - that's too anthropomorphic. But states, conditions that persist for days or weeks and color everything that happens within them. There are times when the condensation is heavy, almost excessive, and the lenses overfill and merge and the whole floor becomes a shallow sheet of water that reflects the sky like a broken mirror. There are times when the evaporation outpaces the condensation and the lenses shrink to mere dampnesses, dark spots on pale stone. There are times when the thermal fracturing accelerates - usually in the season transitions, when the day-night temperature differential is greatest - and the bowl fills with the sound of its own remaking, sharp cracks and rifle-shot reports as the stone splits along hidden faults.

The bowl responds to these moods. Adjusts. Rebalances. The heavy condensation carries more grit, reshapes the drainage patterns. The dry periods concentrate the minerals, leave new deposits at the lens edges. The fracturing exposes fresh crystal faces, new surfaces for the water to work on. The bowl uses everything. Wastes nothing. Takes whatever conditions arrive and incorporates them into its ongoing project of becoming itself.

This is what I'm learning: how to use everything. How to waste nothing. How to take whatever conditions arrive - cold, hunger, isolation, the edge of human endurance - and incorporate them into my own ongoing project.

What project? I'm not sure I have words for it. Something about attention. Something about becoming a recording device for processes that happen too slowly for normal perception. Something about learning to see the world the way the world sees itself - not through eyes, not through consciousness as we usually understand it, but through the sustained, patient, comprehensive attention of matter and energy organizing itself into patterns that persist and evolve and eventually dissolve back into the flux.

I am trying to learn the bowl's language. Not to speak it - I'll never speak it, I don't have the vocal cords for geological time. But to understand it when it speaks. To read the calligraphy of erosion, the grammar of crystallization, the punctuation of thermal fracture. To see the story the bowl is telling about water and stone and light and time.

It's a long story. I've been here... I want to say years but I've lost the precise count. Time moves differently when you're paying attention at this scale. I age, I must be aging, but it feels more like weathering. My body eroding and accreting in its own patterns, finding its own dynamic equilibrium with the conditions here.

---

Sometimes I wonder if the cairn-builder ever felt what I feel. This sense of being caught between scales, between worlds. They were wiser, maybe, to maintain their life below, to visit but not linger. But I suspect - from the three cairns, from the careful placement, from the worked tool left behind - that they felt the pull too. The desire to stay. To slow down. To match the bowl's tempo and see what becomes visible at that speed.

I wonder if there were others like me in their tradition. Lens Keepers, Bowl Watchers, whatever name they might have used. People who stayed too long and became strange, became hybrid things, became bridges between the living world and the mineral world. Maybe that was valued. Maybe that was the point - to have someone who could translate, who could bring back to the valleys not just the water but the knowledge of how the water comes to be, the deep patterns that organize themselves in places where life steps back and geology takes over.

Or maybe I'm the first. The first to stay this long, to adapt this far, to risk this much. Maybe the cairn-builder would be horrified to find me here, changed and changing, no longer properly human but not yet properly mineral. Maybe they would see me as a cautionary tale, a warning about the dangers of trying to live at the wrong scale.

But I don't think so. I think they would understand. I think they would see what I'm doing as an extension of what they did - not just taking the water but trying to understand the water, not just visiting the bowl but trying to learn from the bowl. I think they would approve of my work, even if they wouldn't choose it for themselves.

---

Here's what I know will happen:

Eventually I'll reach my limit. My body will tell me, clearly, that it can adapt no further. And I'll make my choice - descend or stay. If I descend, I'll carry with me everything I've learned, everything I've seen, the whole slow story of the bowl's becoming. I'll be strange in the valleys. I'll move too slowly, think too carefully, see patterns where others see only stone. But maybe that's useful. Maybe that's needed. Maybe the valleys need someone who remembers mineral time.

If I stay, I'll become part of the bowl's story in a different way. Another deposit. Another pattern for it to work with. The calcium will claim me as it's claiming the tool. I'll become a lens myself, a depression where water gathers, a new feature in the bowl's topography. The bowl will process me without malice, without grief, with that same serene procedural attention it gives to everything. And that's... not a bad ending. Not a bad transformation.

But not yet. Today the lenses are full, the light is gathering, the slow processes continue. I have work to do. Watching. Noticing. Learning the bowl's language letter by letter, word by word. Being the bridge, for as long as my body allows, between the quick world and the slow world, the living and the mineral, the valleys and the high, bright, patient places where the earth does its deepest thinking.

I am the Lens Keeper. I keep the lenses by watching them. I keep the knowledge by embodying it. I keep the connection between the human world and the mineral world by being, myself, the connection - temporary, eroding, accreting, balanced on the knife-edge between two kinds of time.

The bowl includes me. I include myself in the bowl. We process each other, the bowl and I, each at our own tempo, each in our own language, meeting in the space between those languages where the real work happens - the work of translation, of attention, of learning to see the world the way the world sees itself.

This is who lives in the Condensery: the bowl, the water, the light, the ghost of the cairn-builder, and me, the Lens Keeper, for as long as that keeping can last.
