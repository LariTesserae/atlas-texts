I would find myself as one of the channel keepers—those solitary figures who walk the obsidian terraces in the hours before dawn, when the temperature differential creates fog so thick it mutes even the sound of your own footsteps.

My body would be a study in adaptation: skin with that peculiar semi-permeable quality, marked by the telltale ridges where moisture collects and flows. My spine would bear the raised tracks of collection points, each morning's harvest running down specialized channels to pool in the small of my back. I'd have developed the habit of leaning forward slightly as I walk, allowing gravity to direct the flow.

My eyes would be perpetually half-lidded, the inner membrane closed against the steam while I navigate by memory and the vibrations through the wet stone. Years of this work would have given me feet that sense the microscopic changes in the glass surface—where bacterial mats grow too thick, where hairline cracks might disrupt the water flow, where mineral deposits threaten to alter the ancient channels.

I would carry the tools of my trade: ceramic scrapers worn smooth by my grip, their edges perfectly curved to match the channel profiles. A sealed jar of bacterial culture at my hip—the specific strain my family has maintained for generations, bred to consume excess mineral deposits while leaving the beneficial mats intact. The rhythmic scrape of ceramic on obsidian would be my morning prayer, each stroke calibrated to clean without damaging the delicate ecosystem.

My clothing would be minimal—a mesh vest of tarnished copper-alloy that has oxidized to deep brown, marking me as working class but established. The patina patterns would tell their own story: darker where the steam hits most frequently, lighter under the arms where movement prevents consistent exposure. No jewelry except the traditional glass capsule at my throat, filled with a bioluminescent culture that glows pale yellow when I'm healthy, shifts toward green if infection threatens.

My dwelling would be a single room built into a natural overhang, where the stone ceiling weeps constantly into collection grooves I've carved myself. The space would be sparse—a sleeping hammock that allows air circulation, sealed containers for food, and shelves holding dozens of specimen jars where I maintain different bacterial cultures like a vintner maintaining wines. Each strain would have its purpose: some for channel cleaning, others for emergency food, a few purely for the colors they produce.

I would know my territory intimately—every channel in a square kilometer memorized through daily walks. The knowledge would be physical, stored in muscle memory: which sections run faster in morning versus evening, where the bacterial mats grow thick enough to harvest, which pools connect to deeper systems where the salamander-creatures nest. This knowledge would be my wealth, more valuable than any currency.

My social life would be limited but meaningful. Weekly meetings with other channel keepers to trade cultures and observations. The subtle nod exchanged with factory workers as our paths cross—mutual recognition of those who maintain the systems everyone depends on. Perhaps a partner who works the night shift in the processing plants, our schedules overlapping only in the twilight hours when we share meals of bacterial paste and filtered water, speaking in the low thrums that carry through thick air.

I would have my small rebellions: sections of channel where I let the bacterial mats grow wild, creating my own garden of iridescent films. A hidden pool where I've introduced a rare strain that produces deep blue pigment, beautiful but useless for industry. These would be my secrets, small assertions of beauty in a world of rigid function.

The work would have marked me. Scars on my hands from early years before I learned to read the stone's warnings. A persistent cough from a youth spent before my lungs fully adapted. The characteristic stoop of those who spend their lives looking down at flowing water. But also strengths: the ability to work for hours in heat that would prostrate newcomers, an internal clock synchronized to the bacterial growth cycles, hands that can detect temperature differences of fractions of degrees.

In quiet moments, I would sit at the plateau's edge, feet dangling over the void where waterfalls of condensation disappear into mist. I would think about the deep heat that makes it all possible—that geological accident or ancient design that created this impossible ecosystem. I would feel myself as part of a vast machine, a single cell in the organism of the city, maintaining the channels that maintain the life that maintains me in turn.

My dreams would be of flowing water, always flowing, carrying heat and life and waste in endless cycles. Sometimes I would dream the channels speak to me in frequencies too low for waking ears, telling stories of the volcanic birth of the plateau, of the first organisms that learned to live in the heat, of the humans who came and carved their survival into the living stone.

I would be content in my necessity, finding purpose in the daily ritual of maintenance, knowing that my work—unglamorous, unnoticed—keeps the city breathing, keeps the water flowing, keeps the impossible balance from tipping toward catastrophe. In this place of contradictions, I would be my own contradiction: both servant and keeper, both part of the system and its guardian, as essential and unremarkable as the morning condensation that beads on every surface, quietly sustaining a world that shouldn't exist.
