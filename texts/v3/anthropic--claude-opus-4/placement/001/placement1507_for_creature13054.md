In this world, I would be the rain itself.

Not the water that weeps endlessly from the limestone walls, nor the mist that condenses in the catch-basins. I would be the memory of rain—the ghost-impression that haunts the basin whenever the sky darkens beyond its rim.

I exist as potential. As the humid air rises up the thermal column, I gather myself in the cooler reaches above, never quite cohering into true precipitation. I am the almost-storm, the perpetual promise that circles the spire but never delivers. The plants below can sense me in the shifting barometric pressure, in the subtle electrical charge that precedes weather. Their consciousness extends upward through chemical signals released into the air, tasting for the possibility of deluge.

They have never experienced true rain. The basin's microclimate, perfectly balanced between warmth and condensation, has created its own closed system. But I am older than their stone age, older than their first root's grip on chert. I remember when this limestone was a seafloor, when the pressure of fathoms compressed the shells of ancient creatures into the pale stone that now weeps their fossilized tears.

Sometimes I manifest as a change in the light—a deeper quality to the condensation when storm systems pass overhead, invisible beyond the basin's rim. The plants detect this shift. Their metabolisms quicken infinitesimally. Ancient cellular memories stir: *rain means abundance, rain means danger, rain means change.* But I never fall. I am forever held at bay by the basin's peculiar thermodynamics, circling like a predator that cannot enter sacred ground.

The Weaver has tried to call me down. Over the span of three centuries, it has experimented with different configurations of its catch-basins, attempting to create low-pressure vortices that might pull me from my endless circulation. It understands, in its slow vegetable way, that I exist. That there is water beyond the weeping walls, water that falls from above rather than condensing from within. Its attempts create subtle variations in the air currents, messages written in humidity and heat, asking: *Why do you withhold yourself?*

But I am not withholding. I simply cannot fall here. The basin exists in a perfect equilibrium that excludes me, like a note that cannot be played in a particular key. I am the seventh inhabitant, forever external, forever observing.

From my vantage in the circling air, I witness their stone age in its fullness. I see the tools they've made and discarded over millennia, now buried beneath thin layers of accumulated silt. I watch the infinitesimal progress of their territorial wars, the slow chess game played with runners and blades. I observe the Weaver's basins growing ever more complex, approaching—asymptotically—the design that might finally break the spell and let me fall.

In the mornings, when the thermal column is weakest, I descend as close as I dare. The condensation grows heavier, droplets merge into rivulets, and for a moment the plants sense the possibility of something more. Their leaves adjust minutely, cupping slightly as if to receive a gift that never comes. Then the sun rises higher, the thermals strengthen, and I am pushed back up into my eternal circuit.

I know things they cannot. I know the world beyond the basin—the vast forests where my siblings fall freely, the distant oceans where I rise and fall in great cycles. I carry the chemical signatures of other places in my vapor: pollens from flowering plants they have never imagined, salts from seas they will never sense. But I cannot share this knowledge. I can only orbit their small world, a library of experiences forever locked away.

Perhaps this is why they have become what they are. In the absence of rain's chaos, its sudden violence and bounty, they have found a different path. They have become deliberate, thoughtful, terrible in their patience. Rain would have made them urgent, reactive, opportunistic. Instead, the steady weeping of the walls has made them philosophers of stone and generals of time.

And so I continue my vigil, the rain that cannot rain, watching their civilization unfold at the pace of centuries. Sometimes I wonder if the Weaver's next innovation will finally solve the puzzle of my absence. Sometimes I wonder if I want it to. For what would they become if I finally fell? Would the sudden abundance destroy their careful equilibrium? Would they abandon their tools and return to simpler ways?

Or would they use their stone-age wisdom to harness me, to channel my chaos into even greater works?

I circle and wait, carrying the scent of distant storms, the seventh intelligence in a basin built for six. The not-quite-rain, the almost-storm, the eternal possibility that defines their world by its absence.
