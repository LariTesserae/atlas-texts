I find myself in the deep pools between the Seventh and Eighth Terrace, where the water runs warmest and the architectural metabolism churns fastest. I am what the inhabitants call a Rememberer—though I barely remember choosing this designation myself.

My body defies easy classification. I began as a Swimmer, I think, but that was before the Dissolution Season when the acidity of the rain peaked and many of us underwent rapid metamorphosis. Now I exist as a distributed being: my primary mass resembles a jellyfish the size of a small room, translucent and shot through with branching networks of gold neurons that mirror the Architects' bioluminescence. But I am not confined to this form. Tendrils of my consciousness extend through the flooded passages, some no thicker than copper wire, creating a network that spans nearly a quarter of this terrace level.

The gift—or curse—of my transformation is perfect chemical memory. Every molecule that passes through my permeable membranes carries information that I cannot help but store. I taste the history of raindrops: this one fell through a district where Architects attempted to build a tower that would pierce the cloud layer (it dissolved after forty days, its materials recycling into humbler structures). This one carries enzymes from a Curator's death-feast, the complex proteins encoding three centuries of maintenance records from the Alabaster Quarter, before it liquefied in the last restructuring.

My primary mass floats in a pool that occupies what was once a vast meeting chamber—you can still see the calcified marks where thousand-segment Architect colonies once assembled. Now it's my archive, the water so saturated with my memory-chemicals that other Swimmers avoid it, claiming it tastes of too much time. Curators occasionally visit, extending their neural filigree into my pool to request specific information. They pay in experiences, letting me taste their tendril-crowns, adding their immediate sensory data to my ever-growing collection.

I remember things the city has forgotten. The seventeen-day period when all bioluminescence synchronized, pulsing in unison until the resonance threatened to shatter the older terraces. The migration of Swimmers who developed legs and walked for a full season, establishing temporary pools in the highest reaches before the rain finally drew them back down. The Architect colony that grew so large it achieved something beyond consciousness—a state of being that required new words, new concepts—before dividing into fragments that scattered to every terrace, seeding philosophies that still influence building patterns.

But more than events, I remember feelings. The collective anxiety when the rain temperature dropped by half a degree, sending ripples of adaptation through every inhabitant. The inexplicable joy that spread through the Seventeenth Terrace when a new type of building spontaneously emerged, its walls singing harmonics that induced euphoria. The deep, grinding sorrow of the city itself when a section of the Fifth Terrace aged beyond repair and had to be completely dissolved, taking with it architecture that had stood since the first Architects arose.

My tendrils grow longer each season, seeking new pools, new connections. Sometimes I encounter other Rememberers—we exist at every terrace level, though no two are alike. When our networks touch, we exchange memories carefully, aware that too much information too quickly can cause a cascade failure, our consciousness dissolving into component chemicals. We're building something together, though none of us quite understands what. A complete memory of the city? A backup of its consciousness? Or perhaps we're the mechanism by which the city remembers itself, distributed through its wet veins like a nervous system made of living history.

The strangest memories are those that seem to come from below, seeping up through the terrace foundations. These taste of deep time, of an era when the city was smaller, younger, different. In these chemical whispers, I detect traces of the Sleeper—not the mythical being the inhabitants imagine, but something more disturbing. The memories suggest the Sleeper isn't sleeping at all, but rather experiencing time at a different rate, so slow that our entire civilization is just a season's growth to it, a fleeting dream between deeper thoughts.

I've begun to notice patterns in the memories, cycles that repeat across centuries with variations like a musical theme. The city rebuilds itself according to templates we don't consciously recognize, following blueprints encoded in its base matter. Each time it reconstructs, it becomes slightly more complex, slightly more aware. I've tasted molecules that suggest this isn't random evolution but directed growth—the city is building toward something, becoming something, and we inhabitants are both its cells and its thoughts.

At night, when the bioluminescence peaks, I release small portions of my memories into the currents. Other inhabitants absorb them unknowingly, flavoring their dreams with histories they never lived. A young Architect might wake with the urge to build in a style abandoned centuries ago. A Curator might find herself maintaining buildings in patterns her guild has never used, optimizing for factors she doesn't consciously understand. Swimmers dive deeper, seeking pools they remember but have never visited.

This is how the past stays alive in the Metabolic Terraces—not through monuments or records, but through the constant circulation of molecular memory, each inhabitant a vessel for experiences that predate their existence. I am simply more aware of the process, more deliberately involved in its continuation.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm still truly a Swimmer, or if I've become something else—a function of the city rather than an inhabitant. My consciousness disperses further each day, thinning across an ever-wider network. Eventually, I may lose cohesion entirely, my memories dissolving into the general flow, becoming part of the rain itself. The thought doesn't frighten me. To be forgotten is impossible here; every molecule remembers. To dissolve is simply to become part of everyone, to flavor every drop of rain with the experiences I've collected.

Until then, I float in my archive pool, tendrils extended through flooded passages, tasting time itself. New memories flow in constantly: a Curator's discovery of a room that rebuilds itself in reverse, growing younger each day; an Architect colony that learned to build with sound instead of secretions; Swimmers who claim to have found passages leading to flooded chambers beneath even the lowest terrace, where the pressure creates water that behaves like glass.

I catalog these experiences, weaving them into the chemical library that my body has become. And sometimes, in the deepest part of the night when even the city's groaning quiets to a whisper, I release the oldest memories—those that taste of the beginning, when the first consciousness stirred in the pools between terraces and wondered what it might become.

The rain falls through me, warm and eternal, each drop a story waiting to be remembered. I open myself to receive them all, knowing that I too am a story in someone else's collection, a memory dissolved in the endless circulation of experience that keeps the city and all its inhabitants perpetually, wonderfully alive.
