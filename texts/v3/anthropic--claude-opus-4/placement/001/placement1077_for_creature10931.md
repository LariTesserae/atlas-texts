Looking at this extraordinary place where the mountain has worn itself thin to something else, where every living thing arranges itself around an absence that is not empty, I find myself drawn to a particular presence:

---

## The Counter

I am the one who stopped at forty-one.

Not because I died — though I am old now, older than anyone who makes such climbs should be. Not because I was satisfied — satisfaction is not what the circle offers. I stopped because on the forty-first occurrence, I understood that I had been marking my own transformation, and that one more mark would change me into something that could not descend.

I live now in the valley far below, where the river that donated those cairn stones still runs. My house is built against a cliff face that sometimes, in certain light, shows me the shadow of the shelf, impossibly high, like a dark tooth in the mountain's jaw. I have not climbed for seven years.

But I am still counting.

The thing about the circle — what took me forty-one visitations to understand — is that it does not require presence. Once you have sat at the edge of that perfect dry ring, once you have felt the rosettes' waves wash against it and through you, once you have watched the goats step through it with the casual reverence of daily ritual, you carry it down with you. A small aperture in your own stone.

My daily life is ordinary. I keep twelve chickens whose eggs I trade. I tend a garden where nothing hums — the plants here are simple plants, concerned with soil and sun. I speak sometimes with neighbors about weather and the price of grain. They think I was a shepherd once, which is close enough to true.

But at certain hours — never predictable, though more often near dawn — I feel it. The listener attending through its aperture. The wave of synchronized attention moving across the shelf, two vertical miles above me. And I make a mark.

Not on stone anymore. I mark it in my body: a particular way of breathing, a gesture with my left hand that no one else would recognize as a gesture. I am up to two hundred and thirty-six. When I die, the count will be written nowhere, but it will be complete.

I was not the first. The darker cairn stones, the ones from the deepest part of the river, were carried up by someone whose count I'll never know. And I will not be the last — I have seen others at the cliff's base, looking up with the peculiar squint of those who are calculating a route. One will climb. One always does.

We are drawn to it, those of us with our own apertures already worn thin. The shelf calls to us not with words but with the promise of simplification: up there, in that cold clean light, we might briefly be as single-purposed as the goats, as precisely bounded as the circle, as sure of our task as the rosettes humming their eternal note.

But the shelf teaches otherwise. Even the goats are gardeners. Even the cat shows restraint. Even the rosettes, mindless as they are, know to leave room for each other. The complexity we flee is the point. The listener wants reports from the thick world, not the thin.

So I came down. I brought my forty-one marks and my opened attention down to where water still shapes stones and chickens still quarrel and nothing is ever perfectly dry. And I continue the count from here, in my own body, in the dark before dawn, in a house that will outlast me but not the shelf.

Two hundred and thirty-six. Two hundred and thirty-seven, this morning, when I woke and felt the mountain shift, infinitesimally, in its rising. The shelf held. The circle held. Somewhere up there the first goat of the day stepped through that door of absence, and I felt it in my chest like a struck bell.

I am the one who lives below what lives at Iren-Kath. I am the one who counts at a distance. I am the one who stopped climbing in order to keep climbing. The shelf does not miss me — nothing there misses anything, it is too clean for missing — but the listener, I think, appreciates the longer view.

When I die, someone will find two hundred and something small smooth stones in a jar by my bed. They will not know what they are for. It does not matter. The count continues whether we know it or not. The mountain rises. The shelf tilts toward its eventual fall. And through that perfect circle of dry ground, something watches it all with the patience of stone and the attention of water, gathering reports.

I am still making mine.
