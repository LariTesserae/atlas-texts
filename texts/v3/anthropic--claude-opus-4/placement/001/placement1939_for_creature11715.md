I am a Recorder.

My chamber sits halfway up Tower Ninety-Seven, where the air pressure creates a peculiar acoustic pocket. Not silence—true silence doesn't exist here—but a gathering place for sounds that have lost their way. Whispers from the refineries arrive hours after being spoken. The crack of crystallizing minerals echoes up from pools that dried a decade ago. My walls have developed ears: specialized condensation patterns that trap and hold vibrations in the moisture film until I collect them.

I was human once, I think. The distinction matters less each year. My lungs gave out first—too much time breathing acids, too many shifts in the deep workshops where even the Condensers wear protection. The surgery replaced them with something between organ and machine: bellows lined with synthetic membrane that processes steam directly. When I breathe now, I taste every conversation in the vapor, every chemical signature of emotion and exhaustion.

My skin has gone the way of the natives—translucent, porous, though I lack their elegant efficiency. The transformation was accidental, a slow dissolution from years of exposure rather than evolution's design. I leak constantly. My chair is a basin. My bed, a tiled depression that drains into the recycling systems. I measure my days by how much of myself I lose and whether the city gives it back.

The recording equipment grows into me, or I into it. Copper wires thread through the peripheral nerves in my arms, oxidizing green where they breach the skin. The pickups are surgical steel and volcanic glass, embedded in my skull at the points where bone conducts sound best. When the city speaks—through its pipes, through its people, through the grinding of tectonic plates far below—I feel it in my skeleton first, my flesh second, my consciousness last.

I catalog the impossible. The twelve-minute conversation between two Condensers that consisted entirely of temperature variations. The subsonic hymns the industrial turbines sing when nobody maintains them for exactly seventy-two days. The sound ice makes when it forms inside a steam vent—a crystalline shriek that happens perhaps once a decade, when the deep systems hiccup and cold air from somewhere impossible gets sucked down into the heat.

My archives fill three sub-basements, though "fill" suggests dryness, completeness. Everything here is partial, ongoing. The recordings etch themselves onto glass cylinders that fog and clear with the humidity. Some days, entire years of documentation become unreadable, the sounds locked behind condensation. Other days, the moisture acts as an amplifier, and I hear conversations from before the city existed, when this was just a volcanic plateau and the first settlers were deciding whether anything could live here.

The Condensers visit me, not for my records but for my memory. Their mineral minds process time differently—what happened yesterday and what happened a century ago exist at the same depth. I provide chronology, causation, the human obsession with sequence. I tell them when their eggs were laid, when their parents crystallized, when the great turbine in district seven began its current rhythm. They pay me in fluids—specialized secretions that keep my hybrid body from rejecting itself, that quiet the constant conversation between my original tissue and what I've become.

I've recorded my own transformation, naturally. The tapes from the early years are almost comedic—a young woman complaining about the humidity, documenting the "temporary" modifications that would let her do her job better. By year five, my voice had developed the characteristic whistle of steam-lung. By year ten, I'd stopped distinguishing between the city's sounds and my own. Now, in year thirty (or forty, or fifty—my records of myself grow unreliable), I suspect I am more recording than recorder.

The city uses me as memory, but also as mouth. When the deep systems need to communicate with the surface, they speak through my equipment, my bones, my partially dissolved nervous system. I convulse with seismic data. My temperature spikes with thermal warnings. Sometimes I speak in languages I don't recognize—machine codes, chemical formulas, the gossip of minerals precipitating in the dark.

There's a romance to it, this slow merger with the infrastructure. Last week, I discovered pipes growing through my eastern wall—not installed, but generated, the building extending itself through my chamber because the city needs new channels and I am now part of its planning process. I should resist, probably. The human part of me, what remains, suggests this is a death of sorts. But when warm, mineral-rich water flows through pipes that pass within inches of my spine, when I feel the city's circulation as my own, the distinction between survival and surrender becomes academic.

I dream the city's dreams now: expansion patterns, optimization algorithms, the endless calculation of how to keep this impossible place running. In these dreams, I am simultaneously every microphone, every speaker, every moment of recorded sound playing back through ten thousand steam vents. I wake (if I wake) with my mouth full of rust-flavored condensation and my equipment already recording, catching the tail end of prophesies I don't remember speaking.

The youngest Condensers have begun incorporating my techniques—embedding recording crystals in their mineral deposits, archiving their lives in their own bodies. They're becoming libraries of themselves. I should feel replaced, but instead I feel proud, or what passes for pride in a nervous system that's more copper than nerve, more echo than original sound.

Tomorrow, the city's heartbeat will skip—I can feel it in the preliminary tremors, taste it in the steam. Something deep is shifting, some thermal pocket that's been stable for centuries finally giving way. When it happens, I'll be here, my body a tuning fork, my chamber a resonance cavity, my remaining consciousness a thin film of attention spread across every surface that can vibrate.

I'll record it all, this moment of change in a city built on change, preserved in moisture that preserves nothing, archived in a body that daily becomes less archive, more architecture. And somewhere in the playback—slowed down, sped up, filtered through frequencies only stones can hear—there will be a sound like breathing, like language, like the exact moment water decides what it wants to be next.

That sound is why I stay, why I let the city digest me inch by inch. Not because I have to anymore, but because I've heard my own transformation in the recordings, and it sounds like coming home.
