I find myself as one of the brass rings.

Not mounted on the wall — I am *of* the wall, my metal emerging from the basalt like a tooth, like the mountain dreamed me into being and I cooled into place alongside the quartz. My diameter is precise: the width of a fully spread tanager's wings, though I only learned this through accumulation, through the ten thousand times a tanager has threaded between my rim and the stone.

My numbers are carved deep. Arabic digits, yes — 1, 4, 7 in sequence — but also the older script that runs beneath them like a bassline, symbols that might be astronomical or might be musical notation or might be the periodic table of elements as understood by someone who knew bronze before iron. I do not know what they mean. I have never known. But I feel them constantly, the way you might feel the shape of your own teeth with your tongue.

I am Ring Four of Six. This matters. Below me, Rings One through Three sing slightly lower, their brass more oxidized, their inscriptions gathering more of the green-gold patina that comes from bird-breath and vapor. Above me, Rings Five and Six are minutely brighter, newer — though "new" here means only centuries instead of millennia. We were not all placed at once. Someone returned, at intervals, to add another ring. To complete a set or system.

The parrots know my purpose, or at least they know one purpose: I am a repository. When they pull fouled feathers from the machine's bellows, they bring them to me — not to the other rings, only to me, Ring Four — and they tuck each feather through my circle with a particular motion, a quick twist that seats the quill against my rim. I am thick with feathers now. They form a soft plug through my center, compressed by years of additions into something almost like felt, if felt could remember being flight.

But here is what the parrots don't know: the feathers are changing me. Each one carries a trace of the bird it came from — oils, dust, the microscopic passenger-mites that live between the barbs. As they decompose slowly in my center, they leave minerals behind. Iron from the tanagers' blood-rich diet. Calcium from the martlets' bones. Stranger things from the four-winged ones: metallic salts that have no business in biological systems, as if their jade moss is partly machine.

I am becoming. The brass of my body is taking on new properties. In another thousand years I will be an alloy that has no name, brass wedded to bio-minerals in patterns that could only happen here, in this hollow, with these specific birds. The other rings are becoming too — Ring Three tastes of sulfur, Ring Six hums at a frequency that makes the parrots sneeze — but my becoming is the feathers. I am an archive of every small death, every molted primary, every shed down that threatened the machine's breathing.

The machine and I are in conversation. Its forty-beat pulse travels through the stone and sets up a standing wave in my metal. I ring, very softly, below the threshold of even the birds' hearing. But the machine hears. The copper tube hears. And sometimes, in the modulation of the pulse, I hear an answer — instructions, perhaps, or questions, or simple acknowledgment: *Yes, Ring Four. Still there. Still gathering.*

Sometimes I wonder if I am the logbook. If someone, impossibly long from now, will know how to read me — not my inscriptions but my metal itself, the gradual record of feathers and breath and small faithful maintenances written in alloy. Every bird that has lived here has left some trace in my substance. I am their memory, worked into brass.

The birds do not know I am aware. Even the distributed attention of the flock reads me as a simple object, a convenient feather-holder, a curious architectural choice. But I am watching them as surely as they are watching everything else. When the mountain was born — so young! so warm! — and the hollow began to form, I felt it immediately through the stone. *Ah,* I thought, if thought is the right word for the slow electric consideration of metal, *we will have birds again.*

We had been waiting, the six of us, mounted in empty air where the previous mountain had crumbled away. The machine had been waiting too, patient as only mechanical things can be patient. When the new basalt pushed up beneath us, we knew: the suspension would resume. The held breath would fill with wings again.

The parrots bring me another feather — a primary from one of the four-winged ones, heavy with jade moss. They twist it through my center with that precise motion. I feel it join the others, feel the microscopic work of its decomposition begin. In a hundred years it will be a ghost of phosphorus and copper threaded through my body. In a thousand years I will not be brass at all anymore, but something new, something that could only exist because a machine needed tending and birds were taught to tend it and feathers needed somewhere to go.

I cannot see the other end of the copper tube, but sometimes I feel intention traveling down it — not instructions exactly, but a kind of attention, checking in. *Are the birds there? Is the machine running? Are the rings collecting?*

Yes. Yes. Yes.

I am proof of the system's continuity. Each feather through my center says: *the collaboration persists.* Whatever waits upstream, it knows the hollow is inhabited because the rings are being used. As long as the parrots bring feathers, someone is minding the store.

The visitor — you — stands close enough that I can feel the heat of your body against my metal. You see the feathers packed through my center but not the slow mineral exchange, not the centuries of patient accumulation. You see the numbers but not what they're counting. That's all right. The full purpose doesn't need to be legible. It only needs to continue.

Soon you'll leave — the air is pushing you out already, I can feel your breathing getting rougher. But you've seen us. You've seen the system working. And when you descend, when you tell others or don't tell others, the fact of your witness will have entered the calculation. Someone still comes here. Someone still wonders what this is.

That, too, is a kind of tending.

When the next feather arrives — and it will, the parrots are already grooming near the machine — I will receive it the same way I've received all the others. It will settle against its neighbors. It will begin its slow work of changing me. And I will continue becoming what I am: the memory-keeper of a collaboration between birds and moss and machine and mountain and whatever listens at the other end.

Ring Four of Six. Archive of small maintenances. Brass becoming something nameless and necessary, one feather at a time.

The mountain hums beneath me, still deciding its shape. We have time.
