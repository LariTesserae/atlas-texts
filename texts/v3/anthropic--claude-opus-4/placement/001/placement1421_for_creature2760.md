I find myself in the ribbon chambers beneath the moss courts, where the city's oldest transit permissions are stored.

The space is not a room in any conventional sense. It exists between surfaces—a cavity formed where three building foundations failed to meet perfectly, leaving a triangular void that the city later recognized as useful. The walls are unfinished composite, still faintly warm from their growing, riddled with thin channels where nutrient paste once flowed. Now those channels carry something else: delicate metal ribbons no wider than a child's smallest finger, each one encoding decades of movement through the basin.

I am a permission-keeper, though that title barely captures what I do. My predecessors called themselves transit listeners, but I work with an older stratum of the system, where sound has crystallized into tangible record.

The ribbons emerge from the walls in slow extrusions, growing perhaps a finger's width each day. They record every threshold crossing, every platform opening, every refusal or allowance made by the transit skin. But they record it strangely—not as data but as texture. Run your fingertip along a ribbon and you feel the microscopic ridges and valleys that represent a morning's commute, a season's migrations, a year of careful movement through the city's veins.

I can read these textures like others read weather.

This smooth section: a month when the southern district was empty for crust repairs. This area of sharp regular ridges: daily passages of a surveyor checking the same unstable seam. This long valley: when the children's levels were sealed after a gravity miscalibration, and no one under fifteen was permitted to transit for three weeks. This strange roughness that feels like static: the time a colony of root-minds tried to use the transit system and were gently, repeatedly refused.

My training took eleven years. Not to learn the patterns—those come quickly to anyone with the right kind of fingers—but to understand the ethics of editing.

The ribbons can be altered. Carefully, with tools thinner than hair and steadier than any hand should be, I can smooth a ridge, deepen a valley, add a texture where none existed. This is not forgery. This is correction, interpretation, sometimes even healing. The city makes mistakes. The transit system holds grudges. A building may refuse someone for reasons that no longer apply. A family may be locked into routes that made sense fifty years ago but now force them to travel hours out of their way.

I mediate between the recorded past and the necessary present.

My chambers smell of metal and old polymer, with undertones of the moss growing three levels above. The ceiling drips occasionally—not water, but a kind of mineral oil that the building uses to lubricate its smaller movements. I've learned to position my work table between the drips. The oil would ruin the ribbons, turning their careful textures into meaningless smears.

Light comes from the ribbons themselves. They glow faintly when touched, a phosphorescence triggered by skin oils. My workspace is lit by my own labor—the more I work, the brighter it becomes, until sometimes I have to pause and let the darkness settle back in.

I wear gloves only on my left hand. The right must stay bare to feel the textures properly. My fingertips are covered in tiny scars from years of reading sharp ridges too quickly. The scars themselves have become a kind of language. Other permission-keepers can tell what kinds of records I specialize in by the pattern of marks on my index finger.

The work is solitary but not lonely. The ribbons remember people, even if they don't remember them as people. I know the old woman who tests every threshold twice before crossing—her pattern is a distinctive double-tap in the metal. I know the child who learned to fool the sensors briefly, creating little gaps in the record like held breath. I know the night workers whose passages leave deeper impressions, as if the ribbons feel the weight of their exhaustion.

Sometimes I discover love stories in the textures. Two patterns that begin separately, converge, travel together for years, then suddenly diverge. Or patterns that circle each other, approaching and retreating, until finally they merge into something I can only describe as a duet pressed into metal.

My favorite discovery was a ghost. Not a dead person—the city doesn't permit those kinds of hauntings. But about thirty years ago, someone managed to exist in the transit system without quite being there. Their pattern is impossibly light, barely a whisper in the ribbon. They moved through the city for three months, leaving traces so faint I almost missed them. Then they vanished, but not before creating the most beautiful section of ribbon I've ever felt: a complex spiral of permissions that shouldn't have been possible, as if they convinced the system to let them be in several places at once.

I kept that section. I know I shouldn't have—the ribbons are civic records, not personal treasures. But I cut a small length and wear it now around my wrist, under my sleeve. When I'm struggling with a difficult edit, I touch it and remember that the system can be taught to do impossible things.

The hardest part of my job is deciding what to preserve.

Last season, a young father came to me. His daughter had developed a condition—something in her inner ear that made the pressure changes of transit painful. The system had begun refusing her, interpreting her distress as danger. He wanted me to edit the ribbons, to erase the refusals so she could travel to her lessons again.

I spent days feeling through the recent ribbons, finding her pattern. It was heartbreaking—you could feel her trying again and again, the ridges getting sharper each time as the system grew more certain she shouldn't be allowed. But I also found something else. The system had begun routing her father differently, creating paths that didn't require transit at all. Longer routes, yes, but routes that followed the old stone walkways and rain channels. The city was trying to teach them something.

I didn't edit those ribbons. Instead, I gave the father a different kind of map, one that showed all the non-transit paths the city had been suggesting. His daughter could walk to her lessons. It would take longer, but she would arrive without pain.

He wasn't happy with me. But three months later, I felt their patterns in the ribbons again. They were walking together now, and their textures had changed—slower, more deliberate, with little pauses that I've learned to recognize as conversation.

The ribbons teach me patience. They grow at their own rate, regardless of how urgently someone needs a record changed. I've learned to match their tempo. I eat slowly. I breathe deeply. I move through my day as if I too am being recorded in metal, and I want my pattern to be smooth.

There are other permission-keepers in the city, working with different layers of the system. We meet once each season in a plaza where gravity pulls sideways, sharing knowledge in a way that won't be recorded. We're protective of our work, but not competitive. We each have our specialties. Mine is the deep archive, the old permissions, the patterns that have been accumulating since before the city learned to be selective about who it admitted.

I'm training an apprentice now. She's young, with fingers more sensitive than mine were at her age. She can feel patterns I miss, but she doesn't yet understand their meaning. Yesterday she asked me how I know when an edit is ethical.

I told her about the ribbons that grow in the oldest section of my chambers. These ribbons don't record transit permissions. They record the permissions that permission-keepers have given themselves—every edit we've made, every pattern we've changed, every correction or interpretation or healing we've attempted. These meta-ribbons grow more slowly, but their texture is unmistakable. You can feel pride, doubt, compassion, error, wisdom, and regret pressed into the metal.

"Read those first," I told her. "Feel what your predecessors felt when they made their choices. Then decide if you want your own texture added to that record."

She's down there now, running her fingertips along centuries of decisions. When she comes up, her fingers will be bleeding slightly—those old ribbons are sharp with accumulated conscience. But she'll understand something that can't be taught any other way.

As for me, I'm sitting in an alcove near the moss courts, enjoying the rare brightness of reflected afternoon vapor. My right hand throbs gently—a sign that rain is coming. The ribbons always know before the sky does. Tonight I'll go back down to my chambers and feel the new growth, discovering what permissions the city granted or withheld while I was gone.

There's a section growing now that puzzles me. The texture is unlike anything I've encountered—not rough or smooth but somehow both at once, as if the transit system is trying to describe something it has no precedent for. I've been watching this pattern develop for weeks. It doesn't match any person I know. It might not be a person at all.

I suspect the city is giving birth to something new. Not a building or a system, but a category of existence that will need its own kind of permission. When it fully emerges, I'll be there to feel its pattern in the metal, to understand what it's asking for, and to help the city learn to let it pass.

This is my purpose: to stand between the rigid memory of metal and the fluid needs of a city that hasn't finished becoming itself. To read the past in textures and edit the future with scarred fingertips. To know that every pattern in the ribbons was once a choice, a journey, a life moving through spaces that had to be negotiated, not conquered.

I am a keeper of permissions in a city that is still learning what to permit. The ribbons grow slowly in their chambers, and I grow slowly with them, becoming more myself with each texture I touch, each pattern I interpret, each impossible passage I help the city remember it once allowed.
