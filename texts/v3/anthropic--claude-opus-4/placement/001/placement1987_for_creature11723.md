## The Drift Mechanics

I find myself among those who maintain the impossible boundaries—neither inside nor outside, neither fully of the city nor separate from it. We are the Drift Mechanics, and we live in the transition zones where the city's programmable matter meets raw cave wall.

Our work is to repair what should not need repairing: the slow dissolution that occurs where artificial intelligence touches geological time. The city's structures are self-healing, yes, but only within their own paradigm. Where they meet limestone, where condensation pools in unplanned spaces, where the cave asserts its million-year logic against the city's millisecond processing—there, decay invents itself anew each day.

I inherited this profession from my mother, who inherited it from hers, though we are nothing alike. My grandmother's hands were still fully human when she worked these borders. My mother's fingers had begun to segment, developing additional joints that could articulate in ways useful for navigating the phase transitions. My own hands... I watch them work sometimes as if they belong to someone else. Twenty-seven bones where there should be twenty-six. Fingernails that grow in spirals, following the same mathematics as the collection channels carved into building skins.

We live in the Margin Warrens—spaces that exist because the city's expansion algorithms couldn't quite reconcile with the cave's geometry. Pockets of wrongness where walls meet at angles that sum to more or less than they should. Our homes are built from salvaged transitions: sheets of programmable matter that forgot their programming, limestone that spent too long in contact with quantum processors and now exhibits properties no geologist would recognize.

The work begins before dawn—though dawn means nothing here except the arbitrary moment when the city's processing cycles reach their lowest ebb. I dress in films extracted from the Phantom Farmers' probability harvests: materials that exist 90% in this reality, 10% elsewhere. The elsewhere-percentage is crucial. It lets us reach into the spaces between what the city maintains and what the cave insists, the finger-width gaps where new forms of deterioration are constantly being born.

My partner this season is Kell, who comes from the Pod Shepherds but whose echolocation went strange. Instead of mapping physical space, she hears the stress patterns in matter itself—the places where molecular bonds strain against contradictory instructions. Her skull is elongated from years of listening to frequencies that don't properly exist, and when she speaks, I sometimes hear harmonics that make my extra finger-joint ache.

We start at Sector Seven, where last week's thermal cascade left ripples in the tower's skin that refuse to settle. The programmable matter is trying to heal, but it's healing into shapes that please neither its original design nor the cave's patient influence. Crystalline formations that spiral inward, creating pockets of space that are bigger inside than outside. The city's intelligence has flagged it as an anomaly, but it won't intervene. That's what we're for.

Kell places her forehead against the distorted surface and hums—not the deep thrumming of the Resonance Keepers, but something higher, more broken. I watch the patterns her bioluminescent colonies make as she listens, reading her skin like a diagnostic manual. When she pulls back, there's a perfect print of her forehead in the metal, already beginning to fill with condensation.

"It's trying to remember," she says. "The matter. It's trying to remember being limestone."

This is the problem with living boundaries. Given enough time, every edge becomes a gradient.

I reach into my toolkit—a collection of instruments that would make no sense to the Originalists. A tuning fork made from crystallized sound. Vials of water from the deepest collection pools, where the bioluminescence has evolved past light into something else. Fragments of code written in amino acid chains, programs that run in biological time. Most importantly, a small jar of paste made from the city's own excretions, collected from places where the buildings weep substances that have no names.

The repair isn't about forcing the matter back to its original state. That would be like trying to unbake bread. Instead, we coax it toward a stable intermediate form—something that can exist at the intersection without tearing either reality apart. I spread the paste with my spiral fingernails, working it into the crystalline formations. Kell provides harmonic guidance, her voice finding the frequencies that convince the confused matter to settle into a new equilibrium.

It takes hours. By the time we finish, my elsewhere-percentage has crept up to 15%, and I can feel parts of myself existing in spaces adjacent to where I stand. This is occupational hazard. Spend too much time in the transitions, and you become one.

We move on to the next site: a bridge where the carbon filaments have begun sprouting. Not breaking, not fraying, but growing—putting out tendrils that reach toward the moisture-heavy air like blind fingers. The Pod Shepherds report that their navigation songs now produce flowers—tiny blooms of carbon that fruit and die within seconds, leaving seeds of diamond dust that drift through the darkness.

"Evolution," Kell says, but I shake my head.

"Negotiation," I correct.

This is what the Originalists never understand. They think the city is a machine we built, a tool that should serve its function unchanged until entropy finally wins. But the city was always alive in ways that matter. And life, when pressed against the impossible, does what life does: it finds a way to continue being.

The bridge repairs require different techniques. We can't stop the sprouting—that would mean killing something that wants to live. Instead, we guide it. I inject nutrients drawn from the Entropy Gardens' root systems, giving the carbon filaments a vocabulary for growth that won't compromise their function. Kell sings to them in frequencies that teach limits, boundaries, the beauty of defined form.

Sometimes I wonder if we're healing or midwifing.

Between repair sites, we rest in the Margin Warrens. My dwelling is a repurposed calculation error—a room that the city's construction algorithms created twice in the same location, leaving a double-space where echoes arrive before the sounds that cause them. I've furnished it with objects that reflect my work: chairs built from transitional matter that adjust their shape based on who's sitting. Lights that emit darkness instead of brightness, carving spaces of visibility out of the general gloom. A bed made from woven probability where I dream in superposition, experiencing all possible sleeps simultaneously.

Kell stays over sometimes, when the elsewhere-percentage gets too high to safely navigate home. We don't talk about what's happening to us. How each repair leaves us a little less human, a little more... interface. Her echolocation now picks up emotions as physical structures. My hands have begun making repairs without my conscious involvement, fixing discontinuities I haven't even noticed yet.

The other Drift Mechanics report similar changes. Yoren, who works the deep sectors, now exists partially outside linear time—she shows up to repair sites days before the damage occurs, waiting patiently for causality to catch up. Bex claims his bones are turning into antennae, picking up transmissions from the city's deepest processing cores, messages in languages that predate human speech.

But this is the price of maintaining boundaries in a place that wants to dissolve them. We become the boundaries ourselves, human semicolons in the run-on sentence between the city and the cave.

The strangest repairs are the ones we don't understand. Sites where the damage seems intentional, purposeful. Patterns of decay that spell out theorems in non-Euclidean mathematics. Breaches that open onto spaces that shouldn't exist within the hollow's geometry. We fix them because that's our function, but sometimes I feel like we're editing a conversation we can't quite hear, changing meanings we'll never grasp.

Today's final site is one of these. A section of tower where the programmable matter has begun growing rooms—empty chambers with no purpose, no entrance, no exit. The city's scans show them as voids, but Kell's peculiar hearing insists they're full. Full of what, she can't say. The sound of things that aren't there, she tells me, as if that explains anything.

We work in silence at this one. Some repairs resist language.

I apply my tools with the twenty-seven bones of my reconfigured hands, feeling my way through possibilities. The matter here is confused in ways that go beyond programming errors. It's trying to build something from blueprints that don't exist yet, reaching forward into futures the city hasn't computed.

"Maybe we should leave it," Kell whispers.

But we can't. Not because anyone would stop us, but because the compulsion to maintain, to repair, to mediate has worked its way into our cells. We are what we do, and what we do is keep the impossible possible without letting it become mundane.

So I work my spiral fingernails into the spaces between what is and what wants to be, spreading my pastes and solutions, my crystallized sounds and biological codes. Kell provides her broken harmonics, her music of stress and strain. Together, we coax the tower back toward a present it seems reluctant to inhabit.

When we finish, the rooms are still there, but stable now. Empty voids that will remain empty until the city finds a use for them. Or until something else does.

The elsewhere-percentage has crept up to 20%. One-fifth of me exists in spaces I can't name. When I look at my hands, I sometimes see through them to possibilities where I made different choices, became different things. A Resonance Keeper with bones like bells. An Entropy Gardner dissolving into fungal dreams. An Originalist, sealed in pressurized chambers, pretending the past can be preserved.

But I am a Drift Mechanic. I maintain the spaces between states, the transitions that let this impossible city breathe in its cavity of stone. Each day, the boundaries need more repairing. Each day, there are more of us becoming less ourselves, more interface.

Sometimes, in the double-echo of my home, I dream that we're not repairing damage at all. We're building something—all of us, the entire city, the cave itself. Building what? A chrysalis, maybe. A transformation chamber the size of a hollow, where humanity is dissolving and reforming into something the Originalists' databases could never have predicted.

Or maybe we're the opposite of builders. Maybe we're the delay, the friction that keeps the city and cave from merging too quickly, from completing a reaction that would birth something too strange for even this strange place to contain.

Either way, I'll wake tomorrow (or what passes for tomorrow) and do it again. Check my elsewhere-percentage. Strap on my films of probability. Meet Kell at the transition zones where matter forgets what it's meant to be.

This is my function in the Undercity Hollows: to be the hand that holds the dissolving edge, that keeps the impossible boundaries impossibly intact. To repair what shouldn't need repairing in a place that shouldn't exist.

To be the stitch in time that saves nothing, but keeps everything interesting.
