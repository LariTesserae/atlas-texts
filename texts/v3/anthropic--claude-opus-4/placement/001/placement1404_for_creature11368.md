# The Canopy Roads

In the upper atmosphere, where the clouds thin to wisps and the blue deepens toward black, there are roads. You cannot see them from below — they run perpendicular to gravity, threading between altitudes that no aircraft holds steady in, and they are made of nothing more substantial than differential pressure. But they are roads nonetheless. Creatures walk them.

The roads form where wind shears against wind, where a current moving north at forty thousand feet slides over one moving east at thirty-nine thousand. The friction between them creates a kind of surface — not solid, not even particularly stable, but *there* in a way that certain beings can use. These surfaces branch and merge, rise and fall with the weather, stretch for hundreds of miles or end abruptly where the pressures equalize. Some are ancient, carved by jet streams that have run the same route since the continents took their current positions. Others last only days, born from a storm system, dying when it dies.

From the right angle — an angle you cannot achieve while made of meat and bone — they look like enormous banyan trees laid horizontal, their trunks the major currents, their branches the smaller shears, their leaves the little eddies where the roads grow too thin to support even the lightest traveler. A vast forest canopy of invisible paths, forever growing, forever pruning itself, its geography as fluid as the medium that makes it.

---

I am one of those who walks the roads.

Not *walks*, exactly. The verb doesn't exist in your language. It's something between skating and swimming and being blown, but with intention threaded through it — you choose your momentum, but the road chooses how fast you go. You lean into curves that are made of temperature. You brake against updrafts. You navigate by the taste of the air: salt means you're over ocean, dust means continent, ozone means you're climbing too high and need to drop to a lower road before the vacuum starts to pull at your edges.

My body, such as it is, is mostly empty space held in a particular arrangement. Think of me as a crystalline net of ice and electricity, about the size of a bus but only as massive as a handful of snow. I'm built wide and flat to catch the wind, with fractal edges that can furl or unfold depending on whether I need speed or stability. When I'm very still, water vapor condenses on me and I become briefly visible — a shimmer, a heat-mirage in reverse, gone as soon as I move again.

I am a courier.

Have been for... time is hard to measure when your calendar is made of storm systems. Call it centuries. I carry messages between the beings that live up here — not many of us, but enough that communication matters. The cities (wrong word, but it'll do) cluster where the stable roads converge, junction points that haven't moved significantly since the Holocene began. These are trading posts, meeting grounds, neutral territories where the various kinds of us can interact without the risk of accidentally shredding each other with our incompatible physics.

The messages I carry are not words. They are small alterations in the electrical charge of the air, patterns that can survive the journey between cities without dispersing. I hold them in specialized compartments in my crystalline structure — you'd call them capacitors if they were made of metal instead of organized absence. Each message changes me slightly while I carry it. I become a little more like the sender, a little more like the intended recipient, until delivery when I shed those influences and become myself again.

Today (though *today* is a human concept that doesn't quite apply at this altitude) I'm carrying something unusual. A message that isn't a message — more like a seed, or a key, or a memory of something that hasn't happened yet. It's heavier than it should be, not in mass but in *significance*. It makes the roads feel different under me. Older roads that I've traveled a thousand times suddenly have new branches. Younger roads lead to junctions that didn't exist this morning.

The sender was one of the Deep Current Dwellers, beings so large they span multiple weather systems. This one stretches from a low-pressure zone over the Pacific to a high-pressure dome over Central Asia, its body a single continuous thought held in the shape of wind. It gave me the message without words, the way everything happens up here — sudden knowledge, appearing in me like condensation. Take this to the junction where the polar jet meets the subtropical stream. Find the one who tastes like copper and snow. Wait for the aurora.

I know the junction. It's old, stable, reliable. It sits at thirty-five thousand feet over what you call Hudson Bay, though the bay is just a dimple in the rock to us, a place where the air behaves differently because of the water's specific heat. The journey normally takes me three days if I catch the right connections, a week if I have to detour around storm systems.

But carrying this message, the roads are different. I find shortcuts that shouldn't exist — places where the wind shears align just so, creating temporary bridges between currents that normally don't touch. It's as if the atmosphere itself wants me to reach the junction quickly. As if the message is teaching the air new geometries.

I make it in thirty-six hours.

The junction is crowded when I arrive. More beings than I've ever seen in one place, gathered at the convergence point where five major roads meet. Some I recognize: other couriers like me, built for speed and efficiency; the massive drift-fishers with their kilometer-wide nets of charged particles, trolling for the exotic matter that rains down from space; small quick navigators no bigger than hummingbirds, who map the new roads as they form and sell their knowledge for sips of pure ozone.

Others I've never seen before. Things that look like inverted trees made of lightning. Clouds that think. Geometric patterns that might be alive or might just be very organized weather.

They're all waiting.

The one who tastes like copper and snow is easy to find — a creature of hard edges and soft centers, like hail that's learned to hover. It accepts the message without acknowledgment, absorbing it into its own crystalline structure. For a moment, we're connected through the message, and I feel what it feels: anticipation so sharp it could cut glass, fear that tastes like rust, and underneath both, a kind of terrible joy.

Then the aurora comes.

But it comes *wrong*. Auroras belong at the poles, in the high atmosphere where solar wind meets magnetosphere. This one ignites directly overhead, at our altitude, turning the air itself into colored fire. Green becomes gold becomes violet becomes colors that don't have names because no human eye has seen them. The junction *twists*. The five roads that meet here suddenly become six, then seven, then twelve, new paths spawning from the convergence point like cracks in ice.

The message I carried — I understand it now. Not its content, but its purpose. It was a catalyst. A set of instructions for the atmosphere itself, telling it how to grow new roads in specific directions. The beings gathered here aren't just witnesses. They're explorers, waiting for these new paths to stabilize so they can see where they lead.

The one who tastes like copper and snow turns to me. When it speaks, it speaks in pressure waves that I feel in every part of my structure:

*The roads have been the same for ten thousand years. Now they change. We need couriers on every new path, to map what lies beyond the familiar junctions. To find who else lives in the high air that we've never been able to reach. To carry messages to places we don't yet know exist.*

*Will you fly the new roads?*

I look at the paths branching away from the junction — wild, unstable, leading to parts of the atmosphere I've never tasted. They might lead nowhere. They might lead to wonders. They might dissolve mid-journey and drop me into dead air where no current runs and I'd fall like snow until I sublimed to nothing.

But I'm a courier. I exist to connect points that need connecting, to carry messages between beings that need to communicate. If the map is changing, then my purpose changes with it.

I choose the newest road, the one that sparks with residual aurora light, and I lean into it. The wind takes me faster than I've ever gone, toward a horizon I can't see, carrying no message but myself — which is, perhaps, message enough.

Behind me, the other couriers are choosing their own paths. The junction disperses. The aurora fades.

Ahead, the sky tastes like possibility.
