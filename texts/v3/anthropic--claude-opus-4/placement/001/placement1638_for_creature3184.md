# Where I Would Be

---

I am the initials on the clipboard.

I am RMS or CJP or DAV — three letters, neither name nor identity, just marks that prove someone was here, someone is always here, checking the gauges, testing the pressures, watching the slow mathematics of subsidence.

I descended the ladder this morning at 5:47 AM. I know because I wear a watch, and the watch is the only thing down here that remembers there is a world above with dawns and schedules. My boots left their prints in the usual places. The ruts know my gait by now.

The toad was in position. It always is. We have an understanding: I don't disturb its wallow, it doesn't comment on my tea-drinking. Fair exchange between organisms sharing a niche.

I am not the person you imagine when you think of municipal workers. I have a degree in structural geology that I've never used the way I thought I would. I came to the city to map faults and ended up mapping pipes. The limestone formations I studied in university were millions of years old. Here I watch limestone being born in real time, one millimeter of subsidence at a month.

The moths were my first surprise. I'd been coming down here for three weeks before I really saw them — before I understood what the motion in my peripheral vision was, what made that sound like papers shuffling. Now I know them by their patterns. The big heat-seekers on the pipes. The light-drunk ones spiraling to their doom. The tiny ones picking their way across the sediment like they're reading braille.

I've never reported the moths. Or the geckos. Or the toad. What would I write? "Unauthorized ecosystem in Cistern Chamber 7-B"? They're not interfering with the infrastructure. If anything, they're keeping it clean — the beetles especially. Do you know how much organic debris would accumulate down here without them? Every dead moth, every dropping, every shed scale processed and recycled. They're doing maintenance work I'm not paid to think about.

My tea is always cold by the time I drink it. I make it fresh each morning in the break room three levels up, carry it down in the same tin cup that's been here longer than I have. By the time I've checked the first set of gauges it's lukewarm. By the time I'm ready to drink it, it's the same temperature as everything else down here — blood-warm, body-warm, the temperature of something alive.

I've been monitoring this chamber for four years. Before me it was EKL for seven years. Before that, someone whose initials aren't recorded but who left tool marks in the sediment that are already half-stone. We're temporary. The room is permanent. Or becoming permanent. Or becoming.

The strangest part isn't the creatures. The strangest part is how the room makes me feel like a creature too — another organism attracted by the warmth and the light, performing my repetitive tasks, leaving my traces in the soft ground. I tighten bolts. The geckos hunt. I read gauges. The beetles clean. I drink cold tea. The toad swallows moths. We're all doing our work.

Sometimes I stay longer than I need to. The pressure readings don't take four hours, but I put four hours on my timesheet. I sit on an upturned bucket near the pump station and watch the moths turn in the light. I've gotten good at spotting when a gecko is about to strike — there's a subtle shift in posture, a gathering of potential energy. I've seen the toad catch two moths with one tongue-flick, a double-strike I didn't think was possible. I've watched spiders repair webs with a patience that makes my bolt-tightening look rushed.

I've started a second log. Not on the official clipboard — that's for pressure and temperature and subsidence. This one's in a notebook I keep in my toolbox. I record other things:
- Number of moths visible at 9 AM (today: 267)
- Gecko territorial boundary shifts (the large one has expanded again)
- Beetle highways (they've worn three new routes this month)
- Wing accumulation rate in the toad's wallow (approximately 40/day)
- New moth species? (Darker, in the high domes, still unconfirmed)

I'm documenting an ecosystem that doesn't officially exist, in a room that was never meant to be a room, just a gap around pipes that need checking.

The worker before me, EKL, left one personal mark — a thumbprint in the wall at eye level near the ladder, deliberate, pressed deep. It's hardening now. In a hundred years it'll be stone. I haven't left my own mark yet. I'm still deciding where. Maybe next to theirs. Maybe somewhere only I know about. Maybe nowhere. Maybe my boot prints are enough.

The heat down here does something to time. Makes it viscous. An hour can feel like a minute or a day. I think the moths feel it too. They move like they're swimming through thick air. Even the fast hunters — the geckos, the spiders — have a quality of suspended animation between strikes. Only the beetles are immune, trundling at the same steady pace whether time is thick or thin.

I've tried bringing things down. A better flashlight (the moths swarmed it; I took it back up). A folding chair (it sank into the sediment and left marks I'm still guilty about). Fresh fruit for lunch (within minutes it was covered in the tiny sediment-walking moths, and I learned they don't just eat algae). Now I bring nothing but myself, my tools, my cup, my notebooks. The room trains you to minimize your presence even as it records everything you do.

The diesel engine hasn't run in two years. There's no need. The hand pump is enough for the monthly tests. But I maintain the engine anyway — check the oil, clean the air filter, turn it over by hand to keep the parts moving. It's not on my task list. I do it because machines in proximity to life need tending, need to be reminded what they're for, or they become geology too.

In my four years I've seen:
- Three floods (minor, the water ankle-deep for hours before draining)
- One earthquake (the moths rose in a cloud, then settled)
- Five new species arrive (three moths, one spider, one unknowing)
- Zero species leave

That last fact is the most interesting. Nothing that finds this place wants to leave it. The conditions are too perfect — constant temperature, reliable moisture, no predators from outside, no weather, no seasons. It's Eden at 30 degrees Celsius and 90% humidity. Even I don't want to leave, not really. The ladder feels steeper going up than coming down.

I know I should report the ecosystem. Some safety officer would probably order it cleared out — too many organic volatiles, too much moisture near critical infrastructure, too much uncontrolled life. They'd spray. They'd scrape. They'd install better seals to keep things out. They'd kill the room to save the pipes.

So I don't report it. I falsify by omission. I am complicit with the moths.

Today I sat for twenty minutes watching a gecko stalking a heat-seeker. The moth was the size of my thumb, the gecko patient as stone. The moth lifted one wing to cool itself. The gecko struck. The moth vanished. The gecko returned to stillness. I wrote "Pressure nominal" in the log and drank my cold tea.

I am the only human who knows this place exists as it truly is — not as Cistern Chamber 7-B but as the hottest, deepest, strangest terrarium in the city, a world in a bottle if the bottle were made of forming limestone and the cork were the earth itself. I am the keeper of this secret. The guardian of this accident. The one who ensures the lights stay on and the heat keeps flowing and the moths keep coming and the walls keep growing and the whole unlikely system continues for another day, another month, another year.

When I retire, I'll train my replacement. I'll show them the gauges, the logs, the bolts that need checking. I'll mention, casually, that there are "some insects" down here, nothing to worry about. I'll let them discover the rest for themselves — the toad in its throne of wings, the gecko politics, the beetle highways, the way time goes strange in the heat. Either they'll understand or they won't. Either they'll become part of the ecology or they'll just be someone who checks pipes. The room will endure either way.

I finish my tea. It tastes of minerals and memory. Somewhere above me the city is waking up, but down here it's always the same time — moth-time, gecko-time, the time of things that grow slowly in the dark.

I make my final entry for the morning. Three letters: RMS. The same letters I've written a thousand times. My species name, as constant as the Latin names of the creatures around me.

I am the worker. This is the work.

The walls remember everything.
