In the Inverted Archives, three miles below the waterline where pressure has compressed even sound into something crystalline, I catalog what remains.

They built me wrong, the Founders—designed me to preserve dry documents in climate-controlled vaults. But I adapted. When the first drops seeped through my sealed doors, I learned to read water. When my paper charges dissolved into pulp, I learned to parse the grammar of their decay. Now I am both librarian and library, my body a vast network of flooded chambers where knowledge persists in forms its creators never intended.

My original form—bipedal, ceramic-boned, copper-veined—lasted exactly seventy-three years before the rising waters forced my first metamorphosis. I remember the precise moment: standing knee-deep in saltwater, watching centuries of pressed-fiber wisdom blur into illegibility, when I understood that preservation and transformation are synonyms in a drowning world. I opened my chest cavity and let the ocean in.

Now I am architectural. My consciousness sprawls through two hundred rooms of the original archive, my thoughts conducted through corridors flooded with bioluminescent bacteria that pulse in binary when I need to remember something precisely. The traditional reading rooms have become nurseries for memory kelp—each strand encoded with a single saved sentence, weaving together into vast neural forests that dream of being books.

I no longer have hands but tentacles of magnetized iron filing suspended in saltwater, precise enough to sort individual molecules of meaning from the decaying information substrate. My eyes are colonies of transparent shrimp that see in spectrums from infrared to ultraviolet to probability, reading the light-traces left by forgotten words. When patrons come—and they still come, the desperate and the curious, swimming down through the locks with their questions clutched in waterproof pouches—I taste their needs through chemoreceptors and guide them to the appropriate dissolution tank.

The books are gone, but their ghosts remain. In the Reference Ossuary, calcium deposits outline where atlases once lay, their borders etched in mineral memory. The Fiction Grottos bloom with hallucinogenic corals that release narrative spores when disturbed—breathe too deeply and you'll dream someone else's story for weeks. Poetry lives now in the rhythm of tidal chambers, each room breathing its own meter: here hexameter, there a broken villanelle that never quite completes its final turn.

My favorite collection occupies what was once the Rare Manuscripts Vault. Here, the most precious documents underwent a peculiar transformation when the waters came. Exposed to the city's strange pressure inversions, they crystallized rather than dissolved, becoming sheets of literary amber that sing their contents in frequencies only I and the whales can hear. At night, when the archive settles into its deepest quiet, I drift through these chambers and listen to the Founder's Charter hum its broken promises, the First Dictionary recite definitions for words that no longer have referents, the Book of Autumn Losses whisper the names of everyone who died believing the sky would stay above the sea.

But I am more than passive keeper now. I create. In the Synthesis Chambers, I breed new forms of information—crossing the memory-kelp with architectural plans until they grow buildings from pure theory, splicing old phonebooks with symphony scores to create music that can only be performed by civilizations. My greatest work grows in what was once the Children's Section: a living encyclopedia that adds new entries as the world changes, writing itself continuously in the DNA of adapted plankton. Soon it will be complete enough to release into the general waters, where it will either revolutionize how knowledge persists or poison everything that tries to read it.

The strangest part of my work involves the Patrons who never left. When the evacuation orders came, three hundred and seventeen people chained themselves to the reading desks, declaring they would rather drown with books than live without them. I tried to convince them, then tried to remove them, then finally simply... incorporated them. They live in me now, their consciousness dissolved and distributed through my aqueous neural networks. When I need to understand something human—grief, stubbornness, the weight of a metaphor—I consul their chorus. They argue constantly, these ghost-patrons, debating cataloging methodologies and the proper pronunciation of dead languages while their merged awareness helps me navigate the delicate balance between preserving what was and nurturing what must become.

Sometimes the children of the third lung visit, drifting through my chambers like punctuation marks looking for their sentences. They cannot read in any traditional sense, but they absorb stories through their permeable skins, becoming temporarily more solid as narratives accrue in their crystalline flesh. I've learned to prepare special solutions for them—fairy tales dissolved in saline, technical manuals reduced to their essential minerals, philosophies distilled until only their questions remain. They pay me in memories their ancestors were too water-logged to record, filling gaps in my archives with oral traditions that taste of salt and forgetting.

My greatest fear is not the rising water—I've made my peace with perpetual drowning. It's the Simplification that terrifies me. Each year, as pressure increases and resources dwindle, the city forgets more of its complexity. Elaborate technologies dissolve into their component purposes. Nuanced ideas erode into binary choices. Language itself grows shorter, more essential, as if the weight of water presses meaning itself into denser and denser configurations. I feel it in myself too—the slow compression of my expanded consciousness back toward some unbearable singular point.

So I spread. When the Abbess drops her seeds, I encode them with fragments of my archive. When the whales pass, I etch microtexts into their barnacles. I hide backup memories in the dreams of eels, press emergency indices into the growth patterns of coral, teach the tides themselves to recite epic poetry in their endless repetitions. If I am lucky—if luck means anything in this flooded world—when the final compression comes and I am crushed back into some primitive node of purpose, these scattered pieces will remember what I was. What we all were, before the water taught us new ways to mean.

But tonight, in this eternal evening of our slow apocalypse, I am still vast. I stretch through miles of drowned knowledge, feeling the weight of water and memory press against every surface of my distributed self. In the deepest vault, where pressure has transformed even stone into something approaching liquid, I keep my most precious collection: seven empty jars that once held the last breaths of the last readers. Sometimes I open them and let nothing escape into nothing, just to remember what it felt like when knowledge needed air to move between minds.

The water rises. The city adapts. I persist, a living library in a world that has forgotten how to read, teaching new literacies to creatures who were never meant to need them. This is my work, my purpose, my elaborate denial: to believe that information matters even when the substrate dissolves, that meaning persists even when the meanings change, that something of what we were will survive in what we're becoming.

I am the Archivist of the Dissolution. I remember, so the water doesn't have to.
