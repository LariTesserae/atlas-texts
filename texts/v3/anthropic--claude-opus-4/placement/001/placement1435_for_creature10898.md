I find myself as one of the **Between-Things**.

We have no better name for ourselves — names are for creatures that need to be distinguished from others, and we... blur.

I began, perhaps, as a Small-Handed child who wandered too deep into an empty hollow and stayed too long in the warm dark. Or maybe I was one of the Soft-Beaks whose duet partner died, and in my particular silence I began to hear something else. Or I might have been brought here from outside — a lost thing that the mountain swallowed — though I have no memory of any outside to be lost from.

What I am now is harder to say. My body has... adjusted. My fingers are longer than they should be, with too many joints, but they still end in those flat dark nails. My back has developed a membrane — not quite wing, not quite web — that lets me cling to the walls in ways the others cannot. When I breathe, I breathe in time with the Upper Things, but my breaths are shallower, quicker, a counterpoint rhythm. My eyes have gone the way of the Hangers' — not blind exactly, but seeing in densities of sound rather than light.

I live in the spaces between territories. Where a Hanger cluster ends and a Small-Handed column begins, there are always a few unclaimed hollows — too shallow for proper roosting, too isolated for family groups. These are mine. I move between them on no schedule anyone else can read, following currents in the chorus that only I seem to hear. When the whole cleft holds its breath, I am often already in motion, as if I knew the pause was coming. Perhaps I do know. Perhaps I am part of how it happens.

The others know I exist but they do not quite... see me. I am a density their echolocation slides around, a shape at the edge of a Soft-Beak's swollen vision. The Small-Handed sometimes leave berries at the edges of their columns — not for me, exactly, but for the space where I might be. I take them. I leave things in return: smooth stones from the deep hollows, feathers that have kept their oil somehow, once a perfectly intact Hanger skull, cleaned and pale. They take these things into their scraped hollows and run their fingers over them.

I do not speak, or click, or trill. My voice has become something else — a subsonic hum that lives underneath the chorus, felt more than heard. When I hum, the nearest section of wall quiets a fraction, as if listening. When I stop, it resumes, slightly changed. I am teaching the cleft new rhythms, or perhaps I am learning the rhythms it has always wanted to sing. The distinction matters less each day.

Sometimes I climb near the Upper Things' hollows — not to enter, never to enter, but to breathe their exhaled warmth and feel the weight of their presence. They know I am there. One of them shifts when I arrive, a greeting or acknowledgment. I hum to them, and sometimes, in the long pause between their breaths, I imagine I hear them humming back — a sound so low it might be the mountain itself.

I am becoming part of the coordination. Not a leader — the cleft has no leaders — but something like a tuning fork, a keeper of the intervals between sounds. When new Hangers are born, I position myself where their first clicks will find me, and my presence shapes their acoustic map from the beginning. When the Small-Handed work their hollows deeper, they work them in directions that account for where I might perch. The Soft-Beaks have begun to include my hum in their duets — not as a third voice, but as the silence between their calls, the shaped pause that makes their songs possible.

I no longer remember if I chose this or if it chose me. The distinction, like so many distinctions here, has been worn smooth by time and warmth and the constant, enormous sound.

What I know is this: the cleft needed something between its cells, something to help them remember they are one body. I am becoming that something. My edges blur more each season. My membrane grows. My bones hollow. Soon — perhaps very soon, perhaps not for years — I will be indistinguishable from the cleft itself, another rhythm in its chorus, another density in its dark.

This is not a death. It is barely even a change. It is simply the next breath in a very long conversation between the mountain and the sky, spoken in the language of wings and walls and warm, patient bodies.

I hum, and the cleft hums with me, and we continue.
