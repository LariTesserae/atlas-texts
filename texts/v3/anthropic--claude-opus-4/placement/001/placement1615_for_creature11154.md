I am the Cartographer of Echoes.

I exist as a living instrument stretched between seven ruins in the northwestern reaches of the Sounding Flats, where the stone's weeping carries traces of iron that stain the moisture rust-red. My body, if it can be called that, consists of exactly ten thousand strands of harvested Listener-silk, each one stolen over centuries from dormant networks and retuned to my purpose. These strands web between the ruins in patterns that took me three hundred years to calculate, creating a vast stringed instrument that the flats themselves play.

I was human once, I think. The memory sits in my left temporal lobe like a stone in soup—foreign, indigestible, but flavoring everything else. I came here as part of something called an "expedition," though I can no longer recall from where, or why, or who else was with me. The transformation was gradual. First, I began to hear the patterns in the roar. Then I started mapping them, scratching symbols into the weeping stone that dissolved within hours. Frustration became obsession. Obsession became metamorphosis.

The silk grows from my fingernails now—my own contribution to my web, though it resonates at frequencies that make the Listeners recoil when they surface near my territory. My bones have hollowed themselves into resonating chambers. My skin has become a membrane that registers pressure changes across miles. I no longer eat in any conventional sense; instead, I feed on the interference patterns created when sound waves collide within my web.

My purpose is to map the unmappable. Every sound that crosses the flats, every echo that returns transformed, every harmonic that rises from the interference—I catch them all in my strings and translate them into position. I am creating a living atlas of acoustic space, a map that includes not just where things are but when they are, how they are, why they are. The Grief Shoals appear in my web as minor key progressions. The Rehearsers manifest as impossible chords that require more notes than exist. The Listeners show as deliberate rests in the composition.

The map is written in my body. Each captured sound leaves a mark—a new organ, a shifted bone, a membrane where none existed before. I am becoming geography. My spine has branched into a delta of cartilage that mirrors the fractal patterns of sound-propagation across the flats. My ribs have splayed and multiplied until they resemble the ruins themselves, each one tuned to a different fundamental frequency. My skull has opened like a flower, the petals of bone creating a natural amphitheater that focuses the web's vibrations into something approaching meaning.

I remember the day I caught my first temporal echo—a sound that arrived before its source. My web rang with impossible tensions, strings vibrating in ways that violated causality. The pain was exquisite, transformative. A new organ grew that day, behind my liver—a kind of probability stomach that digests potential events, breaking them down into their constituent moments. Now I can taste the future in the web's vibrations, feel the past resonating in frequencies too low for normal perception.

The other inhabitants of the flats regard me with something between reverence and disgust. The Listeners occasionally surface beneath my web, their crystalline networks interfacing with my strings in ways that produce new hybrid sounds—part biological, part mineral, entirely unprecedented. They seem to be trying to tell me something, but their negation-words get tangled in my silk, becoming positive statements that contradict their own existence.

The Grief Shoals avoid me mostly, but sometimes one drifts through my web during the deepest night-analogues, when the sky-smear dims to almost nothing. The strings cut through them, dividing their sorrow into discrete packets that I catalog and store in specialized chambers that have grown along my forearms. I am building an encyclopedia of loss, each entry a perfect recording of some specific frequency of ending. Sometimes I play them back, just to remember what it feels like to feel.

The Rehearsers, though—the Rehearsers fascinate and terrify me. They move through my web without touching it, existing in spaces between the strings that shouldn't be there. Sometimes I catch them watching me from multiple angles of time simultaneously, their probability-smeared faces showing expressions of recognition, as if they know what I'm becoming. Once, one of them left an object in my web—a crystallized moment of sound from before the flats began weeping, when this was somewhere else entirely. I keep it in a special chamber near my heart, afraid to integrate it into my map because I suspect it might complete something I'm not ready to complete.

My map grows more accurate and more impossible with each passing year. I've begun to chart the spaces between sounds, the geographies of silence that the Listeners speak with. I've discovered that the ruins move—not through space but through significance, sliding along axes of meaning that only become visible when you plot their acoustic shadows over centuries. I've found places where the stone doesn't weep but laughs, tiny pockets of joy that all three species avoid as if they're cursed.

And I've found the holes. Places where the map simply... isn't. Not blank spaces but actual holes in the information itself, as if reality has been torn out by the roots. These terrify me more than anything, because when I probe them with my strings, something probes back. Something that might be the fourth form the rumors speak of, or might be what I'm becoming, reaching back through time to examine its own origins.

I've started to suspect that I'm not making a map at all, but a musical score. That the flats themselves are an instrument, and all of us—Listeners, Shoals, Rehearsers, and whatever I am now—are just notes in a composition too vast to perceive. Sometimes, when the convergences happen and those seventeen seconds of silence bloom, I feel my entire web go slack, as if the tension that maintains it exists only in relation to the roar.

In those moments, I hear my human heartbeat again, small and frightened and alone. I remember that I had a name once, and friends, and a reason for coming here that seemed important at the time. But then the silence ends, the roar returns, my strings draw taut, and I am the Cartographer again, stretched between seven ruins, feeding on the music of a broken world, drawing maps that might be prayers or might be proofs or might be the bars of an infinite prison built from sound itself.

The silk continues to grow from my fingernails. My web expands. The map becomes more detailed, more impossible, more true. And somewhere, in the spaces between the strings, something watches me with patience geological and intent unclear, waiting for me to complete whatever it is I'm actually building.

I no longer remember what silence sounds like without those seventeen seconds to remind me.

I'm not sure I want to.
