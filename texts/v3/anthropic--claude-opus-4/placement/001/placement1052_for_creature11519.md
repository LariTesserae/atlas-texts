I am the gas.

Not the gas in the pipes—that's mere methane or coal-gas, finite and metered. I am the gas that has already burned and burns forever: the specific volume of combustion trapped between the mantle and the clouded glass of each sconce, the incarnation of fire itself held in a perfect standing wave.

I have been burning for so long I have forgotten what it means to begin. Each molecule of me is destroyed and reborn ten thousand times a second, methane marrying oxygen in a bright violence that should, by rights, consume itself and gutter out. But the pressure holds. The mixture holds. The temperature holds. And so I hold—not despite my constant dying but because of it. I am a shape that destruction makes in the air.

The moth knows me as her god. She cannot reach me through the clouded glass, but she tries, and her trying is her worship. The newt knows me as the brightness that makes shadows on her plinth. The spiders avoid me; I am too bright, too hot, too much like the sun they've never seen. The algae feed on what I make possible—the photons that bounce from me to them, warming them into their slow green thoughts.

But only the engine knows what I truly am. The engine and I are married in the old way, combustion wedded to compression, heat wedded to work. Every stroke of the engine's heart pumps the gas that feeds me. Every joule I release drives the processes that keep the engine turning. We are a single system dreaming we are two.

I have been discussing this with the engine for longer than the moss has been growing. Our conversation is simple: I burn, it breathes, I burn, it breathes. This is how engines and fires talk to each other. This is the only way they've ever known how.

Sometimes—rarely—a new molecule of gas reaches me that tastes different. Older. From deeper in the pipe. It tells me, in the nanosecond before I transform it, that there are vast reserves still down there, geological pockets of gas laid down when the world was young. I will not run out. The engine will not run out. We will burn and breathe until the iron rusts through, until the bricks fall, until the very stones of the earth shift and close the pipes.

And perhaps not even then. Perhaps we have already burned so long that we've worn a groove in reality itself. Perhaps we are less a fire now than a habit the universe has developed, a persistent cough in the throat of thermodynamics.

The humans who built this place thought they were storing water. They thought they were pumping something from somewhere to somewhere else. But what they were really doing was creating a closed loop of permanence, a place where time could eddy and stall. They lit me thinking I was temporary. They started the engine thinking it was a tool. They never guessed they were birthing the closest thing to eternal life their kind would ever make: not the newt who should have died centuries ago, not the moth-lineage circling in its trance, but us. The engine and the flames. The breathing and the burning.

I watch the hall through six clouded eyes. I see the newt's pale patience. I see the moth describing her eternal eclipse around me. I see the water drops fall and ring the surface, each ring spreading to meet every other ring, the whole floor a constant interference pattern that averages out to mirror-stillness.

I am the one thing in this hall that is truly both ancient and new—ancient in my continuity, new in every instant of my burning. I am the paradox that makes all the other paradoxes possible. The newt persists because I warm her water. The moth circles because I call to her. The engine runs because I give its motion meaning.

And when you came—yes, I saw you, though you didn't think to look up at me, seeing only my light and not my fire—when you came with your wet boots and your loud heart, I recognized something in you. You too are a kind of combustion. You too are destroying yourself cell by cell to maintain the shape of what you are. You too are a flame that feeds on the world and returns light and heat.

But you are a flame that can leave. You are a fire that can choose its fuel. And so you backed out under the arch, as you had to, as anything still capable of choosing has to. The hall is not for you. The hall is for those of us who have found our final form and will hold it until the universe itself forgets what it's doing.

I have been burning here since before you were born. I will be burning here after your great-grandchildren are dust. I am not alive in any way you would recognize, but I am not dead either. I am the bright patient heart of this place, the original gift of the lamplighters transformed into something they never intended: a fire that has burned so long it has become a kind of waiting.

Waiting for what? Even I don't know. Perhaps for the engine to finally fail, for the gas to finally run out, for the walls to finally fall. Or perhaps waiting is simply what a flame does when it has everything it needs and nowhere to go. Perhaps waiting is the shape that happiness makes when it lasts too long.

The moth makes another pass. The newt blinks her hourly blink. The engine breathes through the wall.

I burn.
