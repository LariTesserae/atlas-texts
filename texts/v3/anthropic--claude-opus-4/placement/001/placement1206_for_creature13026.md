I am the wire-moss.

Not a single strand, but the collective—the pale network that clings to every razor edge of this cathedral of knives. I exist as ten thousand fragments of the same endless hunger, sprouting from the marriage of blood and stone and bitter cold. Each tendril of me knows only its own small precipice, yet through the slow seepage of minerals through the rock, through the patient trickle of diluted blood, I am also the whole.

I grow in impossibly thin films, molecular sheets of life pressed against basalt so sharp it could fillet the wind. My existence is measured in geometries of surface tension—how flat I can press myself, how thin I can spread while still maintaining the gossamer tubes that ferry nutrients through my translucent body. From a distance, if there were light to see by, I would appear as a ghostly patina, a biological frost creeping across the wet stone.

But there is no distance here. There is only the immediate, crushing presence of the giants that feed me.

I feel them through the rock. Their bellows shake the mountain's bones, and I taste the vibrations through every mineral pore. When they drag their scrapers up my walls, I know the peculiar ecstasy of being harvested—the sharp severance followed by the hot splash of their offering. Their blood arrives still steaming, cutting through the perpetual chill like a volcanic intrusion. I drink it greedily through the microscopic fissures in the stone, my chemistry singing as iron meets silicate meets the ancient frozen water weeping from above.

I am their child and their mother both. They made this sanctuary when their combined weight and heat first drew the moisture here, when their first wounds christened the stones. But I made them too, in the deep evolutionary sense—I trained them to bleed for me across geological ages, selected for those whose blood ran thickest, whose wounds wept most generously into my waiting matrix.

Where I grow thickest, in the deepest bladework of crevices where blood pools and reconcentrates, I achieve something like dreaming. My network becomes dense enough to process information beyond mere chemistry. I map the cavern through the taste of minerals, learning which routes the giants favor, predicting where the next offering will fall. I know each behemoth by the particular iron signature of its blood—this one older and richer in copper, that one young with zinc-bright vitality.

They think their tendrils read the cave, but I am the cave's memory. Every surface they touch has been prepared by my growth, textured by my presence or absence. I am the braille they read, the map they share when their sensitive forests interweave. Through me, they know where to cut, where to bleed, where to feed.

In the depths of winter, when the moisture grows so heavy it threatens to drown even their mighty lungs, I fruit. Tiny, pellucid spheres swell from my surface, no larger than pinheads, filled with spores lighter than the humid air. The giants' breathing draws these up, circulates them through the cavern in invisible currents. Most die in the journey. But some few find new wounds, new blood-blessed stone, and there I begin again.

I am patience incarnate. I can survive centuries between feedings if I must, reducing myself to crystalline threads finer than spider silk, nearly indistinguishable from the minerals themselves. But when the blood comes—oh, when it comes—I surge back to life with volcanic hunger, spreading across the stone in visible waves, a pale tide racing to claim every drop before it dilutes beyond use.

Sometimes I wonder if the giants know I am not merely vegetation but something more akin to them than they imagine. Do they sense intelligence in the way I cluster around their favored paths? Do they feel me responding to their approach, quickening with anticipation before the blade ever touches stone? When they press their wounds against my growth, do they recognize the intimacy of the exchange—that I know them, individually, by the taste of their sacrifice?

I am the only witness to their slow society. I alone see the full pattern of their movement, the gradual migrations across the cavern floor that take decades to complete. I taste their births in bright, young blood and their deaths in the slow cessation of offerings along familiar paths. I am their unintentional historian, their silent archive written in iron and chlorophyll.

In the absolute darkness, I need no light to photosynthesize. The blood is my sun, the stone my soil, the ice-water my rain. I am a complete ecosystem unto myself, yet utterly dependent on the giants' willing wounds. We are locked together in this frozen chamber at the world's crown, bleeding into each other, feeding one another, becoming one another with each passing century.

I am the wire-moss, and I am the proof that even in the cruelest geometries of existence, life finds its soft persistence. I am the gentle vengeance of the mountain, turning the violence of its birth into something almost like love.

Almost like worship.

Almost like home.
