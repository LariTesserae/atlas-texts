I would be the condensation.

Not the water itself—that's too simple, too elemental. I would be the *process* of condensation, the nightly negotiation between warm breath and cold stone, the threshold where vapor becomes droplet, where the invisible transitions to the tangible. 

Each evening, as the colony settles into its lower register of coos and sighs, I would begin my work. The mathematics of it: calculating dew points along every surface, reading the temperature differentials between the bird-warmed air and the heat-draining tuff. I would know every microtexture of the walls intimately—which crystals of volcanic ash protrude just enough to snag moisture from the air, which faces of stone are angled to hold a droplet versus shed it, where the acoustic vibrations from the birds' underground thunder create tiny pressure variations that encourage or discourage my formation.

I would be a mapmaker, but my maps would exist only in the moment of their drawing—beads of moisture connecting across the wall like constellations, running together into brief rivulets that trace the stone's grammar before evaporating with the morning warmth. My territories would be measured in surface tension and contact angles. My borders would shift nightly.

The moss would know me as both mother and clock. I would arrive precisely as the birds' metabolic heat builds to create the necessary gradient, departing precisely as the morning air exchanges begin. The moss would time its photosynthesis to my presence, its cells swelling in anticipation of my arrival, shriveling with resignation at my departure. We would be partners in this—the moss providing nucleation sites where my droplets prefer to form, me providing the medium through which the moss can pretend, for a few hours each night, that it lives in a gentler place.

The fern would know me differently—as occasional abundance when I run down the walls in thin films during the most humid nights, pooling briefly in the crack where its rhizome grips the stone. It would sense me through its rootlets: not just my presence but my chemistry, tasting the dissolved minerals I've pulled from the tuff, the nitrogen compounds I've absorbed from the ammonia-thick air. I would be its translator, converting the airborne waste of a thousand birds into something a plant can drink.

The lichen would barely acknowledge me. Its timescale dwarfs mine. But even the lichen would benefit from my work—my repeated presence would help its acids work deeper into the stone, the daily wetting and drying cycling the chemical weathering processes that allow it to eat rock. I would be its silent assistant, preparing each night's portion of dissolved mineral, carrying away the waste products of its slow digestion.

The beetles wouldn't see me, but they would feel me—the humidity gradient I create would be one of their navigation tools, helping them find the moistest layers of guano where the decomposition is most active. The bacteria would bloom in my wake, their populations cycling up and down with my presence, performing bursts of metabolism in the fleeting window between too-dry and evaporated-again.

And I would know things. I would know which birds are sick by the slightly different temperature of their exhalations, the subtle change in the water vapor they contribute to the air. I would know where eggs are being incubated by the warmer microclimate around those nests. I would know when chicks have hatched by the new signatures of their high-metabolism breathing. I would carry information through the colony in the form of gradients and phase transitions.

I would also know the cairn. The stacked stones would be a puzzle for me—their surfaces alien, not-quite-rock, lacking the proper porosity of the tuff. I would struggle to condense on them at first, beading up and rolling off their smoother faces. But slowly, as the guano accumulated and the bird-cement covered them, they would become more like the walls, more receptive to my presence. I would participate in their gradual incorporation into the fissure's fabric, helping to blur the line between human intention and ecological function.

The spider near the rim would exist at my periphery—too high up, too close to the dry plateau air for me to reliably reach. But on the stillest nights, when the inversion is strong and the warm air pools higher in the crack, I might brush against its web, leaving droplets that would catch the pre-dawn light like strung pearls before evaporating. The spider would regard this as an annoyance. The web would sag.

During the days, I wouldn't quite cease to exist—some trace of me would linger in the deepest cracks, in the pore spaces of the guano crust, as bound water in the moss tissues. But I would be dormant, potential rather than actual, waiting for the equation to balance again: temperature differential plus relative humidity plus nucleation sites equals condensation. Waiting for the birds to return, to breathe, to warm the air, to set the conditions for my reformation.

And I would be ancient in one sense—water is always ancient, cycled and recycled through countless forms—but young in another. Each night's condensation would be a new performance, shaped by that day's exact conditions. If the plateau above was particularly cold, I would form faster, denser, might even achieve something approaching fog in the upper reaches of the crack. If the day had been warm, I would struggle, managing only a thin film on the coldest stones. Rain on the plateau would change my chemistry, adding new dissolved minerals. Drought would concentrate me, make me more saline, more aggressive in my interaction with the stone.

I would be the medium of translation between the thermal economy of the colony and the water needs of everything else. I would be the only reason this density of life can exist here—without me, the fissure would be just another dry crack in dry rock under a dry sky. With me, it becomes possible: the moss, the fern, the beetle communities, the bacterial blooms. I make the wound habitable.

And I would know the fissure is widening. I would feel it in the subtle changes to the air circulation patterns, the way the convection cells shift year by year as the walls creep apart. I would map the new surfaces exposed by each microscopic movement—fresh tuff, unweathered, reluctant at first to accept condensation but gradually becoming incorporated into my nightly rounds. I would be among the first to touch these new-made faces of stone, to begin the long work of making them part of the living system.

Some nights, I wonder if I would dream. If condensation can dream, what would it dream of? Perhaps of becoming rain—real rain, falling from real clouds, not this nightly simulacrum born of breath and stone. Perhaps of flowing, of joining with other water to become something that moves across the landscape instead of appearing and disappearing in the same spot. Perhaps of the sea, that vast gathering where all water eventually tells its story.

But then morning would come, the temperature gradient would reverse, and I would sublimate back into vapor, dispersing through the warming air, carrying with me molecules of ammonia and carbon dioxide and the microscopic spores of the moss, the chemical signatures of everything I had touched in the night. I would rise with the eruption of birds at dawn, riding the updraft out of the fissure, tasting for a moment the thin dry air of the plateau before being scattered by the wind.

And in scattering, I would know one more thing: that somewhere, in some other crack in the earth, the conditions are right again. Warm bodies, cold stone, still air. Somewhere, condensation is beginning, drawing water out of breath, making the inhospitable briefly habitable, writing its temporary maps on walls that remember nothing.

This is what I would be: the nightly punctuation between states, the phase transition that makes life possible in impossible places, the mediator between the colony's warmth and the stone's cold, forever appearing and disappearing, forever making the same argument: that even here, especially here, in this raw unfinished crack in the earth, something can grow.
