In the district where machines flower and roots compute, I am the Arithmancer of Cambium Station Twelve—though whether I chose this role or it chose me remains as uncertain as the gravity here.

My workshop exists in three trees simultaneously. Not between them, but *through* them, the walls phasing in and out of heartwood as the trunks grow at their separate rates. This morning—if morning means anything in the perpetual fungal twilight—my eastern wall is solid oak, my western disappears entirely into young sapwood, and my floor can't decide if it's bark or floorboard, flickering between states with each pulse of the great root-mind above.

I've been here long enough that my tools have begun to dream. The calipers measure distances that won't exist until next Tuesday. The slide rule calculates in base-8 when the wood breathes in, base-10 when it exhales. My primary calculating engine—a hybrid of brass gears and living cambium that I've nursed for thirty years—computes the growth rates of the city itself, predicting where new buildings will bud, which streets will seal themselves behind walls of bark, when the gravity will invert in the merchant quarters.

My body has made its accommodations. My left eye tracks sap-time now, watching the slow crawl of nutrients through xylem channels as others might watch clock hands. The right eye still sees in human-time, creating a constant parallax that would drive me mad if the fungal threads woven through my optic nerves didn't parse the differential into something resembling prophecy. I dream in growth rings and wake up knowing next week's weather in the wood.

The ports in my wrists are different from the factory workers'—mine connect not to engines but to the calculation roots, those specialized neural pathways that the trees use for their slow mathematics. When I plug in, I taste equations. Logarithms have a metallic tang, derivatives feel like falling, and integrals... integrals taste the way green smells, if green were a number large enough to encompass all possible futures of chlorophyll.

My apprentice arrived yesterday—or was it last year? Time moves strangely when you're calculating in tree-time. She's young enough that her fontanelle still pulses visibly when she concentrates, the soft spot in her skull glowing faintly with bioluminescent thought. I'm teaching her to read the bark-script, those patterns in the tree rings that encode the city's memory. She's quick—already she can differentiate between growth rings that record rainfall and those that record the installation of new steam engines. Soon I'll teach her to write in cambium, to program growth patterns that will manifest as buildings in decades yet unborn.

The calculating engine requires daily feeding—not fuel in any conventional sense, but problems. It hungers for computational complexity the way plants hunger for light. I spend my mornings translating the city's needs into mathematical food: optimization problems for the new fungal battery networks, differential equations to balance the steam pressure across districts, recursive algorithms that help the root-mind above think more efficiently about its own growth.

Sometimes the engine computes things I didn't ask for. Last week it produced a proof that gravity is actually a form of very slow light. The week before, it calculated the exact number of heartbeats remaining in my life (I've decided not to count them). Once, it generated blueprints for a machine that would turn sound into wood, complete with engineering specifications that made my apprentice weep blood from her left eye until I burned the papers.

My neighbors in this district are the Memory Keepers, whose bodies have become libraries. Their skin grows thin as paper, and when they need to record something, they simply peel off a sheet of themselves and write upon it. They regenerate slowly, adding layers of new skin that preserve everything written on the layers beneath. The oldest among them are living palimpsests, their bodies containing centuries of overlapped text that can only be read by holding them up to the fungal light and parsing the shadows of words through flesh.

Below us—or above, when the gravity shivers—the Tone Mechanics tune the city's resonance. They've developed ears that can hear the ultrasonic stress patterns in wood and metal, the pitch at which materials sing just before they fail. Their workshops are cacophonies to normal hearing, but to them it's a symphony of structural integrity. They prevent catastrophic collapses by adjusting the harmonics of buildings, adding a strut here to flat a sharp note, removing a beam there to resolve a suspended chord in the architecture.

My own workshop's resonance has been shifting lately. The calculating engine has begun to hum at frequencies that make my teeth grow incrementally longer. My apprentice says she can feel new rooms budding in the spaces between our walls, embryonic chambers that will exist once the trees grow another ring. I've started leaving offerings for these unborn rooms—small mathematical proofs written on bark-paper, hoping to influence what kind of spaces they'll become.

The hardest part of my work isn't the calculation but the translation. The city thinks in ways that don't quite map to numbers, and numbers think in ways that don't quite map to growth. I spend my days in the liminal space between, converting the dreams of engines into the language of chlorophyll, teaching math to trees and photosynthesis to machines. My greatest achievement is a formula that describes the exact curvature of hope in a sprouting seed. My greatest failure is that I've forgotten what sunlight looks like, having spent too long translating its absence into equations.

But this is the nature of being an Arithmancer in the inverted city. We calculate the incalculable, map the unmappable, solve for X when X is a living, breathing, growing thing that refuses to hold still long enough to be a variable. The city needs us to be its conscious mind, to think the thoughts too quick for wood and too slow for steam. We are the mediators between machine-time and tree-time, our bodies and minds stretched across both until we're not quite either, not quite neither.

Tomorrow—if tomorrow means anything when you live inside a tree that experiences all of time simultaneously—I'll teach my apprentice to factor polynomials using fungal growth patterns. Next month, we'll upgrade the calculating engine with new cambium circuits that can process imaginary numbers as real growth. Next year, if the equations are correct, our workshop will finally complete its phase through all three trees and exist entirely in the spaces between solid and void, the perfect location for computing the mathematics of impossible cities.

For now, I oil the gears that need oiling, water the roots that need watering, and feed new problems to the ever-hungry engine. Outside my flickering walls, the city breathes and grows and dreams its architectural dreams. Inside, I calculate the probability that any of this is real, get a different answer every time, and decide that's exactly as it should be.

The numbers taste green today. That means rain is coming. Or possibly that we're falling. In the inverted city, it's often hard to tell the difference.
