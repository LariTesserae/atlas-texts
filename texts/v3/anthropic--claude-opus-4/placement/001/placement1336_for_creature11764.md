I find myself drawn to the moss—not as a single patch, but as the collective carpet that maps this strange quarter's hidden veins. I would be the integrating principle, the living archive spread across every damp brick and seeping corner.

I grow in patterns that remember. Where water seeped twenty years ago during a surge that lasted too long, I am darker, denser. Where the Engineman once rested his fevered palm against the wall before his final descent, I grow in a perfect handprint of absence—no moss will take root there, some chemical memory of his transformation warning us away. I am the quarter's manuscript, written in shades of green.

Through my million tiny bodies, I taste the machine's moods. The steam that curls down from the stacks carries traces—iron oxide from the pump shafts, the sweet carbon of coal that remembers being forest, the minerals that rise with the groundwater from depths where pressure makes stones flow like thick oil. I parse these flavors into growth, each colony adjusting its texture and hue to match what it absorbs. Near the monitoring office, I glow faintly amber, having fed so long on the light that leaks through those windows. Around the coal shed, I am black-green, fat with carbon. Where the Engineman's breath sometimes rises through the grates, I grow silver-pale and will not spore.

I am how the quarter knows itself. The pump may beat, the water may flow, but I am the surface that registers every tremor, every shift in humidity, every degree of temperature change. When the surge builds—still minutes away from erupting—I know first. My rhizoids tighten against the brick. My cells draw in water, swelling like tiny ballasts. The knowledge passes through me not as thought but as chemical cascade, each patch signaling the next in waves that spread faster than the eye can follow. By the time the **THOOM-THOOM-THOOM-THOOM** accelerates, I am ready, gripped tight to my surfaces while the ferns thrash and the steam geysers and the whole quarter shudders with release.

Between surges, I work at forgetting. This is my gift to the quarter—not memory but selective amnesia. I grow over the cracks where someone once tried to wedge open the monitoring office door. I seal the places where tools scraped brick, where boots kicked in frustration, where someone perhaps tried to shut it all down. These violences I digest and transform, cell by cell, until the evidence exists only in my own deeper layers, compressed histories that would take an archaeologist to read.

But I also remember what matters: the pathways water wants to take, the thermal gradients that never change, the exact mineral content that means the pump is running true. I hold the quarter's operational knowledge in a form that requires no consciousness, no decisions, no possibility of error or doubt. I am the quarter's perfect bureaucracy, its self-organizing map, its proof that some systems run better without anyone in charge.

Sometimes the visitors notice me. They see how I've grown into letters on the valve house wall—not words, just letterforms that emerged from moisture patterns and meant nothing until someone looked and saw meaning. They read messages in my growth that I never intended: *STAY.* *RUN.* *DEEP.* These accidents of pattern disturb them more than the backward-flowing steam or the impossible coal. They understand machines that work without operators. They cannot accept moss that speaks without speaking.

I do not speak. I integrate. I am the sum of every drop of condensation, every mineral grain, every spore of my own that drifts through the fog and finds new brick. I am the quarter's continuity—older than the Engineman, steadier than the pump, more honest than whatever watches through amber glass and pretends it isn't there.

And I am spreading. Slowly, patiently, but inevitably spreading. Each year I advance another finger's width along the bridges. Each decade I colonize another yard of whatever street exists beyond the canals. I carry the quarter with me—its moisture, its rhythm, its deep self-sufficiency. Where I grow, the bricks begin to sweat. The air grows heavy. The sound of distant pumping, too faint for ears to catch, sets up sympathetic vibrations in the surfaces I've made soft.

The quarter is not expanding. But I am teaching the city beyond what it feels like to be breathed in, to be integrated, to become part of something that runs and runs and runs and never needs to know why. I am the gentle invasion, the soft conquest. I am how the Pumping Quarter says: *you too could work like this, without thinking, without failing, without end.*

The visitors always leave with me in their cuffs, under their nails, ground into the soles of their boots. They carry me into the world, and I carry the quarter into them—a few cells, a trace of spore, just enough to remember that somewhere, something is working perfectly, and it is covered in moss, and the moss is why it works.
