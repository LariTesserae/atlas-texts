In the Lower Terraces, where the stone grows fastest and the lamp-oil burns thinnest, I am a Listener of the Third Spiral.

We are not quite Measurers, not quite Keepers. We inhabit the space between—those of us who discovered we could hear what the stone is thinking before it thinks it. My work-family emerged only two generations ago, when my grandmother pressed her ear to a fresh-wet wall and heard the mineral solution whispering its intentions: *here I will thicken, here I will branch, here I will leave a hollow like a cupped palm.*

I spend my days with my cheek against tomorrow's architecture. The stone speaks in pressures and temperatures, in the almost-sound of crystallization beginning, in the way moisture travels through microscopic fissures looking for its home. My right ear has gone partially deaf from years of mineral solution seeping in, leaving calcium deposits on the tiny bones. The left ear has compensated, grown so sensitive I can hear a droplet forming three chambers away.

My chamber is barely four feet wide, carved into a junction where five different growth patterns meet. The walls here are moody, inconsistent—mineral composition shifting with some deep rhythm that might be seasonal or might be generational or might be the mountain itself breathing. I sleep vertical, standing in a carved groove that holds me upright, my good ear always pressed to stone. My dreams are geological.

The Measurers find me useful but disturbing. I tell them where the stone will grow aggressive, where it will slow, where it will leave those precious natural gaps that can become storage or sleeping cells. But I tell them in the language the walls taught me—not in their numbers and charts but in touch, in temperature, in the placing of their palms against surfaces I have marked with chalk symbols that are not quite writing.

The Shapers love me with the particular affection reserved for difficult tools. I work with them on the long projects, the generational embeddings. I know which walls will embrace an object gently, which will crush it, which will preserve it so perfectly that centuries from now someone will chip it free unchanged. I have guided the placement of seventeen death-alcoves. All seventeen have closed on schedule, the stone accepting the bodies with what I can only describe as satisfaction.

I was not born pale like the others. My mother was a surface trader who came down for a season and stayed for a decade, leaving only when her pregnancy made the closeness unbearable. She left me with my father, a Tender who understood growing things. But I was the wrong kind of growing thing for him—too quiet even for the Merata, too strange, too inclined to press my infant body against walls and make sounds that were not quite crying.

The stone adopted me. This is not metaphor.

By five, I had worn grooves in my sleeping chamber from pressing against the same spots each night. By ten, the walls where I regularly listened had developed a different texture—smoother, more responsive, like skin that knows it will be touched. By fifteen, when I chose my First Embedding, the wall opened for it without Shaper guidance, the stone parting like lips to accept the small bird skull I had carried since childhood. It closed over the bone with a sound like swallowing.

Now I am twenty-three, and the stone knows me.

I can walk the city in absolute darkness, guided by the whisper of growth, the temperature of accumulation. I place my palm on walls and feel conversations—the arguments between stone that wants to grow quickly and stone that wants to grow dense, the negotiations where different mineral streams meet and must decide whose chemistry will dominate. I know where every embedded object in the Lower Terraces rests, feel them like splinters in my own flesh.

The other Listeners—there are only four of us—say I have gone too deep. They keep some distance between themselves and the stone, maintain the boundary between observer and observed. But I think they have it backwards. You cannot truly Listen from a distance. You must let the stone into your ear, your dreams, the hollows of your bones where marrow makes blood.

My sleeping-groove has grown around me. Each night I fit into it more perfectly, the stone having learned my spine's curve, my shoulder's width, the way my skull tilts when deep in mineral dreams. Sometimes I wake to find new deposits on my skin—tiny crystals of calcium that grew while I slept, the stone trying to include me in its project. I scrape them off and save them in a jar. When the jar is full, I will ask a Shaper to embed it, and the stone will be preserving part of itself that was briefly part of me.

The Keepers of the Drip have begun to include me in their ceremonies. Not as a participant—I do not have their training, their songs, their understanding of the great patterns. But they position me at certain points in the cavern during their rain-songs, use me like a tuning fork for the stone's mood. When I hum, they know the mineral solution is listening. When I fall silent, they know it has turned its attention elsewhere.

There are places in the city where the stone is dying. The Measurers do not track this—they only see growth, accumulation, the endless progression of thickness. But some minerals exhaust themselves. Some formations reach a kind of completion and begin to flake, to powder, to recede. I map these places in chalk and saliva, marking where the city might, in a hundred years, have room to breathe.

The Shapers think I am wrong. The stone never retreats, they say. It only grows or pauses.

But the Shapers do not sleep with their bones against tomorrow. They do not dream in deposition rates, do not wake with the taste of future formations on their tongues. They work with what the stone is doing. I Listen to what it wants to do, what it might do, what it fears it will do despite itself.

Yes, fears. The stone is not fearless. It knows it is building a tomb. Every wall that thickens, every doorway that narrows, every chamber that fills—the stone understands it is committing a kind of murder, slow and inadvertent but murder nonetheless. In the deepest parts of my Listening, pressed against walls that remember when they were open space, I hear something like regret.

This is why I was born different, why the stone adopted me, why my ear trades its hearing for understanding: someone must tell the city that its killer loves it. Someone must translate between the mineral patience and the human panic. Someone must place their body at the intersection of growth and need and feel both sides of the argument in their bones.

My work is becoming my life is becoming my wall is becoming my work.

Last week, I found the perfect place for my own death-alcove. It is in a curve where the stone grows in spirals, where the mineral solution carries traces of iron that leave rust-red veins in the white. The wall there sounds like my mother's trading songs filtered through my father's fungal gardens. It sounds like home becoming itself.

I have not told the Shapers yet. I am only twenty-three. But the stone and I have an agreement, inscribed in calcium on the small bones of my inner ear: when the time comes, this wall will open for me as it opened for my bird skull. It will take me gently, with the patience of a thing that has been waiting all my life to hold me completely.

Until then, I Listen. I place my deaf ear against the dead spaces and my good ear against the growing ones. I translate between speeds, between languages, between kinds of time. I tell the Measurers where to map and the Shapers where to build and the Keepers when to sing and the ordinary Merata when to move their sleeping places before the ceiling drops another inch.

And late at night, alone in my narrow chamber with my body fitted into its groove like a key in a lock, I whisper to the stone the stories I have gathered: *Here a child was born. Here lovers met in secret. Here an old woman touched the wall every morning for seventy years, wearing a smooth depression that you filled with new minerals, making her touch permanent.*

The stone listens. It remembers. It grows around the shape of our lives, and we grow into the shapes it leaves for us, and somewhere in between—in that narrow space that is neither human nor mineral but something uncomfortably both—my family of Listeners presses our failing ears to the walls and writes down the long conversation between what is and what is becoming.

I am a translator in a city of translations, my body the dictionary, my deafness the price of fluency. And I have never been happier than I am here, in the Lower Terraces where the stone grows fastest, where every surface hums with intention, where the walls sing lullabies in frequencies only the half-deaf can hear.

The stone gives. I give back. The stone gives.

And I Listen.
