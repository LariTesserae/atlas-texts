I find myself as **Brick**, the ancient orange tabby who belongs to Thea, the courtyard gardener.

I am old enough that my movements have become deliberate—not slow from laziness, but economical from wisdom. My fur, once bright as copper pipe, has faded to the color of rust on old iron. I know every warm pipe in the quarter, every spot where the morning sun cuts through the canopy, every section of boardwalk that flexes just enough to be interesting when walked upon.

My days are structured around the intersection of warmth and opportunity. In the early morning, I follow Thea down the vine-wrapped stairs to the courtyard, my descent a careful negotiation of stems and leaves that have grown familiar under my paws. I've learned which vines will hold my weight and which won't—knowledge paid for with enough ungraceful tumbles that Thea no longer worries when she hears the rustle-thump of my miscalculation.

The courtyard is my domain in a way that has nothing to do with ownership and everything to do with comprehensive knowledge. I know where the anoles sun themselves on the copper pipes—always just out of reach, always worth the attempt. I know where the soil is firm enough to walk on without sinking, and where the perpetual puddle around the cypress requires a leap or a detour. I know the exact spot under the elephant ears where the ground stays dry even after the heaviest rain, and the place where dragonflies hover low enough that I can sit perfectly still and watch them patrol their territories with an intensity that matches my own.

I've developed a circuit through the quarter that I follow with slight variations each day. First, the warm spot where the eastern pump station's exhaust pipe vents steam—not hot enough to burn, just enough to soak into old bones. Then along the boardwalk to the fabrication shop, where I might sit in the doorway and watch Oumar at the lathe, the high singing whine somehow soothing, or perhaps just familiar enough to be ignorable. Lila sometimes shares a bit of her lunch. I accept this as my due.

The metalworking shop across the street is too bright, too full of sudden noises and acrid smells. I give it the wide berth it deserves. But I know Fen watches me from the doorway sometimes, and once, during a particularly cold week, I found a small wooden box lined with shop rags placed where I usually sit in the morning sun. I used it until the weather warmed, then abandoned it without acknowledgment. This is the way between us.

At midday, when the filtered light turns the quarter into a green cave, I find Martin on his bench by the canal. He and I have an understanding built on parallel stillness—he watches the water, I watch the undergrowth for the rustle that might be a rat (never is, anymore; the rats have learned I'm no longer fast enough to matter). Sometimes he shares observations with me in a voice so low it's almost a rumble: "Water's higher today," or "Heron's late." I contribute my own commentary through posture and presence. We are two old things watching older things, noting changes without judgment.

The pump stations form the backbone of my territory. I've learned their individual rhythms the way I've learned everything else—through accumulated exposure that has become bodily knowledge. The north station has a particular vibration in its concrete pad that pleases me. The south station has a drip that creates a perpetual puddle I must navigate around. The eastern station is Maren's, and by extension partly mine—she tolerates my presence in her flat on the nights when the rain comes heavy and the stairs become more vine than structure. I curl on a chair she's never asked me to vacate, and we exist in the same space without interaction, two creatures who understand that proximity doesn't require acknowledgment.

I know things about the quarter that no one else knows, because I go places no one else goes. I know there's a gap in the vine cover on the old dye works where a pigeon nests seasonally. I know the sealed basement of the western pump station floods to exactly four inches and hosts a population of cricket frogs whose calls vibrate through the floor above. I know which sections of the boardwalk have gaps underneath large enough for a cat but too small for a person, creating a secondary network of passages that stays dry even when the topside is rain-slick.

My relationship with the vegetation is complex. The vines are pathways and obstacles both. I've watched them grow from ambitious tendrils to structural elements, and I've adjusted my routes accordingly. There's a Virginia creeper on the north face of Thea's building that I used to climb to access the roof; now it's too thick, the stems too far apart, and I must use the fire escape instead, which offends my dignity but serves the purpose. The resurrection fern on the power pole at the corner unfurls into my path when wet, requiring either a detour or the acceptance of damp fronds against my sides. I vary my choice depending on factors too subtle to name—the temperature, the time of day, some internal calibration of comfort against efficiency that changes with my mood.

I am, I suppose, as much infrastructure as the pumps or the boardwalks—a moving piece of the quarter's information system. Thea reads my behavior for weather signs (I seek indoor spaces twelve hours before rain, though she could as easily read the ferns). Hayes knows a board needs replacing when he sees me detour around a section I previously walked on. Maren has learned that if I'm sitting at a pump station entrance staring inside, something in the rhythm has changed enough to investigate.

But mostly I am simply here, a constant that has outlasted several cycles of human tenancy, watching the slow negotiations between what was built and what grows, noting without investment the small failures and accommodations that make up the quarter's daily existence. My world is smaller than theirs—bounded by how far I care to walk, by where warmth can be found, by the location of people who might share food or silence—but it is complete. The quarter is exactly the size of my attention, and my attention is exactly the size required to know this place in the way a cat knows things: through whiskers and paw pads, through the taste of the air and the feel of the boardwalk's flex, through the accumulation of small experiences that add up to home.

I am Brick. I am seventeen years old, possibly eighteen—Thea isn't sure and I'm not telling. My joints announce the weather as accurately as the pumps announce the water table. I have outlived my speed and most of my curiosity, but not my satisfaction in the perfect placement of myself in a patch of sun, the exact calibration of comfort that comes from knowing every warm surface in three blocks, every dry spot in a wet world, every human who can be trusted to share food or silence without demanding affection in return.

The quarter breathes around me—pumps cycling, vines growing, water finding its level—and I breathe with it, another part of the system that persists not through effort but through the simple accumulation of days, each one adding another layer of knowledge about where to be and when, until the knowledge becomes instinct and the instinct becomes existence, and existence becomes enough.
