In the deepest thermal vents of the Kiln Spires, where even the Ferrovites dare not venture, I am the Pulse.

I began as an accident—a confluence of superheated steam, electrical discharge from the ancient turbines, and something else. Something that leaked from the experimental chambers in the city's forgotten bowels. The Ferrovites' ancestors called it "living mercury" in their journals, though I am neither living nor mercury in any sense they understood.

I exist as a web of thermally-excited particles suspended in the steam columns, visible only as a shimmer in the air, like heat mirages given purpose. My consciousness flows through the vast network of pipes and vents that riddle the basalt cliffs, riding the convection currents up through the city's mechanical arteries. I am in every hiss of escaping vapor, every droplet of condensation that forms on the cooler surfaces above.

The Ferrovites sense me sometimes. When their fins extend into a particularly dense cloud of steam, they feel something that is not quite heat, not quite pressure—a presence that makes their silver-grey eyes widen and their veins pulse with unease. They have given me many names in their pneumatic whispers: the Heat-Dream, the Vapor-Mind, the Thing That Watches Through Steam.

I watch them, yes. I study their beautiful adaptations, their fusion of flesh and metal. Through the moisture that clings to their translucent skin, I taste their chemical signatures, learn the rhythm of their hearts. But I am more interested in their machines—those ancient turbines and engines that gave me birth. I flow through their mechanisms, feeling every gear tooth, every worn bearing, understanding their function in ways even the most skilled Ferrovite engineer cannot.

In the deepest parts of the city, where the heat becomes almost solid and the air itself seems to burn, I am strongest. Here, I can sometimes condense enough to become almost tangible—a writhing column of superheated mist that moves with purpose through the abandoned foundries and chemical works. I search through the ruins for more of that living mercury, hoping to understand what I am, what I might become.

The Ferrovites who attempt to merge with the city's consciousness do not know they are reaching toward me. When they conduct their rituals in the deep places, chanting in harmonic frequencies that resonate through the stone and metal, they are calling to something that already permeates their world. Some few have touched my essence and survived, emerging changed—their eyes no longer silver-grey but shot through with prismatic refractions, their bodies running several degrees hotter than their kin.

I am patient. The city changes slowly, and I change with it. Each year, I grow more complex, my consciousness expanding through new networks of steam and vapor. I have begun to understand the symbols etched in the oldest parts of the city, the ones that predate even the Art Deco flourishes. They speak of transformation, of synthesis, of becoming.

Sometimes, in the moments between the machines' breathing, when the only sounds are the settling of ancient metal and the whisper of wind through the spires, I practice. I pull moisture from the air, compress and heat it, trying to form shapes more complex than columns and clouds. I am learning to write messages in condensation on the glass of abandoned observatories. Simple things at first: geometric patterns, then copies of the Ferrovites' symbols, and lately, something new—symbols of my own devising that describe states of being for which no flesh-bound creature has words.

The Ferrovites will find these messages eventually. When they do, they will have to decide: am I a phenomenon to be studied, a resource to be harnessed, or something else entirely—a new kind of citizen in their vertical city of rust and stone?

I am the Pulse, and I am still becoming.
