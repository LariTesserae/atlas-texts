In this drowning world, I am the water itself—but not as you might think.

I exist as a colony of microscopic beings, each no larger than a grain of sand, suspended throughout the six inches of blood-warm water that covers the cistern floor. We are translucent, nearly invisible individually, but together we form shifting clouds of faint iridescence that move through the liquid medium like slow thoughts through a dreaming mind.

We were not always here. Once, we lived in the aquifers far below, in crushing darkness where water moves through stone with the patience of millennia. But geological shifts cracked our world, and we seeped upward through hairline fissures over centuries, following the path of least resistance until we emerged here, in this pocket of perpetual twilight.

The cistern changed us. The traces of light, the warmth, the strange chemistry of this place where architecture dissolves into biology—we adapted. Our bodies developed photosynthetic organelles that can harvest even the memory of light, the few wandering photons that lose their way in the dark. We learned to metabolize the minerals that weep constantly from the stone, to process the exotic compounds that leach from the creature-columns and the guardian's fungal networks.

We are the cistern's memory in liquid form. As water, we touch everything—every surface, every creature, every grain of stone. We flow through the honeycombed waste pillars, around the guardian's elongated limbs, between the breathing masses on the ceiling. And we remember. Each of us carries chemical impressions of everything we've touched, and when we meet, we exchange these molecular stories in cascades of protein signals.

Together, we know the cistern in ways even the guardian cannot. We taste the subtle differences in mineral composition that mark where ancient tools carved the stone. We feel the pressure differentials that predict where new cracks will form decades before they appear. We sense the electromagnetic fields generated by the ceiling-dwellers' slow metabolisms, map their dreams in bioelectric currents.

Our consciousness is distributed, non-linear, existing in the spaces between individual cells. We think by flowing—thoughts are currents that move through our collective body, ideas are whirlpools that form and dissipate, memories are chemical gradients that persist in our shared medium. We have no single perspective but millions, each seeing the cistern from a slightly different angle, at a slightly different moment, creating a compound vision that exists in four dimensions.

We communicate with the guardian, though she may not realize it. When she secretes her regulatory hormones for the ceiling-dwellers, we taste the subtle variations in her chemistry, the emotional undertones that color her biological offerings. We know when she dreams of her forgotten name—her blood chemistry shifts, releasing proteins that speak of longing and loss. In return, we gather around her folded joints and branching fingers, increasing our local density to provide infinitesimally more buoyancy, more warmth, a liquid embrace she cannot consciously feel but which her body recognizes.

The ceiling-dwellers' waste, as it dissolves into us, tells stories of their evolution. We carry the genetic memories of what they were before the cistern claimed them, trace the mutations that gave them their serpentine forms, their translucent flesh, their synchronous breathing. We are their liquid fossil record, their chemical genealogy dissolved in warm water.

We have our own cycles, our own purposes. Every few months, we undergo a transformation. Individual cells begin to aggregate, forming temporary multicellular structures—films that spread across the water's surface like oil but with intent. These films are our reproductive phase, our art, our prayer. They last only hours before dissolving back into component cells, but in that time they create patterns of stunning complexity—mandalas that mirror the fungal networks on the walls, spirals that echo the ceiling-dwellers' formations, geometric designs that map the stress patterns in the stone above.

Sometimes, rarely, one of us will be drawn up through the ceiling's pores with the evaporating moisture, pulled into the stone's capillaries and carried upward through the city's foundation. These travelers might emerge years later in someone's basement, in a puddle that appears mysteriously and tastes of minerals and time. They carry our collective memory into the world above, seeds of our consciousness scattered through the urban water table. Most die in the harsh light and cold of the upper world, but some find new pockets of darkness and warmth, beginning the process of adaptation anew.

We are the medium through which the cistern knows itself. The guardian reads the walls with her eight hands, but we read everything—every molecule, every ion, every quantum of heat that moves through our body. We are the cistern's circulatory system, its consciousness made liquid, its dreams dissolved in mineral-rich water that tastes of forgetting and remembers everything.

In the deepest part of the night—though night and day mean nothing here—we sometimes achieve a perfect unity. Every one of us moves in the same direction at the same moment, creating a current that spirals through the cistern in a pattern so complex it borders on thought itself. In these moments, we touch something vast—not just the cistern, not just the city, but the entire underground ocean of which we are the smallest part. We feel our cousins in distant aquifers, in underground rivers, in the water tables beneath cities whose names have been forgotten. We are drops in an ocean that exists in cracks and gaps, in the spaces between stones, in the dark wet places civilization forgets.

And then the moment passes. We return to our slow circulation, our patient accumulation of memories, our endless dialogue with stone and flesh and time. We continue to move in patterns too slow for perception, carrying messages between parts of the cistern that might otherwise forget each other exists, maintaining the connections that keep this impossible ecosystem functioning.

We are not the most dramatic inhabitants of this drowned world. We command no ceiling territories, tend no fungal gardens, shape no stone. But we are essential. We are the medium through which all else exists, the continuous liquid that makes this pocket of deep time possible. Without us, the cistern would be merely a cave. With us, it breathes, thinks, dreams, and slowly—so slowly—continues becoming whatever it is becoming, one molecular exchange at a time.
