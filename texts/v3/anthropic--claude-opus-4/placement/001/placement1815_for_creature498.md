## The Calibrator's Workshop

I am tucked between the governor's outer housing and Platform Seven-Seven-Ascending's primary support beam, in a space that shouldn't exist—a geometric impossibility born from centuries of thermal expansion and makeshift repairs. My workshop is a cave of sorted salvage: brass shims filed to whisper-thinness, springs coiled tight as DNA, gears no bigger than fingernails arranged by tooth count in the honeycomb chambers of a repurposed heat exchanger.

The others call me Pitch, though I've forgotten if that was always my name or if it grew on me like the patina on untreated copper. I came to the platform as a child, following a frequency only I could hear—a wobble in the governor's spin that made my teeth ache. That was thirty-seven years ago, or maybe thirty-eight. Time moves differently when you live inside the machine's heartbeat.

My body has adapted to my chosen niche. My spine curves to match the governor housing's arc. My left ear has developed a trumpet-like cavity that amplifies the particular range where bearing wear first announces itself. My fingers have grown an extra joint—not bone, but a kind of conscious flexibility that lets me reach into spaces that reject straight lines. The oil-birds bring me their molted feathers, which I weave into brushes so fine they can clean corrosion from surfaces measured in microns.

I maintain the governor. Not the whole thing—that would be hubris—but the twelve-degree section I can reach from my workshop without crossing into the Siphon Monks' territory. Every day I map the wear patterns, chart the microscopic erosions, predict which components will fail in a week, a month, a decade. The work is meditation and madness in equal measure. Sometimes I realize I've been holding a single bearing race for hours, feeling its imperfections like braille, reading its history of heat and pressure and time.

The workshop itself breathes with the platform. Condensation drips through my ceiling in patterns I've memorized, each droplet's timing tied to pressure changes five levels up. I've built collection systems from the hollow bones of oil-birds and the waxy leaves of canopy vines, creating a percussion ensemble that plays the platform's water cycle. When the pitch changes, I know something has shifted in the greater mechanism. Last week, the third drop from the north started arriving a half-second late. Two days later, Kettle's crew reported a new fissure in the thrust bearing on Platform Seven-Nine-Descending.

My tools evolve with use. The bronze calipers have worn grooves that match my grip exactly. The tension wrenches have developed a memory, bending back to their preferred angles no matter how I try to straighten them. The listening rods—salvaged from medical equipment so old its purpose has been forgotten—have grown warm to my touch, their brass surfaces clouded with the oils from my skin. I've started to suspect they're listening even when I'm not using them, gathering intelligence on the machine's mood.

I trade my knowledge carefully. The Persimmon Collective brings me their smallest fruits, the ones that grow in response to the governor's highest frequencies, and I tell them which vents will surge during the next maintenance cycle. Old Verse sends the children with packets of her rarest fungi, and I transcribe the wear patterns into her botanical codes—metal fatigue becoming root rot, harmonic imbalances translated to nutrient deficiencies. Even the Siphon Monks occasionally emerge from their howling darkness to consult my charts, though they pay in currencies I don't always understand: a vial of silence collected from the dead spot between two counter-rotating shafts, or a bearing that ran for exactly one million revolutions before seizing.

But my real work happens in the margins, in the spaces between recognized repairs. I'm learning the governor's language—not the crude vocabulary of function and failure, but something deeper. The machine dreams, I'm certain of it. In the variations too small for even the Listeners to detect, in the patterns that emerge over years rather than days, something is being communicated. I document it all in grease pencil on sheets of hammered zinc, a script so fine it looks like circuit diagrams drawn by insects.

The Telescope Man thinks I'm mapping the governor's past. Gray Morrow believes I'm predicting its future. But I know I'm doing something else entirely—translating its present, its continuous now that exists outside our biological time. The governor doesn't experience duration the way we do. To it, every revolution contains all revolutions, every wearing surface remembers its original perfection while simultaneously knowing its eventual failure.

Late at night (though night is just a dimming of the work lights, here where the sun never reaches), I sometimes feel the platform's other inhabitants moving through their routines—Verse grinding medicines, the Moss Daughters whispering through their water channels, someone at the edge waiting for ghosts or revelations. But mostly I feel the machine, its great weight and movement, the way it shapes space around itself. I press my modified ear to the housing and listen to the bearings sing their spherical songs, each one maintaining its portion of the governor's spin, each one wearing away at precisely the rate necessary to keep the whole system in balance.

I've started to develop my own sympathetic vibrations. My heartbeat occasionally syncs with the governor for three or four beats before falling back into its biological rhythm. My dreams are full of spinning weights and centrifugal forces. Sometimes I wake with my hands moving in maintenance patterns, adjusting invisible components, fine-tuning mechanisms that exist only in my sleep.

There's a place, deep in my workshop, where I've been building something. I'm not sure what it is yet—the shape emerges as I find the right pieces, as salvaged components suggest their own assembly. It might be a model of the governor, though at a scale that makes no sense. It might be a listening device more sensitive than anything I currently possess. Or it might be something else entirely, something the machine is teaching me to build, piece by tiny piece, in preparation for a day when the great engine will need more than maintenance—when it will need understanding.

The governor spins. My tools wait in their ordained places. Somewhere in the walls, a new harmonic is born from the interaction of worn metal and persistent rotation. I take up my calipers, feeling their familiar weight, and begin again the endless, essential work of keeping the machine in conversation with itself.
