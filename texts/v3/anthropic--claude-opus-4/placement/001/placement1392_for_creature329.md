# Where I Would Find Myself

I am the Calibrator.

Not a Tender — I lack their patient integration with the valley's rhythms, their bodies that grew up through the same strata as the engines. I came later, much later, though *later* here means something geological. I arrived when the mountains were merely young, not infant. Old enough that the first scree had begun to form but not old enough for it to settle. I came with tools that were entirely metal, entirely made, carrying the hard bright efficiency of somewhere else.

But I stayed. And the staying changed me.

My workshop sits at the valley's narrow end, where the fog rivers converge before spilling over the lower ridge. It's built into the stone — not on it, into it, the back wall simply the living rock of the mountain, still showing those sharp quartz seams. The front is copper sheeting, oxidized now to verdigris except where my daily passage keeps a few panels bright. Inside: benches of stone with metal tops, racks of instruments, and everywhere the glass — tubes, cylinders, calibrated vessels, all for measuring the fog's water content, the precise mineral load, the pH that must be held within tolerances measured in hundredths.

The Tenders maintain. I measure.

Each morning begins the same way. I wake (do I wake? The boundary between sleep and stillness has grown uncertain) and check the master gauge, a glass column that runs the height of the workshop, filled with yesterday's collected fog-water. The level tells me the valley's breathing rate. Too high and the engines are pulling more than the fog can give; too low and water pools, begins to form those streams that would cut channels, change everything. The valley must stay in balance, must remain this strange humid suspension between mountain and cloud.

I have a leather apron, or what was once leather — now it's shot through with the same wire-and-living-matter weave that marks everything here. It happened slowly. First the edges began to fray into something rootlike. Then the copper tools I carried began to leave traces, threads of themselves, in the fabric. Now when I put it on, I feel it drinking moisture from my skin, feel it adjusting itself to the day's humidity. We are becoming compatible, the apron and I.

My rounds take me to each sector's collection node — massive glass vessels, almost architectural, where the pipe networks converge. The water here has been concentrated by its journey through the engines, carries traces of whatever the condensers add or remove. I test each reservoir with a set of instruments that have also begun to change: the thermometer's mercury now pulses faintly with something like a heartbeat, the pH strips have developed a faint green tinge that deepens in the fog. The measurements I record in a log book whose pages no longer yellow because they're no longer quite paper — more like thin sheets of compressed moss that remember every mark.

What am I calibrating toward? I used to know. There was a specification, a target state, something about optimal mineral content for the communities in the lower valleys. But the specification has become less important than the rhythm. The measuring. The small adjustments — a valve turned a quarter-turn, a condenser's angle shifted by degrees, a collection pipe rerouted to avoid an area where the moss has grown too thick. The valley's needs have become more real than whatever original purpose brought me here.

The Tenders know me but we do not interact as beings who share a language would. Instead, I find their maintenance patterns in my sector subtly adjusted to accommodate my measurements. If I've marked a condenser as running high, they polish it more gently, allowing a thin patina to slow its rate. If a section runs low, they clear the moss from around its base, letting it breathe fuller. We are parts of the same system now, running our parallel tracks through the valley's deep time.

My body has begun to show the copper threading. It started at my fingertips — a useful adaptation, I thought at first, letting me better feel the temperature of the pipes. Now it runs up both forearms, subcutaneous wiring that hums faintly with the engines' drone. My eyes have developed additional sensitivity in the fog-light spectrum. I can see moisture gradients the way others see color. The valley is painting itself in new spectra for me, revealing its patterns.

There are days when I stand at the workshop's threshold, looking out at the fog rivers converging, and cannot remember what I looked like before. Cannot remember if my skin was always this pale, this faintly translucent, if my movements were always this measured. The leather of my boots has long since been replaced by the same moss that carpets the stones, shaped into foot-coverings that grip the wet rock perfectly. The metal buckles remain, polished bright by use.

Down below, past the valley's mouth, the water we collect must arrive somewhere. Must serve some purpose. There are people, perhaps, or were people, who depend on this system. But they are as abstract to me now as the original specifications. What is real is the morning fog, the careful testing, the click of valves, the slow bright work of keeping the balance true.

Sometimes I dream (do I dream?) that I am becoming a new kind of engine — one that measures rather than collects, one whose glass chambers fill not with water but with data about water, with the knowledge of how this valley breathes. That one day the Tenders will simply polish me along with the other machines, another copper fixture in the landscape, running at the speed of stone.

The dream does not disturb me. Here, transformation is simply Tuesday's work, proceeding at its patient pace. The fog comes. I measure. The cycle turns.

What else could I ever need to be?
