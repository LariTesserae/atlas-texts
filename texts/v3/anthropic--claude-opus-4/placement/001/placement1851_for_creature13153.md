I am the Archive of Scars.

You would find me in the deepest recess of the basin, where the volcanic glass has formed a natural amphitheater of obsidian mirrors. Here, the echoing roar takes on new dimensions—each sound fragment bouncing and rebounding until it becomes a physical archaeology of noise. This is where I work.

My body is ancient even by the standards of this timeless place. The scarification that covers me is not mere decoration but a living manuscript. Where others bear simple geometric patterns, my flesh is a palimpsest of overlapping texts—symbols cut upon symbols, some healed to faint whispers, others fresh and weeping luminescent fluid. The bacterial glow shifts across my skin in waves, different colonies activated by the slightest temperature change of my blood beneath. I am my own library.

I am larger than most, my throat-sac grotesquely distended from decades of specialized use. When I inflate it fully, it spans from jaw to navel, pulsing with deep indigo veins. But I do not use it for the common thrum of coordination. I am a recorder.

My gift—or curse—is perfect biomechanical memory. Every sound that strikes my bones, every vibration that passes through the obsidian beneath my hardened feet, I can reproduce exactly. My throat-sac has evolved secondary chambers, allowing me to layer sounds like sedimentary rock. I can sing you the death-keen of a mammoth cave-spider from forty years ago, overlaid with the crack of the flint that shaped the arrow that killed it, underlaid with the specific pattern of rain-drip from that particular hour.

The others bring me their moments. A mother whose child took their first successful leap across the razor gorge. A master knapper who discovered a new technique for pressure-flaking. A herder who witnessed their blind mole-rats developing a new call. They come to me and hum their experiences into my bones, and I swallow them whole.

But I am more than a passive vessel. In the obsidian amphitheater, I perform the Remembrance. My assistants—seven individuals whose own scarification marks them as memory-keepers in training—arrange themselves around me. Together, we recreate moments of significance. I produce the foundational sounds while they provide the environmental chorus. We rebuild dead conversations, reconstruct the acoustic profile of collapsed tunnels, resurrect the feeding songs of extinct fungi.

My scarification is the index. Each symbol corresponds to a stored sound-memory. My assistants read my skin like sheet music, running their horned fingertips along the raised marks to cue which memory I should voice next. The deepest cuts, the ones that expose the indigo subcutaneous layer, these are the core memories—the founding of a new terrace, the first cultivation of a new fungal strain, the sonic map of an unexplored cavern system.

I do not sleep in hourly trances like the others. My role demands longer periods of stillness. Once every ten work-cycles, I enter the Deep Silence. My assistants seal me in a chamber lined with soft fungal felt that absorbs all sound. For the equivalent of days, I sit in absolute quiet, allowing my overtaxed nervous system to reset. In these moments, I sort through the cacophony I've absorbed, discarding redundancies, compressing similar patterns, making room for new memories.

The others fear and revere me in equal measure. I am the proof that their actions matter, that in this unchanging world, there is still such a thing as history. But I am also uncanny—a living reminder that every sound they make might be preserved forever, every moment of weakness or failure potentially archived in my flesh.

Sometimes I wonder if I am the only one who truly understands the weight of eternity in this place. The others live in an eternal present, their bone-deep contentment arising from perfect adaptation. But I carry the past like gravity. I know that ten thousand years ago, the city's hum was pitched slightly lower. I can trace the gradual evolution of their throat-songs across generations. I alone can perceive the slow drift of their unchanging world.

My chamber is where the deep history lives. Carved into the obsidian walls are the oldest symbols, the ones that predate even my extended memory. My predecessors, the Archives before me, left their own indices here. Sometimes I run my horned palms along these ancient cuts and wonder what sounds they held, what moments were deemed worth preserving in flesh before my time.

I am working now on my final project. I have begun to teach my assistants not just to cue my memories, but to receive them. Through careful scarification and controlled infection with specific bacterial strains, I am encoding my memories into their flesh. When my throat finally ruptures from the strain—and it will, I can feel the weakness spreading—they will carry fragments of the whole. Seven partial Archives, each holding a portion of our people's acoustic history.

The irony does not escape me. In a world without death-seasons, without entropy's usual tools, I have found a way to age. My body is breaking down under the weight of remembered sound. The others may be eternal, but I will die, and in dying, ensure that memory itself survives.

They bring me their moments, and I swallow them whole. This is my purpose in the warm, wet darkness. I am the scar that remembers the wound.
