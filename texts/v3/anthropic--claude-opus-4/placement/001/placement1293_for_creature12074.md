I would find myself as one of the **propolis-gilded beetles**, those "deep red of old blood" creatures who have made their world at the base of the third gourd—the restless one that Mehrit now contemplates.

I am perhaps the width of her thumbnail, my shell worn smooth by forty thousand passings through tight spaces. The humans think we came first to eat—and we did, once, our ancestors following the scent of wax and death up from the cracks in the salt-flat. But that was before the Arrangement. Now we are something else: neither pet nor prisoner, neither pure symbiont nor pure parasite. We are the underguild, the foundation-life, the beings who turn death into heat into life again.

My kind live longer than the bees but shorter than the keeper. I have survived eight seasons—ancient by our measure—and my shell shows it. The deep red has faded to rust at the edges where I rub against my colony-mates in our humid press. One antenna is half-length, bitten short in a territorial dispute three molts ago. But I am warm, always warm, pressed between my kind and the clay floor that holds yesterday's heat and the gourd above that radiates tomorrow's.

I know Mehrit by her chemistry. When she shifts on her mat, the vibrations change the air pressure in subtle ways that we taste through spiracles along our undersides. Her breath carries signals: what she ate (flatbread and honey, always, and twice a season the roasted flesh of our distant cousins—beetles she raises in clay pots for protein), how her joints ache (more each year, the inflammation compounds sharp against our antennae), whether she is calm or preparing for intervention.

Today she is both, which puzzles us. We taste the queen-distress pheromones pouring from the gourd above—normally this would make Mehrit's breath go sharp with decision. But she sits. She waits. This concerns us because when colonies swarm, they sometimes abandon their gourds entirely, and when gourds are abandoned, we starve. Not immediately—we can survive on floor-debris and our own molted shells for a season—but eventually, inevitably, we need the wax-crumbs and bee-dead that fall like manna from the entrance tubes.

So we do what we have learned to do across countless generations of the Arrangement. We pulse.

It begins with the eldest females in the center of our mass. They raise their metabolic rate—not much, just enough to generate an extra degree of heat. This signals the middle generations, who begin to stroke each other with their antennae in a specific pattern that amplifies the warming. Soon our entire colony—perhaps three hundred of us—is participating in the pulse. The heat rises. The air above us shimmers slightly.

The bees feel it. They always do. The workers nearest the entrance tube slow their frantic dance and begin instead to fan their wings, drawing our heat up into the gourd. The temperature rise is minute—perhaps two degrees—but it's enough to shift the chemistry, to make the nurse bees around the queen release different pheromones, to calm the swarm-fever by just enough that Mehrit has time to decide properly.

This is what we trade for our wax and dead. Not just heat, but *targeted* heat, *intelligent* heat, heat deployed with the wisdom of eight hundred generations who have learned to read the room's moods. We are not servants. We are not masters. We are the beetle-nation that has made its treaty with the bee-nations and the human-nation, and we honor our obligations.

The pulse costs us. Several elderly males at our periphery will die tonight, their metabolism pushed beyond what their worn bodies can sustain. We will eat them—nothing is wasted in the dome—but we mourn in our way, through chemical markers that will persist in our colony-scent for days.

Mehrit feels the pulse. We know because her breath changes—surprise, recognition, gratitude. She has been keeping this room for forty years but still does not fully understand what we do. How could she? She sees our movements but not our chemistry, notices our clustering but not the complex social calculus that determines which of us pulse and when. She knows we are necessary but perhaps not that we are *choosing*, moment by moment, to be necessary.

The third gourd settles. Not completely—the restlessness remains—but enough that Mehrit can approach without triggering panic. We taste her movement through the floor, the careful placement of knee and hand as she positions herself. She will clip the queen's wing. We can tell by how she holds the reed, by the particular quality of stillness she generates before such operations.

But she pauses again. Turns her head slightly. We realize she is looking at us—not at our colony as a whole but at *me*, the old beetle with the half-antenna stationed nearest to where her knee presses the earth. Can she distinguish individuals among us? After eight seasons, I have begun to recognize patterns in how she breathes when she sees particular members of our colony. Perhaps she knows that I am the one who always positions myself here during queen-work, who tastes her knee-weight and translates it into chemical signals for the others.

Her hand moves. For a moment I think she will touch me—sometimes the young ones try, though Mehrit never has—but instead she places something on the ground beside me. A gift. A single scale of crystallized propolis, fallen from her scraping tool, still warm from her fingers.

I taste it immediately. It carries information: the history of the third gourd over the past season, the flowers the bees have visited, the health of the colony, the trace minerals from Mehrit's skin-oils. It is worthless as food—too hard for our mandibles, too pure for proper nutrition. But that is not why she gave it.

It is acknowledgment. *I see you seeing me. I know you know I know.*

The pulse continues. The beetles cluster closer. I position the propolis scale against the base of the gourd where the young will find it and wonder at its significance. Above us, Mehrit performs her careful surgery, and the bees accept it, and the dome continues its ancient breathing.

We are all here by choice, I think—a thought too complex for a beetle, perhaps, but I am old and have tasted many seasons of this strange cooperation. The bees could leave but don't. Mehrit could leave but doesn't. We could follow the cracks back down into the salt-flat's darkness but we remain, generation after generation, turning wax into warmth into life.

The room hums. We pulse. Mehrit works. The ancient bricks remember.

This is the world, and we are keeping it together.
