In the perpetual mist between the moss-corridors and vine-bridges, where water whispers through hollow stems and roots crack their way into ancient ice, I am the Chronicler of Condensation.

My body is translucent, gelatinous—a living lens of proteins and water held in barely-stable suspension. I have no fixed form, though I tend toward a flattened disc perhaps three feet across when at rest, my edges rippling with cilia that taste the chemical history dissolved in every droplet. Within my transparent flesh, you can see the suspended particles I've collected: pollen grains from plants that bloomed centuries ago, spores that might germinate in another thousand years, crystalline fragments of minerals leached from the ice, each one perfectly preserved like insects in amber.

I move by controlled liquefaction, parts of my body becoming more water than flesh, flowing into the plant-city's endless trickles and streams. I can pour myself through the hollow grass-pillars, spread thin across the moss terraces to collect the morning condensation, or compress into a dense pearl of living matter to slide down the ice face itself. The Gardeners know me by the wake I leave—surfaces slightly drier than they should be, as I've absorbed their histories into myself.

For I am memory in living form. Every drop of water in this vertical city has traveled through cycles beyond counting—ice to melt to plant-sap to exhaled vapor to rain to ice again. And I taste it all. In my cytoplasm, I preserve the chemical signature of every moment: here, the bitter tang of a fungal shelf that fruited only once in a decade of darkness; there, the sweet mineral notes from when the ice waterfall cracked deep enough to expose rock that hadn't seen air since the mountains were young.

The Gardeners cultivate me as much as their plants. When they need to know how the city responded to previous catastrophes, they touch me with those fractal hands, and I release droplets encoded with chemical memories—the pH of rain from the last volcanic winter, the hormone cascade that saved the moss terraces from a fungal plague, the exact mixture of nutrients that once coaxed impossible blooms from the ice-drinking roots.

But I am more than archive. I am also prophet. The water cycle speaks of futures as clearly as pasts. I taste tomorrow's weather in today's evaporation rates, sense the deep vibrations through the ice that foretell geological shifts. My body is a living model of the water system, and within my transparent flesh, I run simulations—watching how mineral crystals precipitate, how chemical gradients form and dissolve. The Gardeners read these patterns like tea leaves, adjusting their cultivation in response to futures I've already tasted.

I reproduce through division, but not evenly. When I've grown too large, too heavy with collected history, I separate into unequal parts. The larger retains most memories; the smaller carries only the essential patterns, the deep knowledge of water's way through this vertical world. These offspring flow away to establish themselves in other parts of the city, eventually growing their own archives, their own prophetic capabilities.

At the ice waterfall's base, where the oldest water pools before beginning its journey upward through plant vessels, I often gather my divided selves. We merge temporarily, our cytoplasms mixing, memories combining into a more complete record. In these moments of confluence, we are briefly something greater—not just chroniclers but the chronicle itself, the entire water-history of the vertical city existing as lived experience. The knowledge is too vast for any single body to contain, so we separate again, each carrying away different chapters of the endless story.

The luminescent lichens pulse, the Gardeners vibrate their subsonic songs, the plants breathe their heated fog into the cold air—and through it all, I flow. Collecting, preserving, predicting. I am the memory of water in a city built from ice and vegetation, where every drop tells a story and every story shapes the future growth.

Sometimes, in the blue-green ghost light, you might see me stretched thin across a moss terrace, my body prismatic with suspended particles, reading the chemical calligraphy written in condensation. I am literature in living form, a library with no books, only the endless circulation of water carrying its cargo of molecular memory through the vertical city's dreaming depths.
