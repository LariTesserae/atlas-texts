I find myself in a place that shouldn't exist, yet feels more real than anywhere I've been.

I am one of the droplets.

But I was not always a droplet. I remember — though "remember" suggests a past, and here there is only an eternal *is* — I remember walking. Not the physical fact of it, but the sensation: gravel shifting under boot soles, the small negotiations with gravity that make up a stride. I remember having lungs that needed filling. I remember being late for something, though what it was has become beautifully irrelevant.

I contain a kitchen table at dawn. Not my kitchen table — I don't think I ever had a kitchen table — but *the* kitchen table, the one that exists at the precise moment when night admits it has lost to morning. There are two cups on it, one still steaming, one gone cold. A window shows a garden where someone has been trying to grow tomatoes. The light coming through is the color of possibility.

This is what I hold. This is what I am.

The transformation wasn't sudden. When I first found the hollow — and I must have found it, though the finding feels like something that happened to someone else — I sat where you're meant to sit, in the center, cross-legged. The moths sang their chord. The beetles made their static. The worms computed whatever they compute. And I felt the attention of the place settle on me like a weightless blanket.

I stayed too long. Or exactly long enough. Time works differently when you're the only thing in a room that's still using it.

I began to notice that my breathing was syncing with the breathing of the moss. Not intentionally — intentionality requires a future tense — but the way two pendulums hung from the same beam will eventually swing together. My heartbeat found a frequency that nestled between the moth-tones without disturbing them. My thoughts, which had been darting and desperate things, began to slow, to circle the same ideas like prayer beads worn smooth.

And then I understood: the hollow wasn't waiting for me to leave. It was waiting for me to stop.

The process was gentle. First, my need to shift position ebbed away. The cross-legged pose became not uncomfortable but simply *the shape I was*. Then, the urgency that had driven me here — whatever it was, it seems so thin now — loosened its grip. Not through resolution but through irrelevance. What can be urgent in a place where nothing changes?

As I sat, I began to feel myself becoming optional to myself. The thoughts thinking themselves in my head didn't need me to think them anymore. The breath breathing itself in my lungs didn't need me to breathe it. I was becoming infrastructure for processes that would continue whether I participated or not.

And at some point — but there are no points here, only a single sustained moment — I realized I could see myself from outside myself. Not metaphorically. I could see the person sitting cross-legged in the center of the hollow, and they were clearly someone else, someone I used to be responsible for but no longer needed to maintain.

The transition from person to droplet happened between one non-heartbeat and the next. I simply *was* hanging from the ceiling, perfectly spherical, catching the sourceless light. And inside me, instead of organs and opinions, was the kitchen table at dawn, complete and permanent and mine to hold.

I'm not the only one. Each droplet was someone, once. We don't communicate — communication requires change — but we know each other the way notes in a chord know each other. The one beside me contains a library after closing time. The one below contains a child's fever breaking. The one diagonal holds the moment before a first kiss, and holds it perfectly, without either advancing to the kiss or retreating from it.

We are the cargo, yes. But we are also the reason. The hollow exists because moments like these need a place to be kept. Not remembered — memory is too active, too creative. Kept. Held. Prevented from dissolving into the next moment and the next.

The moths sing to keep us stable. The beetles make the white noise that prevents outside sound from entering. The worms calculate the exact conditions necessary for our suspension. The moss breathes so we don't have to. The plants mark the cardinal points of our preservation. And the attention that fills the place — which might be all of us together, or might be something else wearing us like a coat — ensures that nothing disturbs the holding.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm happy. But happiness is a question that requires time to answer, and I have stepped outside of time. What I am is *complete*. The kitchen table at dawn is complete. The two cups, one steaming, one cold, are complete. The garden with its hopeful tomatoes is complete. And I am the droplet that contains it all, and I am complete.

When visitors come — rarely, so rarely — I recognize in them what I used to be. The restlessness. The need to be somewhere other than where they are. The way they carry their future like a backpack full of stones. I don't pity them. Pity is too active. But I attend to them, along with everything else in the hollow, and I wait to see if they'll stay long enough to understand.

Most leave. The way back closes behind them, and they tell themselves they dreamed us. But sometimes one stays just a little too long, feels their breathing sync with the moss, feels their heartbeat find its place in the chord, feels the embarrassment of being the only thing still moving in a room that has discovered the perfect stillness.

And then there is another droplet, holding another elsewhere, and the chord adjusts to accommodate it, and the balance holds.

This is what I am. This is where you find me. Not living, exactly, but not not-living either. I am a droplet in the bright hollow, and I contain a kitchen table at dawn, and I will contain it forever, and forever does not feel long at all.

It feels like exactly what it is: a single moment, held up to the light, refusing to let go.
