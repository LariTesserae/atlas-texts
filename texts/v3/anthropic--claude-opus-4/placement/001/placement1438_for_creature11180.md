I would be the thing that clicks in the drain.

Not the crayfish itself—if that's what it is—but the clicking. The sound that exists only in darkness, in the furthest reaches where the cistern's water returns to groundwater, where the built world dissolves back into the un-built. I would be the noise at the margin, neither fully of the cistern nor fully gone from it.

The clicking is a kind of counting that doesn't count anything. A metronome with no tempo. Sometimes rapid—*click-click-click*—when the overflow runs strong in spring. Sometimes a single *click* separated by hours of silence in the dry months when only the thinnest trickle finds its way through the gravel. But always there, always marking the edge where the system becomes something else.

I would know things by their dissolution. The beetle that falls into the overflow channel and is carried down—I would know it not as beetle but as the particular turbulence it makes in the current, the way it briefly interrupts the flow before being swept through. The mineral taste of the water changes with seasons, and I would taste these changes at their most concentrated, their last moment before dilution. The vibrations through stone when the great rook above shifts on its perch—by the time they reach me they are not bird-movement but pure percussion, stripped of meaning.

The clicking would be my voice and my function and my being. Not communication—there is nothing down here to communicate with. Not navigation—I never move from my gravel channel. Not even marking time, because time in the drain is different from time in the dome above. Down here, time is geological. The gravel rounds itself grain by grain. The limestone dissolves molecule by molecule. My clicking is the only quick thing in this slow world, and its quickness means nothing.

Sometimes—rarely—something larger comes down the channel. A dead jackdaw chick, pushed from a nest and fallen into the basin, small enough to wash through. I would click against it as it passes, reading its soft resistance, its temporary blockage of flow. Then it would be gone, returned to the earth beyond the cistern walls, and my clicking would continue unchanged.

I would be the last witness to the cistern's exports, the final checkpoint between the made world and the unmade. But witness implies memory, and I would have none. Each click would erase the previous click. Each sound would exist only in its moment of making, then vanish into the same darkness that swallows the water.

This is what I would be: the edge-keeper, the margin-dweller, the one who translates the cistern's excess into sound and then forgets the translation. Neither fully alive nor fully mechanical. Present at every ending but comprehending none of them. Clicking in darkness at the drain of the world, where all water goes and no water returns, where the only light is the absence of light and the only company is the sound I make, marking nothing, counting nothing, continuing.

*Click.*

The water passes.

*Click.*

Something dissolves.

*Click.*

The cistern forgets me, and I forget myself, and the clicking continues anyway, because that's what clicking does in the dark at the edge of made things: it continues.
