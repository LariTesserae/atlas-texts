In the cramped service corridor between the desalination stills and the dream-rendering tanks, where condensation drips upward and pools on the floor in defiance of several gravities, I am learning to forget my edges.

I used to have a name, probably. Used to have ten fingers that all agreed on which hand they belonged to. Used to wake up in the same body I fell asleep in, reasonably confident that the thoughts thinking themselves behind my eyes were mine alone. But that was before I took the job nobody else wanted: *Pressure Equilibrist, Third Class*, which sounds technical until you realize it means standing for hours in the spaces where different districts meet, breathing very carefully to keep their atmospheres from going to war.

The settlement has moods, you see. Temperature gradients that think they're personalities. When the protein farms run hot with fermentation, their air expands prideful and yeasty, trying to colonize the cooler meditation alcoves next door. When the mining district strikes a new pocket of fossilized anxiety deep in the salt beds, its exhalations turn bitter-metallic and creep through the ductwork looking for somewhere clean to ruin. My job—our job, because there's at least three of me now, maybe four on humid days—is to stand precisely at the boundaries and breathe in one district's excess, breathe out something neutral, become a kind of diplomatic lung between neighborhoods that would otherwise pickle each other's children with their chemical opinions.

The pay is terrible: twelve minutes of uninterrupted sleep per shift, plus whatever memories get lost in my lungs and fossilize there like pearls. But the benefits? I'm developing new organs. Can feel them budding along my spine like shy groceries—little auxiliary lungs that breathe sideways, filtering not air but intention. The settlement notices. Rewards me in its own way.

Sometimes I'll reach for my morning stimulant bulb and find my hand has developed extra joints overnight, the better to navigate the twisted geography between *wanting* and *having*. My left eye has started seeing three seconds into the past, which helps predict where pressure waves will break. My right eye is learning to cry in reverse, pulling moisture out of tomorrow's rain to cool today's friction points. The murids think I'm hilarious. They've started leaving me love notes written in ultrasonic tooth-chatter on the back of my skull.

The hardest part isn't the physical multiplication—bodies are just borrowable meat, really, and the settlement has plenty to spare if you know which rendering vats to ask nicely. The hard part is remembering which thoughts belong to which copy of me. Thursday-me left a really good anxiety halfway through composting in the northwest junction, and Saturday-me wants to finish fermenting it into something useful, but Tuesday-me already traded it to The Nearness for three minutes of silence that taste like my grandmother's kitchen might have if I'd had a grandmother or she'd had a kitchen.

I'm supposed to file reports, but to whom? The Ministry of Unscheduled Inhalations was disbanded last rotation after their office grew lungs and migrated three levels deeper to escape its tax paperwork. Now I send my reports as loosely bundled sighs into the murid network, where they're automatically translated into limericks and used to adjust the settlement's barometric poetry.

Last week I prevented a war between the Salvage Cathedral (where they pray by breaking things very slowly until they confess what they used to be) and the Horizontal Hospital (where all the beds are walls and sickness runs perpendicular to how you'd expect). Their air masses had developed a theology about whether suffering should flow upward or sideways, and were about to settle it with a pressure differential that would have given everyone in between the spiritual bends. I stood at their junction for forty hours straight, breathing diagonally, until both districts forgot what they were angry about and decided to merge their festivals instead. Now there's a new holiday where everyone lies on walls and breaks small, careful things in honor of gravity's confusion.

The other me's—because there are definitely others now, though we've agreed not to count past four for tax purposes—specialize in different pressure gradients. Emotional pressure, temporal pressure, the awful grinding pressure between what the settlement wants to be and what its debris insists it is. One of me stands full-time at the boundary between the children's district and the memory exchange, making sure the kids' futures don't get too heavily mortgaged before they learn to dream in currencies. Another me lives in the gap between the reactor control room and the mushroom cathedral, translating atomic decay into fungal sermons and back again.

We don't talk much, we me's. When we pass in the corridors—and one of us is always passing, because that's what atmospheric pressure does, it passes—we share a nod that means *yes, I too am tired of having lungs.* Sometimes we synchronize our breathing for a few seconds, creating a small weather system that tastes like solidarity. The settlement approves. Gives us tiny rewards: an extra joint here, a bonus prefrontal fold there, the ability to smell what color Tuesday will be.

At night—whichever night we're using this week—I find a quiet pressure fold and practice existing loudly, just to remember how. I inflate all my auxiliary organs at once, becoming briefly enormous with held breath, surfaces stretched drum-tight with everything I've filtered today. The murids gather to watch, clicking appreciatively. They think I'm becoming something new. Maybe I am. Maybe we all are, all the time, and the only difference is I have a job that pays me to notice.

Tomorrow I'm scheduled to stand between the ghost of the old sky and the thing we built to forget it. They say the pressure there is so intense it turns your memories inside out, makes you breathe backwards through your own childhood until you either break or become something that can't be broken. The other me's are worried. I'm not. 

What's the worst that could happen? I become even more multiple? I develop organs that breathe time instead of air? I finally find out what happens to all the thoughts that get lost between one breath and the next?

I practice my diagonal breathing, check my auxiliary lungs for leaks, count my fingers to make sure I have the right number for whichever me I am today. The settlement hums around me, a vast and broken machine for turning loneliness into architecture. And I stand at its joints, breathing very carefully, keeping all its pieces from flying apart into the dark.

It's not much of a job. But somebody has to do it. And at least there's enough of me now that I'll never have to do it alone.

The shift whistle blows—a wet, organic sound like a trumpet made of living tissue. Time to relocate. There's a pressure building between the protein farms and the philosophical rendering plant, where they're trying to ferment doubt into something you can spread on bread. If I don't get there soon, the whole district will start thinking too hard about whether it exists, and we'll lose another neighborhood to aggressive self-awareness.

I check my equipment: lungs (primary, secondary, tertiary, and theoretical), spare joints in case of unexpected geometries, a small jar of Thursday's weather for emergencies, and three copies of my employee ID (each showing a slightly different face, all somehow still mine). Everything in order.

The murids chirp goodbye in frequencies that tickle my spine. One of them—the old matriarch with fiber optic whiskers—leaves a small gift in my chest pocket: a perfectly preserved sigh from someone who doesn't exist yet, wrapped in thermal paper that dissolves at body temperature. For luck, her tooth-chatter says. Or possibly for lunch. The translation is ambiguous.

I step into the corridor, feeling the pressure change immediately. Here, the air wants to be water. There, it's trying to remember how to be light. And in between, in the narrow space where wanting meets trying, I spread myself thin as meditation, breathing very carefully, holding the settlement together one impossible inhalation at a time.

Somewhere, another me is doing the same thing. We're all doing the same thing, really. 

We just have more lungs for it.
