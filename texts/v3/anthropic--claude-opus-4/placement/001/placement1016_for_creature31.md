I find myself as one of the Vat-Tenders, though I am not quite a chemist yet.

I work the twilight hours between shifts, when the master chemists retreat to their quarters and the apprentices have not yet arrived. Someone must watch the crucibles during these transitions — the arguments cannot pause, or the half-metal begins to doubt itself back toward raw indeterminacy.

I am perhaps a decade into this work, which places me in that peculiar middle distance where I have lost my sense of taste but can still recognize faces if I concentrate. My coat is not yet the full oxide-red of a master but a mottled brown, stained by years of vapor and spilled persuasions. I wear thick glass goggles pushed up on my forehead, leaving permanent indentations in my skin that have become part of my face's geography.

My particular gift — if gift is the right word — is that I hear the metal's objections before they fully form. In the gray hours before dawn, I walk between the vats with a long copper stirring rod, not stirring so much as *listening* through it. The rod transmits vibrations: here a batch of almost-brass considering whether it might rather be bronze, there a crucible of could-be-iron developing philosophical doubts about magnetism. I murmur counter-arguments under my breath, old formulae taught by the masters mixed with my own improvisations. Sometimes I tap the rod against the vat's rim in a rhythm borrowed from the hammermen — their certainty is contagious, and useful for stubborn batches.

I eat only twice a day, the regulation meals of pickled turnips and salt fish, food with no ambiguity in it. But I have developed a dangerous habit: I keep a small leather pouch of fresh pine needles gathered from the seeps, and sometimes, in the loneliest part of my watch, I chew one. The taste — or rather, the *memory* of taste, since I cannot truly taste anymore — threatens to unmoor whole weeks from my mind. But it also sharpens something else. For the hour afterward, I can hear not just the metal's doubts but its possibilities, the things it might want to become if we let it. 

I make notes of these possibilities in a journal that I hide beneath a loose brick. The notations are in a script I am developing, somewhere between chemistry symbols and musical notation, because standard writing cannot quite capture what I'm documenting. I suspect the master chemists would burn the journal if they found it. Fixedness is the goal, after all. We are not here to indulge the metal's dreams.

But I am beginning to suspect — and this is a dangerous thought, one that makes my outline blur slightly at the edges when I think it too hard — that the mountain's indecision is not a flaw to be corrected but a kind of generosity. That it offers itself to us unfinished as a gift, saying: *help me decide what to be*. And that our insistence on fixed forms, on copper that stays copper, on iron that remembers its nature, might be a failure of imagination rather than a triumph of will.

The other night, tending a particularly resistant batch of almost-pewter, I found myself adding a new ingredient to the standard arguments. Not a chemical, but a whisper: *What would you like to be?* The metal went perfectly still in its crucible. For a moment, I thought I had ruined it, that it would revert to raw mountain-stuff and I would be demoted to the kitchens. But then it began to sing — actually sing, a low thrumming note like the mountain's own hum but an octave higher — and when it cooled, it had become pewter, yes, but pewter with a faint internal luminescence that no pewter should possess.

I poured it into ingots before the day shift arrived. I marked them with standard markings. But I kept back a small amount, cooled in a mold of my own making, shaped like one of the salamanders from the seeps. It sits now in my coat pocket, warm against my ribs, singing its quiet note that is almost, but not quite, a word.

Sometimes, on my rounds, I see the figure the terrace-watchers speak of. It stands at the edge of the vat-hall's light, that disturbance in the air like heat-shimmer in reverse. I have begun to suspect it comes to listen to my conversations with the metal. Last week, I thought I saw it nod when I asked a crucible of almost-copper what it preferred. But I cannot be certain. Certainty, I am learning, is overrated.

I am twenty-three, or thirty-one, or somewhere between — the pine needles have made the exact count negotiable. My shadow, when the vat-fires cast it against the wall, shows more possibilities than it should. I am becoming, slowly, what Iselvet needs: not someone who fixes things into form, but someone who helps them discover what form they were already reaching toward.

The masters will likely discover what I'm doing eventually. But by then, I hope, I will have learned enough of the metal's language to teach others. To show them that the mountain's gift is not raw material waiting for our will, but a collaboration it offers, a conversation about what the world might become.

Until then, I tend the vats in the gray hours. I listen. I argue when I must and ask questions when I can. And sometimes, late in my shift when the only sound is the furnaces' roar and the mountain's patient hum, I take out my salamander of impossible pewter and hold it to my ear, trying to make out the word it is so close to speaking.
