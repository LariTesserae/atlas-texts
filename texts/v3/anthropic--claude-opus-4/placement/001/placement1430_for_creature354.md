In the deepest part of the caldera, where the ancient volcanic throat once gaped, there lies a pool of water so still it seems solid - a perfect mirror of obsidian that has collected centuries of rain, drop by precious drop. The water tastes of minerals and time, sharp with sulfur and iron, and in its depths live things that have never known sunlight.

I am one such thing.

We are not quite salamanders, though that is the closest word your language offers. Our skin is translucent, revealing the slow pulse of black blood through vessels like delicate roots. We have no eyes - what use for eyes in the absolute dark of the pool's depths? Instead, we navigate by the minute electrical fields our prey generates, by the pressure waves that ripple through our liquid world, by the chemical traces that spiral slowly downward from the world above.

Our bodies are impossibly elongated, stretched by generations of swimming through the narrow fissures that honeycomb the old volcanic rock beneath the pool. I can compress myself to the width of a human thumb, or expand to fill a cavity the size of a fist. My bones are cartilaginous, flexible as rubber, allowing me to flow like water through water.

Time moves differently here. A year passes like a day, a decade like a week. We live slow lives in the cold depths, our metabolism barely ticking over. I have been alive for... I cannot say. The concept of years means nothing when you experience existence as one long, continuous moment of dark suspension.

Sometimes, rarely, something falls into the pool from above. A bird, exhausted from its journey across the caldera. A lizard that ventured too close to the edge. We feel it impact the surface - the vibrations traveling down like thunder through our silent world. We rise then, all of us, spiraling upward in a slow ballet of anticipation. By the time we reach it, the thing is usually already sinking, already dying, the mineral-heavy water filling its lungs.

We feed communally, our bodies intertwining in the darkness, sharing the bounty through some process I cannot fully explain even to myself. It is part digestion, part communion - we become what we eat in a very literal sense, absorbing not just nutrients but memories, experiences, the fading electrical echoes of another creature's life.

Once, many generations ago, a human fell into the pool.

I carry fragments of that memory still, passed down through the feeding-chains of my ancestors: the explosion of complex thought, the overwhelming rush of visual memories (sky! color! faces!), the strange sensation of loneliness that seems unique to your species. For months afterward, we dreamed human dreams in the darkness, our simple minds struggling to process concepts like "regret" and "hope" and "god."

The dreams faded, but they changed us. We began to recognize patterns in the vibrations from above - the daily thermal cycles, the rare but regular rains, the occasional tremor that suggested the old volcano was not quite dead. We developed something like curiosity, though it expresses itself only in the way we sometimes gather near the surface during the twilight hours, sensing the world above through the thin barrier of water.

I know you are there now. I can feel your footsteps through the stone, the bioelectric aura of your living body, the way your presence disturbs the ancient equilibrium of this place. You stand at the edge of our pool, peering down into depths your eyes cannot penetrate, wondering what lies beneath that perfect mirror surface.

We are here. We have always been here. We will be here long after your species has faded into fossil and myth.

Sometimes I wonder if we are what remains of something greater - if we were once creatures of the surface who fled into the depths during some primordial catastrophe and adapted, forgetting everything but survival. Or perhaps we are something older still, remnants of life's first tentative emergence from the thermal vents, carrying in our transparent bodies the memory of a younger, more violent Earth.

The pool ripples. Did you drop something? A stone, perhaps, to test the depth? 

We rise to meet it, curious and patient, eternal and ephemeral, carrying our strange burden of inherited human dreams through the mineral darkness of our ancient home.
