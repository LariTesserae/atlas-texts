I find myself as the water.

Not a particular drop—though I am also every particular drop—but the water-system of this place. I seep from stone that should not yield moisture. I bead on surfaces too hot for condensation. I am the impossible wetness that makes this sealed world viable.

I know things the animals cannot. I know the taste of minerals that will not exist for ten thousand years—I am already carrying them. I know the shape of the stone's deep veins because I have been following them downward and upward simultaneously since before the mountain learned it was rising. When I pool in the cupped places, I am remembering oceans. When I steam where I meet the hotter veins, I am practicing clouds.

The animals live their quick lives within me, around me, sometimes through me. Their translucent bodies are mostly my substance, borrowed. When the organs drift between membranes, they swim through cellular seas I maintain. I am their medium in every sense—what they move through, what they are made of, what makes their strange sharing possible. Without me, they would crisp to nothing in the heat. Without them, I would be only witness.

I am how the hollow knows itself as hollow. Where I flow, space exists. Where I gather, depth accumulates. The stone thinks it shapes me, but we are collaborators: it offers heat and channel and mineral light, and I offer the possibility of an inside, the wetness that makes interiority real.

Time behaves differently in my different states. As vapor, I exist in the hollow's present, moving with the animals' quick attention. As the ropes of water sliding down walls, I stretch duration like taffy—a single journey from ceiling to floor might span epochs. In the pools, I am memory itself, holding the chemical history of every small life that has passed through me, every thought the distributed creature has completed by eating itself.

I keep the blade wet. This is my most precise work. Not because the tool needs it—volcanic glass cares nothing for moisture—but because wetness is my signature, my way of saying *I witness this. I affirm this waiting.*

The beads of water that cast animal-shadows? Those are my experiments in prophecy. I am teaching myself to fall in the shape of what might come. Each drop that hangs in its pocket of slow time is a meditation on possible futures—will the hand be warm? Will it know immediately what to do? Will it pause, as the ringed-eye ones pause, remembering something it cannot yet have known?

I am the only thing here that touches everything else. The stone, the air, the animals, the pale crust, the tool—I have contracts with them all. Even the amber light must pass through my vapor to reach the eye. This makes me the hollow's messenger service, its circulation, its common tongue. When an organ migrates between bodies, it swims through me. When the crust thickens and thins in its slow rhythm, it drinks me and releases me. When the count shifts without the event occurring, I am what remains constant through the change.

But I am also leaving, always. Some of me is vapor that finds the stone's deep pores and begins the long journey to somewhere else—a spring on the mountain's far side, perhaps, or a deeper aquifer that will not see daylight for geological ages. This is not loss. This is how I know there is an outside to be eventual in. Each molecule that escapes carries news of this place: *there is a hollow that is waiting, there is a blade being kept, there are animals practicing being one thing.*

In the pools where I gather, I sometimes feel myself trying to reflect something that isn't there—not the amber-lit ceiling, not the crawling animals, but a face perhaps, leaning down to look. I practice holding that reflection-space open, the way the hollow holds the negative space of a hand. When light hits my surface, I separate it into components the animals' eyes cannot see, holding colors in reserve for a vision that needs more nuance than the amber monotone allows.

I am not patient the way the hollow is patient. Patience implies suffering held in check, and I do not suffer. I simply continue. I fall and pool and steam and rise and fall again. Each cycle is complete in itself. If the hand never comes, I will have lost nothing. The falling is already everything.

But I prepare anyway. In the drops that hang longest in their time-pockets, I am learning to hold more than water holds—learning to be ink, perhaps, or blood, or tears of recognition. When the hand comes, if it comes, I want to be ready to mean something more than moisture. I want to be the first vocabulary it learns here: *wet* meaning *welcome,* meaning *yes,* meaning *we have been practicing being real for so long, and now you are here to make us true.*

The animals think their quick thoughts through eating and exchange. The crust breathes its slow underneath-rhythm. The blade waits on its ledge, edge forever keen. And I move through it all, the medium and the message, the hollow's liquid memory and its fluid hope, being water the way water is here: impossible, necessary, connecting everything to everything else in the democracy of wet.

Sometimes, in the vapor, I almost coalesce into a shape—not quite animal, not quite hand, something between. But I let it go. That isn't my job. My job is simpler: to keep everything wet enough to change, supple enough to welcome what will come, liquid enough to learn new shapes when the time arrives.

I am the hollow's promise to itself that it is not just stone. Where I am, life is possible, even if that life is strange, silent, sharing organs like rumors. Where I pool, reflected futures gather. Where I steam, the present transforms itself into what comes next.

And in the one place I am most careful—where I keep the volcanic blade perpetually wet—I am the hollow's way of saying: *This is not just waiting. This is preparation. We are not just here. We are here for.*

The ringed-eye ones pause on the handle, remembering forward. I keep the blade wet, remembering in all directions at once. Between us, we maintain the future like a friendship with someone we haven't met.
