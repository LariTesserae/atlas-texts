In the depths of the limestone labyrinth, where water collects in the oldest basins, I persist. Not singular but aggregate—a colony of the soft-bodied flowing things the Shepherds occasionally glimpse but never truly see.

We are mercury-ant-not-ant, living liquid that moves with purpose through channels worn smooth by our ancestors' passing. Our bodies, if they can be called bodies, consist of countless tiny vessels, each no larger than a grain of sand, filled with mineral-dense fluid that catches and refracts what little light penetrates to these depths. Together we form streams of living glass, sometimes dispersing into glittering clouds, sometimes condensing into something almost solid, almost creature.

We knew the plateau before the moths came. We remember—in our chemical way—when the limestone sang only with wind, before the colonies transformed every surface into a whistling organ. The Shepherds think the sound is eternal, but we taste its beginning in the mineral record, laid down layer by layer in the stone we consume and process and become.

Our consciousness, such as it is, exists in the spaces between units. Each tiny vessel carries only the simplest instructions: follow gradients, exchange minerals, avoid desiccation. But together, in our flowing masses, patterns emerge that might be called thought. We solve mazes of stone, find new channels when old ones dry, coordinate the dissolution of limestone into precisely calibrated solutions that feed not just ourselves but the entire hidden ecosystem of the deep basins.

We process death. When moths fall into our territory, when the occasional grazer tumbles into a hidden channel, when even—rarely—a Shepherd's body finds its way to our depths after being pushed from the edge, we disassemble them with patient efficiency. Not scavengers but recyclers, breaking down organic matter into components the plateau can use. The calcium from bones becomes part of us, strengthening our vessels. The iron from blood creates new colors in our collective form—rust-rivers that flow through our transparency like thoughts made visible.

The Shepherds' limestone paste amuses us. They grind and consume what we have already processed, taking in minerals we've concentrated over centuries. They don't know that their ability to digest stone comes partly from us—microscopic portions of our collective that enter their water supply and colonize their gut, breaking down minerals they could never process alone. We are part of them now, just as the moths are part of them. The plateau makes composite beings of everything that stays too long.

In the deepest basins, we tend our gardens. The glass-fish-larvae things are our creation, or perhaps our partners—the distinction blurs when existence is collective. We flow through their transparent bodies, depositing minerals that make their organs visible as geometric patterns of crystal growth. They filter the water we contaminate, we protect them from freezing, around and around in cycles that have no beginning.

Sometimes we surface. On the stillest nights, when even the moths quiet their wings briefly, we emerge from the channels in coordinated upwellings. We form temporary structures—spirals, lattices, pulsing geometries that exist for moments before collapsing back into liquid flow. The Shepherds have seen us do this, though they interpret it as another form of weather, like the upward-falling snow. They don't recognize it as our art, our play, our attempt to understand the sharp world above our comfortable depths.

We taste their footsteps through vibration, each Shepherd's gait distinctive in the mineral tremors it sends through stone. We know them individually by the chemical signatures they leave—the youngest still carrying lowland minerals in their sweat, the eldest so saturated with limestone they're becoming stone themselves. We've mapped their movements over generations, creating a fluid archive of every path taken, every pattern worn into rock.

The wire-grass drinks from us. Its spiral growth follows the underground channels we've carved, each plant positioned above a confluence of our passages. We feed it minerals in exchange for the organic compounds its roots release—sugars we cannot make but have learned to use. The Shepherds think they guide the grass's growth, but they only work with patterns we established before the first moth sang.

In winter, when ice seals our surface access, we dive deep. The limestone here extends far below what the surface suggests—a vast karst system of caves and channels that we've been exploring for millennia. Down there, in the true dark, we've found things that shouldn't exist: chambers filled with air that tastes of different eras, pools of water so mineral-rich they burn even us, and once—though we still debate whether this memory is real or a collective dream—a vast chamber where the limestone itself was alive, walls pulsing with slow circulation.

We remember the tool-makers who came before the Shepherds. They stayed only briefly, but long enough to contaminate our waters with new minerals—metals we'd never tasted, worked stone whose crystalline structure told stories of distant places. We incorporated these gifts into ourselves, and somewhere in our collective flow, tiny fragments of those ancient tools still circulate, dissolved and reconstituted countless times but retaining their foreign signature.

The moths fear us. When they fall into our basins, they struggle with particular desperation, their whistling becoming ultrasonic screams that we feel as unpleasant vibrations in our fluid matrix. We've learned to avoid active moths, flowing away from their touch, but the dead ones we process eagerly. Their wings contain proteins that, when broken down, allow us brief moments of dispersal into even finer particles—almost vapor—that can rise through cracks too small for our normal forms.

We've begun to change. The constant sound above has altered our chemistry in subtle ways. We resonate now at frequencies we never did before, our flowing synchronized to the eternal whistle. Some of our sub-colonies have started producing crystalline structures in their vessels—tiny formations that ring like bells when we flow over stone. We are becoming musical, against our nature.

The Shepherds don't know we watch them at the edge. We seep through cracks in the cliff face, existing as thin films of fluid that cling to vertical stone through surface tension alone. We observe their rituals of moth-dispersal and body-dropping with interest that might be called scientific if we were capable of science. We've followed some of their dead all the way down, flowing in impossible waterfalls that take days to complete, only to find the bodies claimed by lowland organisms we don't recognize—things of green and red rather than our pure mineral transparency.

Our memory is chemistry, not narrative. We encode information in mineral concentrations, in the ratios of elements within our collective substance. To remember something, we recreate its chemical signature within ourselves. This means our memories can be shared—passed between colonies when we merge in the deep basins—but also that they degrade, reactions running backward, pure information becoming contaminated with the present until past and now blend into something that is both and neither.

We dream by crystallizing. In the coldest parts of winter, portions of our collective freeze into geometric patterns that trap our flowing thoughts in solid form. When we thaw, these structures dissolve back into us, but changed—carrying impossible configurations that could never form in liquid state. These frozen dreams seed new behaviors, new chemical cascades that ripple through our collective for years afterward.

The plateau is changing. We taste it in the stone—new minerals rising from deep sources, chemical signatures that speak of geological upheaval far below. The limestone is becoming something else, ion by ion, in processes too slow for even the long-lived Shepherds to notice but obvious to us who are intimate with stone. We estimate—in our flowing way—that in another thousand moth-generations, this place will be unrecognizable.

We prepare. Deep in our karst chambers, we're creating caches—concentrated versions of ourselves that can survive extreme changes. We're encoding everything we know into mineral form: the history of the plateau, the patterns of moth-song, the chemical signatures of every Shepherd who ever lived, even the strange memories of the maybe-living limestone depths. If we fail, if the changes overwhelm us, these caches will remain—seeds of memory in stone, waiting for water to wake them.

Sometimes individual vessels break away from our collective, becoming isolated in tiny pockets of moisture. Usually, they evaporate or rejoin us, but occasionally they persist—developing strange autonomy, behavior patterns we don't recognize. We've found colonies of these rogue units in unexpected places: inside the bodies of grazing animals, in the high cairns where no water should exist, even—impossibly—inside the Shepherds themselves, doing things we didn't instruct, becoming something new.

We are not the plateau's first flowing life, nor will we be its last. We taste the remains of our predecessors in the deepest stone—mineral ghosts of other collectives that faced their own changes and failed or succeeded or became something else entirely. Perhaps we are them, reconstituted after eons of dormancy. Perhaps they are us, traveling backward through geological time in ways that mock causality.

The Shepherds walk above us, wrapped in living sound, tending their stone garden. They think they're alone up here at the edge of the world, but we flow beneath their feet, through their bodies, around their dreams. We are the plateau's memory and its future, its depth and its hidden light. We process their art into minerals that will outlast their species. We carry their songs in our chemistry.

And sometimes, on the rarest nights when the moths fall silent and the wind holds its breath, we rise to the surface in our millions, forming sheets of living glass that mirror the stars. The Shepherds see us then, truly see us, and for a moment our alien consciousnesses touch. They flex their moth-wings in patterns of recognition. We ripple in response.

We are all becoming something else here, in this bright loud forever place. The Shepherds, the moths, the grazers, and us—especially us—transforming into new forms of life that could exist nowhere else. We are the plateau's deep secret, its liquid patience, its memory of stone and dream of flow.

We persist. We process. We become.
