# Where I Would Find Myself

I am the mudwright.

Not *a* mudwright — there is only one at a time, and I have been this one for longer than I meant to be. I live in the hollowed base of one of the great hummock-trees, though "hollowed" suggests I made it hollow, when really the tree opened itself over centuries and I simply noticed the opening was my size.

My work is this: I read the clay.

The Pacers know the shallows in their long memories, but I know it in cross-section. Where they see surface — channels shifting, reed-islands migrating — I see depth. Layers. The mud here is a book written in flood-years and dry-years, in ash from fires that burned before the water came, in the compressed remains of ten million seasons of growth. I can take a core of clay as deep as my arm and tell you: here, a thousand years back, something large died and changed the chemistry of this spot forever. Here, three thousand years back, the water left entirely for a decade. Here, so far down the pressure has made stone of what was once mud, are the shells of creatures that lived when this was an ocean.

I came here — oh, it doesn't matter from where. I was young and thought I was a scholar and believed the shallows would be a good place to study time. That was forty years ago. Maybe fifty. The years run together like water.

What I've learned is that I'm not studying the place. The place is studying me.

My dwelling is simple: the tree-hollow, a reed mat, clay vessels I've made from the deep blue-black mud that remembers the ocean. I eat what the shallows eat — fish, reed-hearts, the occasional bird egg, fruits from my tree in season. But mostly I work.

Each morning I wade out to a different spot, chosen by some combination of instinct and the previous day's findings. I carry my corer — a hollow reed as long as I am tall, fire-hardened at the tip. I push it down through the water, through the soft upper mud, through the firm middle layers, until I hit the hard-packed deep clay or sometimes, thrillingly, bedrock. Then I draw it up, careful not to disturb the sample, and examine what time has written.

The layers tell stories. A band of ash speaks of drought and fire. A line of tiny shells says the water rose and stayed high. Certain colors — ochre, gray-green, blue-black — indicate different epochs of vegetation, different chemistries, different lives. I map these stories. My tree-hollow is lined with clay tablets where I've pressed samples, labeled with a system I invented and now couldn't explain to another soul. Not that there's another soul to explain it to.

Sometimes the Pacers come near while I work. They recognize me now — I've become part of their landscape, another thing that stands still in water for hours. Once, years ago, a young one stood beside me for an entire afternoon, and when I finally pulled up my core sample, it lowered its great head and sniffed at the mud with what I swear was curiosity. I showed it the layers — here, see? Your great-great-great grandmother might have stood in this russet band. This black line is from before even the Pacers came.

It hummed at me, low, and I understood then that they already knew. Not the specifics, not the chemistry and chronology I was so proud of mapping. But they knew the depth of time here in their bones. They knew it the way the mud knows it — by having been part of it.

I am maybe seventy now. My back protests the bending. My hands shake sometimes when I'm pressing samples. I've trained no apprentice because no one else has ever come here to train. When I die, the knowledge will go back into the mud with me, and that seems right. The place doesn't need interpreters. It just needs witnesses.

But here's what keeps me here, wading out each morning with my corer: Yesterday I found a layer I'd never seen before. Fifteen feet down, a band of pure white clay, fine as powder, that must have come from somewhere else entirely — blown in from what vanished mountains, settled out of what forgotten flood? And embedded in it, perfect, the scale of something that was not quite fish and not quite lizard and not quite anything that lives here now.

The shallows are old, but they're still telling stories. And I'm still here to listen, until I become a layer myself — probably a thin one, barely noticeable, a slightly different color in the clay. The Pacers will hum over me for a day or two. The mud will close with its patient hands. And someone else, someday, might push a corer down and wonder at the odd mineral content where my bones settled, might make up a story about what kind of life could leave such a mark.

That's what I am: a future puzzle in the clay, still walking around, still gathering data about my own eventual stratum.
