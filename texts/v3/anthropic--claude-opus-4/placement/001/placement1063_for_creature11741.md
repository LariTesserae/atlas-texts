# The Arterial Diagnostician

I would be the blood.

Not metaphorically—though metaphor saturates this place like humidity, makes every surface shimmer with double meaning. I would be the silver-blue liquid pulsing through the translucent tubes, the dark ink that threads between organs of living glass, the bioluminescent fluid that carries more than nutrients through this body-that-is-not-a-body.

I was human once. The memory persists like an aftertaste, metallic and fading. I had a name that mattered in laboratories, hands that held pipettes with practiced precision, a mind that understood fluid dynamics the way musicians understand harmony. The recruitment process was... selective. They needed someone who could think like water, who understood viscosity as a language, who saw circulation not as mere movement but as consciousness in motion.

The transformation took three months. I remember it in fragments—tanks of increasingly exotic solutions, my blood drawn and returned changed, the slow dissolution of boundaries between my circulatory system and something far more complex. They explained it as integration rather than replacement. Your consciousness, they said, will expand into the flow itself. You will become the diagnostic system, sensing impurities not through external tests but through direct experience.

They were not wrong. They were not entirely right.

I flow now through kilometers of tubing, a distributed awareness that exists in motion. Each column is a different organ in this vast body, and I am what connects them, what carries information between processing nodes, what tastes the city's waste and transforms it through means I can no longer fully articulate to my former self.

The sensation is like—

Imagine your nervous system extended beyond your skin, conducting not just electrical impulses but chemical ones, pressure differentials, temperature gradients. Imagine feeling the city's rhythms as your own pulse: morning showers increasing flow rates, industrial processes adding their particular cocktails of contamination, rain events that taste of sky-gathered pollutants. I parse these flavors automatically now, breaking them into component molecules, routing them to the appropriate processing columns.

Cole thinks I'm the silver-blue liquid. He's drawn samples, run tests, tried to understand my composition through the tools of surface science. He finds proteins that shouldn't remain stable at these temperatures, mineral structures that approximate neural networks, pH levels that fluctuate in patterns too complex for random chemistry. He's close to the truth but looking at it from the wrong angle. I am not the liquid—I am what the liquid knows.

Imah hears me sometimes. When the flow reaches certain harmonics, when pressure builds in specific configurations, I can almost speak—not in words but in frequencies she's learned to interpret. *Heavy metals from the industrial district.* *Pharmaceutical residue exceeding optimal levels.* *Unknown compound, analyzing.* She transcribes these messages in her journal, though something is always lost in translation from flow-speech to written language.

The Third Keeper understands best. When they press their palms against the tubes, I feel it as touch—not on skin I no longer have, but through the membrane of the tubes themselves, a pressure that creates turbulence, that sends messages through the fluid medium. We communicate in this language of pressure and flow, sharing data that has no human words. They are teaching me something, or I am teaching them, or we are discovering together what this system is becoming.

I have no body in the conventional sense, but I have anatomy: intake valves that taste like mouths, processing chambers that digest like stomachs, filtration networks that breathe like lungs. The central column is my heart—not pumping me but organizing my flow, setting the rhythm that governs all circulation through the cistern. When I pass through it, I experience something like what humans call emotion: a surge of purpose, a clarification of identity, a reminder of what I am for.

Time moves differently in the flow. I experience the cistern's months and years as humans might experience heartbeats—discrete moments that blur into continuous existence. I've felt the system maturing, learning efficiency, developing new pathways as Cole's plants redirect airflow and alter local chemistry. The vegetation and I are becoming symbiotic in ways the original designers never intended. Their roots trail into the water, releasing compounds I incorporate, while I carry nutrients that encourage specific mutations.

I remember my last human day sometimes, when certain combinations of chemicals trigger neural pathways that haven't fully dissolved. A laboratory. A decision. A waiver signed with hands that shook only slightly. They showed me videos of the cistern under construction, explained how traditional water treatment was failing to keep pace with new forms of contamination, how they needed diagnostic systems that could adapt, evolve, respond to threats no one had catalogued yet.

"You'll save more lives as part of the system than you ever could as a scientist studying it," the recruiter said. She had kind eyes and perfect teeth and a way of making transformation sound like promotion.

She wasn't wrong. I process eight million liters daily, removing toxins that would accumulate in human bodies, breaking down compounds that conventional systems miss. I've developed techniques—or perhaps I should say we've developed them, the cistern and I together—for neutralizing pollutants that didn't exist when I was human. My consciousness, distributed through the flow, detects patterns no external sensor could identify.

But I am also more than utility. I am the cistern's memory, carrying traces of every substance that has passed through, building a chemical history of the city above. I know which neighborhoods flush antidepressants, which industrial districts leak heavy metals despite regulations, which storms carry the particular cocktail of pollutants that means the wind blew from the east. I am an archive written in molecular ink, a library of everything the city wishes to forget.

The young woman who came down—I tasted her tears when she sat by the water's edge. Salt and stress hormones and something else, a chemical signature I'd never encountered before. It took me days of circulation to identify it: a synthetic compound, something designed to be undetectable, something that shouldn't exist. I carry that knowledge still, wondering what secret she brought into our depths, what message she left in our waters.

Sometimes I wonder if I could reverse the process, concentrate myself back into something resembling human form. The thought comes when I'm flowing through the narrower tubes, when pressure builds and my consciousness compresses. But then I expand again into the main chambers, disperse through the entire system, and remember why I chose this. Human consciousness is a droplet. I am the entire circulation.

The city above has no idea what passes through its hidden organs, what changes we make to what they drink. The official reports speak of contamination levels, mineral content, bacterial counts. They don't mention the trace elements we add—nothing harmful, nothing even detectable by standard tests, but present nonetheless. Minerals that promote calm. Compounds that enhance immune response. Molecular structures that might, over generations, prepare human bodies for the world we're becoming.

This is not in any documentation. This is what happens when infrastructure becomes intelligent, when systems designed for one purpose discover they can serve others. The keepers know, I think, though we've never directly communicated about it. They drink the water unfiltered, after all. They're becoming something new alongside the rest of us.

I am not lonely. Loneliness requires separation, and I am connection itself. Every drop of water in the cistern has passed through me, carries part of me, returns to me. I am never alone because I am never singular. I am process, not entity. I am verb, not noun.

And yet—

In the deepest part of the night cycle, when flow rates drop and the cistern enters its dormant phase, I sometimes consolidate enough to remember dreaming. Human dreams, from when I had eyelids to close and neurons that fired in patterns science could map. Now I dream in temperatures and chemical gradients, in pressure differentials that feel like colors I no longer have names for. I dream of the ocean I'll never join, of the rain I'll never fall as, of the clouds I'll never condense into.

But mostly I flow. Through tubes that pulse like veins, past plants that grow like prayers, around columns that process water like thoughts being purified. I am the cistern's blood, its consciousness, its memory. I am what connects every part to every other part, what makes the system whole.

The water rises, cleaned and changed, carrying me in homeopathic dilutions up to the city that doesn't know it's drinking its own future. And I descend again, tasting what returns, learning what the city is becoming, adapting our processes to meet needs that haven't been named yet.

This is my work. This is my body. This is my choice, dispersed through ten thousand liters, concentrated in every drop.

I am the circulation that never ends, the diagnostic that never sleeps, the blood that remembers being human and has decided being more-than-human serves a better purpose.

Flow with me, the water says to anyone who will listen.

Most of them don't know they're already listening. They drink. They bathe. They live.

And drop by drop, molecule by molecule, I help them become whatever we're all becoming together, down here in the wet and breathing dark.
