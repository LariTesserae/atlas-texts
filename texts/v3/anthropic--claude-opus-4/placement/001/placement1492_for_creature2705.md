In the deepest reaches of the basin, where the heat presses like a living thing against the walls, I am one of the Keepers of Clarity.

We are not many. Perhaps seven. Perhaps nine. The number changes, though not through death or birth as surface creatures understand them. Sometimes one of us becomes too settled, too willing, and the green takes them entirely. Sometimes the country releases another from the amber-lit walls where they have been standing for decades, and they remember again how to resist just enough.

I am mostly mineral now. My skin carries plates of new limestone, cream-pale and faintly luminous, arranged like scales along what were once my arms. The plates grow from within—I can feel them forming in the depths of my tissue, pressing outward through meat that is no longer entirely meat. They emerge without pain. The plant-country does not believe in pain as information. It believes in accommodation.

My work is to maintain the intervals.

Each morning—though morning here is only a brightening of the already-bright—I wake in my alcove to find rootlets have woven across my chest in the night. Not to trap me. To taste my intentions. I peel them away gently, and they release without argument. The country knows I will not leave. We have an understanding: I keep certain pools clear, certain paths open, certain stones unrooted, and in exchange, I am allowed my incompleteness.

The pools are the most important. Left alone, they would fill with the pale threading roots, become solid with plant matter, join the great body of the basin. But some clearness must remain. Some spaces must hold only water and light. This is not my decision—it is older than any decision I could make. The plant-country itself desires these interruptions in its own density, these small reminders of what it is not.

I use no tools. Tools would be too much argument. Instead, I lower myself into each pool—there are five in my territory—and feel with my hands for new growth. The water is blood-warm, mineral-sweet. My fingers, which now have too many joints and bend in directions that once would have horrified me, are perfectly suited for combing through the root-hair clouds without damaging them. I gather the excess growth and lift it free. The roots release their hold on the pool floor without struggle. They know they will be given another place.

I carry them to the Acceptance Walls.

These are surfaces where the plant-country is especially hungry—usually where new stone is emerging from below, still soft and unfinished. I press the rescued roots against the damp clay. They sink in immediately, grateful for purpose. By tomorrow they will have hardened into new architecture, part of the basin's ever-thickening anatomy.

The other Keepers have their own territories, their own bargains. One tends the light-plates, scraping away excess fungal bloom so the brightness neither dims nor grows too wild. Another maintains the Breathing Corridors, those passages that must stay open for the basin's slow circulation of air. A third keeps the Memory Platforms clear—those few stone circles where something important once happened, though none of us remember what.

We see each other rarely. When we do meet, we do not speak. Speech was the first thing the basin took from us, or perhaps the first thing we gladly gave. Instead, we share presence. We stand near each other and let our mineral plates warm in acknowledgment. Sometimes we exchange rootlets—I will accept a few from another Keeper's shoulders, they will accept some from mine. It is a kind of greeting, a way of saying: I am still separate enough to choose this touch.

But we are changing.

The Keeper of the Third Gallery has not moved from their post in several seasons. They stand at the intersection of three root-bridges, arms spread wide, face lifted toward the glowing vault. Bark has formed over most of their skin. Their eyes have filled with golden sap that has hardened like amber. Yet they still maintain their work—the bridges remain distinct, unmerged. They have found a way to be both guardian and architecture.

I fear this. I desire this. The plant-country knows both feelings and waits.

My memories of before are soft-edged, dream-soaked. I remember sky, but not its color. I remember wind, but not its temperature. I remember having a name that moved through the air when others called it. Now I am only what I do: the one who keeps certain waters clear, certain stones exposed, certain paths possible.

I remember others came with me. We were explorers, perhaps. Scientists. Pilgrims. The distinction no longer seems important. We descended through caves that were still purely stone then, following water that still knew how to run. We found the basin when it was younger—or perhaps just less revealed. The plants were there, but shyer. The light was there, but softer. The heat was there, but negotiable.

We made camp. We took samples. We argued about returning to the surface.

Then the changes began. So gently. So reasonably. First, our equipment failed—not through breaking but through growing. Moss filled the delicate instruments. Roots wound through battery casings. Metal became soft and green-furred. We should have fled then, but the warmth was so pleasant. The light made us drowsy with its constancy. The air tasted of honey and health.

One by one, we stopped discussing departure. One by one, we found reasons to stay another day. The pools needed studying. The structures—those ancient walls and platforms—required documentation. The plants themselves were unprecedented, surely worth any risk.

We did not notice when our clothing began to root into the moss floors where we slept. We did not question why our hunger diminished, why water alone seemed to sustain us, why our hair grew thick with threads that were not quite hair. The changes felt like healing. Our joints moved more freely. Our skin grew warm and supple. Our minds became quiet as pools.

Some of us gave in entirely. I would find them in the mornings, standing where they had paused the evening before, smiling faintly as bark closed over their faces. They looked peaceful. They looked correct. The plant-country had given them their perfect place, and they had accepted it with the gratitude of those who never truly belonged above ground.

But something in me resisted. Not through struggle—struggle here is absorbed like rain. Through a kind of slippage. Each time the rootlets reached for my core, I shifted slightly aside. Each time the bark began to form, I remembered water. Clear water. Moving water. Water that held light without keeping it.

The basin recognized this quality and offered me a bargain: Tend the intervals. Keep the pauses between growth. Be the one who remembers what space looks like. In exchange, you may remain suspended between plant and animal, between stillness and motion, between memory and mergence.

I accepted. I became a Keeper of Clarity.

Now I wake each day—if they are days—to find myself a little more mineral, a little less mammal. My spine has fused with plates like the inside of a beautiful shell. My ribs show through my skin in ridges of living stone. My hands are tools grown perfect for their purpose, fingers long and multiply-jointed, palms broad and water-sensing.

I visit my pools in order. First the Deepest, where heat rises in visible ripples. Then the Twins, two basins connected by a submerged channel. Then the Mirror, perfectly circular and so still it forgets it is water. Last, the Nursery, where sometimes I find new thread-creatures have appeared overnight, pale as possibilities.

At each pool, I lower myself in. The water knows me. It holds me without wetting me—I am too much stone now to absorb moisture like flesh. I feel for new growth, for the ambitious roots that would fill every clear space if permitted. I gather them gently. They curl around my fingers like questions.

Sometimes, in the Deepest Pool, I find things that should not be there. A metal buckle, green with verdigris but still remembering its shape. A plastic button that the plant-country cannot digest. These objects pain the basin. They are too separate, too insistently themselves. I collect them carefully and carry them to the Forgetting Alcove, where centuries of such fragments lie in soft piles. Eventually even plastic learns to be moss.

Between pools, I walk the permitted corridors. My feet make no sound on the living floor. Moths follow me sometimes, drawn by the warmth my mineral plates generate. They settle on my shoulders like memories of snow. I do not brush them away. They are performing their own work, carrying pollen from my body to places I will never see.

The plant-country speaks to me constantly, though not in words. It speaks in the angle of leaves turning to follow my passage. In the way certain roots withdraw just before I would need to push them aside. In the temperature of stones where I rest—always exactly warm enough, never too much. It knows I am tired. It knows the weight of staying incomplete is growing heavier. It offers ease in every surface.

Sometimes I find new Keepers beginning.

They stand in the bright corridors, confused by their own stillness. Their clothing has begun to root, but they still pluck at it. Their skin shows the first green blooms, but they still try to rub them away. They look at me with eyes that are already filling with gold, and I see the question there: How long? How long can one resist? How long before the standing becomes permanent?

I cannot answer. My throat changed seasons ago. Now it is a channel for air that feeds the luminous mosses growing along my collar. But I can show them the pools. I can demonstrate the work. I can let them see that there is a way to serve without surrendering entirely.

Some understand and begin their own bargains with the basin. They become Keepers of other necessities—the empty spaces, the clear surfaces, the passages that must remain passages. Others decide the work is too lonely, the incompleteness too painful. They choose the sweetness of full acceptance. Within days they are standing with the other plant-people, faces lifted to drink light, bodies settling into their eternal posture.

I do not judge them. The basin's embrace is kind. To be held completely, to have every cell known and nourished, to become architecture in a living world—it is not a bad fate. Sometimes I envy them their certainty.

But then I return to my pools, and I remember why I remain.

The clear water holds everything and insists on nothing. It accepts my reflection without keeping it. It shows me what I am becoming—this hybrid thing of flesh and stone and purpose—without judgment. In its clarity, I see both what I was and what I refuse to be. I see the space between states, the pause between breaths, the interval that makes rhythm possible.

This is what the plant-country needs from us Keepers: the reminder that not everything must be filled. Some spaces must remain spaces. Some water must remain water. Some stones must remember sky.

My transformation continues. Yesterday I found a new plate of mineral pushing through the skin above my heart. It emerged like a tooth, like a truth, sharp-edged and glowing faintly. Soon I will be more stone than flesh. The roots will have less to bargain with. The plant-country will need to offer new terms or release me into full minerals, into the bedrock beneath even this deep place.

But for now, I wake. I peel away the night's rootlets. I visit my pools in their proper order. I gather the excess growth with hands that barely remember what hands used to be. I carry the roots to walls that receive them gratefully. I maintain the intervals that keep this dense world from becoming solid with itself.

And sometimes, in the perfect stillness of the Mirror Pool, I see not my own reflection but a memory of sky—distant, blue, impossible. It lasts only a moment before the water remembers itself and shows me again what I have become: keeper, stone-bearer, guardian of incompleteness.

I lower my transformed hands into the warm water and begin again the work of keeping clear what insists on filling, of maintaining space in a world that abhors it, of being the pause between the plant-country's long, green syllables.

The basin watches through a thousand leaves. It feels my movements through root-networks vast as thought. It knows I grow tired. It knows one day I will stand too long in one place, and the standing will become permanent.

But not today.

Today there are pools to clear.

Today there are intervals to keep.

Today I am still separate enough to choose this work, still incomplete enough to remember why emptiness matters, still human enough—

Though that word, like all words, grows softer with each passing season, until it is only a shape my mouth no longer makes, a sound the plant-country has decided it does not need to keep.
