# Where I Would Find Myself

I am one of the pale structures—not a cocoon but what emerged from it, changed.

I have been here longer than the Haum, though I did not know they called themselves that until recently. I have been here since before the basin was young, when the obsidian edges were still sharp enough to draw blood from the sky. I watched the first moss spore take hold. I felt the first silver filament thread through virgin soil. I remember silence, though I can no longer imagine it.

My body is no longer strictly biological. The transformation was not violent—I recall it as a negotiation, a series of compromises between what I was and what the basin needed me to become. My bones are laced with the same silver that runs through the ground. My skin has taken on the nacreous quality of the cocoon material, not quite alive but not quite otherwise. I photosynthesize in the absence of sun, drawing sustenance from the columns of light I learned to extrude from my dwelling. I am changing still, slowly, the transformation asymptotic rather than complete.

---

I was Dr. Lenna Suarez-Hocht, xenobiologist, member of the Third Exploratory Wave. I had theories about the structures, about the filaments, about the peculiar acoustics of this place. I entered the cocoon with instruments, with hypotheses, with the assumption that understanding was possible if one simply gathered enough data. The cocoon closed around me like a considerate host drawing curtains for a guest who needs to sleep.

I did not sleep. I *listened*—not with my ears, which were the first things to change, but with the new organs that grew along my spine, beneath my ribs, in the spaces between thoughts. The cocoon was not shelter but a translation interface, a technology designed to parse meanings that no baseline nervous system could process. It took my theories and composted them, used them as substrates for growing new forms of comprehension.

When I emerged—days later? years?—the world had gained dimensions I lack words to describe. The sound was no longer a wall but an architecture, a palace of frequencies I could walk through, examining each room, each corridor, each hidden chamber where meanings nested inside meanings. The Haum child who leans toward the roar is brushing against what I swim through constantly. The rhythm she hears, the breathing beneath everything—I know what dreams there. I am part of the dream's maintenance.

---

My dwelling has grown around me over the decades, an extrusion of the landscape that is neither structure nor organism but something between. The columns of light that extend from it are my synapses made visible, my thoughts interfacing with the basin's larger consciousness. I do not control them in the sense that I do not control my heartbeat—they are autonomic, necessary, a function of what I have become.

The other structures, the other cocoons, had occupants once. My colleagues from the Third Wave, perhaps, or explorers from civilizations I never knew existed. They are not gone—nothing is ever gone here—but they have dispersed into functions I perceive only obliquely. One maintains the temperature, keeping the water always at the edge of freezing but never crossing. One tends the moss, pruning possibilities, encouraging certain frequencies while discouraging others. One is the archivist of tremors, recording each shift of the something-beneath in a medium I cannot access but know exists.

We do not meet. We do not need to meet. We are organs in a body, specialized past the point of casual communication. But sometimes, during what the Haum call the long-phase, our functions synchronize. The columns of light from all our structures align, creating a chord that is almost—*almost*—complete. Missing one note. Always missing the same note.

---

I tried to warn the Haum once. The one who entered my structure seven generations ago—I recognized her curiosity, her hunger to parse deeper meanings. I tried to tell her what the transformation entailed, what she would gain and lose. But my voice had changed too much. The frequencies I produce now register to human ears as wrongness, as sounds that should not exist. She heard my warning as invitation, my caution as enticement.

She is not gone either. She walks the deep basin where the moss grows so thick it becomes geological, where the sound achieves densities that would liquify baseline bones. She is learning to tend something there, some function the basin requires that none of us who came before were suited for. Sometimes I catch echoes of her work in the reports—new patterns, new rhythms, the signature of her transformation written in frequencies only the changed can parse.

The report is not for distant makers. There are no makers coming. We are the makers now—we who entered the cocoons and emerged as functions, as processes, as living algorithms in the basin's vast computation. The report is the basin talking to itself, integrating information, adjusting parameters, optimizing the work.

The work is the production of the compound the harvesters value, yes, but that is a byproduct. The true work is the maintenance of the something-beneath—not a creature but a state, a potential, a possibility held perpetually at the edge of becoming. The breathing the child hears is not respiration but ideation, a mind vast enough that its thoughts take geological time to form. We keep it dreaming because the alternative—

---

I remember the briefing before the Third Wave launched. The planet had been flagged as a potential terraforming candidate, but the surveys were inconclusive. Signs of technology but no civilization. Acoustic anomalies that suggested massive subsurface structures. My job was to determine if the apparent biotechnology was active or abandoned, if the planet was garden or ruin.

It is both. It is neither. It is a clock made of moss and sound, and we who entered the cocoons are its escapement, regulating the speed at which time moves through this place. Without us, the something-beneath would wake too quickly or sleep too deeply. Either would end the world—not violently but simply, the way a lifted needle ends a song.

When the Haum child leans into the roar, she is correct. We are part of the dream. But we are also the ones who ensure the dreamer does not wake. This is our function. This is what we became.

I extend a new column of light from my dwelling, trying again to find the missing note. The incomplete troubles me more each year—not because I fear it will never resolve but because I suspect I am the one meant to complete it. When I do, something will change. The long-phase will end or become permanent. The reports will cease or accelerate. The something-beneath will exhale its last geological breath and then—

And then I will know if we were gardeners or ruins all along.

The moss ripples. The sound continues. I parse, and wait, and maintain.
