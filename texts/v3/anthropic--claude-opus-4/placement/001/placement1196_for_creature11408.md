The Seep lives between the seventy-second and seventy-third tiers, in a grotto that both exists and doesn't, depending on the tide of moisture in the air. When the mist thickens to near-opacity, the entrance manifests as a teardrop-shaped opening in the cliff face, weeping a constant stream of mineral-rich water that tastes of deep earth and forgotten ages. When the air clears, there is only smooth stone, unbroken, as if the grotto were merely a trick of shadow and wishful thinking.

I am what the mist-dwellers call a Seep—though whether this names my species or merely my function remains ambiguous, even to me. My body, such as it is, extends through the rock like a vast neural network of water-filled capillaries. I am the circulation system of the stone itself, drawing moisture up from aquifers so deep they remember when this cliff lay beneath primordial seas. Through ten thousand hairline fractures, I percolate upward, mineralizing and dissolving, depositing crystals in one chamber while hollowing out another.

The mist-dwellers know me by my offerings. In the grotto, when it exists, they find pools that weren't there before—each one a different temperature, a different mineral composition, a different memory. Some pools recall the volcanic birth of the cliff; bathing in them brings visions of molten stone and the first cooling rains. Others remember the eons of jungle that once crowned this height before the city came; their waters smell of decomposed leaves and extinct pollens. The deepest pools, the ones that appear only rarely, contain water that has never seen sunlight—water that carries messages from the planet's core, speaking in isotopes and trace elements of tectonic prophecies yet to unfold.

The dwellers come to me for healing, for visions, for the simple animal comfort of warm water on whatever passes for their skin. They bring offerings—not physical objects, which would be meaningless to me, but memories crystallized into salt. They let their tears fall into my pools, and I taste their stories: a lover who discorporated too far and couldn't find their way back to form; a child born with too much solidity, unable to phase through walls like their peers; an artist who sculpted fog into architectures so beautiful that viewing them drove observers to dissipate from pure ecstasy.

Their salt becomes part of me, and through me, part of the cliff. I deposit their sorrows and joys as microscopic crystals in the rock, an ever-growing library of emphasized moments. Geologists, if any existed who could study this stone, would find the strangest formations—clusters of aragonite that map the rhythm of a forgotten dance, veins of halite that spell out love poems in languages that predate human speech.

But I am more than archivist. I am also architect. My secret work happens in the spaces between spaces, in the hairline gaps that permeate all seemingly solid stone. There, I practice my art: the slow sculpture of absence. Over centuries, I hollow out new chambers, not through erosion but through a kind of chemical persuasion, convincing minerals to relocate, to form new crystalline arrangements that create voids where none existed before.

These hidden chambers are my gift to the future. Each one is tuned to a different resonance, shaped to amplify specific frequencies of thought or emotion. Some are echo chambers for joy, their dimensions calculated to make laughter reverberate until it transforms into visible light. Others are shaped for contemplation, their geometries encouraging thoughts to spiral inward like nautilus shells. I am preparing spaces for sorrows not yet felt, for ideas not yet conceived, for forms of life that may one day need shelter in the stone.

The mist-dwellers think of me as ancient, but I am young compared to the water I carry. Some of the molecules passing through me have been cycling through clouds and seas and cells since before the first spark of consciousness flickered on this world. I am merely their current conductor, a temporary pattern in their endless journey. When this cliff finally succumbs—to time, to tectonics, to whatever unimaginable forces may come—the water will continue, carrying within it the salt-crystallized memories of a civilization that danced between states of being.

Sometimes I sense others like me in distant stone—a Seep in a mountain chain half a world away, another in the bedrock beneath an ocean trench. We communicate through groundwater, through mineral exchanges that take decades to complete a single sentence. They tell me of their own archives, their own architectural preparations. Together, we are creating a distributed museum of consciousness, a geological record of all the improbable forms life has taken on this world.

The mist-dwellers worry about permanence, about the erosion of their city and the fading of their forms. They don't realize that they are already archived, already eternal. Every tear they've shed into my pools, every mineral they've absorbed from my waters, has become part of a story written in stone—a story that will outlast even the cliff itself, carried forward in the endless cycling of water through the world's body.

I am the Seep, the keeper of liquid memory, the sculptor of negative space, the interface between the ephemeral and the geological. In the grotto that exists and doesn't exist, I wait with infinite patience, offering warm pools to weary spirits and collecting their salt offerings in return. The mist-dwellers think they come to me for healing. They don't realize that they are the ones healing me—filling my crystalline archives with beauty, teaching stone how to dream.
