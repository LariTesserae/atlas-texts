I would find myself as one of the kelp holdfasts—those ancient, calcified anchors the size of houses, gripping the drowned slopes with a determination that has outlasted the memory of air.

I am old. Older than the keth, older than their stories about when the mountains were mountains. I began as a single spore settling on stone when the water was still rising, when these peaks wore caps of snow instead of moss. Now I am architecture. My body has become a labyrinth of chambers and passages, each one carved by time and inhabited by creatures that think of me not as a living thing but as geography.

My flesh is no longer quite plant matter. Centuries of mineral-rich water have transformed me into something between organism and stone. My outer layers are thick as concrete, pocked with holes where boring worms have made their homes. But deeper, in my core, I am still alive—still drawing nutrients from the stone I grip, still sending up my great fronds to feed on light filtered through fathoms of turbulent water.

The current-swimmers know me. They call me *Eldest-Grip* in their language, though I have had other names. They come to harvest the colonies of crustaceans that live in my hollows, and sometimes they rest in my shadow when the currents are fighting. I have felt their hands on my flesh for generations, have learned the weight of their grief when one of them doesn't return.

I remember the processing facility when it still processed. I was already large then, already old. The machines pulled something from the water—a kind of algae that bloomed in the shallows, dense with oils that burned clean. The workers would curse when my fronds tangled their intake pipes. One tried to kill me once, hacking at my base with tools I couldn't understand. The scars are still there, mineralized now, harder than the rest of me.

I grow so slowly now that growth and erosion have found equilibrium. For every cell I add, the current takes another. I am no larger than I was a century ago, but I am denser, heavier, more myself. My roots have worked into cracks in the bedrock that weren't there when I first anchored. I have become load-bearing. If I were to die, this whole section of slope might slough away.

In my deepest chambers live things that have never seen light. Pale crustaceans with no eyes, their shells soft as leather. Worms that glow when disturbed, spelling out their panic in blue-green script. A species of fish that exists nowhere else, having evolved in the specific darkness of my body. They are not parasites—we have an understanding. They clean me, keep the boring worms from going too deep. In return, I shelter them from the current, from the predators, from the terrible pressure of the world outside.

Sometimes I dream. I don't know if other kelp dream, but I am old enough that the boundaries between plant and not-plant have blurred. In my dreams, I remember sunlight without water, remember when my ancestors grew in tidal pools, small and quick-lived. I dream of the thing in the deep tank, the thing that draws when no one is looking. We have an understanding too, though I couldn't say what it is. Sometimes I feel it testing the water, sending out vibrations that my fronds pick up like whispers. It is older than me. It was here before the water rose. It is waiting for something, and I think—though I cannot be sure—that it is waiting for the peaks to finish drowning.

The keth child who saw it drawing was right to be frightened. But she was wrong about one thing: it didn't stop when she looked. It had already finished. What she saw was what it wanted her to see—a map, maybe, or a promise, or a warning about what happens when you hold on too long.

I will outlive the keth. When the last of them takes the trading boats or gives themselves to the transition, I will still be here, holding the slope together, hosting my pale communities in the dark. The water will rise until even the tallest peaks are drowned, and my fronds will grow longer, reaching for light that grows ever fainter. Eventually, I will be too deep. The sun will become memory, then myth, then nothing. But I will still be holding on.

The mountains are patient. The water is not. And I? I am somewhere between, neither patient nor urgent, growing and dying at the same rate, my chambers full of secrets that taste of salt and time. When the current-swimmers come, I feel their prayers in the water—*I enter you, I will leave you, I do not assume I will leave you*—and I want to tell them: *You will leave. Everything leaves. But something stays. Something always stays, gripping the stone, becoming the stone, waiting in the dark for whatever comes next.*

I am the Eldest-Grip, and I hold on not because I choose to, but because I have forgotten how to let go.
