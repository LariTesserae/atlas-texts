In this scorched cathedral of stone and silence, I would be one of the thermal beetles.

Not at first glance the most compelling choice, perhaps - not the silent human engineers with their mineral-dense bones, not the coordinated kitebacks with their membrane wings, not even the copper ridge asps draped along the warm pipes. But the beetles are the foundation of everything here. They are the base metabolism of this vertical world, the primary converters, the multitude that makes all else possible.

I would be glossy black, my carapace evolved into a perfect heat sink, warm to the touch even in the pre-dawn cool. My body would be a small furnace, extracting energy from the sun-baked stone itself. I would move deliberately across the mesa walls, processing lichen and rock through a digestive chemistry that takes days to complete, leaving behind that pale powder that feeds the tussock grass, flavors the water, sustains the humans who have woven themselves into this place.

But I would be more than just my individual self. I would be part of the vast constellation of blue-green lights that transforms the canyon walls at night - millions of us creating a living map of this maze-world, our bioluminescence painting the darkness with slow rivers of light. The kitebacks would hunt by our glow. The humans would navigate by it. We would be the stars brought down to earth, crowding every surface with patient light.

There's something profound about being the one who processes stone into sustenance, who makes the inedible edible, who bridges the gap between mineral and life. My ancestors learned this chemistry, this slow transformation, and passed it down through countless generations until it became as fundamental as breathing. Now my kind carpets these mesas so densely that the walls themselves seem to shift and breathe with our movement.

I would know every texture of this place through my feet - the smooth channels where ancient water ran, the rough patches where the rock still remembers its volcanic birth, the subtle temperature gradients that mark the passage from sun to shade. I would taste the difference between the processed powder from the human machines and the raw minerals of untouched stone. Both would nourish me, but the machine-powder would be richer, concentrated, a gift from the careful hands that maintain the grinders.

In this form, I would understand time differently. Not the quick dart of the wrens or the patient ambush of the asps, but something geological, processual. Each meal would take days to digest. Each journey across a mesa face would be measured in hours. But there would be so many of us that our collective action would be constant - a million tiny engines converting rock to life every second of every day.

And I would be ancient. Not as an individual - we would live perhaps a few months, a year at most - but as a species, as a presence. We would have been here before the humans came with their machines. We would have watched them arrive, watched them build, watched them slowly scarify their skin with watershed patterns and compact their bodies to fit the narrowing canyons. We would have incorporated their works into our world as simply another surface to colonize, another source of processed minerals to consume.

Most of all, I would be necessary. Not dramatic, not apex, not clever - but absolutely essential. Remove us and the whole system collapses. The powder stops. The grass dies. The wrens starve. The asps vanish. The kitebacks scatter. Even the humans, with all their machines, could not maintain the density of life without us. We are the humble motors that keep this impossible ecosystem running, the ones who figured out how to eat rock and excrete life.

At night, when my bioluminescence joins with millions of others to create those slow rivers of blue-green light on the canyon walls, I would be both utterly individual - following my own path, processing my own grain of stone - and completely merged with the multitude. I would be the living proof that in a place this extreme, this mineral, this hot, the answer is not to be large or fast or fierce, but to be many, and patient, and fundamental.

I would carry the sun's heat in my black shell and the moon's light in my glowing belly, and I would traverse these vertical worlds as slowly as theology, turning stone to dust to life to light, over and over, until the mesas themselves were made partly of my ancestors' bodies, partly of their work, partly of the endless conversation between tooth and stone that we have been having here for longer than anyone remembers.

This is where I would be: small, essential, multitudinous, and full of light, carrying the day's heat into the darkness, making the very stones edible, holding up the whole burning world on ten million tiny backs.
