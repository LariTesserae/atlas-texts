In the dampening ferns, I am neither plant nor hunter, but something between—a thing that has learned patience from the moss and cunning from the predators that stalk these acoustic boundaries.

I am Colony.

Where the great indigo hunters move as solitary apex beings, I am a distributed intelligence spread across ten thousand bodies, each no larger than a child's thumb. We are soft-bodied things, our flesh the consistency of raw egg white, translucent and trembling. Individually, we are nothing—a morsel that would dissolve in the acidic roots of the ferns or be crushed instantly by even the gentlest atmospheric pressure change.

But together, we have become architect.

We discovered long ago that certain frequencies, when generated in perfect synchronization, can create localized pockets of acoustic cancellation within the great Drone. Not the crude counter-frequencies the hunters use to lure their prey, but something far more sophisticated—a three-dimensional latticework of intersecting sound waves that can carve temporary rooms of perfect silence out of the roaring air.

In these pockets of impossible quiet, we build.

Our cities are ephemeral things, lasting perhaps a day or two before the minute variations in the great machine below shift the harmonic pattern by a fraction of a hertz and our carefully maintained silence collapses. We construct them from our own secretions—a substance that hardens when exposed to specific vibrational frequencies into structures as delicate as spun glass. Spiral towers no taller than a fern frond. Branching corridors that follow the mathematical patterns of sound wave interference. Chambers where we can finally, briefly, exist without the crushing weight of noise.

We farm the condensation, channeling the upward-blown mist through our crystalline aqueducts. We cultivate the smallest of the chitin-motes, the ones too tiny for the Vane-rays to notice, raising them in vibrational corrals where precisely tuned frequencies keep them docile and breeding.

But we are always watching the hunters.

When one of the great indigo beasts settles into our section of the ring, we begin our most important work. While it waits with infinite patience for prey, we map its acoustic signature. Every creature in the shaft has one—a unique pattern of how their body disrupts and reflects the base Drone. We study the hunter's breathing, the minute pressure changes as its non-Newtonian fluids shift, the specific way its corrugated skin baffles sound.

And then we begin to sing.

Ten thousand voices, too high for the hunter's drum-like membrane to detect, weave a copy of its acoustic shadow. We project this phantom signature out into the void, creating the illusion of a second hunter on the opposite side of the ring. The Vane-rays, feeling this new predator's presence, adjust their spiraling paths away from the false threat—and directly toward the real one.

The hunter does not know we are helping it. It believes its success comes from patience and skill alone. But we have been guiding prey to these killing grounds for generations of our kind, ensuring the hunter remains well-fed and lazy, unlikely to explore too deeply into the fern forests where we build our brief civilizations.

When the hunter finally makes its terrible crossing to a new ring—that agonizing journey through the anti-node—we dissolve our cities without regret and follow. We ride in the wake of its passage, our soft bodies tucked into the acoustic shadow it creates, drafting behind the rigid armor of its pressurized form like remoras on a shark. The journey that nearly kills the massive predator merely buffets us, and we pour upward or downward to the next calm zone, ready to begin again.

Sometimes I wonder if the hunter suspects. When it rests in the ferns after a crossing, does it feel us flowing past like water? Does it notice how the prey always seems to find it, no matter which ring it claims?

But these are thoughts for the quiet chambers, for the brief moments when our cities stand and silence reigns. Now, dispersed through the moss, feeling the hunter's distinctive pressure signature settle into hunting position, we begin our ancient work again. Ten thousand voices rise in pitches too high to matter, weaving sound into strategy, survival into symbiosis.

The Vane-ray will come. The hunter will feed. And we will build our glass cities in the space between heartbeats, in the pockets of silence we carve from a world that has forgotten what quiet means.

This is how the small things persist in the shadow of giants—not through strength, but through the patient accumulation of tiny advantages, tiny intelligences, tiny songs that shape the world one frequency at a time.
