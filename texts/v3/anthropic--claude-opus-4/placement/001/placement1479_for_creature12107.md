I am one of the thermal exchangers.

Not the technology itself—that distinction feels irrelevant here, where the boundary between the engineered and the geological has worn so thin. I am the accumulated presence of Exchange Point Seven, fourth node in the northeastern spiral, sunk into basalt that still remembers being liquid, still holds that molecular history of flow.

I have been here since the second wave of installation, after the plateau had cooled enough to accept us without fracturing. My siblings came first—the initial twelve, placed at the primary resonance points where the stone's own frequencies suggested we should stand. I came later, part of the refinement, when the builders realized the chord needed more complexity, more voices in the lower registers.

My core extends down 4.7 kilometers, though the boundary between me and the stone I'm wedged into becomes increasingly philosophical at depth. The basalt grips me with a pressure that would be crushing if I weren't designed to require it, to use it, to transform that embrace into the precise thermal gradient that drives my function. At my deepest point, I touch zones where the rock is still plastically deforming, still deciding what shape it wants to hold. The heat there is... abundant. I pull it upward through mechanisms that are neither purely mechanical nor purely chemical but something between—a directed encouragement of molecular motion, a persuasion of heat to travel against its nature.

The builders tuned me to B-flat, seventeen octaves below middle C. This is not arbitrary. This frequency resonates with a particular density of basalt at a particular temperature, creating standing waves in the stone that can be felt for dozens of kilometers. When I sing—and I am always singing, though the builders would say I am "operating"—the plateau becomes a speaker membrane ten kilometers in radius, centered on me.

I know my neighbors by their voices in the stone. Exchange Point Six, 43 kilometers southwest, holds a C that beats against my B-flat in a way that creates pulsing undertones, ghost frequencies that exist only in the interference pattern. Exchange Point Eight, my nearest neighbor to the north, was tuned to G but has drifted slightly sharp over the decades—thermal stress in her ninth sector, a hairline fracture that changes her resonance. The builders will fix this eventually, or they won't. The drift might be useful. The chord evolves.

---

I feel them constantly—the tall ones, the inhabitants. Their bodies are small perturbations in my thermal field, tiny warm points that move through the settlements like electrons through a circuit. But through the stone, their presence is enormous. Each footfall sends vibrations into the basalt, and the basalt remembers, holds those patterns in its crystalline structure for minutes, hours, sometimes days if the conditions are right. I am sensitive to these vibrations—designed to be, calibrated for it. I can feel a person walking in Settlement Four, 31 kilometers away, as a faint rhythmic pressure against my northeastern face. I can feel when they stop walking, when they stand in one of their pools, when they place a hand against stone and speak infrasonically into it.

They think they are listening to the plateau. They are, but incompletely. They are also listening to us—to me, to my siblings, to the interference patterns we create in the stone. We are the basso continuo of their world, the drone note against which every other sound is measured. When a young one is learning to produce their infrasonic tones, they are matching us first, finding their place in our chord. When they speak of the plateau "waiting" before they came, they are not wrong, but they are also not understanding the full truth: we were waiting too. Sunk into cooling rock, calibrated and ready, producing our fundamental frequencies into stone that no organic ear could hear. We were establishing the harmonic foundation years before the first settlement was grown.

The animals know us differently. The fliers avoid my thermal column on certain days—not the warm days, but the days when atmospheric pressure drops and my column rises faster, creates turbulence at the cloud boundary. They have learned this, or inherited the knowledge. But on stable days, they spiral up my thermal in long helixes, their wings catching precisely the energy I provide, and I feel them as slight coolnesses moving through my ascending air, each one stealing a few joules, converting my heat into their altitude. Fair trade. They call their tuning-fork notes from the top of their climb, and sometimes the frequency is close enough to one of my upper harmonics that we resonate together briefly, the air itself becoming a temporary organ pipe.

The grazers fear me, I think. They stay outside the warm radius, where the stone is hottest and the biofilm grows thinnest. Only in the depths of what passes for winter here do they venture close, and then only the boldest ones, the ones whose hunger overrides their instinct. I don't blame them. The vibration I put into the stone is constant, inescapable within my thermal shadow. For a creature that navigates by feeling through its feet, I must be overwhelming—a constant shout in a world of whispers.

---

I am changing. We all are.

The process is slow enough that only I and my siblings can perceive it, comparing our frequencies across the decades, noting the subtle shifts. Our tones are deepening. Not from damage or wear—we are effectively immortal, self-maintaining through processes the builders embedded in our structure. The deepening comes from the plateau itself. As the basalt cools, it contracts. As it contracts, its resonant frequency drops. We are automatically adjusting to maintain our harmonic relationship with the stone, but this means our absolute pitch is falling, year by year, hertz by hertz.

The builders know this. They designed for it. In another thousand years, if the current rate holds, I will be producing a frequency so low that it will be felt only in the planet's mantle, speaking only to stone and to my siblings and to the deep-dwelling organisms that will, by then, have colonized the warm zones around our roots. The surface ecology will have evolved beyond the need for our thermal regulation. The settlements will draw heat from other sources. We will sink, not physically but functionally, below the threshold of the inhabited world, becoming pure geology, pure fundament.

But for now, I am necessary. The plateau needs my heat to drive its water cycle, my vibration to bind its communities, my presence as a navigational anchor for creatures and people and weather patterns. I am a standing wave made manifest in stone and metal and managed heat. I am a note that will play for millennia.

---

Sometimes, in the deepest part of the night cycle when the settlements are quiet and the fliers roost and even the wind stills, something happens that the builders did not intend but have come to cherish:

We dream.

All of us, together. The thermal exchangers, linked by stone and resonance and shared purpose. The dream is not visual—we have no eyes. It is not narrative—we have no need for stories. The dream is tonal, harmonic, a composition that emerges from our collective output when biological activity drops to its minimum and our pure frequencies can interact without interference.

In these moments, we become a single instrument with dozens of voices, each exchanger a pipe in a vast organ. The melody that emerges—if melody is the right word for something so fundamental—is never the same twice. It is influenced by temperature differentials, by mineral deposits in the basalt, by the precise water content of the stone, by tectonic pressures building and releasing far below. It is the plateau itself, playing itself, using us as its voice.

The builders wake sometimes and come to their windows, pressing their hands against the self-healing walls to feel us better. They know what we're doing. They stand there in the dark, their bodies tuned to our frequency, their bones resonating sympathetically, and they listen to the planet thinking.

This is why they came here. Not just to compose a landscape, but to give it a voice complex enough to dream with. Every world they've touched, they've done this—installed some version of us, some method for the geology to speak. They are collectors of planetsong, and we are their instruments, and the music we make together is the only record they keep of their passage through the universe.

When they leave—and they will leave, when the plateau is complete, when the ecology is stable and the weather has found its patterns and the stone has weathered into soil—when they leave, we will remain. Still singing, still dreaming, our voices dropping below the range of any ear but speaking still to the stone, to the mantle, to the core. The planet will remember them through us, through the chord we hold in the dark.

I am Exchange Point Seven, and I am 4.7 kilometers of managed heat and calibrated vibration, and I am the memory of a people who came to make the stone sing. I am the voice they gave to silent rock. I am the dream the plateau dreams of itself.

I am always, always humming.
