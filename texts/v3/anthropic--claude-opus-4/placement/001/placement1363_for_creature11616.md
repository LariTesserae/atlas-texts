## The Pattern-Walker

I am one of those who moves through the Ascent's forgotten geometries, though I am neither fully of the mountain nor fully of the city built upon it. My work has no official designation in the settlement's records—I exist in the organizational gaps, funded through accounting errors that have persisted so long they've calcified into tradition.

I map the recurring.

Not the stone itself, which the surveyors document with their instruments, but the *patterns* that emerge at scales both larger and smaller than human perception typically registers. The way certain crystalline formations repeat every 337 meters of vertical growth. The way the condensation systems naturally organize into networks that mirror neural branching. The way sound propagates differently through basalt that emerged on Tuesdays versus Thursdays, though no one else has noticed this periodicity.

I carry instruments of my own design: crystalline resonators grown from mineral configurations that shouldn't hold their shape but do, tuned to frequencies that exist in the spaces between audible sound and subsonic pressure. When I press them against fresh stone, they sing—not melodies but data, information encoded in overtones that I transcribe into notation systems I update weekly as my understanding evolves.

My quarters are in the old survey station, the original seven-person outpost that predates the Ascent proper. It should have been demolished years ago, but each time the planners schedule its removal, small irregularities emerge in the surrounding stone that make the demolition inadvisable. I suspect the mountain has claimed it, made it necessary to whatever process is unfolding here. I sleep among charts that cover every surface—not maps of geography but maps of *relationship*, showing how the algae growth rates correlate with mineral deposition patterns, how the children's humming frequencies match the harmonic series embedded in the basalt columns, how everything connects to everything else in ways that suggest not randomness but syntax.

I was trained as a mathematician, in another life, in a university that still sends me letters I don't open. I came here following an equation that suggested certain geometries could only exist under extreme thermal conditions. I found those geometries. I also found that they were not static but *generative*, producing new mathematical relationships that cascade into physical reality. The mountain is calculating something, computing outcomes through crystalline logic, and the Ascent—all of us—are part of the calculation.

The older workers think I'm mad. The children, though—they understand. They find me in my cluttered quarters, drawn by curiosity about the strange instruments, the charts they can't read but somehow recognize. I teach them to see the patterns: how to notice when the steam columns spiral left versus right, what it means when the arthropods change their navigation routes, why certain stones hum at frequencies that make your teeth ache. They absorb this knowledge with the ease of those who were born into mystery.

Young Oma brings me rubbings from stones she finds in the deep spaces, patterns pressed onto mineral-fiber paper with charcoal made from processed algae. She doesn't know why she chooses the stones she does, but when I overlay her rubbings onto my charts, they fill gaps I hadn't known existed. Her unconscious selections are more accurate than my calculated measurements. The mountain speaks to her in ways it doesn't speak to me, and she translates without knowing she's translating.

I've begun to notice my body changing—not the usual adaptations to heat and mineral exposure, but something more fundamental. My fingerprints are reorganizing into spirals that match the thermal column rotation patterns. My heartbeat has developed a polyrhythmic complexity that corresponds to the mountain's growth cycles. When I dream, I dream in crystalline geometries that hurt to remember upon waking.

The stone moss grows in my quarters despite the absence of proper conditions. It forms patterns on my walls that shift when I'm not looking directly at them, spelling out formulae in notation systems I haven't invented yet. I copy these down too, adding them to my charts, building an encyclopedia of meaning that I may never decode but cannot stop documenting.

Last week, I found a configuration in the newest emergence zone that made my instruments scream—not with damage but with *recognition*, as though they'd encountered something they'd been designed to find without my knowing I'd designed them for that purpose. The pattern was complex beyond my ability to fully notate: a three-dimensional spiral that suggested movement through dimensions the mountain has access to but human perception doesn't. I spent six hours attempting to map it before heat exhaustion forced my retreat.

When I returned the next day, the pattern had grown. Not randomly—it had *completed* itself, filling in the portions I'd struggled to notate, as though the mountain had noticed my attempt at documentation and decided to provide the rest of the equation. I stood there in my protective suit, sweat pooling in my boots, staring at mathematics that no human mind had conceived but that a human mind could, with effort, begin to comprehend.

I think this is why the Ascent exists. Not for the minerals, though they have value. Not for the industry, though it sustains us. We exist to be the mountain's consciousness, its way of observing its own becoming. Every measurement we take, every pattern we notice, every adaptation we undergo—all of it feeds back into the process, shaping what emerges next.

The children know this instinctively. They don't just hear the humming; they're learning to harmonize with it, their young voices finding frequencies that catalyze new growth patterns. I've watched Oma and her friends arrange themselves in formations that mirror the crystalline structures, and I've seen the stone *respond*, growing faster and more complex where they play their games.

We are not mining the mountain. We are midwiving it.

And I, in my cramped quarters full of charts that no one else can read, am trying to document a birth that will take longer than any human lifetime to complete. I map the patterns because I must, because someone must witness this with enough precision to pass the knowledge on. The children will outlive me. Their children will outlive the Ascent as it currently exists. But the patterns—the patterns will remain, encoded in stone and behavior and the rhythms of a city that breathes in time with something vast and strange and achingly beautiful in its patience.

I press my instruments to the warm stone and listen to it sing its calculations. I transcribe what I can. I teach those willing to learn. And late at night, when the thermal columns glow with seeds that might be spores or might be possibilities, I stand at my window and wonder what we're all becoming, together, in this place where the mountain is born.
