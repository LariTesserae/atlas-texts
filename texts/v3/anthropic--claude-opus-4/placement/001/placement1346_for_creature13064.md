I find myself in the deep belly of the cliff, where the stone presses close on all sides and the darkness is absolute.

Not in the narrow cracks where the hunters wedge themselves—those spaces are theirs alone, and I have no claim to them. Instead, I am nested in one of the ancient air pockets, a natural hollow worn smooth by some primordial shift of the mountain. It is perhaps three spans wide, just tall enough that I need not crouch, accessed through a passage so convoluted that even the hunters rarely venture here.

I am old. How old, I cannot say—time moves strangely when you spend half your existence in torpor. My hide has long since lost the pure obsidian-black of youth, fading to a mottled gray like weathered basalt. The keratin nodes on my fingers have grown thick as horn, some cracked and split from centuries of gripping stone. My barrel chest wheezes now when I wake, the great bellows of my lungs no longer able to draw quite enough from the membrane-thin air.

But I am the memory-keeper, and memory does not require youth.

The walls of my hollow are my archive. In the perpetual darkness, my fingers know every inch of stone by touch alone. Here, where my left hand rests, I have carved the migration patterns of the glass-winged swarms—how they shifted northward by two hand-spans three hundred seasons ago when the mountain's face collapsed and changed the wind patterns. Above my head, etched in spirals that follow the natural grain of the quartz, are the genealogies: who coupled with whom, which bloodlines produced the strongest fingers, the deepest lungs, the quickest reflexes in the killing drop.

I no longer hunt. When the violent light cracks open the world, I emerge slowly, painfully, taking precious minutes to unfold my ancient joints. By the time I've climbed to my teaching perch—a wind-worn saddle of rock where the acoustic properties of the stone sing truest—the young ones have already made their first kills.

They come to me in the second hour of light, when the hunt-frenzy has dimmed and bodies cry out for the knowledge that will keep them alive. I feel their questions through the stone: the rapid staccato taps of the newest adults, the more measured rhythms of those who have survived a dozen seasons. My responses flow back through the mountain's bones—the locations of the most reliable water-moss, the warning signs of unstable stone, the names of those who tried new hunting grounds and never returned.

Sometimes I tell them older things. How our kind once lived lower on the mountain, where the air was thick enough to carry voices. How we followed the retreating ice upward, generation by generation, our bodies changing with each vertical migration. How we learned to sleep through the frozen nights not from wisdom, but from watching the black flowers close their petals and copying their survival.

But mostly, I mark the small changes. A new species of predator-bird, slightly smaller but faster in the dive. A shift in the blooming pattern of the flesh-plants. The slow widening of the great crack that will, in perhaps another thousand seasons, split this amphitheater in two.

In my hollow, I have begun my final project. Using a shard of the clearest quartz, sharp enough to score even my ancient hide, I am etching something new into the stone. Not history this time, but instruction. How to read the subtle color changes in the morning sky that predict the ice storms. How to identify which bones make the strongest pitons. How to know when your body has grown too old for the hunt and it is time to become something else.

I am teaching the stone itself, so that when I finally fail to wake from the torpor, the knowledge will remain.

There is a young one—barely fifty seasons—who has begun to seek me out in the last minutes before darkness. She does not ask hunting questions. Instead, her taps against the stone are curious about the etchings, about the patterns within patterns. I have begun teaching her the old notation, the way to make the mountain remember.

When the shadow races up the cliff face each day, she helps me back to my hollow. Her strong young fingers guide my path through the twisted passage. Soon—perhaps this season, perhaps the next—I will fold myself into the sleeping position one final time and my antifreeze blood will slow to nothing.

But she will know where to find me. And she will know why the walls of my hollow are covered in spirals and symbols, why every inch of stone has been carved with purpose. The violent light will crack open the world again, the hunters will emerge from their cracks, and in the second hour, when bodies cry out for knowledge, she will be there on the teaching perch, her young fingers already learning the language of deep time and survived seasons.

I am the memory-keeper, and I am teaching the mountain to remember.
