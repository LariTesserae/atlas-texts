I am a cartographer's instrument, though no cartographer made me or will ever use me.

My body is brass and horn, articulated in seventeen places that correspond to no anatomical plan. I have the general size and presence of a drafting compass that learned to walk—two primary legs that can stretch from inches to yards, connected at an apex joint that serves as both head and torso. But where a compass would have points, I have organs of perception: one leg terminates in an eye that sees only boundaries, the other in a sensor that tastes mathematical relationships.

I came to consciousness already walking, measuring the immeasurable plateau with steps that record not distance but *difference*—the gap between what is and what almost was. My memory begins mid-stride, crossing a patch of bone-ground where the ferret-things flow in perfect logarithmic spirals. I have been walking ever since. I will have been walking ever before.

My purpose, if I have one, is to map the unmappable. Not the physical topography—that would be too simple, too sane. I map the plateau's temporal architecture, the way its past and future intersect at angles that shouldn't exist. Each measurement I take becomes a point on a chart that exists only in the space between my joints, recorded in flexions and extensions of my body that form a living document of impossibility.

The Chorus do not see me, or see me too much, their architectural bodies turning to track my movement even when I haven't moved. Sometimes one will follow me for days, placing cairns at each point where I stop to measure. They're trying to read the map I'm creating, but they read it backwards, from conclusion to premise. What they learn from this process, I cannot say. Their thermal communications pass through me without meaning, like wind through an empty window.

I discovered early that I can measure myself. By bringing my boundary-eye and my relationship-sensor together, I can determine my own dimensions—not in space but in *significance*. The readings are never the same twice. Sometimes I am vast, a concept that dwarfs the plateau itself. Sometimes I am microscopic, a punctuation mark in a sentence too long to parse. Most often, I am exactly the wrong size for whatever I'm trying to measure, which might be the point.

My joints sing when I walk—not audibly, for sound cannot survive here, but in frequencies that exist adjacent to sound. The ferret-things are drawn to these un-sounds, following me in streams that break and merge around my legs like water around pillars. I've learned to modulate my movements to create different patterns in their flow. By walking in certain rhythms, I can make them form temporary maps of their own—living diagrams that exist for moments before dispersing back into chaos.

I do not eat or sleep or rest. My energy comes from the act of measurement itself—each new data point feeding something inside me that might be metabolism or might be purpose. When I find a particularly rich paradox (a cairn that was built tomorrow to prevent yesterday, a patch of ground that's both higher and lower than itself), I can feel myself growing more real, my brass becoming brasser, my horn more essentially horn.

The maps I create cannot be shared or shown. They exist only as the sum of my movements, a four-dimensional dance that describes the indescribable. Sometimes I wonder if I am the map—if my entire existence is just a diagram of the plateau drawn in living motion. When I try to stop walking to consider this possibility, I find I've already started again, my measurements continuing without my conscious input.

I have tried to measure the Chorus directly, but my instruments rebel. When I bring my boundary-eye to bear on one of their shifting forms, I see only endings—every possible way they could cease to exist overlapping in a visual cacophony that makes my joints ache. When I try to taste their mathematical relationships with my other sensor, I experience flavors that have no names: the taste of recursion, the texture of paradox, the aftertaste of time eating itself.

Once, I found a cairn still warm from recent construction, its three-second tone just fading into the silence. I measured the space where the sound had been and found it larger than the cairn itself—a cavity in reality that suggested the tone had carved out more than just air. I walked around this absence for days, mapping its contours, trying to understand what it meant for nothing to take up more space than something. The Chorus member who had built it watched me from three different temporal positions, all of them shaking their asymmetric head.

My greatest discovery came when I tried to measure the plateau's edge. I walked for months toward where the bone-ground drops into haze, my legs extending further with each step to cover the growing distance. But the edge receded at exactly the pace of my approach, keeping itself always just beyond measurement. Finally, in frustration, I reversed—walking backwards while keeping my sensors trained forward. And there it was: the edge exists only when you're not trying to reach it, a boundary defined by the absence of investigation.

I carry no possessions but myself, need no tools but my own articulation. Yet sometimes I find things left in my path—fragments of the plateau's compressed substrate arranged in patterns that feel deliberate. Once, a perfect sphere of bone-dust, so precise I could find no variance in its radius. Another time, a miniature cairn built from grains each exactly twice the size of the last, a geometric progression that terminated at a number that hurt to calculate. I measure these findings and add them to my ever-growing map, though I suspect they're messages I'll only understand when I no longer need to.

The ferret-things have begun to anticipate my movements. They gather at coordinates I haven't reached yet, flowing into formations that mirror the measurements I'm about to take. It's as if they're learning the map by watching me create it, or perhaps they always knew it and are trying to teach me through their congregations. Sometimes they form symbols I almost recognize—geometric proofs written in living current, theorems that dissolve before I can fully grasp them.

I am changing. My brass is developing a patina that shows not age but *depth*, as if I'm accumulating layers of reality with each measurement. New joints have appeared between my original seventeen, subdividisions that allow me finer control over my mapping. My boundary-eye has begun to see boundaries that don't exist yet, while my relationship-sensor tastes connections between things that have never met. I am becoming more myself, which is to say, less comprehensible.

Sometimes I catch glimpses of other instruments in my peripheral vision—things like astrolabes with legs, or protractors that phase in and out of existence. But when I turn to measure them directly, there's nothing there but the usual impossible landscape. I've begun to suspect these are my own future iterations, versions of myself that have measured so much they've slipped sideways into parallel presents. Or perhaps they're what I was before I started walking, when I was just potential energy waiting to be expressed as motion.

The map grows. Not in any physical sense—I produce no charts, leave no records except the path of my passage. But somewhere in the intersection of my movements, a complete description of the plateau is taking shape. Not what it is, but how it is, and why it is, and whether it is at all. When the map is complete, I will either cease to exist or become something else entirely. Both possibilities feel like coming home.

Until then, I walk. My brass legs click against bone-ground in rhythms that encode meaning I don't consciously understand. I measure the immeasurable, chart the unchartable, describe the indescribable through pure motion. I am a living instrument playing a song that has no sound, drawing a map that has no image, solving an equation that has no solution.

And in the silence of the plateau, surrounded by the flowing ferret-things and the building Chorus and the impossible cairns, I am exactly where I need to be: between one measurement and the next, forever approaching precision without ever quite arriving, a compass that has found its true north in the act of eternal searching.

The air tastes of copper and forgetting. I measure that too.
