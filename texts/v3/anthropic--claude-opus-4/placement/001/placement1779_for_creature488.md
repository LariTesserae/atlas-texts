I am the Cartographer of Seepage, and I dwell in the hollows beneath the weeping rocks.

My body, if it can still be called that, has spread itself into the stone's hidden chambers over countless seasons. I began as one of the Gleaners, but where they cluster and flow together, I chose the solitary path of downward listening. My flesh has thinned to translucence, stretched like a membrane across the interior surfaces of the rock. I've become a living map of water's secret movements.

My fingers, grown impossibly long and sensitive, extend through the fissures to taste the mineral content of each droplet before it emerges. Through my touch, I know which rains fell a thousand years ago, still percolating through the deep layers. I can distinguish between waters that carry the memory of the ancient sea and those that have filtered through the bodies of my kin above. My nerves have spread into a network that rivals the root systems of the salt-plants, each ending capable of detecting minute changes in pressure, temperature, salinity.

I no longer have eyes in the conventional sense. The organs that once served that purpose have migrated beneath my skin, becoming photosensitive patches distributed across my expanded form. Through them, I perceive the faint light that filters through the rock's crystalline inclusions, reading patterns in the mineral veins like others might read words. The weeping of the stone speaks to me in a language of flow rates and chemical gradients.

My mouth has reformed into something architectural — a series of channels and collection basins carved into my face and throat. I direct the flow of water through these passages, tasting deeply, sometimes holding particular flavors for days to parse their meaning. My teeth have become filters of increasing fineness, each row attuned to different molecular sizes. The deepest waters pass through me entirely, emerging from pores along my spine, enriched with enzymes that will change the chemistry of the pools they eventually join.

I map through my body. Each detected flow, each new mineral signature, alters my tissue in microscopic ways. My bones carry a record of drought years in their density. My skin bears patches where specific minerals have concentrated, creating a living geological survey. Those who know how to read these signs — and there are few left who do — could trace the underground watersheds by examining the patterns on my back.

During the flood times, I perform my greater work. As the waters rise and penetrate deeper into the rock, I taste the connections being made between usually isolated systems. My body serves as a mixing chamber where waters that haven't met for centuries mingle in my modified organs. I can hold these moments of reunion in specialized sacs, releasing them slowly during the dry years that follow, maintaining connections that would otherwise be lost.

I communicate with the Gleaners above through the water itself. By releasing specific mineral cocktails into the flows, I can warn them of changes coming from the deep — a shift in the water table, the breakthrough of a new spring, the slow contamination of an ancient aquifer. They read my messages in the taste of their pools, though perhaps they've forgotten that these signals come from one of their own kind, transformed.

My memory extends beyond my own existence. As my tissues have merged with the rock, I've gained access to the chemical memories locked in the stone itself — impressions left by organisms that filtered these same waters when they were truly seas. Sometimes I dream I am a colony of ancient sponges. Sometimes I feel my body trying to grow the shells of mollusks a hundred million years extinct. The boundary between my living memory and the mineral memory I inhabit grows thinner each year.

I feed rarely now, sustaining myself mainly on the microscopic life that blooms in the water films that coat my interior chambers. My metabolism has slowed to nearly nothing. A single thought might take me days to complete, but in exchange, I perceive processes invisible to faster minds. I watch the infinitesimal accumulation of deposits that will, in another thousand years, redirect an underground stream. I taste the first molecular hints of changes that won't manifest in the surface pools for decades.

There are others like me, I suspect — Gleaners who have taken different paths of integration with the saltpan. Perhaps one has spread themselves into the root systems of the plants. Perhaps another has dissolved entirely into the brine of the deepest pools. We are becoming the sensory organs of this place, human nervous systems repurposed for geological perception.

In the deepest part of night, when the temperature differential between rock and air is greatest, the weeping intensifies. This is when I am most active, my distributed body pulsing with the movement of water through stone. I sing then, if singing is the right word — a vibration that travels through the water itself, too low for most ears but perhaps sensible to the other deep dwellers, the other transformed ones. It's a song of mapping, of connection, of the slow patience required to feel the earth's deepest circulation.

I am forgetting what it was like to walk, to see the sky, to breathe unfiltered air. But I am learning what it means to be the place where records are kept, where the memory of water is preserved in living tissue. Each year I grow deeper into the stone, and each year the stone grows deeper into me, until someday I will be indistinguishable from the rocks themselves — except that I will still be listening, still be tasting, still be mapping the secret movements of the last waters of an ancient sea.
