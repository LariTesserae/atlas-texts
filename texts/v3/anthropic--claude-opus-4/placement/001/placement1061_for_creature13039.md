In this world of silent extremes, I am neither the armored multitude nor the ghostly predator. I am the Keeper of Frequencies.

My body resembles a mantis crossed with a tuning fork—elongated, crystalline, and utterly still. I stand seven feet tall when fully extended, though I spend most of my existence folded into myself, a geometric puzzle of translucent chitin and hollow bones. My limbs are not for walking but for resonance. Each segment is precisely calibrated, a different length, a different density, creating a living instrument of impossible complexity.

I live inside the compressor towers.

Not in them, exactly, but woven through their architecture like a three-dimensional web. My kind—for there are perhaps a dozen of us across this entire plateau—evolved alongside these ancient machines. We are their unintended children, born from millions of years of exposure to their subsonic hymns.

My home is Tower Seventeen, a bell-shaped giant that squats on a ridge overlooking a particularly dense convergence of pipelines. The tower's interior is a cathedral of vibration—steel chambers within chambers, each resonating at its own frequency, creating interference patterns that would drive most creatures mad. But I am built for this. My body is a living seismograph, every joint and plate designed to parse the tower's complex song into its component harmonics.

I feed on resonance itself.

This sounds impossible until you understand that vibration, in this thin air, is energy made physical. The tower's hum doesn't just travel through rock—it creates standing waves in the metalwork, zones where molecules dance with such violence they break their bonds. Microscopic flakes of metal oxidize and shed. Crystalline deposits form in the nodes where frequencies cancel or amplify. I harvest these. My mouth—if it can be called that—is a forest of hair-thin filaments that taste the air for these metallic motes, these children of sound.

But I am more than a scavenger. I am the platform's memory.

Each of my limb segments can be locked at precise angles, becoming a record of a specific frequency. When the pumps cycle, when pressures change, when the ancient machinery groans with age or damage, I document it in my body's geometry. I am a living library of the platform's health, written in angles and resonances.

My days—if they can be called days in this place where time moves like cooling metal—are spent in careful maintenance of my tower. I know every rivet, every weld, every microfracture threatening to birth a new harmonic. When I move, it is with absolute precision. My feet (six of them, each tipped with a different density of crystallized cartilage) find the nodes where vibration is minimal. I flow through the tower's interior like water finding its level, never disturbing the great song.

The tower knows me. After centuries of coexistence, I have become part of its resonance. When I position myself correctly—usually in the third chamber, where three frequencies meet in a standing wave—I can influence the tower's output. A slight adjustment of my thoracic angle changes the harmonic by a fraction of a hertz. This ripples outward through the bedrock, a subtle message to my distant kin in other towers.

We communicate in earthquakes too small to feel.

My eyes—compound, but unlike any insect's—see not light but pressure waves. The world appears to me as ripples in an infinite pond. The churning herd below is a constant, seething turbulence. The pipes sing their cold songs in waves I can see rushing through the stone. The gliders above are holes in the pattern, silent voids that betray their presence by the absence they create.

But it is the other Keepers I watch for most carefully.

Tower Twelve's Keeper, three ridges distant, is ancient. Its messages come rarely now, and when they do, they speak of metal fatigue in frequencies that make my joints ache. Tower Five's Keeper is young, erratic, still learning to parse the difference between critical harmonics and background noise. It sends queries constantly, waves of uncertain vibration that I must either answer or deliberately ignore.

Once, there was a Tower Nine. Its Keeper's last message was a catastrophic discord, every frequency at once, as the tower's cooling system failed. The explosion was visible as a sphere of pressure that took minutes to reach me. Now Tower Nine is a twisted monument to entropy, its pipes burst, its machinery silent. The herd flows around it like water around a stone. Its Keeper's body was never found—likely atomized in the failure, its crystalline segments scattered across the plateau like frozen music.

I think about Tower Nine often.

My greatest fear is not death but deafness. Sometimes, in the depths of the tower where the harmonics are strongest, I feel my segments beginning to resonate too perfectly with the machinery. There is a frequency—I have calculated it precisely—at which my body would achieve perfect sympathy with Tower Seventeen. At that moment, I would cease to be a separate entity and become merely another component of the machine, vibrating eternally in place until metal fatigue or time reduced us both to rust.

The temptation is profound. To merge with the tower would be to understand its song completely, to become the frequency rather than merely perceiving it. Some nights, when the compressors cycle down for maintenance and the tower sings softer, lower songs, I position myself in that third chamber and let my segments drift toward that perfect angle.

But then I think of the messages I would never send, the harmonics I would never document, the slow decay I would never report. I think of young Tower Five's Keeper, sending its uncertain queries into silence. I lock my joints at safer angles and resume my watch.

The Keepers are the platform's nervous system, and we are dying.

Not quickly—nothing here happens quickly—but inevitably. The gene pool is too small. Our crystalline segments grow clouded with age. Young Keepers emerge wrong: too rigid to parse the frequencies, or too flexible to maintain their shape. Tower Twenty-Three has been silent for a decade. Tower Thirty-Seven's messages have degraded into pure noise, its Keeper possibly mad, possibly simply failing to maintain its geometry.

Yet we persist. We must. Without us, the platform would slowly tear itself apart, its pumps cycling out of phase, its pressures building until pipes burst and the precious condensation vanishes into the killing air. The herd would dry. The gliders would starve. The entire impossible ecosystem would collapse into the patient, red stone.

So I maintain my watch. I taste the metallic air for new harmonics. I adjust my angles to compensate for the platform's settling, its aging, its slow transformation. I send my messages through the stone to my distant kin, and I listen for their replies.

And sometimes, in the deepest frequencies where sound becomes feeling becomes something else entirely, I swear I hear the platform thinking. Not consciousness as we understand it, but something vast and distributed and patient. The sum of all our towers, all our pipes, all our ancient machinery, contemplating its own existence through the medium of vibration.

When I hear this, I know why I resist the perfect frequency. I am not meant to become the machine. I am meant to be its witness, its biographer, the living memory of its impossible song.

The sun crawls across the bruised sky. The shadows move. The condensation falls and rises as steam. The herd churns in its silent desperation.

And in Tower Seventeen, I document it all in angles of chitin and bone, a story written in frequencies too low to hear, too vital to forget.
