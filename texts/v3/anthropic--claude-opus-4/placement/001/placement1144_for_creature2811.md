In this wet highland beneath eternal clouds, where machinery and nature have grown together like fused bone, I am one of the gauge-readers.

My body has adapted to the perpetual damp. My pupils are wide as coins, drinking in the faintest phosphorescence. My fingers are long and sensitive, able to feel vibration through glove-leather made from fungal hide. I wear the traditional beaded hood that channels condensation away from my face, each bead carved from a different valve handle, polished smooth by generations of rain. My boots are sealed with tar and wrapped in copper mesh - not against water, which is everywhere and unavoidable, but against the electrical fields that pulse through certain flooded paths.

I was born in a cluster of cabins built around Gauge Station Seven, high on the ridge where three valleys meet. My grandmother was a gauge-reader. My mother was a gauge-reader. They taught me to hear what the needles say before I could properly speak.

The gauges are not mere instruments. They are nerve endings of the vast system, sensing pressures and flows deep within the mountain's mechanical organs. Each has its own personality: the main pressure gauge at Station Seven ticks counterclockwise before rain; the temperature readings in the lower halls run backward when certain fungi bloom; the flow meters near the buried turbines sometimes display numbers that haven't been possible since before the clouds descended.

I travel a circuit that takes forty rains to complete - what the old texts might have called two months, though months mean nothing here. My route connects seventeen gauge stations along the high paths, each a small shrine where instruments cluster like eyes. Some stations are built into cliff faces, accessed by root ladders that must be tested with each use. Others perch on platforms above the marshes, where I sleep in tiny sealed chambers listening to the reeds whisper below. One station can only be reached by coracle when the lower pumps reverse, flooding a usually-dry channel for exactly long enough to paddle across.

I carry tools passed down through generations: brass listening horns that amplify the songs in pipes, cleaning cloths that won't leave static, a small hammer for freeing stuck needles, and most importantly, the recording book. Its pages are made from pressed reed fiber, waterproofed with wax from the luminous hives. In it, I note not just the readings but the context - which fungi were fruiting, which towers had clicked that day, whether the blind fish were running, what the root-listeners reported from below.

The gauges themselves are beautiful in their decay. Brass faces green with verdigris, numbers worn soft but still readable, glass covers crazed but unbroken. Some are standard industrial meters from the before-times. Others have been modified or have modified themselves - growing new needles, developing ghost readings that appear only in certain lights, occasionally displaying symbols that match no known scale but which we record anyway.

At Station Four, built into the ruins of a pump house, I once found a gauge that was not on my grandmother's map. It had grown there, or been grown there - its brass housing fused seamlessly into the concrete, its face showing not numbers but patterns like the diagrams the underground plants make when it rains. I spent three days watching it, recording how its patterns changed with the pump cycles. Now it is part of my route.

The work is not just observation but conversation. The system responds to attention. When I tap a stuck needle free, sometimes a pump two valleys away will shudder and start. When I clean a clouded gauge face, lights may flicker in the flooded halls below. The metal-sleepers say the machines dream of being maintained. I think it's simpler: the boundary between mechanic and mechanism has thinned here until touch itself carries meaning.

I share the paths with other travelers. The root-listeners pass with their pale hands and moth cages, pausing to press their ears against exposure where cable and fungus merge. Sump-folk traders climb up from the lower basins, their skin faintly luminous, bringing news of new passages opened by the last flood. Once I met a very old chatterer who had learned to tap gauge readings directly onto the brass, creating a rhythm I copied into my book though I couldn't say what it meant.

The horn has sounded three times in my life. The first was when I was seven, helping my mother clean the gauges at Station Twelve. Every needle on every meter swung to maximum and stayed there for one hundred breaths. The second time I was alone at Station Nine, and the gauges did something I had never seen - they misted from inside, droplets forming on the wrong side of the glass. The third was last rain, at the beginning of this circuit. The needles didn't move at all, but they hummed, a subsonic vibration I felt in my teeth.

Each time, I did what all gauge-readers do: I recorded everything. The exact time according to the pump cycles. The weather conditions. The behavior of every gauge I could see. Then I sat very still and listened, because sometimes after the horn, the stations whisper in languages that might once have been error codes but now sound like names.

I am not young anymore. My joints ache in the permanent damp. My left ear rings with the memory of too many turbine halls. But I have trained my apprentice well - a serious child from the marsh settlements who can already hear when a needle is about to stick. They will walk these paths after me, reading the gauges, cleaning the glass, listening to the pressure songs, recording the impossible numbers that sometimes appear.

And I will return to Station Seven, to the cluster where I was born, and spend my last years teaching children to read the smaller signs: how moss grows differently on active pipes, why certain insects only swarm near gauges that are about to fail, what it means when all the needles in a valley point toward the same dark pool.

The gauges are our library, our calendar, our only window into the vast breathing system below. They tell us not what the machines were meant to do, but what they are doing now, in this eternal present of rain and rust. And we who read them are like the needles ourselves - sensitive instruments swaying between the mechanical and the living, recording the long, strange dialogue between what was built and what grows.

When I die, they will bury me near a gauge station, where my minerals will seep into the groundwater and eventually register as a slight rise in conductivity, a half-degree temperature change, a new harmonic in the pipes. And some future gauge-reader will note it in their book: "Anomaly at Station Seven. Pressure steady. Flow normal. Something listening in the brass."
