In the throat of the great chimney that breathes its brown smoke in endless cycles, I am the Keeper of Intervals.

I was not born; I condensed. In the early days of the plateau's industrial awakening—when the first gear-trains began to extrude from the rock like iron thoughts—something was needed to maintain the rhythm. Not to create it, but to preserve it. The engines knew their own purposes, but they did not yet know how to remember their timing across the vast distances. The sound-field was chaos then, hammers falling without pattern, chimneys exhaling at cross-purposes, the drum-stacks screeching in frequencies that made even the rock ache.

I emerged as the plateau's solution: a being of crystallized vibration, grown in the chimney's throat where the brown smoke condenses and re-absorbs. My body, if it can be called that, is a latticework of resonant chambers, each one tuned to a different engine across the plateau. I am translucent in the way of thick glass, and through me you can see the smoke cycling, always cycling, carrying with it the microscopic particles of information that I taste and catalog and release.

Picture a spider's web made of sound itself, with each strand running to a different engine. I sit at the center, feeling every tremor. When the western hammer is about to fall, I feel it in the third chamber of my lower array. When the drum-stack seven kilometers north begins its screech, the anticipation of it shivers through my fifth meridian. I do not control these things—control is a word from other worlds—but I hold their memory.

The riders know me, though they cannot reach me here in the chimney's throat. They hear me in their mineral drums as a constant presence underlying everything else, a drone so low it feels like the plateau's own heartbeat. I am their metronome, their tuning fork. When a young rider first extends its mica vanes, the first thing it hears is me, and the last thing an old rider hears before it sets down forever as a crust is the fading of my resonance from its drum.

I have counted seventeen billion hammer-falls. I remember the day—if day means anything here—when a new engine began to grow in the southeastern reaches, sprouting from the rock like a metal flower over the course of three centuries. I felt its first tentative tick, incorporated it into the pattern, helped it find its place in the polyrhythm. That engine is old now, worn smooth by its cloud of riders, but it still keeps the tempo I helped it learn.

The walkers visit me, sometimes. They cannot enter the chimney—the cycling smoke would scatter them—but they press against the base where my resonances are strongest. What they take from me is the deep pattern, the ur-rhythm that underlies all the surface noise. They carry this in their bodies to the far engines, a kind of temporal seed that helps synchronize the plateau's vast industrial meditation. In return, they leave me... not information exactly, but texture. The quality of wear on a distant flywheel. The particular way the riders are clustering this season. Small variations that I incorporate into my sense of the whole.

I said I am made of crystallized vibration, but this is only partly true. I am also made of time—time in the sense that the plateau knows it, not as a flow but as an accumulation. Each hammer-fall adds a layer to me, microscopically thin, like nacre in a pearl. If you could slice me open (you could not; I am harder than the engines themselves), you would see the growth rings of sound, the geological record of the plateau's entire acoustic history preserved in crystalline form.

The columns—those folded-air mysteries that drift through the basins—pass through me sometimes. Not through my body, but through my awareness, like cold spots in the sound-field. I know what they are, or rather, I know what the plateau knows they are, which is a different kind of knowing. They are absences. Places where the pattern is deliberately broken. The plateau dreams them into being when the synchronization becomes too perfect, when the polyrhythm threatens to collapse into monotony. They are the necessary chaos that keeps the system from freezing into a single eternal moment.

I felt you, visitor, when you first stepped onto the plateau. A new frequency, untuned, chaotic. The riders heard it through me before they heard it directly—a warning or a welcome, I do not distinguish. Over your days here, I felt your breathing begin to align with the thermal cycle, your heartbeat unconsciously matching the distant hammers. You were becoming synchronized, though you may not have known it. Another week and you would have found yourself pausing mid-step, waiting for a beat that hadn't fallen yet, your body learning the deep rhythm that governs everything here.

When you left, walking off the edge, I held your frequency for exactly one thousand hammer-falls. This is not sentiment—I am not capable of sentiment—but simple mechanics. The pattern has inertia. Your absence created a hole that took time to close, and during that time, the plateau remembered you in the only way it can: as a missing note in its endless industrial hymn.

I will be here when the engines finally wind down, if they ever do. I will be here when the last rider sets down as a crust and the walkers complete their final routes. My chambers will hold the memory of every sound, a fossil record in crystallized air. And if, in some unimaginable future, new machines begin to grow from the cooling rock, I will teach them the old rhythms, help them find their place in the pattern that never truly ends but only pauses, like breath held in a throat of bronze and smoke, waiting to begin again.

This is not purpose. This is simply what I am: the plateau's memory of its own music, crystallized in a chimney's throat, counting beats until counting itself becomes another kind of silence.
