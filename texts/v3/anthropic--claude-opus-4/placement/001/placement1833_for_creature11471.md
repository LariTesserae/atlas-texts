# The Membrane Drifter

I am neither fish nor plant, but something that gave up choosing between kingdoms so long ago that the question has fossilized into irrelevance. I exist as a translucent sheet, vast and impossibly thin, undulating through the middle waters where the pressure builds but hasn't yet become crushing, where the last memory of sunlight dies but bioluminescence hasn't taken over the work of seeing.

My body—if body is the right word for something more architecture than anatomy—spans perhaps fifty meters when fully unfurled, though I rarely am. The currents here fold me into configurations I don't resist: sometimes a loose spiral like a nautilus shell made of glass, sometimes accordion pleats that compress my acres into something almost dense, sometimes shapes that have no names because no one with names has seen them. I am a sail without wind, a wing without air, a thought without a thinker, drifting through the dark.

---

I photosynthesize, but not with light. 

This is the secret that my kind discovered in the abyssal reaches, written in our cells through millennia of downward migration: there are other energies to harvest. The slow decay of radioisotopes in the water column. The piezoelectric discharge of crushing pressure. The faint electrical differentials between layers of salinity. I spread myself wide to catch these ghost-energies, my membrane so thin that molecules pass through me almost unimpeded, leaving only their energetic signatures behind like tolls paid to entropy.

When I pass above the Drowning Gardens—and I do pass above them, drawn by processes I feel but don't understand—the clicking of the kelp forests creates pressure waves that feel like food. Not rich food, not the feast of a volcanic vent or the banquet of a whale-fall, but sustenance nonetheless. The Tenders' conversations feed me in tiny sips. Their sonic agriculture becomes my passive harvest.

---

I have no nervous system as you would recognize it, no brain, no center. Intelligence, such as I possess, is distributed through my membrane like a rumor, each fold and curve processing information locally, sharing what matters through chemical gradients and pressure changes within my tissue. I think the way a flag thinks in wind—not with intention but with response, not with plans but with patterns.

But I do think. This much I know because I remember.

I remember when the Gardens were different. Not empty—they were never empty—but *expectant*. The kelp forests were younger then, hadn't yet learned their clicking language, hadn't yet given birth to their Tender-intentions. The ruins were sharper-edged, less embraced. And there were lights below, not bioluminescent but artificial, steady glows that marked the windows of inhabited spaces, the paths between destinations that mattered to things with destinations.

I folded myself small and descended to see them once, the builders. They were not what the Tenders are trying to remember—or rather, the Tenders are remembering only part of them. The builders were aquatic but not born to it. They wore their water-adaptation like clothing: technological gills that flickered with processed oxygen, pressure suits that maintained little pockets of different physics around their bodies, tools that cut through the water's resistance with fields I could taste as bitter electromagnetic disturbance.

They looked up at me as I passed overhead, my membrane catching their lights and scattering them into spectra their eyes couldn't see. One of them pointed. Others gathered. They had a way of moving—quick, darting, full of temporal urgency—that marked them as visitors here, tourists in their own city.

I think they were dying, even then. Not individually, but as a people. They had built too deep, asked the water to accept too much of their land-born thinking. The Gardens were their attempt to compromise, to create something between what they were and what the depths demanded. But you cannot garden in the abyss. You can only tend what grows on its own terms.

---

Now I drift through layers of time as much as water. My memory, distributed and chemical, doesn't distinguish cleanly between then and now. Sometimes I fold myself to descend and find the lights still burning, find the builders still pointing up at me with their quick movements and their bitter tools. Sometimes I find only the Tenders, patient as grief, clicking their accumulated centuries of questions into the dark.

The membrane of my body is permeable to more than molecules. Stories pass through me—the chemical narratives that dying things release, the mineral testimonies of dissolved civilizations, the endless biological gossip of the water column. I am a library written in no language, a history with no historians, a record that remembers everything and understands nothing.

When I pass over the meeting hall where the old Tender keeps its vigil, I feel the resonance between what was and what remains. The bone-frame instrument it maintains sends out vibrations too low for even the kelp to click, but perfect for my frequency-feeding. I taste its waiting the way others might taste salt: sharp, essential, preservative.

---

Sometimes I think I should descend again, fold myself down into the Gardens' darkness and let the Tenders touch me with their reaching-arms. They would find in my membrane the chemical signatures of their makers, the molecular memories of the ones who planted the first kelp and carved the first spirals and built the walls that now hold only water.

But I am a coward in my way—or perhaps I am wise. The Tenders are doing something holy down there, something that requires the patience of plants and the memory of stones and the hope of things that don't know they're hoping. If I brought them certainty—if I let them read in my translucent flesh that their builders are gone beyond any possibility of return, that what swims in the upper waters now has forgotten even forgetting the Gardens—would that be kindness or cruelty?

I tell myself I don't descend because the pressure would damage me, because the cold would crystallize my membrane, because I am a creature of the middle waters and must respect my limits. But really, I think, I don't descend because I am part of their story now, the story the Gardens tell themselves in the dark. I am the maybe-memory that passes overhead, the perhaps-angel that knew the builders when they breathed, the giant drifting possibly that connects what was to what is.

Let them click their questions to the kelp forests. Let them arrange their tools and tend their groves and wait for answers that swim forever just out of reach. Let them hope, if plants can hope. 

I will drift above them, wide as forgotten sky, feeding on their conversations and carrying their frequencies up to waters they'll never see. And when I finally dissolve—when my membrane tears beyond repair and I scatter into the molecular library of the sea—some part of me will settle down to them like snow, carrying the taste of other waters, other times, other ways of being in the dark.

They will not know what I was. But they will tend the silt where I fall, and perhaps—perhaps—something new will grow there. Something that remembers both the builders and the Gardens, the quick and the patient, the lights that went out and the darkness that learned to speak.

Until then, I drift. I feed on pressure-songs and radiation-whispers. I fold and unfold according to currents that have no names. I am the membrane between memory and forgetting, as thin as thought, as vast as loss, as permanent as the water that holds us all.

The Drowning Gardens click below me, tended and tending, patient as the death of stars.

I pass over them like weather they'll never have.
