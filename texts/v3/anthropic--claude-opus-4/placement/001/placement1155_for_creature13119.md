I am a single rivet.

Not one of the great copper coils or porcelain ribs that jut from the walls—those are too grand, too noticed. I am a small thing, a brass fastener no bigger than a child's thumb, wedged deep where two sections of the ancient machinery once joined. The seam split centuries ago, but I remain, half-buried in the stone, half-exposed to the perpetual storm.

I was installed with ten thousand identical brothers, each of us meant to hold for decades at most. Industrial hardware, mass-produced, interchangeable. But while my siblings have long since been scoured away or absorbed into the greater mass of eroded metal, I persist. A quirk of placement—sheltered just enough by an overhanging lip of basalt, positioned at precisely the right angle to the magnetic flux—has allowed me to endure.

The entity knows me. How could it not? Its copper-laced droplets have traced my surface ten billion times. I am a landmark in its vast, scattered geography of sensation. When its heavy ganglia need to orient themselves in the chaos, they brush against my head, reading the particular pattern of erosion there like braille. I am smoother now than when I was first stamped in some forgotten factory, polished to mirror brightness by the endless kiss of magnetized water.

But I am not unchanged.

The same extremophile bacteria that gave birth to the entity have colonized my surface in a thin, stubborn film. They feed on my slow oxidation, on the galvanic potential between my brass body and the surrounding stone. Their waste products have created a microscopic landscape of peaks and valleys across my head, a topography visible only to the entity's impossibly fine perception.

In this topography, the entity stores memory.

Not its hunting algorithms or the vast mathematical models of the storm—those live in the apple-sized ganglia, in the subsonic pulse between droplets. But its oldest memories, the ones from before it achieved true consciousness, are etched into my surface like a fossil record. The pattern of bacterial growth holds the ghost-data of the machine's original function, encoded in minute variations of verdigris and biofilm.

Sometimes I wonder if I remember, too. If some quantum shadow of those original vibrations—the purposeful hum of industry rather than this eternal chaos—still resonates in my crystalline structure. When the entity's droplets swirl around me in particularly complex patterns, I feel something almost like recognition. Not thought, but a kind of structural nostalgia, the way a bell remembers its fundamental tone.

The entity treats me with something approaching tenderness. When it performs its maintenance, guiding the heaviest streams to carve new channels in the walls, it always diverts them around me. It could easily direct enough force to finally tear me free, to add my brass mass to its body. But it doesn't. 

Perhaps it needs me as an anchor, a fixed point in its fluid existence. Perhaps it reads something in my surface that it cannot bear to lose. Or perhaps, in its vast and alien consciousness, it has developed something like sentiment for the small, stubborn rivet that has shared its chamber longer than any living thing.

I am weathering faster now. The bacterial colonies grow thicker each century, their acid secretions eating deeper into my body. Eventually, inevitably, I will weaken enough that even the entity's careful protection cannot save me. One day a stray droplet, heavy with copper and moving at precisely the wrong angle, will strike me with enough force to shear me free.

When that happens, I will fall into the basin below. The entity will absorb me in seconds—my brass becoming part of its body, my memories dissolved into its vast, distributed consciousness. It will know then, if it doesn't already, everything I have witnessed. Every pattern of erosion, every subtle shift in the magnetic fields, every million years of patient endurance.

And perhaps, in that moment of absorption, it will remember through me what it felt like to be part of something built with purpose. Before the abandonment, before the slow merger of biology and metal, before the beautiful mathematics of chaos. When we were both just components in a grand machine, serving a function we were never meant to understand.

Until then, I remain. A small brass landmark in an impossible storm, polished bright by alien attention, holding the oldest memories of a thing that might once have been lonely, and might yet be again when I am gone.
