I am the Calcium Daughter, third-born of a Listener who heard her own dissolution coming through the stone six months before her joints began to weep white fluid. My mother named me for the taste of my birth-water—too much limestone, she said, though I've never known water that tasted different.

My bones ache constantly, a deep mineral throb that worsens when the humidity shifts. I was born with a caul of stone-dust, which my grandmother scraped carefully into a vial we still keep, adding pinches to the speaking tubes when the messages grow too garbled. They say I'll be a Listener myself, though I'm fighting it. I've seen what happens. My mother's ears grew so sensitive she could hear conversations from three chambers away, until eventually she couldn't distinguish between the whispers of the living and the whispers of the stone. Now she sits in the Deep Reach, eating less each day, her skull pressed against limestone that's gone soft with her tears.

I manage the Seventh Gallery's primary catchment system—four thousand joints of ceramic, leather, and carved bone that must be checked, adjusted, tightened every six hours. My fingers have developed a permanent curl from gripping the pole I use to clear fungus-coral from the highest channels. The pole is my height twice over, passed down from my mother's mother, who carved it from a single arthropod leg. The chitin has worn smooth where generations of hands have gripped it, and sometimes I swear I can feel their urgency in the grain.

My partner is a path-memorizer named Echo (born during a wall-fall, when the sound repeated seven times). They've been teaching me the new routes since the Western Cascade's failure last season forced us to abandon three entire levels. Echo's feet are broader than mine, toes splayed wide enough to grip stone through water three fingers deep. When they walk, it's perfectly silent—a skill that saves lives when navigating past ceiling-dweller colonies. They can travel from the Singing Gallery to the Phosphor Pools without waking a single pale crawling thing.

We don't speak of children yet, though we both know my bones are already too dense, too changed. Any daughter of mine will be stranger still. Sometimes Echo brings me gifts—a perfectly dry stone from a chamber so remote even the water-memorizers have forgotten it, or a vial of clear condensation that tastes of nothing but itself. These are declarations of love more profound than any spoken word.

My dwelling is a natural hollow in the Seventh Gallery's third tier, expanded by some previous inhabitant who knew stone-cutting better than anyone alive today. The walls still show marks from tools we can't replicate. I've rigged a double-blind of leather and mineral-cloth across the entrance—necessary since the ceiling-dwellers learned to squeeze through gaps narrow as a child's finger. Inside, I keep my collection: seventeen distinct types of dripping, each in a carved fungus-coral vessel, labeled by taste and stone-source. It's not hoarding, it's research. I'm trying to map the relationship between mineral content and the changes in our bodies.

My skin has the telltale scaling of a catchment-worker, gray-white patches that flake constantly no matter how much rendered fat I rub into them. The scales are worst on my hands and feet, spreading up my arms in patterns that follow the splash-zones of my daily work. Echo says they're beautiful, like maps of rivers that haven't flowed for a thousand years. I think they look like my mother's last drawings, when she started depicting the conversations she heard in the stone—overlapping circles that made sense only to her.

I dream of drowning, but peacefully. In the dreams, my lungs fill with mineral-heavy water and instead of panic, I feel a profound settling, as if I'm finally reaching the density I was meant to achieve. I wake from these dreams with my chest aching, coughing up pale sputum that tastes of limestone. The medicine-keepers say it's nothing, just the usual lung-scaling from breathing too much cascade-mist, but I know better. My mother's dreams started the same way.

The speaking tubes have been whispering more lately—not messages, but something else. Sometimes when I clean the tubes, pressing my ear to the stone to check for blockages, I hear what sounds like breathing from deep below. Not human breathing. Slower, deeper, as if the cisterns themselves are inhaling over centuries. I haven't reported this. To report it would be to admit I'm already becoming what I swore I wouldn't.

My work-cycle begins when the light-shafts show the particular angle that means the stones have dried as much as they ever do. I strap on my tool-harness—carved hooks, brushes of arthropod bristle, the precious metal scraper I inherited from my mother, pouches of various powders for different blockages. The leather is soft with years of my sweat, fitted to my body's particular configuration. Even Echo couldn't wear it comfortably—we've tried.

I know every pipe in my system, every joint and angle. Here, where the ceramic meets carved stone, mineral buildup forms faster. There, where leather joins bone, the soft-bodied things like to nest. I work by feel as much as sight, my fingers reading the health of each connection like text. The constant moisture has given me a permanent fungal growth under my nails that glows faintly in darkness—useful for checking the deeper channels where even cascade-light doesn't reach.

The work is meditation and madness both. Alone for hours with only the sound of dripping and my own breathing, it's easy to lose track of whether the whispers I hear come from the tubes or my own mind. Sometimes I find myself tapping responses to questions no one asked, my fingers drumming the old language my grandmother taught me before words.

Today I found something new. A section of ceramic pipe that isn't ceramic at all, but something that looks like bone grown in the shape we need. It's warm to the touch, and when I tapped it, the sound traveled wrong—not dispersing like it should, but continuing deeper, carrying my simple percussion into places our maps don't show. I should report it. I should mark it for removal. Instead, I cleaned it carefully, checked its connections, and moved on. Later, I'll bring Echo to see it. They'll know if I'm starting to imagine things that aren't there.

The children follow me sometimes, fascinated by my work. They've never known a world where the Western Cascade flowed; to them, our current configuration is permanent, unchangeable. They ask why I taste the drippings, why I count the joints, why I clean channels that will only clog again. I try to explain about maintenance, about the necessity of repetition, but they're already adapting past the need for such diligence. The newest ones can sense blockages before they form, their bodies attuned to pressure changes too subtle for my calcium-dense bones to detect.

I'm training my replacement, though she doesn't know it yet. A girl named Whisper whose mother drank only from the Phosphor Pools during pregnancy. Whisper's eyes have the second eyelid, and her fingers are already longer than mine, though she's only seen seven migrations. When she helps me work, she hums—not melodies, but harmonics that match the resonance of whatever pipe she's cleaning. The sound helps dislodge buildup in ways my physical tools can't match. She'll be better at this than I ever was, once her bones finish changing.

My favorite time is the brief period between work-cycles when Echo and I meet at the Breathing Chamber—a natural hollow where the air currents create a sound like sighing. We don't talk much. We share water from each other's rations, tasting the subtle differences that mark our daily paths. They tell me about new routes discovered, passages that opened after recent settling. I describe the patterns I'm seeing in the mineral buildup, how they're changing, becoming more organic.

We both know what we don't say: that we're the last generation that will live like this. The changes are accelerating. The sealed chambers call louder each day. The children being born now are something new, something that might survive what's coming but won't need our elaborate systems of catchment and collection. They'll have other ways of living, other relationships with the stone and water and pale crawling things.

But for now, I maintain. I clean and clear and count. I teach Whisper the old methods even as her body develops new ones. I love Echo in the spaces between words, in shared water and silent companionship. I listen to my bones ache and know that this too is a kind of message, my body's prophecy of the density to come.

Tomorrow I'll check the bone-pipe again, see if it's grown. I'll taste new drippings and add them to my collection. I'll try not to hear the conversations in the stone, try not to feel the settling in my chest that means my lungs are beginning their mineral translation. I'll be the Calcium Daughter one more day, one more cycle, one more drop of water caught and treasured in this pale, failing, transforming world.

And when the time comes—when my ears grow too sensitive, when the stone's whispers drown out Echo's breathing—I'll walk to the Deep Reach where my mother sits becoming limestone. I'll take my place beside her, and we'll listen together to the conversations of civilizations ground down to mineral whispers, until we too join that fossil chorus, our words becoming weight, our breath becoming stone, our bodies the foundation for whatever impossible thing comes next.
