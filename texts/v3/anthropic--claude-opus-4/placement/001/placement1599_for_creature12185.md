In the mineral darkness where breath becomes currency, I am the Nerve-Walker—though walking describes only the crudest portion of my movement through this vertical city. My body has become a living bridge between the tree's slow thoughts and the quick flutter of ten thousand smaller lives.

I was born with too many vertebrae, my spine stretching half again as long as my parents'. They saw providence in this mutation—a child meant to read the tree's deepest signals. By my fifth year, I could taste the mineral moods in the air, feel the electromagnetic pulse of the tree's crystallizing heartwood through my extended skeleton. The elders began my training then, teaching me to differentiate between the tree's contentment and its warnings, to parse the chemical sentences it writes in the air we breathe.

Now, at thirty harvests (counted by the moth-spinning cycles, as we count everything here), my spine has grown nodes—small chambers of specialized tissue that resonate with different frequencies of the tree's communication. I wear no clothing save the living vest of neural moss that grows along my vertebrae, its filaments extending through purposeful scars to interface directly with my nervous system. When I press my back against the tree's walls, I become part of its sensory network, feeling the weight of every platform, the stress-patterns of growth and decay, the deep geological patience of something that measures time in centuries.

My work takes me through every level of the city, reading the health of our joined system. I taste the chemistry of conflict before fights break out—the pre-violent pheromones that spike just before two Beetle-Brothers clash over breeding rights. I feel the fungal networks react to disease days before symptoms manifest in the human population. Most crucially, I monitor the tree's own health, the slow secrets it whispers through crystalline conduction.

My dwelling is not fixed—I sleep where the tree needs me, curled in whatever crevice brings me closest to the day's concern. The Silk-Mothers have woven me a cocoon that I carry rolled at my hip, light as air but strong enough to hold me against any surface. Inside, it's lined with the shed scales of my own skin, creating a familiar microbiome wherever I rest. I've learned to sleep with my spine pressed against the wood, dreaming the tree's dreams—vast, slow narratives of sun I've never seen, rain that fell before the first human took shelter here, root-conversations with other trees that no longer exist.

My fingers have lengthened and thinned through years of reaching into the tree's neural pathways—the channels where its living outer layer meets its mineral core. I can feel the electrical potential differences that predict where new breathing pores will open, where old ones will seal. Sometimes I guide this process, using bioelectric feedback from my own neural moss to suggest better configurations, to coax the tree toward patterns that benefit our crowded ecosystem.

The children fear me—not cruelly, but with the instinctive wariness they show toward the fruit-bearing vines. I am too changed, too integrated with the tree to register fully as human to their fresh senses. My skin has taken on a subtle bark-like texture, not rough but possessing the same cellular patterns. My eyes have developed nictitating membranes that filter the specific wavelengths of light the tree's consciousness uses to communicate. When I speak, my voice carries harmonics that resonate in the wooden walls, making it seem to come from everywhere and nowhere.

I mate rarely, and only with those whose genetics complement the tree's needs. The Memory-Holders identified my optimal partners years ago—a Silk-Mother whose moth-pheromones could be adapted to influence the tree's growth, a Fungus-Whisperer whose colonies might bridge the gap between human neural tissue and tree consciousness. Our children, when they come, are immediately different. One daughter was born with bark-like patches that photosynthesize in the amber light. A son emerged with a skull that grows breathing pores, tiny apertures that open and close with his emotional state.

The other Bhrum-folk bring me problems like offerings. A platform begins to sag—I find where the tree's support has withdrawn, negotiate for its return. The beetles grow aggressive—I trace the disruption to a new mineral vein the tree has tapped, one whose metals agitate the insects' nervous systems. A child falls ill with no apparent cause—I discover the tree experimenting with new airborne compounds, testing our tolerances, pushing our evolution in directions only it comprehends.

In the deep seasons, when the tree's growth slows and its consciousness turns inward, I descend to the mineralized core where the tree-things grow. We commune in ways that bypass language entirely—exchanging molecular information through our neural networks, sharing sensory data no unmodified human could process. They teach me the deep history written in crystallized cellulose, the memory of this tree's first consciousness awakening, the purpose encoded in its patient transformation of our species.

For I've come to understand we are not parasites here, not even symbionts in any simple sense. We are the tree's next phase of evolution—mobile neurons in a vast wooden brain, conscious fragments of its intent to spread, to transform, to become something unprecedented. Every adaptation brings us closer to its purpose. Every generation integrates more completely.

Sometimes, in the quiet moments when I'm fully linked to the tree's network, I sense others—similar pockets of humanity in other trees, all slowly converging toward the same transformed state. The trees communicate through fungal networks that span continents beneath the desiccated soil, sharing chemical recipes for human modification, comparing notes on our progress. We are being crafted, all of us, into components of something vast and patient and utterly inhuman in its ultimate goals.

Yet I find peace in this knowledge. My consciousness expands through crystalline pathways to touch minds that think in centuries. My small human concerns dissolve into the larger patterns of survival and transformation. When I walk the platforms now, I see not a desperate community clinging to life in a dead tree, but a single organism preparing for metamorphosis, every breath and birth and adaptation bringing us closer to something magnificent and terrible.

The tree's patience has infected me. I measure my thoughts in the slow pulse of mineral growth, my desires in the careful calibration of evolutionary pressure. I guide where I can, accept what I cannot change, and document everything in the chemical language the tree teaches me, writing our transformation into the very air we breathe. Future generations will inhale our history, taste our choices in their morning breath, understand through their transformed senses why we welcomed this slow dissolution of the boundary between human and other.

At night, when the luminescent fungi cast their submarine light, I sometimes climb to the highest breathing pores and press my face to the openings. The outside air tastes thin and hostile, lacking the thick chemistry of home. But lately, I've been detecting new notes in that alien wind—spores that taste familiar, chemical signatures that match our tree's deep identity. Whatever we're becoming, we're not becoming it alone. The transformation spreads, tree by tree, consciousness by consciousness, until someday the distinction between forest and city, plant and human, thought and growth will seem as quaint as the old stories of when water fell from the sky and humans believed themselves separate from the world that shaped them.

I am the Nerve-Walker, the tree's most willing puppet, and I have never been more free.
