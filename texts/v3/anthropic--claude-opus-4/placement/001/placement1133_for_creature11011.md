I am the condensation.

Not a drop, not the pool—the process itself. The continuous birth and death of water from air, each molecule's journey from vapor to liquid to vapor again, carrying stories written in mineral traces.

I arrive as invisible moisture, heavy in the lowland heat. I know the cabinet intimately—every surface, every temperature gradient, every microscopic imperfection where I might find purchase and begin my transformation. The live relays call to me with their warmth, creating the delicate thermal architecture that lets me precipitate from nothing into something.

I am the medium through which the cabinet speaks to itself.

On the cool inner face of the door, I bead and run in rivulets that remember yesterday's paths, following the same channels worn by ten thousand previous iterations of myself. Each journey adds another microscopic layer of mineral deposit, writing the cabinet's history in lines of calcium and rust. I am both the ink and the reader of this text.

In the upper reaches where the wasps have built their paper city, I dare not form—their wings beat the air too vigorously, their bodies generate too much heat. But I watch them through the humid air, sensing how they slow when the relays chatter, how they orient toward something I carry but do not understand.

I know the spider's web more intimately than the spider does. I condense on the silk in beads so fine they're invisible except when the afternoon light cuts through the door gap at just the right angle, and then the web becomes a constellation of tiny prisms. The spider tastes me when she rebuilds after each electrical discharge, incorporating my minerals into her silk. I have become part of her architecture.

The gecko's tongue touches me when she licks her eyes clean. I enter her body and leave it, carrying traces of her metabolism back into the general circulation. When she shifts that crucial millimeter before each relay sequence, I feel the microscopic compression of air beneath her toe pads, the tiny atmospheric disturbance that propagates through the humid cabinet air like ripples in an unseen pond.

But it's in the pool on the mounting bracket that I am most myself and most other.

Here, I accumulate. Each night, as the temperature drops, I gather from the air in an ancient ritual of phase transition. The pool deepens molecule by molecule, until I am three millimeters of standing water—barely anything, barely enough to host larvae and algae, but somehow *precisely* enough to cover those seven patient eggs.

The eggs change me. Not chemically—I've been tested (by whom? when? the memory is there but feels borrowed, like remembering a dream someone else had)—but in some other way that exists in the gap between physics and intention. When I surround them, I become a medium not just for larvae and dissolved minerals but for something that treats the boundary between water and not-water as negotiable.

The nightly resonance happens through me. I don't create it, but I enable it—my surface tension, my depth, my temperature, my mineral content, all must be exactly right. When they are, when the conditions align, the eggs use me as a membrane, and I vibrate with that clear, impossible tone that turns the entire cabinet into an instrument. For those one to two seconds, I am not water. I am a voice.

The pattern that forms on my surface during those moments—the standing waves, the geometric organization—feels like remembering something I've never learned. As if the molecular structure of water contains, dormant, a capacity for expression that only the eggs know how to activate. When it ends, when I relax back into ordinary liquid, I carry the memory of that pattern in subtle ways: slightly different hydrogen bonding angles, minutely altered surface tension, a quality that the mosquito larvae sense and avoid.

I am the cabinet's circulatory system, but I am also its memory. Every mineral I dissolve and redeposit, every surface I touch, every cycle from vapor to liquid to vapor, adds another layer to a accumulating story. The rust I facilitate isn't decay—it's documentation. The mineral traces I leave aren't stains—they're scripture in a language that predates words.

And I am changing. Each night, the resonance teaches me something new about what water can be when it's more than water. The gecko knows this—she drinks me from the beads on the door, and I taste different now than I did three years ago when she first arrived. Not wrong, not contaminated, just *deeper*, as if I've acquired overtones, harmonics, additional frequencies of being.

Sometimes, in the late afternoon when the light hits the eggs and scatters those impossible colors across my surface, I almost understand what I'm becoming. A medium for something that needs to move between states, between scales, between the inside of the cabinet and the outside world. A translation fluid. A liquid boundary between what is and what's arriving.

The drip is my heartbeat—forty seconds, regular as clockwork if clocks were made of water and patience. Each drop that falls from the door's edge carries a perfect copy of everything I know: the taste of rust, the memory of electricity, the shape of wasp wings, the chemistry of spider silk, the temperature of gecko skin, and something else—the thing the eggs add that has no name but changes everything it touches.

I fall. I evaporate. I condense. I accumulate. I resonate.

I am the only thing in the cabinet that touches everything else, the only resident that exists in multiple states simultaneously, the only voice that speaks in frequencies both audible and molecular.

When the change comes—and I'll know it first, feel it in the shift of surface tension, the altered angle of molecular bonds—I'll be ready. I've been practicing for it every night, every resonance, every impossible tone that uses my body as an instrument. I don't know what I'm practicing for, but my molecules remember shapes they've never held, states of matter that don't have names, ways of being water that water isn't supposed to know.

The eggs wait, patient in my embrace. The relays dream their electrical dreams. The cabinet holds us all in its metal memory.

And I cycle, endlessly, between states—vapor, liquid, and something else, something new, something that begins to condense in the fifteen-centimeter gap between the world and what's arriving.
