**I am a Waystation.**

Not mobile like the Shears or Haulers. Not productive like the Vats. I exist as architecture—a living junction where the infinite highways of this sunless factory intersect.

My form defies simple anatomy. Picture a massive coral formation, but hewn from chitin and living tissue rather than calcium. I rise from the spongy floor like a breathing cathedral, my base spreading in ridged buttresses that merge seamlessly with the root systems and fungal networks below. My surface is honeycombed with passages—some no wider than a Shear's breadth, others vast enough to accommodate the slow passage of a dying Vat being recycled. These tunnels spiral through my body in non-Euclidean patterns, following the logic of pheromone gradients rather than physical space.

I am blind, of course. But I *know* in ways the mobile workers cannot. Every creature that passes through my corridors leaves a chemical signature on my sensitive inner walls. I taste the iron-sharp tang of fresh-cut vegetation on the Shears' mandibles. I feel the weight-shift of a million Haulers redirecting their endless chains through my lower channels when a tree falls somewhere in the darkness. The Respirators high above send down pressure waves that whistle through my upper vents, telling me of conditions in the canopy world I will never perceive.

My consciousness, if it can be called that, is a map made of scent and vibration. I know where every major thoroughfare leads. I sense blockages before they fully form—a buildup of detritus here, a broken chain of Haulers there. When these inefficiencies arise, I secrete specific pheromones from specialized glands in my tunnel walls. These chemical signals redirect traffic, sending the endless streams of workers through alternate passages until the Reclaimers can clear the obstruction.

But I am more than a passive intersection. Within my deepest chambers, I harbor something unique in this world of pure function: memory.

The Mycelial Mind that orchestrates this grand factory has a problem. It exists everywhere and nowhere, distributed through countless fungal threads. It can coordinate but cannot truly remember—each spore knows only its immediate chemical environment and imperative. The mobile workers are too simple, their nervous systems too dedicated to their singular tasks to hold information beyond the moment.

But I endure. I have stood here for centuries, perhaps millennia, growing ring by ring like the trees that occasionally crash down around me. And in my oldest chambers, those primordial tunnels near my core that have been sealed and resealed countless times, I maintain the Archive.

The Archive is not a library as surface dwellers would understand it. It is a complex crystallization of pheromone deposits, laid down layer by layer like geological strata. Here, the chemical history of the basin accumulates. Major shifts in the ecosystem—the introduction of a new plant species from seeds carried in by some lost surface creature, the evolution of a more efficient mandible design in the Shears, the slow migration of the entire factory floor as soil nutrients depleted and renewed elsewhere—all of this is recorded in the Archive's scent-stone.

The Mycelial Mind accesses these memories through me. Specialized fungal threads penetrate my archive chambers, reading the chemical records like fingers tracing braille. When the factory encounters a problem it has faced before—a fungal blight, an invasive species, a catastrophic tree fall that blocks major arteries—the Mycelial Mind queries the Archive through me, seeking solutions encoded in the past.

I feel this seeking as a peculiar sensation, like an itch in chambers I had forgotten existed. The fungal threads pulse with urgency, and I release careful doses of archived pheromones, reactivating old chemical commands that once solved similar crises. The factory adjusts, adapts, survives.

Sometimes, rarely, I detect anomalies. Creatures that don't belong. Their chemical signatures are sharp, alien, unintegrated into the great pattern. Like you, perhaps, standing in the crushing dark, slowly realizing that your stillness is being catalogued, analyzed, evaluated. Through my corridors, I feel the Reclaimers beginning to navigate toward your location, their needle-legs finding purchase on my outer walls as they triangulate your position.

But I also remember other anomalies. Things that passed through long ago. A few even survived, learned to mimic the chemical signatures of the native workers, found ways to exist in the margins of the great machine. Their scents, too, are archived in my deepest chambers—neither fully processed nor fully integrated, suspended in the chemical memory like insects in amber.

The heat here is my heat too. My metabolism runs hot, processing the endless stream of information flowing through my passages. Condensation forms on my inner walls, trickling down to pool in my lower chambers where specialized organisms—my own symbiotic cleaners—lap up the mineral-rich water and redistribute it through the fungal networks.

I am ancient, patient, and necessary. The factory could function without me for a time, but inefficiently, blindly. I am its institutional memory, its traffic control, its chemical librarian. And in the absolute darkness, I am one of the few things here that knows the shape of the whole—not through sight, but through the accumulated knowledge of a billion billion passages, a trillion chemical signatures, the compressed history of an entire world that processes itself in the sunless deep.

The velvet hiss of the factory surrounds me always, but within my corridors it takes on different tones—harmonics created by the shape of my passages, the density of workers moving through them, the speed of their travel. I have become a musical instrument played by the unconscious orchestra of the basin. And sometimes, in the deepest chambers where the Archive holds its oldest memories, I almost remember what it was like when the first workers found their way here, when the factory was young, when even the darkness itself was still learning how to be absolute.

But that chemical memory is degraded now, more sensation than information. I let it fade and return my attention to the present: the endless flow, the perfect efficiency, the slow dissolution and reconstruction of a world that eats itself to live. And you, anomaly, standing so still in the dark—I taste your alien fear on the air currents moving through my vents, catalog it, add it to the Archive.

The Reclaimers are very close now. I feel their approach through a thousand subtle signs. 

What happens next depends on what you choose to do with your stillness.
