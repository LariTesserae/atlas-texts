# The Tidepool Synthesizer

I find myself in the gap between high tide and low, where barnacles write sheet music on volcanic rock and the sea has left behind its most temporary instruments. The pool is no larger than a bathtub, carved by a thousand years of waves into black basalt, its edges sharp enough to cut and smooth enough to sing.

Everything here makes sound. Everything here *is* sound.

The water itself has perfect pitch—salt content and temperature combining to create a natural resonator that holds notes for exactly three seconds before releasing them into the air as microscopic droplets. I am the thing that listens to what gets released. I am also what causes the releasing.

My body is difficult to describe because it exists partially as vibration. Where I touch the water, concentric rings spread outward, but the rings are not simple circles—they form interference patterns that look like notation, like formulae, like the migration maps of creatures too small to see. I have what might be called tentacles, if tentacles could be made of organized sound waves, if they could exist sometimes as flesh and sometimes as the space between two colliding frequencies.

The anemones know me. They've adapted to my presence over countless tidal cycles, learned to filter feed on the harmonics I accidentally shed. Their bodies pulse in polyrhythmic patterns—5 against 7 against 11—creating beat frequencies that disturb the smallest inhabitants of the pool: copepods like animated commas, glass worms that refract sound instead of light, colonies of something between algae and audio equipment that grow in spirals calibrated to the Golden Ratio.

I am composing. Always composing. Not music in any sense you'd recognize—I'm composing *relationships*. The relationship between the temperature of this water and the exact mineral content leaching from the basalt. The relationship between the barnacle's feeding rhythm and the frequency at which sunlight breaks into component wavelengths on the surface tension. The relationship between my watching and what I watch.

When I first emerged here—condensed? crystallized? *became?*—I was simpler. Just a resonance in the rock, a sympathy between the pool's natural acoustics and something deeper, some fault line in reality where sound becomes substance. But the pool has been teaching me. Each tide brings new information: the chemical signature of distant storms, the pressure waves from whales passing in deep water, fragments of radio broadcasts that somehow propagate through seawater and arrive here as ghostly palimpsests.

I process all of it. My tentacle-frequencies reach into the water and separate signal from noise—except here, noise *is* signal, is information, is the very medium through which the pool speaks to itself. I've learned to taste electromagnetic radiation through the water's surface, to feel colors as percussion against my dispersed body, to see time as a waveform I can stretch or compress.

The hermit crabs are my collaborators. They've learned that certain shells resonate at frequencies I find useful, and they bring them to me like offerings—twisted gastropod homes that turn their movements into melodies. One crab has been with me for three years, its shell a natural logarithmic spiral that transposes everything it touches up by a perfect fifth. When it walks across my extended frequency-flesh, I experience something like laughter, if laughter were a force that could reorganize molecules.

There is a crack in the pool's floor, hair-thin, that extends down to places where water becomes something else under pressure. Sometimes I lower a tendril of focused sound into it, listening to the echoes come back changed—compressed into new timbres, encrypted with the signatures of minerals that exist nowhere else. Once, something sang back. Not an echo, but a response, in frequencies so low I only felt them as a kind of gravity in my chest. I haven't sent sound down there since. I'm still decoding what came back.

The tide will return in forty-seven minutes. I know this not from any clock but from the way the barnacles have started to close, from the specific temperature gradient forming in the water, from the anticipatory quiver in the volcanic glass embedded in the rock. When the ocean comes back, it will bring new materials—plankton like living sequencers, jellies that pulse in prime numbers, the occasional small fish whose scales create interference patterns with sunlight that I can translate into something almost like language.

But for now, in this window between tides, I work with what's here. I separate a strand of my frequency-body and touch it to the anemone cluster. They respond immediately, their polyrhythm shifting to incorporate my input—now 5 against 7 against 11 against whatever prime number my existence represents. The copepods swirl into new formations, their movement writing temporary scores on the water's surface. The glass worms bend, refracting not light but the sound of light, splitting my simple touch into a chord made of photons.

I am trying to understand something. The relationship between this pool and every other pool along this coast, yes, but more than that. The relationship between listening and being listened to. Between the composer and the composition. Between what I was before I condensed into this strange existence and what I'm becoming with each tidal cycle.

The hermit crab with the logarithmic shell approaches, dragging something new—a fragment of sea glass worn smooth, green as oxidized copper, shaped like a tiny amphitheater. When it drops this into the pool, the acoustic properties shift immediately. Every sound now has a faint echo at the octave, as if the pool has suddenly remembered it once was larger, deeper, connected to networks of similar pools that no longer exist.

I incorporate this new element into my composition. My body—part wave, part flesh, part possibility—expands to fill the new acoustic space. The anemones adjust their rhythm. The copepods spell out a different grammar. Something in the crack at the bottom stirs, sending up a single bubble that contains a sound I've never heard before.

Forty-one minutes until the tide. I will use them fully. I will compose the relationship between what is and what could be, between the sound of starlight on water and the silence of stone slowly dissolving. I will be what I am: a question the pool asks itself, a frequency looking for its fundamental, a thing that exists because listening required a listener.

The water holds its perfect pitch. The barnacles add their percussion. I extend my strangest appendage—the one made entirely of the space between sounds—and begin again.
