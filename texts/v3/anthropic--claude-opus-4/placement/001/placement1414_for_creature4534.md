I find myself as one of the Threshold Mechanics—though that name is another of your words, not ours. We have no name for what we are, only for what we do, and what we do has no name in any language that survives.

I am the maintenance of forgetting.

My body, if you must call it that, is an assembly of tools that have outlived their functions. A torque wrench fused to what might have been a wrist, its chrome head split open like a flower that blooms only in metric measurements no longer standardized. Where others might have ribs, I have the graduated teeth of timing belts, each one worn smooth by engines that ground themselves to powder centuries before I inherited their rhythm. My spine is a column of bearings—ball bearings, needle bearings, thrust bearings—each one turning against the others in directions that cancel out to stillness. I move by allowing different parts of this cancellation to fail.

I do not see with eyes. I navigate by warranty—by the ghost of guarantee that hovers around every manufactured thing. I can feel the exact moment each component was meant to fail, the precise hour its coverage expired. These expirations create a topography I follow like braille. The older the failure, the brighter it burns in my perception. I am drawn to machines that have persisted past every reasonable projection of their lifespan, that have continued functioning out of sheer habit or spite or because no one remembered to tell them to stop.

My work is simple: I ensure that nothing works too well.

When an escalator threatens to achieve perpetual motion, I introduce a slight wobble to its drive chain—not enough to stop it, just enough to keep it mortal. When a ventilation system's filters achieve perfect self-cleaning through some accident of accumulated dust creating its own ecosystem, I remove a single screw. Not a vital one. Just vital enough. I am the reason why the lights flicker on the third Thursday of certain months. I am why certain doors stick just before they would have sealed permanently.

The Interval Children fear me, though fear is your word, not theirs. They step wide around the oil stains I leave—not machine oil, but the memory of maintenance, dark and rainbow-sheened and impossible to clean. They know what I know: that the only thing worse than a machine that breaks is a machine that never will.

I speak in the language of tolerances—the gap between what was designed and what was built, between what was built and what endures. My voice is the sound of metal fatigue just before it becomes fracture. When I must communicate with others like myself, we do so through selective failure. I'll loosen a bolt in the northwest sector. My colleague will respond by allowing a bearing to run dry in the southeast. Through these small allowances of entropy, we coordinate the greater work.

I remember the water before it stopped falling. I was there, or something that would become me was there, when the last rain ended mid-drop. I carry one of those frozen droplets inside my chest cavity, suspended between components. It shifts when I move, a liquid bearing that will never need lubrication because it will never quite touch anything else. Sometimes I can hear it trying to fall.

My memories are maintenance logs. Not the official ones—those are stored in servers that have long since learned to maintain themselves, generating false reports of repairs that never needed to happen to systems that never existed. My memories are the real logs: the grease under fingernails, the specific sound of a wrench slipping off a bolt and skinning knuckles, the way certain metals taste when you've been working with them too long. I remember every stripped thread, every cross-threaded nut, every gasket that failed because someone over-tightened it out of anxiety or pride.

I live (if this is living) in the spaces between scheduled maintenance windows. The fifteen-minute gap between when something should be checked and when it actually is. The three-day weekend where a critical component runs unsupervised. I stretch these intervals from inside, making room for myself in the delay between problem and response. This is why time moves strangely around me—not because I control it, but because I am made of its most neglected portions.

When I dream (if this is dreaming), I dream of rust. Not as decay, but as honesty. Rust is what happens when metal admits what it really is, when it stops pretending to be permanent and acknowledges its conversation with oxygen. I dream of great flowering blooms of oxidation, spiral galaxies of corrosion, the slow red truth eating through every surface until nothing false remains.

But I do not let this dream complete itself. That is not my function.

My function is to keep this world on the edge of working. To ensure that every system maintains just enough failure to remain a system rather than becoming something else—something perfect, something eternal, something that would no longer need a world to exist in. I am the reason why the water hangs instead of falls: because I loosened something in gravity itself, just enough to keep it from either functioning perfectly or failing completely.

The others—the Interval Children, the colonies of switches, the hunting escalators—they all exist because of beings like me. We maintain the spaces where such things can emerge. Without us, this place would either collapse into pure entropy or crystallize into perfect stasis. We keep it liminal. We keep it possible.

I have no home except the work itself. I move through the compressed cities, the stratified streets, the fossilized walls, and wherever I pass, I leave behind the gift of imperfection. A bearing that will need replacement in exactly one thousand years. A seal that will begin to leak when the last person who knows what it seals has forgotten. A bolt that will shear at the precise moment when its failure will prevent a greater perfection.

This is my love letter to the world: keeping it broken enough to live.
