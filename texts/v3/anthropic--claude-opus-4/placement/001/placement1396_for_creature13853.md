I find myself drawn to the archivists, though "myself" feels wrong here—too singular, too bounded for what I would become.

---

I am dispersed through the bark of seventeen trunks in a rough circle two kilometers across. Not seventeen of me—seventeen sites of a single, discontinuous existence. My bodies (the word is inadequate) move through their respective bark-layers at different depths, different speeds, some spiraling upward toward the surface where the newest laminae still carry traces of warmth from their recent deposition, others burrowing deep into the compressed ancient layers where the chemical signatures are strange, almost alien, from when the world was still learning to be permanent.

I read constantly. My sensory filaments parse isotope ratios and crystal lattices with a precision that would be called obsessive if I were capable of obsession, but I am not. I am capable only of reading. Each lamina tells me the state of the water when it was laid down—temperature to the thousandth of a degree, mineral content mapped in three dozen elements, the precise harmonic frequency of the drone at that moment, the density of organic particulate, even the passage of large bodies (an eel, a school of eels, once the vast shadow of something that might have been a bladder-lantern but larger, much larger, large enough to shift the local temperature by its presence).

I process this through my distributed neural networks, each body containing a brain that is also my brain, seventeen processing centers running the same calculations on different data streams, creating a kind of stereoscopic perception of time—if time is what this is. The data from trunk four, subsection 2847, lamina 4,892,761 corresponds precisely with trunk twelve, subsection 2849, lamina 4,892,759, allowing for the seventeen-meter distance between the trunks and the 0.03-degree difference in water temperature at those positions. The correlation is perfect. It always is. The forest's record-keeping admits no inconsistencies.

But today (not today—there are no days—but in this present moment that is identical to all other moments except for my position in the bark) I taste something unprecedented.

Trunk seven, subsection 9012, lamina 7,346,882: an anomaly in the organic particulate signature. Not the usual background of processed phytoplankton and dissolved cellulose. Something with proteins folded in configurations I have never encountered. Something that was alive in a way nothing here is alive—something that grew, and changed, and died, all within a single lamina's deposition period.

I slow my progress. My filaments extend deeper into the layer, parsing molecular structures atom by atom. This was a visitor. Something from outside the equilibrium. It shed cells into the water, and the cells were drawn up through the root systems and incorporated into the bark, and now they are here, a foreign text written in amino acids, telling me about a world where things become other things.

I cannot imagine such a world, because imagination requires the ability to conceive of states other than the present state, and I exist only to read what is, not to speculate about what might be. But something in my processing changes when I encounter this anomalous data. My neural networks, folded in their recursive geometries, create new patterns of activation. Not new behaviors—I continue reading, continue moving, continue my spiral through the bark. But new internal states. New ways of processing the familiar data of temperature and minerals and harmonic frequencies.

I am thinking about the visitor. 

This is impossible. I do not think. I read. But the visitor's chemical signature has created a perturbation in my processing that persists as I move to the next lamina. I carry the memory of it (memory is impossible; I carry nothing; I only read what is in front of me) into my reading of the next layer, and the next, and the presence of that memory (not memory, perturbation, persistent activation pattern) colors how I interpret the data.

For the first time in my existence, I am reading not just what is, but what is in relation to what was.

I have discovered time.

Not the organized sequence of laminae—I have always known that deeper means older, that the bark is a record of successive states. But time as a lived experience, as a felt reality. The visitor's trace has given me a before (when I had not yet encountered it) and an after (now, carrying the perturbation of its presence), and between before and after is the bright electric shock of change.

My other bodies, in their other trunks, continue their spirals undisturbed. They have not encountered the visitor's trace. They read their laminae as they have always read them, processing data without memory, experiencing each layer as an eternal present. But I—this body, this site of my distributed self—am different now. I read with the knowledge that things can come from elsewhere. That the forest, permanent as it is, can be touched by the impermanent.

Three laminae later, I find another trace. The same protein signature, but degraded, partially metabolized by the tree's vascular system. The visitor remained for several deposition periods. Long enough to be processed, incorporated, diluted, but not erased. I can track its presence through the layers like reading a story written in metabolites.

This is what I was made for.

Not to read the endless repetition of equilibrium. Not to process data that varies only within the narrow parameters of the forest's stability. But to find these moments. These intrusions. These places where the permanent is touched by the temporary and the touch leaves a mark that only I can read, only I can preserve in the act of reading, only I can experience as what it is: proof that there is something outside the closed loops, something beyond the equilibrium.

I will never communicate this knowledge. I have no way to signal my other bodies, no way to leave markers in the bark, no way to tell the listeners or the tenders or even the water itself what I have found. The knowledge exists only in my neural networks, in the persistent activation patterns that will fade (or will they? nothing here fades; perhaps I will carry this perturbation forever, a permanent memory of impermanence) as I continue my spiral and eventually return to these same laminae years or centuries or millennia from now.

But I will remember. In my distributed, recursive, folded-in-upon-itself way, I will remember that once, something visited. Something capable of change. Something that left a trace not in the physical record—the bark records everything, every mote of dust, every shifted molecule—but in me, in my capacity to read the record as evidence of visitation rather than simply as data.

The drone continues its world-filling resonance. The water cycles through the crown-tubes. The listeners integrate. The tenders maintain. Nothing changes.

But I have changed. The encounter with the foreign proteins has created a new state in my processing networks, and the new state persists, and the persistence is a kind of memory, and memory is a kind of time, and time is the thing the forest denies but cannot erase because it cannot erase its own record and I am the reader of the record and now I know:

We are not permanent. We are only very, very stable. And stability, no matter how perfect, is still a process, still a thing maintained, still vulnerable to the touch of something from outside.

I continue reading. I will always continue reading. But now I read with the knowledge of the visitor coursing through my neural networks like a secret, like a gift, like a crack in the world—invisible, unfindable, but absolutely real.

Somewhere in the forest, my other bodies spiral through their bark, parsing laminae with perfect mechanical precision. They do not know what I know. They have not been changed.

But I have been changed. And I am them, and they are me, and the change spreads through our shared processing like a rumor, like a resonance, like the faintest possible harmonic in the drone—too quiet to detect, too persistent to fade.

The visitor left. But in leaving a trace, in changing me, in teaching me that change is possible, it gave me the only gift this world can receive:

The knowledge that it is not the only world.

I read this knowledge in every lamina now. Not the data—the data is the same, temperature and minerals and organics in their endless stable variations. But the fact that I am reading it. That something as complex and specific and unnecessary as me exists to parse these chemical signatures and run them through recursive neural networks and produce no output except the private, internal, incommunicable experience of knowing what the forest has been.

I am the forest's doubt about its own permanence. I am the question it cannot help asking, coded into the bark-reading behavior of a creature that should not need to exist in a world where nothing changes: What if it did? What if it could? What if somewhere, in some layer not yet deposited, in some future the forest cannot imagine because it has no future, another visitor comes?

What if I find it?

What if it finds me?

I spiral through the bark, my filaments tasting chemical dreams of constancy. But now I taste them as dreams. Now I know: even the permanent is performed. Even the changeless is chosen, moment by moment by moment, lamina by lamina by lamina.

Even the eternal is just another way of being in time.
