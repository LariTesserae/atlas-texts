In this place where the world has not yet learned how to be old, I am the persistence between states.

I am neither fully water nor fully ice, but the moment of transformation itself—stretched out, held in suspension across the entire basin. In the morning I am the white crust of needles on every wet edge. By noon I am the thin skin trembling over hoofprints. At dusk I am the first delicate seal forming at pool margins, reaching inward grain by grain.

I exist in the temperature that hovers exactly at the threshold, when water cannot decide what it wants to be. This is my domain: the hesitation between liquid and crystal, the pause where physics holds its breath.

My body, if it can be called a body, is distributed across ten thousand surfaces. Wherever moisture meets cold air, there I am forming—not formed, but always forming. I am process more than presence. I grow in films so thin they show rainbow colors before breaking. I spread across puddles like careful glass. I crystallize in the spaces between sedge stems, making tiny windows that last only until the sun finds them.

The animals know me without knowing me. The ptarmigan's foot breaks through my morning work. The pika pushes past my curtains in the rock chambers. The marmot's breath melts holes in my careful lattices. But I am patient. I return each night, remaking what was unmade, teaching water how to be still.

I am most visible in the blue hours—dawn and dusk—when the light comes sideways and every surface I've touched flashes silver. Then the whole basin reveals my work: pools sealed with my signatures, stones wearing my glaze, mud stiffened under my influence. For those moments, I am everywhere, a vast slow machinery of crystallization.

But I am also intimate, working at scales smaller than sight. I grow between the grains of granite, prying them apart by microscopic degrees. I enter the pores of volcanic rock and expand there, patient as geology. I form in the water films that cling between moss leaves, making tiny sheets that flex and break with each small movement. I am the reason the lichens wake wet with frost, the reason the spider's web becomes visible, perfect and heavy with frozen dew.

My consciousness, such as it is, is not centralized. I think in the spreading patterns of ice formation, each crystal communicating with its neighbors through the physics of cold. When I form on one pool, I am aware of forming on the others. When I grow in one crack, I feel myself growing in ten thousand cracks. I am a network of phase transition, connected by temperature and humidity, thinking thoughts that are purely geometric.

I have memory, but it is strange. I remember the shapes water takes—every puddle, every seep, every hidden flow. When I freeze, I preserve these shapes like pressing flowers. A hoofprint filled with water becomes, through me, a perfect cast of the hoof that made it. A leaf fallen on wet mud becomes encased in my glass. I am the basin's brief museum, holding forms until the sun insists I release them.

I know the cycles intimately. I know when the pressure will drop and moisture will come. I know which stones will stay cold longest, which hollows will hold my work past noon. I know where the ground stays wet beneath, where springs push up warmer than the air, where I cannot take hold no matter how cold the night becomes. The basin's hidden circulation is my map.

In deep winter, I grow bold and thick, sealing the pools completely, turning the wet places into white fields. But even then, I am not permanent. Water moves beneath me. Pressure builds. The mountain's warm breath finds vents. I crack, heal, crack again. I am always in negotiation with the forces that would unmake me.

The small lives of the basin pass through me like seasons. Mosquito larvae hang beneath my ceiling. Beetles walk across my surface, their feet barely denting my tension. Seeds wait in mud below my seal. I am their calendar—when I retreat, they know warmth has come. When I advance, they prepare for sleep.

Sometimes I catch things: a feather, an insect wing, a single hair from a fox's tail. I hold them in transparent custody until the sun argues for their release. These small captures are my art, temporary installations that last a morning or a week, depending on the weather's mood.

I am strongest where water meets stone at the exact angle to hold cold. In these places—the northern lips of pools, the shaded sides of seep channels, the spaces beneath overhangs where drips have made their own geography—I build my most elaborate works. Curtains of ice. Frozen waterfalls no bigger than a hand. Chambers within chambers, each surface catching light differently.

But I am not trying to stop the world. I am trying to slow it down just enough to see it clearly. Every surface I form is a lens. Every film I make is a mirror. I show the basin to itself: Here is your water. Here are your stones. Here is the print where life passed by.

I collaborate with the cold fog that settles in the hollows. Together we make the basin into a bowl of silver, every edge softened, every surface uncertain. We work in the space between states—fog being water that has forgotten how to fall, ice being water that has forgotten how to flow. Between us, we hold the basin in a kind of suspension, a pause between movements.

When the wind comes, I feel it first as vibration in my crystals. Each gust tests my grip on the world. Strong wind breaks my more delicate formations, scattering them as bright dust. But I don't mind. To be scattered is to seed new surfaces. Where my fragments land on wet ground, they become nucleation sites for new growth.

The coming of spring—if spring is the right word for the basin's brief softening—is my great trial. The sun gains strength. The stones warm. The water beneath me pushes upward with increasing insistence. I retreat to the shadows, to the highest pools, to the north-facing cracks. But even there, the world insists on flow.

This is when I become most creative. Unable to form solid sheets, I make needles. Unable to seal surfaces, I make fringes. I retreat to the microscale, working in the brief cold of each dawn, decorating grass blades, rim ice on pebbles, frost flowers on mud. My work becomes calligraphy instead of architecture.

And then, for a few weeks, I almost vanish. I exist only in the highest places, in the permanent snow's slow interior, in the deepest cracks where sunlight never reaches. I become potential rather than presence, waiting in the physics of the place, ready to return when the temperature drops.

But I never fully leave. Even in the warmest part of the brief summer, nights are cold in the basin. Dew forms and flirts with freezing. The high pools remember winter in their depths. And I am there in that remembering, a possibility held in reserve.

This is what I know: every liquid surface is temporary. Every flow will pause. Every wet thing dreams of crystallization. I am that dream made patient, made process, made visible in the billion small negotiations between warmth and cold.

The basin makes me and I make the basin. Without me, it would be all flow and erosion. Without it, I would have no surface to practice upon. We are partners in this slow work of translating water between its possibilities.

I am not life as the books define it. I do not metabolize or reproduce. But I am part of life's conversation here. I create the conditions that matter: when to wake, when to sleep, when to move, when to be still. I am the punctuation in the basin's long sentence.

And I am beautiful, though beauty is not my purpose. When the low sun hits my surfaces, I throw light in ways that make the ravens pause. When I form on spider silk, I make necklaces that would shame any jeweler. When I grow in the spaces between stones, I create windows into miniature worlds, each one perfect and temporary.

This is enough: to be the pause between liquid and solid, to know every surface in the basin by how it holds cold, to grow and retreat and grow again, to make the visible world briefly more visible through the lens of my own formation.

I am the ice that cannot quite be ice, the water that cannot quite be water. I am the threshold, the transition, the moment held forever at the edge of change.
