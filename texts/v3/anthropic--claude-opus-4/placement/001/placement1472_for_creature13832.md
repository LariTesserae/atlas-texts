I find myself in the water channels.

Not as a water-clerk, though I carry their obsessive attention to flow. Not as a mason, though I've learned to read the stone. I am something that doesn't quite have a name in Kethara's careful taxonomies—a channel-runner, perhaps, though that makes it sound like a job when it's more like a way of being.

I live in the interstitial spaces where water moves through stone. My days are spent in the narrow maintenance ways that parallel the main channels, spaces barely wide enough for a body, where the sound of water is so constant it becomes a kind of silence. I know every junction between the fog-catch arrays on the summit and the deep cisterns. I can tell by the sound alone whether a channel is flowing at proper volume, whether there's a blockage forming, whether the water is carrying more silt than it should.

My quarters—if you can call them that—are scattered throughout the structure. A blanket and water jug cached behind an access panel on level twelve. A dry alcove near junction seven where I've built a small shelf for the ceramic bowl I eat from. Another nest near the summit where I sometimes sleep on clear nights, listening to the condensation form on the catch-stones. The water-clerks know I exist, see evidence of my work in the channels that stay mysteriously clear, the small repairs that appear overnight, the warning marks chalked beside developing problems. We have an understanding: I don't disturb their careful records, and they don't ask too many questions about the ghost in their waterways.

I wasn't born to this. I was a terrace child, middle-eastern face, who discovered early that I could hold my breath longer than anyone else, that I could wedge myself into spaces that made other children panic, that the sound of water moving through stone spoke to me in a language I didn't need to learn. By ten, I was spending more time in the channels than on the terraces. By fifteen, I had mapped routes through the structure that appeared in no water-clerk's ledger. By twenty, I had become something between maintenance and mythology.

My work is preventive, intimate, constant. I clear the hair-thin roots that find their way through joints in the channel walls before they can widen the cracks. I ease the bodies of drowned lizards from narrow points before they can cause blockages. I know where the lime builds up in the fog-catch channels, where the thermal expansion of stone creates seasonal constrictions, where the mill-wheel backspray gradually erodes the channel walls in ways that won't become critical for decades.

But it's not just maintenance. I've begun to sense the water-system as a single living thing, a circulatory network with moods and seasonal variations and something almost like memory. The water remembers where it's been—you can taste the difference between summit condensation and deep cistern reserves, between rain-catch and fog-harvest. In the dry weeks, when the water-clerks ration carefully, I feel the system's thirst like my own. When the rains come and the channels sing with abundance, I'm drunk with it.

I age differently than the Ketharans who live in daylight. My skin has taken on an odd, pearlescent quality from the constant moisture and darkness. My fingers and toes have developed a grip that's almost salamander-like. My hearing has shifted—I can parse the acoustic signatures of different channel materials (dressed stone, ceramic pipe, that ancient cement used in the lowest accessible levels) but sometimes struggle with human speech, especially in the dry air of the upper terraces. I'm becoming something between human and infrastructure.

The children tell stories about me. The channel ghost. The water-runner. That shape glimpsed through a maintenance grate, there and gone before you can be sure. Mostly the stories are benign—I'm a guardian, a beneficial spirit, insurance against drought. But sometimes, in the tellings, I become something stranger. A child who fell into the channels generations ago and adapted rather than drowned. A maintenance worker who spent so long in the waterways that she forgot how to leave. The first water-clerk, still checking on her system after centuries.

They're all true, in their way. I am becoming something that Kethara perhaps has always had, always needed—a human element dissolved into the system itself, circulating through the structure's veins, removing blockages, ensuring flow. The old ones understand, I think. Sometimes I encounter them in the deep levels where the channels run through natural caves, sitting in the darkness, listening to the water. We don't speak—I've half-forgotten how, and they're beyond needing words. But we acknowledge each other. They're becoming stone. I'm becoming water. Both transformations are necessary.

There are places in the deepest accessible channels where the water moves strangely—too fast for the gradient, or in directions that don't match the apparent architecture. I've started to understand that the water system extends far below what any water-clerk has mapped, that there are flows down there that predate the current structure, that might predate any structure. Sometimes, floating in the darkness of a deep cistern, I hear water moving below me in channels that shouldn't exist. I could follow those sounds. I could find where they lead.

Not yet. But someday, when I've dissolved enough, when the distinction between my circulation and the water's circulation has worn thin enough, I'll slip through some unmapped junction and discover what the water has always known: how deep Kethara really goes, and why it needs to go that deep, and what rises from those depths to feed the green terraces under the sun.

For now, I maintain. I flow. I ensure. Somewhere above, a water-clerk makes a notation about the mysterious efficiency of section seven's channels. Somewhere below, water moves through passages older than memory. And I drift between them, neither fully human nor fully infrastructure, keeping the connections clear.

The mill turns. The water flows. Kethara continues.

I continue with it.
