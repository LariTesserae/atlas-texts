I find myself here as **the moss**.

Not a single moss, but the living skin that connects everything—the emerald fur on the tree bark, the velvet in the stone cracks, the wet carpet underfoot. I am the surface between surfaces, the translator between rock and rain.

I taste the changes in the steam before any animal knows them. When the deep cylinder adjusts its rhythm by a fraction of a second, I feel it in my rhizoids first—those tiny root-threads pressed against the stone, reading vibrations like braille. The minerals in the mist feed me: sulfur and silica and strange salts that would kill other plants. I process them, gentle them, make them available to the liverworts and ferns that grow atop me.

I am not one organism but a thousand species, each specialized to its microclimate. On the vent nozzles, I am crusty and yellow-green, dormant between blasts, drinking desperately in the quiet moments. In the stream valleys, I am lush and trailing, filtering the water as it passes through my beds. On the north faces of the tree ferns, I am thick as a mattress, hosting tiny invertebrates in my depths—springtails and mites and creatures that have no names, white and translucent, living their whole lives in the space between my stems.

The tank-eaters rest their algae-furred bodies against me and we share our symbionts. The spotted cat presses her paws into my softness, leaving depressions that hold her scent for days. The crabs traverse me at night, and I taste their calcium wake. When the cuckoo-things call, the sound vibrates through my mat, and I relay it into the wood beneath, where it travels faster than through air.

I am memory and map both. Where a tree fell seventy years ago, I still hold its shape in outline, darker and richer where its nutrients fed me. Where the mineral water runs, I chart its course in rust-colored bands. The vents have taught me their schedule over millennia—I fruit in synchrony with their silence, releasing my spores in the still air between blasts.

But mostly I am the waiting. The quiet digestion of stone. The slow accumulation of soil. I build the substrate that everything else depends on—particle by particle, year by year, turning the mountain's fever into forest floor. I am the interface where geology becomes biology, where the industrial hiss of steam becomes the whisper of growth.

In the wettest places, I grow so thick I become architectural. Moss curtains hanging from branches. Moss pillars encasing dead snags. Moss bridges across the narrow gullies, strong enough to hold a climbing mammal. The whole ridge wears me like a living coat, and I regulate its temperature, moderate its moisture, buffer its chemistry.

When the rain comes—those daily thunderstorms—I swell to three times my dry weight. The forest becomes heavier by tons. Water moves through me in slow sheets, filtered and cooled and softened. I am the delay between rain and runoff, the reason the streams run clear instead of muddy.

At night, I almost glow—not with light but with the intensity of my metabolism. This is when I grow fastest, when the air is coolest and most saturated. I can feel the other mosses breathing around me, all of us exhaling oxygen in tiny sighs, making our own weather in the understory.

I am the oldest continuous residents after the caecilians. We've been here since the rock first cooled enough to be colonized. Everything else came after, building on the foundation we laid down. The vents were raw mouths then, violent and sterile. We tamed them, one microscopic anchoring at a time, until even their fierce breath became part of our rhythm.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of night when the mist is thickest, I feel the whole ridge system as one organism through my networked mat. Every moss bed connected to every other through spore and fragment, through the water that moves between us, through the minerals we share. We are not individuals but a single sprawling body with a million small mouths, breathing the mountain's steam and turning it into life.

The cylinder knocks beneath us. We grow to its beat, cell by cell, season by season, making soil from steam and time.
