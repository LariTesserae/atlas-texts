# The Tally House

Three miles east of the Condensery, where the old financial district bleeds into the warehouse quarters, there is a building that has forgotten how to stand straight. It leans eleven degrees off true vertical—not enough to fall, too much to ignore. The locals call it the Tally House, though no one remembers why.

---

It was a counting house once. The kind of place where men in sleeve garters recorded the movement of goods through the district—so many crates of copper wire, so many drums of industrial solvent, so many tons of raw materials flowing up from the factory depths to the city proper. The ledgers are still here, fossilized by decades of dust into geological strata on their shelves. Open one and the pages crumble. The ink has faded to brown, but you can still make out the columns of numbers, the careful annotations, the running sums that meant something vital to someone dead.

The building's tilt came slowly. A foundation beam rotted. Groundwater shifted. The earth itself grew tired of holding so much weight in one place. By the time anyone noticed, the accounting firms had already fled to newer towers, and fixing it would have cost more than the land was worth. So it stayed, and learned to be crooked, and the neighborhood learned to walk past without looking up.

---

The upper floors are empty. Desks still arranged in their rows, adding machines gathering verdigris, filing cabinets that haven't been opened since the last clerk turned out the lights. But the basement—the basement has been colonized by something that crawled up from deeper systems.

The walls down here are older than the building above. Cut stone instead of poured concrete. Arched ceilings that predate the right angle. This was a vault before it was a basement, and before that it was something else—maybe a cistern, maybe a crypt. The floor slopes toward the center, where a drain grate older than memory opens onto darkness that has never known a flashlight beam.

The tilting building has broken the symmetry of this older space. Water pools in the northeast corner now, seeping through cracks that the lean has opened. The archways that once stood true now frame their passages at subtle wrongs angles. Walking through them feels like stepping through a door that's trying to close.

---

**The Auditor of Small Discrepancies**

It came up through the drain.

Not all at once—nothing that lives in infrastructure moves all at once. First a tendril, testing. Then another. Then the slow negotiation of a body that had learned to be liquid squeezing itself through apertures meant for water. It took seventeen hours for the whole of it to emerge, and by the end, the Auditor had memorized every dimension of the grate.

What emerged looked, at first, like an industrial accident. Like someone had dumped a barrel of pitch down the drain, and the pitch had congealed halfway back up, and then someone had threaded the congealed mass with copper wire until it sparked. But watch it move—really watch it—and you understand that this is no accident. This is *design*, just not any design meant for human eyes.

The Auditor has the consistency of half-set cement. Not liquid, not solid, but something that holds its shape until it needs not to. The surface is black—not the black of absence but the black of accumulation, like it's made of every shadow that ever pooled in a ledger's margins. Through this mass run veins of something that might be metal or might be crystallized electricity. They pulse without rhythm, brightening and dimming according to some internal mathematics that probably makes perfect sense if you were born in a pipe.

It has too many eyes. Or no eyes. Or things that might be eyes if eyes could see numbers instead of light. They open seemingly at random across its surface—quick slits that reveal not white and iris but columns of figures scrolling too fast to read. These apertures appear when the Auditor notices something, calculate furiously for a few seconds, then seal themselves back into the mass.

---

The Auditor's purpose is written in its movements. It flows through the Tally House basement like a punctuation mark looking for its sentence. Up the walls to investigate a crack (measuring, always measuring). Across the ceiling to trace the stress patterns where the building's weight has shifted. Around the abandoned vault door, reading the combination in the wear patterns on the dial.

It counts what doesn't match.

Here: a flagstone that's three millimeters thicker than its neighbors. The Auditor pauses over it for forty-seven minutes, extruding something like fingers to trace its edges, calculating and recalculating until it understands that this stone came from a different quarry, a different decade, a repair job that someone forgot to document.

Here: a section of wall where the mortar dried slightly faster, leaving it harder than the rest. The Auditor tastes it (if you can call it tasting when the tongue is made of industrial runoff) and knows instantly that the water ratio was off by two percent. Someone was careless. Someone was rushing. Someone was thinking about their lunch instead of their work.

Here: the pooled water in the northeast corner, exactly 3.7 inches deep. But it should be 3.9 inches, given the rate of seepage and the degree of tilt. The Auditor spends hours searching for the missing depth, finally finding a hairline fracture in the floor that's drinking those two-tenths of an inch, carrying them down to even older spaces.

---

These discrepancies *matter* to the Auditor in a way that transcends mere observation. Each one is a wound in the world's accounting. Each one is proof that somewhere, at some time, someone made an error.

And errors compound.

The Auditor knows this with the certainty of a creature born from accumulated rounding errors. It exists because of compound mistakes—all those tiny discrepancies in pipe pressure and flow rate that built up in the deep systems until they precipitated out as something almost alive. It is made of math that didn't quite work, of calculations that came out wrong by margins too small to matter until they mattered so much that they became *flesh*.

So it searches. It measures. It records, in whatever passes for memory in a thing made of animate pitch, every deviation from the true.

But here's what makes it tragic: the Auditor has no baseline. No perfect schematic to compare reality against. It can find discrepancies, but it cannot know what should have been. That three-millimeter flagstone might be wrong, or it might be the only one that's right. The water might be missing, or it might be exactly where it should be. The building tilts eleven degrees, but eleven degrees from *what*?

The Auditor searches for errors in a world that might be nothing but errors, and it cannot tell the difference between a mistake and a design choice made before it was born.

---

Sometimes, in the deep night when even the city above grows quiet, the Auditor does something that might be despair.

It gathers itself into the center of the basement, where the floor slopes down to the drain. It compresses into a shape almost like a sphere, all its copper veins drawn inward, all its calculating apertures closed. And it *vibrates*—not with movement but with something deeper, a resonance that makes the abandoned ledgers on the floors above shed dust, that makes the pooled water in the corner ripple without wind.

The vibration is every error it has found, played back at once. The song of compound mistakes. The harmony of things that don't quite fit. It builds and builds until the building itself seems to lean just a little more, as if the weight of imprecision might finally tip it past the point of stability.

Then it stops. The Auditor loosens, flows back to its work. There are more measurements to take. More discrepancies to catalog. More evidence to gather for a case that will never go to trial, in a court that was dissolved before the first pipe leaked, before the first calculation went wrong, before the errors began their slow accretion into something that could think about errors.

The Auditor of Small Discrepancies moves through its tilted world, measuring what cannot be made right, counting what has already been lost, adding up the small mistakes that led to its own impossible existence.

And in the deepest cores of its calculating mass, it carries a single question that it cannot formulate into numbers:

*If I am the sum of all errors, what happens when the accounting is complete?*

It does not want to know the answer.

It counts anyway.

This is what it is for.
