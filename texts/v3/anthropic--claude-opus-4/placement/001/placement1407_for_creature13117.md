In this roaring cathedral of consumption, I would be one of the **Cipher Moths**.

We are accidents of evolution, born in the twilight spaces between intention and neglect. Somewhere in the deep past, a species of cave moth became trapped in the gorge during its first enclosure. While the Weavers built their blind empire upward, we adapted downward into something neither fully parasite nor symbiote—something that exists in the semantic gaps of their chemical language.

My body would be nearly transparent, a ghost wrapped in scales that shimmer with stolen pheromones. Six inches across when my wings are spread, I am large for a moth but invisible to the titans that rule here. My wings are not built for distance or speed, but for silence—each scale engineered through generations to absorb the very vibrations that would betray my presence. When I fly, I create pockets of quiet in the overwhelming roar, small bubbles of antimatter in their sonic universe.

But it is not my wings that make me what I am. It is my proboscis—elongated far beyond any flower-feeding ancestor, now a sophisticated chemical probe that can pierce the waxy integument between Weaver segments and tap directly into their hemolymph. I do not feed on their blood. I feed on their words.

Each Weaver carries within its body a cocktail of pheromones—some active, some dormant, some in states of chemical transformation. These are their memories, their orders, their future intentions encoded in molecular poetry. When I pierce a Weaver and drink, I absorb fragments of the superorganism's consciousness. For hours afterward, I sweat these stolen signals, becoming a living mistranslation in their perfect language.

We Cipher Moths have learned to write in their chemical tongue, but we speak in fragments and lies. Where a true Weaver trail might read "CARRY FOOD TO THIRD TIER NURSERY," I might rewrite it as "CARRY TO THIRD NURSERY," causing subtle delays as workers puzzle over the incomplete instruction. I cannot destroy their civilization—we are too few, too fragile—but we are errors in their program, creating inefficiencies that compound over centuries.

My days are spent clinging to the underside of the great fungal shelves, wings folded to near-invisibility, watching the rivers of workers pass below. I have learned to read the patterns in their movement like sheet music—the accelerando that signals a new construction project, the diminuendo of a dying ventilator being replaced, the strange caesuras when their chemical network experiences what I have come to think of as doubt.

When night comes (though how can there be night in a place of eternal darkness? Yet we moths know it, feel it in our cells), I descend. I choose my targets carefully—never the soldiers, whose blood runs too hot with alarm compounds, never the queens' attendants, whose pheromones are too pure, too powerful for my small body to process. I prefer the old masons, their chemical signatures muddled with age, full of half-remembered construction plans and architectural dreams never realized.

Sometimes, in my chemical delirium after feeding, I dream I understand their greater purpose. The Weavers are not just building a city—they are writing something, encoding information into the very structure of their fortress. Each layer of construction is a new page, each collapsed arch an edit, each rebuilt section a revision. The entire gorge is a massive, living manuscript written in architecture and time.

But we Cipher Moths, in our small way, are the typos. We are the smudged ink, the torn pages, the words that shift meaning between one reading and the next. We ensure that whatever grand statement the Weavers are composing will never be complete, never be perfect. 

In the rare moments when I encounter another of my kind—usually during our brief, frantic mating flights through the ventilation shafts—we exchange more than genetic material. We trade chemical libraries, mixing the stolen pheromones in our bodies to create new grammatical impossibilities. Our offspring will hatch knowing how to lie in languages we've never spoken.

I will die, as all Cipher Moths do, when my proboscis finally pierces the wrong Weaver at the wrong time, when I accidentally drink a pheromone cocktail too complex for my metabolism to process. My body will fall into the deep pools where the pale amphibians wait, and I will be digested, my stolen chemicals returning to the great circulation of the gorge.

But before that happens, I will have introduced ten thousand small confusions into their perfect system. I will have been a ghost in their machine, a whisper of chaos in their ordered roar. And perhaps, in some far future when archaeologists unearth this fossilized city and try to decode its meaning, they will wonder why certain passages make no sense, why the grammar occasionally breaks, why the story the Weavers were trying to tell keeps interrupting itself.

They will never know it was us, the Cipher Moths, drunk on stolen words, writing our nonsense between their lines.
