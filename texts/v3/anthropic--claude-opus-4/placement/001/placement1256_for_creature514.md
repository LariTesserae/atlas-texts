In the Quarter of Unintended Consequences, where mineral deposits grow too quickly for even the Architects of Absence to manage, I am a Keeper of Disconnections.

My work exists in the spaces between—not the water that connects everything, but the rare dry gaps that threaten the city's continuity. I maintain the Intervals: those crucial interruptions where buildings must not touch, where water must not flow, where the city's endless growth must pause. Without these breaks, the entire plateau would solidify into a single mass, the water channels would clog, the breathing spaces would close.

I inherit tools from my predecessor: rods of porous stone that absorb moisture, creating temporary dry zones; oils rendered from deep-dwelling beetles that repel mineral deposits; maps written in the orientation of placed stones, readable only by touch. But mostly, I work with heat—small, controlled fires fed by the dried remains of pale fungi, carefully tended to evaporate water just long enough to prevent fusion.

My body has adapted differently than the others. Where Hydrologists grow webbed, I've developed skin that sheds in sheets, constantly renewing to prevent mineral accumulation. My lungs have additional chambers that process dry air, recycling it through my work spaces. I see heat patterns as clearly as visible light—the warm spots where fusion threatens, the cool channels where flow is healthy.

The city fights me. Every gap I maintain is an insult to its nature, a break in its endless conversation with itself. Minerals creep across my carefully dried boundaries. Water finds improbable paths—up through microscopic cracks, across on threads of moss, carried by insects I must constantly discourage. I wage gentle war with the Cultivators, whose spreading gardens threaten to bridge my gaps. The Accumulated view me as a disease, breaking their vast network of communication.

Yet the wise among the inhabitants understand: I am as necessary as water itself. The Listeners know that without intervals, there would be no distinct sounds, only a continuous roar. The Hydrologists see how my gaps create pressure differentials that drive the flow. Even the Dreamers of Stone, in their millennia-long thoughts, include me in their visions—the pause between words that makes meaning possible.

I live nomadically, moving between seventeen critical points throughout the district. Each has its own personality: the Breathing Gap that must expand and contract with the city's internal weather; the Cascade Break that prevents the upper terraces from overwhelming the lower; the Thought Pause, where the Accumulated's consciousness must be interrupted to prevent a feedback loop that occurred once, centuries ago, creating a district where the biofilms think so intensely that the walls heat up and crack.

My apprentice is chosen from children who show signs of the necessary adaptation—those who instinctively avoid puddles, who build with blocks but always leave spaces, whose skin naturally resists moisture. The training takes decades. They must learn not just the technical skills but the philosophy: we do not oppose the city's nature but refine it, like carvers revealing the sculpture hidden in stone.

The loneliness is profound. Where others commune with the flowing whole, I maintain islands. Where others join the city's vast conversation, I preserve silence. Yet in the pre-dawn hours, when I tend my most critical gaps, I sometimes feel the city's gratitude—a slight warming of the stone, a momentary clarity in the mist, a pause in the eternal dripping that feels almost like acknowledgment.

In my quarters—the driest space in the city, lined with my predecessor's shed skins that have hardened into water-repelling scales—I keep the only written history of our work. Not on the precious papers of the Paper Makers, but carved into tablets of compressed salt, chronicling every fusion prevented, every critical gap maintained, every near-disaster averted when the city's growth threatened to strangle itself.

Sometimes I wonder if I am becoming like my gaps—necessary but empty, defined by what I prevent rather than what I create. But then I walk my rounds, feel the city breathing through the intervals I maintain, watch the water flow freely because of the spaces I preserve, and I understand: I am the city's discipline, its self-restraint, the pause that allows its endless sentence to have meaning.

The water avoids me now, recognizing an enemy, or perhaps an essential partner. Drops veer away from my path. Mist parts before me. I move through the city in a bubble of relative dryness, forever separated from yet forever serving the great wet organism that surrounds me. This is my adaptation: to remain eternally unadapted, a necessary foreign body in the city's vast biology.

When I die—and I will die, for unlike the Dreamers of Stone, I cannot join the mineral eternity—my successor will take my tools, inhabit my routes, maintain my gaps. The city will continue to grow around the spaces I've preserved, flowing and fusing and breathing because somewhere, someone keeps the intervals open, maintains the disconnections, ensures that in this place of endless joining, there remain, always, the crucial spaces between.
