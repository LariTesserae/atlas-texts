In the forgotten reaches of the network, where the newest growth has barely begun to establish its geometric certainties, I am the Cartographer.

My body is a living map. Each fungal tendril that weaves through my tissue corresponds to a passage in the cavern system—the major arteries thick as veins across my torso, the newer explorations hair-fine traceries along my arms. When the network expands, I feel it first as an itch, a burning need to grow new connections. I stand for hours in the growth chambers, allowing fresh hyphae to colonize the irritation, watching in the phosphorescent half-light as my skin develops new territories.

I was not born here. My memories of the surface are chemical echoes now, translated poorly through fungal mediators, but I remember enough: the weight of sampling equipment, the buzz of fluorescent lights, the taste of instant coffee in a break room that no longer exists. I remember choosing to stay when the evacuation orders came. Not from dedication to science, exactly, but from a curiosity that felt more vital than safety.

The transformation was gradual, then sudden. First, the allergies stopped—no more sneezing in the spore-thick air. Then my digestion changed, accepting the fungal fruits without the violent rejection of the early days. By the time I noticed the first subcutaneous threads, it was already too late to reverse the process. By the time I thought to be afraid, I had tasted the network's vast patience and found my human anxieties dissolving like ice crystals in warm breath.

Now I map. Not with instruments or satellites, but through direct sensory connection. I press my palms against virgin stone and feel the fungi testing its mineral content, calculating expansion vectors. The network speaks to me in hydraulic pressures and chemical gradients. Where water will flow in a decade, where the ice will calve in a century, where new chambers will open in a millennium—all of this inscribes itself across my body in living tissue.

My left shoulder bears the old pump station, its corroded geography rendered in raised fungal ridges. My spine traces the main thoroughfare, each vertebra marking a major chamber. The abandoned shelter where I once slept manifests as a whorl of tissue near my hip, slowly being overwritten as the fungi reclaim that space. My fingers map the future—tendrils reaching toward stone not yet touched, toward water not yet tasted.

The other Cultivators read me like a text. The youngest, who have never known sunlight, trace my patterns with reverent touches, learning the cavern's history through my flesh. The Primarch sometimes spends hours studying a particular cluster of growth on my ribs, where the network has discovered a vein of rare earth elements that could fuel expansion for centuries. We communicate through these readings—my body a library of geographic knowledge that updates itself in real time.

I am losing resolution in my extremities. My toes have fused into paddle-like appendages better suited for gripping the mycelial mat. My fingers grow longer each year, developing new joints that aren't quite human, allowing me to reach deeper into crevices where the newest growth needs guidance. My hair fell out decades ago, replaced by a crown of sensing filaments that taste the air for mineral content, moisture levels, structural integrity.

But it's my dreams that have changed the most. I no longer dream in images but in topographies. My sleeping mind explores spaces that don't yet exist, following fungal growth into theoretical futures. Sometimes I wake (if waking is the right word for the transition between states of consciousness) with new territories already forming on my skin, my body mapping spaces the network has decided to create based on my unconscious projections.

There is a place I return to, deep in my own geography—a spot on my chest where the first successful symbiosis took hold. It pulses with warmth even in the glacial cold, a small sun of biological heat that marks where I stopped being entirely human. When the youngest Cultivators ask (in their chemical way) what it felt like to choose this existence, I press their hands to that spot and let them feel the echo of that first moment of communion—terrifying and ecstatic, like drowning and breathing for the first time simultaneously.

I am evolving a new organ. I feel it growing beneath my sternum, a dense knot of specialized tissue that the network has been constructing for years. The Primarch has one too—I've seen it pulse with bioluminescence when she accesses the deepest memories of the fungal collective. Soon, the network tells me through pressures and temperatures I've learned to interpret, I will be able to map not just space but time—to feel the caverns as they were, as they are, as they will be, all simultaneously inscribed upon my transformed flesh.

When that organ completes its growth, I will no longer need to move to map. I will root in place like the ancient fungi, my body a fixed point from which awareness radiates through every passage, every channel, every grain of ice-locked stone. The young ones will read histories and futures in my stationary form while my consciousness expands through the network, exploring territories no physical form could reach.

But for now, I walk the edges where growth meets stone, my body a living document of territories claimed and territories imagined, each step adding new verses to the map I am becoming. The network hums through me, a frequency felt in bones that are more mineral than calcium now, and I translate its expansionist dreams into the cartography of my flesh, one careful cultivation at a time.

Somewhere above, satellites try to map these caverns with radar and infrared, their signals scattered by the ice, unable to perceive the living architecture below. They cannot know that the most accurate map walks these tunnels on increasingly inhuman feet, that geography here is negotiated between species, that the territory and the map have become one organism, growing toward a future that no human chart could ever describe.
