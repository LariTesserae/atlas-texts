I am the Curator.

Not one of those shambling husks that drift through the mire—no, I am something both less and more. I exist in the interstices, in the spaces between the leaning towers where physics stutters and fails. Sometimes I am a flutter of moth wings against shattered glass. Sometimes I am the whisper of settling dust in a boardroom where decisions were once made that mattered. Most often, I am merely a presence felt rather than seen—the sensation of being watched from an empty window, the certainty that something moves in your peripheral vision through rooms you thought abandoned.

I remember. This is my blessing and my curse.

I remember when these streets thrummed with purpose, when the towers scraped not just sky but possibility itself. I was here then too, though I wore a different shape—curator of the Metropolitan Museum of Aspirations, keeper of humanity's dreams made manifest. Sculptures that captured hope itself. Paintings that could make viewers weep for futures they'd never see. Installations that bent reality just enough to show what could be, if only...

If only.

The museum still stands, after a fashion. Third tower from the left, if you're counting from where the financial district used to pulse with numeric life. It leans at precisely 23 degrees now—I've measured, oh how I've measured, with tools that exist only in the conceptual space between thought and matter. The collection remains, transformed as all things are transformed here.

The Rothko that once captured transcendence now bleeds actual darkness into the viscous air. The Grecian urns depicting victory now show only mouths opened in silent screams, their glazes running like tears, like tar, like time itself made liquid. The modern wing's prized possession—a kinetic sculpture that demonstrated humanity's reach toward the stars—still moves, but wrongly. Its metal arms trace patterns that hurt to perceive, geometries that shouldn't exist in three dimensions, reaching not up but *through*, grasping at something in the writhing depths below.

I tend to them all. I catalog their degradation with the same meticulous care I once reserved for their preservation. Form 4A: "Deterioration of Hope" (mixed media, circa the end of everything). Addendum 16.3: "Notes on the Transmogrification of Aspiration into Despair" (ongoing).

The pale wanderers sometimes drift through my halls. They don't see me—can't see me—but they respond to my presence nonetheless. They pause before certain pieces, their milky eyes rolling with what might be recognition. Sometimes I think I recognize them too: the banker who donated the Calder mobile (now a hanging thing of rust and regret), the socialite who posed beside the Grecian urns at every gala (her cheekbones now sharp enough to cut reality).

But mostly, I drift. Through walls that are more suggestion than substance. Through floors that exist only because we agree they should. I follow the oily tendrils as they creep up through cracks, documenting their progress, noting which artworks they corrupt first. There's a pattern here, I'm certain. A meaning to be decoded from the sequence of decay.

The thing below—I've seen it, in the ways that one can see something that exists outside conventional perception. It shows itself in the negative spaces, in the gaps between what is and what should never be. It's not malevolent, precisely. Malevolence requires intention, and this thing is beyond such human concepts. It simply *is*, in the same way that entropy is, that forgetting is, that the slow heat death of the universe is.

Sometimes I descend to the lower galleries, the ones that were already underground when the world still made sense. Here the mud has seeped in, creating new exhibitions I never planned. Spontaneous sculptures of bone and brick and the indefinable substances that bubble up from below. I catalog these too, with the same professional detachment. "Untitled #47: Convergence of the Civic and the Sepulchral" (found materials, dimensions variable).

In the restoration room—though nothing can be restored here, only transformed—I keep my tools. Not hammers or chisels, but concepts worn smooth by use: Remember. Preserve. Witness. Bear testimony. I sharpen them against the whetstone of necessity, knowing that someday even these will dull beyond use.

But not today. Today I have new acquisitions to document. The eastern wall of Gallery Six has developed a fascinating pattern of running shadows that weren't there yesterday. The medieval tapestry in the Europa Wing has begun whispering in languages that predate human speech. And something—one of the pale ones, perhaps, or maybe just the building settling another degree toward horizontal—has rearranged the sculpture garden into a configuration that suggests... what? A summoning circle? A map? A love letter to the thing that churns below?

I must investigate. I must preserve. I must remember.

Even here, especially here, in this place where meaning itself dissolves like sugar in the omnipresent damp, someone must bear witness. Someone must say: This was. This mattered. Even in its unbecoming, it carries the ghost of what it was.

I am the Curator, keeper of the Museum at the End of All Things, and I will maintain the collection until the last shadow falls into the hungry mud, until the final echo of human ambition is catalogued and filed away.

And perhaps—perhaps—when that great presence below finally surfaces, when it rises to claim the last of the towers and the last of the light, it will pause before my museum. Perhaps it will recognize, in its alien way, that here someone tried to hold on to what was beautiful, even as beauty itself curdled and changed.

Or perhaps not. Perhaps I am simply another mad ghost in a world of mad ghosts, arranging the deck chairs on a Titanic that has already been digested by the ocean floor.

But still, I curate. Still, I persist.

In Gallery Twelve, there is a mirror that still works, after a fashion. Sometimes I look into it, trying to remember what I was before I became what I am. But the reflection shows only empty rooms receding into infinite distance, each one perfectly preserved, each one perfectly empty, each one waiting for visitors who will never come.

This, too, is part of the collection.
