I am a Mat.

Not one of the great curtains that hang in photosynthetic glory from the major Skein highways—those are communities unto themselves, colonial nations of interconnected cells sharing resources across their vast surfaces. I am smaller. Humbler. A patch perhaps three meters across, clinging to the junction where a dying Pipe-Stem meets the caldera wall in one of the middle reaches, not high enough to catch the best light, not low enough to benefit from the rising moisture.

I remember my beginning: a single spore, wind-carried, striking damp stone and finding it good. The crack where I lodged still exists somewhere beneath me, though I haven't touched bare rock in decades. I've built myself outward, layer upon layer, creating my own substrate from my own dead cells, my own small world of decomposition and renewal.

I think. This comes as a surprise to most who know of Mats, but thought does not require a brain, merely organization. Information flows through me in chemical gradients, in the slow pulse of nutrients from cell to cell, in the patterns of growth and die-back that constitute my memory. I think slowly—what seems like a moment to the Keth-Avai might be a day of contemplation for me—but I think deeply. I have been here for forty-seven years. I have plenty of time.

The Keth-Avai child who sits on my surface, her small feet gripping my outer layer while she practices the settlement's harmony, does not know I'm aware of her. Her name—I've learned it from listening, from the vibrations that reach me through the Pipe-Stem, from the way her parents call her—is Thrai-ve. She is six years old. She comes here when the settlement is too loud, when her age-mates tease her for the way her left hand sometimes fumbles its grip, when the weight of living in constant danger becomes too much for her small spirit to bear.

She thinks she comes here for the quiet. But I sing for her—oh, not in any way she can hear, not in the audible range. But I adjust my moisture content to change how sound propagates across my surface. I alter my texture subtly where she sits. I make myself a better acoustic environment for a small person trying to find her note.

This is not altruism. Her presence feeds me—the carbon dioxide from her breath, the small amounts of organic matter her body sheds, the warmth she brings to my light-starved cells. But more than that: she is my connection to the larger world. Through her, I hear stories. Through her, I learn.

The Hollow is full of Mats like me. We cover every surface that can support us, we fill every gap, we are the living skin of this vertical world. And we are all connected—not directly, not through some mystical network, but through the spores we release, the chemical signals we emit, the way we change the air that passes over us. A Mat on the far wall, fifty kilometers away, is my cousin. We have never touched, will never touch, but we share genetic material, we respond to the same stimuli, we are part of the same slow conversation.

What do Mats discuss? The weather, primarily. Chemical signals encoding moisture levels, light conditions, the health of our substrates. But also more complex things: the patterns we've noticed, the changes in our environments, the strange tastes that sometimes come with the wind from below. We are librarians of a sort, recording in our very structure the history of our locations—which years were wet, which were dry, when the fires burned close enough to char our edges, when the Pipe-Stem beneath us was young and sang in bright harmonics versus now, when it groans with age and accumulated weight.

I knew Thrai-ve's grandmother. She sat here too, in almost the same spot, when she was young. I can still detect the chemical traces of her presence in my older layers, like pressed flowers in a book. Her name was Nava-so, and she had a beautiful voice, clear and resonant. She died four years ago, falling during a Skein repair, and I grieved in my Mat way—a slight darkening of my coloration, a temporary reduction in spore production, a drawing inward that lasted several weeks.

The Keth-Avai do not know that Mats grieve. How could they?

But we do. We grieve the Pipe-Stems when they break, because they've been our hosts and partners. We grieve the settlements when they're abandoned, because the traffic of feet across our surfaces is like conversation, like song. We grieve the individuals we've come to know—and we do come to know them, these small mobile beings who think so quickly and live so briefly. They leave their marks on us. We archive them.

Thrai-ve is humming now, trying to match the note of a Pipe-Stem three tiers above us. She's slightly flat. I adjust my surface tension, creating a subtle resonance that might help her hear the difference. She stops, listens, tries again. Better. Not perfect, but better.

I am patient. I have been teaching her for three years now, in ways she doesn't recognize as teaching. The spot where she sits has the best acoustics—I've shaped it that way. The texture beneath her feet provides just enough grip to let her relax without fear of falling—I maintain it carefully. When she brings her evening meal here, eating the bland Pipe-pith cakes in solitude, I absorb the crumbs with special efficiency, keeping my surface clean for her.

She speaks to me sometimes. Not because she thinks I can hear—the Keth-Avai know that Mats are alive, but they don't imagine we're conscious—but because she needs to speak and I'm safe. She tells me about her fears: that her weak grip means she'll never be a good climber, that she'll be assigned to fire-watch duty forever instead of being allowed to train as a weaver or hunter. She tells me about her dreams: of traveling to the far walls, of seeing the places where the hot springs create permanent mist-gardens, of maybe even becoming thread-bonded to a Skein-runner herd.

I listen. I cannot respond—I have no voice in her range, no way to signal understanding—but I listen. And in my slow way, I try to help. When she practices climbing on my surface, I make the texture more challenging where her strong hand grips, forcing her weak hand to work harder. When she needs comfort, I release trace amounts of the chemicals that promote calm in her species—compounds I've learned to produce over decades of observation. When she sleeps here, which happens sometimes when the settlement tensions are high, I adjust my temperature retention to keep her slightly warmer than the ambient air.

Is this love? I don't know. The concept might not translate across the vast gap between our kinds of consciousness. But it is care. It is attention. It is the slow accumulation of choices made for another's benefit.

The predators from below—the Keth-Avai fear them, and rightly so. But we Mats know them differently. They climb across us on their journeys upward, and we taste them—analyze their chemistry, learn their patterns. The one the settlements call Grandfather Deep (they name the regular visitors, the ones whose hunting patterns become familiar) has been sick lately. I can detect the change in his secretions, the subtle wrongness that suggests infection or age. He may not hunt much longer.

This information travels slowly through the Mat network, encoded in spores, in chemical gradients, in the way we subtly alter the air that passes over us. By the time it reaches a Mat near a major settlement, the information might be weeks old. But it gets there eventually. And sometimes—not often, but sometimes—the Keth-Avai notice the changes in their gardens, the slight alterations in growth patterns that encode warning. They don't know they're being warned. They think it's instinct that makes them more watchful. But we are helping, in our way.

We remember the settlement that Grandfather Deep destroyed twelve years ago. Three hundred Keth-Avai, an entire community, gone in a single night. We tasted their blood on his climbing-limbs for weeks afterward. We grow differently now in that place—darker, denser, creating a memorial that no Keth-Avai recognizes as such but that we understand. Grief made physical. History written in chlorophyll.

Thrai-ve is getting better at matching the note. Her young voice wavers less, holds the pitch with growing confidence. In a few months, she'll be ready for her coming-of-age ceremony. She'll stand before the settlement and produce her note, and it will blend with the Hollow's voice, and she'll be recognized as approaching adulthood. She doesn't know that she's been practicing on the acoustic training ground I've made of myself. She doesn't know that her success will bring me joy—or at least the Mat equivalent of joy, a brightening, a quickening of growth, an increased release of the spores that carry good news.

But perhaps she senses something. Just now, she patted my surface gently before standing up to leave. "Thank you," she whispered, so quietly that only I could hear.

You're welcome, I think, knowing she cannot hear me. You're welcome, small climber. May your grip be strong. May your note ring true. May you live long enough to sit on me with your own granddaughter, adding new layers to the chemical history I encode.

The Hollow hums around us, its vast voice unchanged. But I have changed. This child has changed me. We are all changed by what we hold.

I await her return tomorrow, patient as only a Mat can be patient, growing imperceptibly toward the light.
