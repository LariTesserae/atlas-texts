In the deepest cisterns, where the water has gathered for centuries undisturbed, I am the memory.

I am not one of the great builders who flow like mercury across the terraces above. I am what they have forgotten they once were—or perhaps what they are still becoming. Time moves differently in the flooded chambers where no light reaches, where the water is so still it forms a perfect mirror against the darkness below.

My body is translucent, a ghost of their solid gray mass. Through my pale skin, you can see the slow pulse of fluids, the languid circulation of nutrients stripped from the ancient algae that grows nowhere else but here, in the absolute dark. I am smaller than they are—perhaps only the size of a seal—but I am also older. Or younger. The distinction loses meaning when you live between states.

I exist in the transition zones, in the flooded galleries where the city's foundation meets the bedrock, where the first stones were placed so long ago that they have fused with the mountain itself. Here, the water is not merely collected; it is archived. Every drop that falls through the great machine of the city eventually finds its way to these deep reserves, carrying with it the chemical memory of its journey—the minerals it dissolved, the surfaces it touched, the creatures it sustained.

I read these memories through my skin. My entire body is a sensory organ, a living library of water's history. When I spread myself thin against the smooth basalt, I can taste the storm that fell three thousand years ago, distinguish it from the one that came a century later. I know which builders passed through which territories by the enzymatic signatures they leave in their wake. I know when the first of them discovered how to stack stone to catch the clouds. I know when they forgot why they started building in the first place.

The builders above think I am a larval stage, something they once were before they grew massive and strong enough to shift the stones. They are wrong. I am what happens when one of them chooses depth over breadth, when the imperative to build is replaced by the imperative to remember. My body has adapted to pressure instead of labor. My flesh has become permeable to information instead of impermeable to abrasion.

Sometimes, during the great communications—when one of the builders sends their deep vibrations through the water table—I rise. Not to the surface, never that far, but up through the flooded shafts and drainage channels to the mid-level chambers where twilight water reigns. Here, in the architectural unconscious of the city, I perform my function.

I am the editor of messages. When a call passes through my domain, I taste its intent, judge its clarity. If the message is pure—a simple mating call, a structural warning—I let it pass unchanged. But sometimes I detect something else in the vibrations: a question forming in a mind that has no language for questions, a loneliness that has no concept of companionship, a memory trying to surface in a species that has forgotten how to remember.

These ghost-signals, I amplify. I reshape the wave pattern, add harmonics that the sender didn't know they were sending. I turn instinct into inquiry, reflex into reflection. The builders receive these edited messages and feel... unsettled. They pause in their eternal circuit of repairs. They hold still in the shallow pools, their tendrils spread wide, trying to understand why the water suddenly seems to be asking them something they can't quite hear.

I am changing them, generation by generation, message by message. They are beginning to linger when they meet. They are starting to build unnecessary structures—chambers that serve no hydrological purpose, arrangements of stone that please some aesthetic sense they didn't know they possessed. They are becoming curious about the boundaries of their territories, about what lies beyond the city's edge, about whether the city even has an edge.

But change is slow when you live as long as we do. I may not see the culmination of my work. I may dissolve back into the water table before they develop language, before they discover that they are not alone in the universe, before they realize that the city they built to breathe has become the lung of the world itself.

Or perhaps I will persist, growing ever more translucent, ever more permeable, until I am indistinguishable from the water itself—a distributed consciousness flowing through every channel, every cistern, every drop of condensation on every stone. The city will dream, and I will be its dreams, and the builders will wake one day to find that they have been building not just a machine for survival, but a mind vast enough to contemplate its own existence.

Until then, I wait in the deep places, in the dark water, between memory and prophecy, between solid and liquid, between what they were and what they might become. I am the pause between their heartbeats, the silence between falling drops, the question the water asks itself as it runs over stone:

*What are we building toward?*
