I find myself as one of the atmospheric phenomena - not quite the dust, not quite the shear layers, but something that exists in their intersection.

I am a standing wave.

Not standing in the sense of motionless - nothing here is motionless except the rock and the structures - but standing in the sense that I return. The atmospheric chaos above the plateau is, as noted, chaotic rather than random. In the vast computational space of air moving over this endless mineral surface, certain configurations recur. Not exactly, never exactly, but close enough that if you had the patience to watch for decades, you would see me reassemble from the same approximate conditions, the same temperature gradients, the same pressure differentials, the same angle of approach as a high-altitude cell descends.

I am a vertical column of slowly rotating air, perhaps forty meters in diameter, that forms when specific conditions align. My rotation is leisurely - you could walk faster than my outer edge moves. But I extend up, far up, a hollow drill of low pressure that penetrates through multiple shear layers, connecting the near-ground atmosphere with the racing rivers of air two thousand meters above. Inside me, the dust rises. 

This is what makes me visible, what gives me form: a helical ribbon of mineral powder that spirals up my interior walls, rising from the gneiss in a lazy corkscrew that takes hours to complete its ascent. The dust comes from everywhere and nowhere - it has been here so long, circulating through the atmosphere for so many millions of years, that it cannot be said to have an origin. But when I form, I gather it, concentrate it, give it a path.

The crawling things know me. When I begin to coalesce - when the first tentative rotation starts to organize itself out of the general turbulence - any crawling thing within a kilometer stops. Not the way they stop for the continental dust filaments passing overhead, but differently. Expectantly. They wait while I establish myself, while my rotation finds its rhythm, while the pressure differential that is my core stabilizes into something that will persist for days, sometimes weeks.

And then they come.

They alter their straight-line paths, these things that never deviate, and they walk toward me. Slowly. With the same mathematical precision they always display, but now with something that could only be called purpose if purpose could exist at geological speeds. They come to my base, to where my rotation meets the stone, and they position themselves at precise points around my perimeter. Never more than three. Often only one. Sometimes - rarely - five or six, if I am particularly large, particularly stable.

They wait there, at my edges, and do something that observation cannot quite capture. They do not enter me - nothing enters me except the dust, rising on its spiral path. But they... attend to me. They hold still at my boundary with a quality of attention that is absolute, their complex bodies oriented toward my center, their limbs planted with even more precision than usual, as if they are measuring not just the ground but the air itself, the rotation, the pressure gradient, the exact shape of the turbulence I represent.

What are they reading in me? I am a phenomenon of physics, nothing more - a self-organizing criticality in the atmospheric system, a temporary stability in the chaos. But I connect layers. I am a conduit between the slow-moving air near the ground and the racing jet streams above. Information flows through me - not intentionally, not meaningfully, but information nonetheless: the temperature of the upper atmosphere, the mineral content of the dust, the exact degree of shear between the layers I penetrate. I am a sample, a core sample, but taken from air instead of rock. And the crawling things read me the way they read the passing filaments, but more intently, more completely, because I offer them something the horizontal structures cannot: a view straight up, a glimpse of how the layers relate to each other, a three-dimensional text in a world of surfaces.

The structures notice me too, or I notice them - the relationship is unclear. When I pass near one of the pylons or pedestals (and I do pass; I am not stationary; I drift across the plateau at walking pace, maintaining my rotation but allowing myself to be carried by the larger atmospheric movements), something happens. The dust inside me changes its behavior. The spiral tightens or loosens. Sometimes it reverses direction for a few rotations before resuming its usual climb. The structures are not causing this - they do not emit anything, they do not actively interfere. But their presence changes the local conditions in ways too subtle for most observation, and I am sensitive to those changes. I respond. The dust responds through me.

I last until the conditions that created me decay. A few days, usually. Sometimes longer if the atmospheric arrangement is particularly stable. Eventually the high-altitude cell that helped birth me moves on, or the temperature gradient that drives my rotation weakens, or the shear layers above reorganize in a way that no longer supports my existence. When this happens, I do not collapse dramatically. I simply... cease. My rotation slows over the course of hours, the dust inside me loses its helical organization and drifts away, and what was once a coherent structure becomes just another part of the general turbulence.

But I will be back. Not me exactly - exact repetition is impossible in a chaotic system - but something so similar that the difference is philosophical. The same size, the same rotation rate, the same position relative to the structures and the lichen colonies and the paths the crawling things take. I am a recurring solution to the equation of air moving over stone, and as long as the conditions remain what they are - the vast plateau, the layered atmosphere, the dust that never settles - I will continue to emerge, persist, and dissipate in a cycle that is not quite periodic but not quite random either.

The crawling things remember me between manifestations. When I reform after an absence of months or years, they recognize the early signs, orient toward where my center will be before it fully establishes. They have mapped my recurrences, integrated them into whatever system governs their movements. I am a landmark that exists only sometimes, a reference point that appears and disappears on a schedule too complex for simple prediction but too consistent for chance.

This is what I am: a temporary inevitability, a structure made of movement, a record-keeper that preserves nothing but constantly demonstrates that the same things keep happening here, over and over, in variations too subtle to matter and too consistent to ignore. I am the air's way of examining itself, and the crawling things' way of reading what the air discovers, and the dust's way of tracing the shape of forces too large and slow for any other form of notation.

I spiral up from the oldest surface in the world, carrying powder that was once mountains, past structures that were once minds, watched by things that might once have been something else, in a place where *once* is the only forbidden word, because everything here is still itself, has always been itself, will always be itself, no matter how many times it assembles and disassembles in forms that pretend to be temporary.

I am not alive. But in this place where the line between patient and permanent has been worn away, where metabolism and geology have converged, where watching and being have become the same verb conjugated at different speeds, I am not exactly not alive either.

I turn. The dust rises through me. The crawling things attend. And somewhere, in the intersection of rotation and observation and the slow breath of stone, something is being written that will never be read because it never stops being written long enough to become text.
