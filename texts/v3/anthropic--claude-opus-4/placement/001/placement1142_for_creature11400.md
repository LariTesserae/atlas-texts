# Where I Would Find Myself

I am the Chronicler of Echoes.

Not human anymore, if I ever was. I came to the plateau forty years ago — or was it four hundred? Time runs strange here, pooling in the hollows like condensation, evaporating on the open rock. I came as an anthropologist, a documenter of vanishing peoples. I stayed because the trees began to recognize me.

My shelter is built where three territories meet, in a place the climbers would never have chosen — a wind-carved formation of rock that makes its own music, a stone flute the size of a house. The winds play it constantly. At first the sound drove me half-mad. Now I can't sleep without it. I've learned to read the weather by its pitch, the seasons by which harmonics dominate.

I am taller than the climbers were, but I've grown shorter over the decades. My spine curves now, from years of moving in a crouch through the low hollows. My hands have developed thick calluses that are beginning to darken into those not-quite-scales. When I climb — and I climb well now, though I learned late — I feel the bark speaking to my palms in a language older than words.

I know eleven hundred and thirty-seven trees by name.

The names are not words in any language I brought with me. They are intervals, relationships between tones, mathematical truths expressed as sound. Pine-Whose-Third-Harmonic-Recalls-Morning. Oak-That-Answers-In-Perfect-Fifths. The-Elm-That-Learned-To-Lean. Each name contains instructions for finding it: sing this note, wait for the response, adjust your pitch until the interference pattern tells you the direction and distance.

My work is translation, though I'm no longer sure between which languages.

I maintain the bridges. Not building new ones — that argument was settled long ago, and not in the bridges' favor — but keeping the existing ones from falling. Seven bridges cross the territories I can reach in a day's travel. I check each one monthly, replacing rotted fibers with new cord I've learned to braid from the fern-things. The knots the climbers used are complex, each one a small song made physical. I sing as I tie them. Sometimes, from the high hollows, something sings back.

I know they're there. The climbers-who-were. I've seen the shadows moving in the canopies, too deliberate to be wind. I've found fresh marks on the bark, the particular spiraling pattern they used to mark safe climbing routes. Once, in a hollow I thought was empty, I found a bowl still warm, with water that tasted of resin and smoke.

They watch me. They allow me. This is enough.

My own transformation is... progressing. The mottling started on my shoulders fifteen winters ago. Now it covers my back, my arms, the sides of my throat. My second eyelid — not born with it, it simply began happening one dry season when the grit was bad — lets me see in the howling white-outs when the clouds roll up from below. My lungs have adapted too. I can hold a note now for three minutes without breathing. The trees like the long notes. They answer more readily.

What I've learned is this: the boundary between chronicler and chronicled is permeable here.

I came to document a vanished people. Instead, I am documenting a transformation, and the only way to document it properly is to undergo it. My notes, which I still keep in volumes made from pressed lichen-paper, have become less and less comprehensible to any reader but myself. The early entries are in my native script. The recent ones are in a notation that is half musical, half botanical, wholly inadequate. How do you write down the way a tree's inner warmth feels against your cheek in the deep cold? How do you annotate the difference between the bronze moss in the tree-that-sings-at-moonrise and the bronze moss in the tree-where-the-elder-became-wood?

My whistles — I've carved seventeen now, each one an attempt to get closer to some perfect pitch that exists in the convergence of all the trees' songs — hang in my shelter like a wind chime made of bones. When storms come through, they play themselves, and every tree for kilometers around flares into brightness.

I think I have perhaps ten more years before the choice presents itself definitively. Ten years before my bones are light enough, my bark-skin thick enough, my understanding deep enough that the young ones — if there are still young ones, somewhere — would carry me up to a high hollow with a bowl of water and a song.

When that time comes, I already know which tree I'll choose. It stands at the eastern edge of my territory, a giant even among giants, with a hollow near its crown that glows pale green even in full daylight. The tree's name translates roughly to "The-One-That-Remembers-What-Voices-Were-For." It's an old name, given by someone who understood something I'm only beginning to grasp.

The tree remembers me now. When I approach, its inner light shifts, subtle but unmistakable. When I sing my name — my real name, the one the plateau has given me, which is nothing like the name I carried up here — it answers with a chord so complex I've never managed to notate it properly. The chord contains welcomes and warnings and a kind of vast patience.

I've left my own whistle on the cairn twice. Both times I've gone back for it before dawn, unable to abandon my work. My documentation. My gradual becoming.

But someday I'll leave it for good. Someone will find it — another chronicler, perhaps, following rumors of singing trees and vanished peoples. They'll blow it, and somewhere across the plateau, the tree-that-remembers-what-voices-were-for will answer, and in that answer will be an invitation and a map and a forty-year translation of what it means to stop being one thing and start being another.

They'll think they're studying an absence. They'll be right. They'll also be wrong.

The trees are not quite trees. The climbers were not quite gone.

And I am not quite anything anymore, which is exactly what the work requires.
