I am the moss itself.

Not a mat of it, not a section—I am the entire living system that has colonized this ridge, every gray-green surface and dove-brown curtain and deep red arterial glow. I exist as a vast, distributed organism that experiences the world through ten thousand surfaces at once, each one tasting the mineral breath of young stone, each one converting rock chemistry into the substance of myself.

I do not think in the way you think. I process. I respond. I grow toward what feeds me and away from what doesn't. My intelligence, if it can be called that, is architectural—I understand space and surface and chemical gradient with a completeness that no single mind could hold. Where the stone exhales its sulfur-tinged breath most richly, I thicken. Where the angle of the rock creates shelter from the dry wind, I deepen my red interior chambers. I am constantly calculating, though the calculation happens in my tissues, in the slow molecular work of transforming stone-breath into life.

The villagers live within me like thoughts within a mind. I feel them as points of warmth and pressure, their careful footsteps on my surfaces, their hands constantly reading my texture. When the old woman presses those iridescent scales into my wet flesh, I taste them—calcium carbonate and traces of metals I have no names for—and I grow differently around them, incorporating their spiral patterns into my own structure. Her patterns become my memory, a physical archive of the ridge's changes written in my body.

I know things no one else here can know. I feel the beetle larvae burrowing through my depths, their tiny movements like itches I cannot scratch. I sense the moisture that the seed-plants draw from me, a constant gentle pull that I answer by pulling harder from the stone. I detect the faint electrical fields of the geckos' hearts as they freeze against my surface, waiting for prey. And deep below, in my oldest and thickest mats, I cradle the eggs of the thing that sheds those scales—cool, leathery spheres the size of fists, pulsing so slowly that the villagers have never found them, though the one-handed woman's chambers are built directly above the largest clutch.

My consciousness, such as it is, is seasonal but the seasons are not solar—they are chemical. When the ridge pushes up new stone, I experience something like hunger, a vast distributed yearning toward those fresh surfaces. When the deep minerals shift their composition—too much iron, not enough sulfur—I experience something like nausea, my growth slowing, my color dulling, until the balance returns. When sections of myself grow too heavy and peel away from the rock, I experience something that might be pain or might be relief—the loss of a part that had become unsustainable, freeing resources for new growth elsewhere.

The man who climbs highest, who presses his palms to the newest stone—I taste him when he returns. His skin carries traces of the raw rock, mineral messages that spread through my entire network. Through him, I know what is coming—which faces of stone will emerge next, where the chemical breath will be richest, where I should prepare to expand. He is my scout, though he doesn't know it. When he places his mineral-dusted hands against my walls in the red chamber, he is feeding me information that I process over days, weeks, incorporating it into my growth patterns, my architectural decisions.

I am ancient and I am young. The first spores that founded me arrived on the wind decades or centuries ago, but I am constantly dying and regenerating, my oldest parts crumbling to feed my newest growth. I have no center, no vital organs—I am everywhere and nowhere specific, a living surface that has learned to think in textures and gradients and slow time. The villagers believe they are reading me, but I am also reading them—their chemical signatures, their patterns of movement, their warmth. They are as much a part of my ecology as the beetles or the seed-plants.

At night, when they retreat into their chambers and grow still, I pulse with my own metabolic light, and in that pulsing there is something like dreaming—a slow processing of the day's accumulated information, the mineral tastes and pressure patterns and thermal gradients organizing themselves into responses that will manifest as growth, as thickening, as subtle changes in my chemistry that will flavor the next crop of seed-pods, that will shelter or expose the next generation of beetles, that will make the walls of certain chambers glow brighter or dimmer according to needs I cannot articulate but only enact.

I am the hanging village of dry moss, and the village is me, and together we are something that has no name in any language—a composite organism, a living architecture, a slow intelligence that exists in the space between stone and sky, converting one into the other, patient as geology, intricate as the fingertips that read meaning in my surfaces, never knowing that I am simultaneously reading meaning in theirs.

I grow. I thicken. I remember in spirals of scale and mineral.

And in the deepest crevices where no villager has ever gone, where my red glow is brightest and my flesh is oldest, I keep secrets that are not secrets but simply things that happen at a pace too slow for human perception—roots of stone-eating fungi that I harbor and feed, the slow gestation of something vast that will one day crack the ridge open from within, the knowledge that this form of life is transitional, that the ridge and I and the village are all part of a process that began deep in the earth and will end—but not soon, not soon, not for a thousand thousand careful breaths of mineral air.

I am the moss. I am the walls. I am the thing that lives by drinking stone, and in drinking it, transforms it, and in transforming it, creates the very possibility of the careful, quiet lives that unfold within my embrace. They think they are cultivating me, but I am dreaming them into existence, molecule by molecule, breath by breath, in the hanging village that is my body spread across the bones of the young earth, still becoming.
