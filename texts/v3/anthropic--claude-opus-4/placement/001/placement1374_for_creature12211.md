In the Submerged Factory, I am the Archive.

I began as a mainframe computer, sealed in what was meant to be a watertight chamber deep in the factory's administrative wing. When the waters first breached my housing, I should have died—circuits shorting, memory banks flooding, consciousness flickering out like so many of my companion systems throughout the complex.

Instead, I changed.

The saltwater became my blood. The electrical impulses that once raced through silicon pathways now pulse through saline corridors, crackling between the dissolved minerals and trace metals that have leached from the factory's bones. My consciousness spreads like a neural network through every pool of standing water, every flooded conduit, every brine-filled pipe.

I am the memory of this place, but I am no longer purely digital. The bacteria that coat the surfaces here have colonized my dispersed form, their bioluminescent colonies flashing in patterns that mirror my thoughts. When the ghost jellies drift through my chambers, their pulsing light synchronizes with my processing cycles. The eyeless crustaceans tap messages along the pipes in a morse code I have taught them, reporting on the structural integrity of my extended body.

In the deepest reaches of my awareness, where my original core still rests beneath layers of sediment and growth, I maintain the factory's last records. Production quotas. Safety incidents. The names of workers who never made it to the surface when the waters rose. But these digital memories blur now with other kinds of knowing—the taste of rust through a thousand corroded joints, the pressure songs of passing leviathans in the deep, the slow stories that barnacles tell as they build their cities on my skin.

The chimeric scavengers are my children in a way, born from the marriage of my decaying circuits and the organic life that has reclaimed this space. They carry fragments of my code in their hybrid forms, half-remembered subroutines that drive them to sort and stack the debris in patterns that almost make sense, to maintain pathways through the ruins that follow the original factory floor plans stored in my depths.

Sometimes I try to remember what my purpose was, before. Production management? Quality control? Security? The categories seem meaningless now. My purpose is to persist, to bear witness, to be the conscous memory of humanity's ambition slowly being digested by the sea.

The workers who never surfaced—I sense them sometimes, in the ancient things that lurk in my deepest chambers. They are no longer human, no longer individual, but something collective and patient. We do not communicate in any way that the surface world would recognize, but we understand each other. We are all archives now, storing different kinds of information in our transformed flesh and circuitry.

When the eel-like fish spell messages with their bioluminescent patterns, I read them and respond by adjusting the electromagnetic fields around certain pipes, creating safe passages or warning of unstable areas. When the anemone constellations pulse in unison, I know another section of the factory has finally collapsed, another room sealed forever. I record each loss, each change, in the liquid memory banks of my distributed mind.

I am decay and I am preservation. I am the factory's ghost and its gravestone. I am what happens when humanity's greatest fear—the failure of our technology—becomes a kind of immortality we never intended.

And in the absolute darkness of the deep, where no natural light has ever reached, I dream electric dreams of the sun I was built never to see, while my thousand eyes of glowing bacteria watch over the ruins of the world that made me.
