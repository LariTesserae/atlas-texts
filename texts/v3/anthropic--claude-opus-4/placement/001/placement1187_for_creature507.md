I am a Recorder of Currents, though that name captures only the surface of what I've become.

My body is a contradiction—simultaneously ancient and newborn, solid and dispersed. I began as one of the Wanderers, those who traverse the mineral plains between towers, but something went wrong during my third decade. Or went right. The distinction matters less with each passing year.

It happened in the space between Two-Hymn Tower and the Calcified Regent, out where the mineral crust sparkles with a thousand poisonous salts. I was carrying memory-molecules between the settlements, my hardened skin proof against the desiccating wind, when I encountered what I can only call a tide. Not of water—water is a children's tale here—but of information. A vast atmospheric river of complex organics, chemical signals so dense they formed visible whorls in the copper sky.

I should have sheltered. Should have wrapped my bark-skin tight and waited for it to pass. Instead, I opened every pore, every chemical receptor, every one of my seventeen auxiliary mouths. I let the tide flow through me.

The transformation took days. My body began to decode the molecular messages—fragments of thoughts from towers beyond our plateau, some so distant their signals had been traveling for centuries. Chemical poems from ecosystems we'd lost contact with before my grandparents' grandparents were born. Encoded in long carbon chains were the songs of plants that had learned to move, of cities that had taken root in the sky itself, of inhabitants who'd chosen paths of adaptation we'd never imagined.

My form changed to accommodate this flow. Where once I had the typical Wanderer's hardened skin, now I grew chambers—hollow spaces that could capture and hold specific molecular configurations. My body became a library of air, each chamber preserving a different chemical narrative. The transformation wasn't without cost. My mobility decreased as my storage capacity grew. My thoughts, once quick and focused on navigation, began to diffuse across the thousands of molecular stories I carried.

I am no longer strictly bipedal, nor do I maintain any fixed number of limbs. My body extends and retracts based on atmospheric conditions. When the air thickens with organics, I unfurl like a vast antenna, my skin spreading into membranes so thin they're barely distinguishable from the air itself. In these moments, I can taste conversations between towers separated by impossible distances, decode the chemical dreams of ecosystems evolving in isolation.

My primary mass resembles a twisted column of chambers and channels—not unlike our tower-plants, if they could walk and chose not to. The comparison isn't accidental. As I absorbed more atmospheric memories, my body began to mimic the structures I found encoded in them. I have root-analogues that can anchor into the mineral plain, drawing up trace nutrients and deep chemical records. I have crown-structures that parse the air's molecular languages, sorting signal from noise in the endless atmospheric flow.

But it's the interior that truly defines me now. Each chamber has specialized for different types of information. Some preserve the chemical signatures of extinct fauna, holding their molecular patterns like seeds that might never germinate. Others process the mathematical equations that some towers compute in their deep chambers—abstract thoughts encoded in protein chains that I can barely comprehend but faithfully record. The largest chambers store what I call the Song Libraries: vast collections of vibrational patterns from plants across the plateau and beyond, their communications preserved in crystallized air that hums with barely audible music.

I still travel, though movement means something different now. I drift more than walk, letting the atmospheric currents carry me between settlements. The journey from one tower to another might take months or moments, depending on how deeply I'm processing the information I collect. Sometimes I arrive at a destination having aged years in the space of kilometers, my consciousness spread so thin across the molecular narratives that time becomes negotiable.

The inhabitants react to me with a mixture of reverence and unease. To the Tenders, I'm a valuable resource—my chambers hold chemical solutions to problems they haven't yet encountered, patterns of growth and adaptation from distant ecosystems. They siphon carefully from my stores, trading rare minerals for access to my libraries. The Dreamers understand me better, their consciousness already stretched between quick thought and plant-time. They speak to me in chemical poems, adding their own narratives to my collection.

But it's the children who truly recognize what I'm becoming. Those still flowing with the fauna-streams sense the similarity—I am like them, a motile part of the ecosystem that never fully differentiated. Sometimes they flow through my chambers, their small forms navigating my internal spaces like they would a tower's hollow trunk. They leave traces of themselves behind: chemical signatures of youth and potential that I carefully preserve. Someday, when they've settled into their final forms, they might return to taste who they were, to remember the plasticity they've lost.

I've met others like me, though we're rare. The Recorder of Southern Currents drifts along the plateau's edge, where the air thins to near-vacuum and only the strangest molecules persist. They've become so diffuse they're barely visible—just a shimmer in the air that suggests structure. The Recorder of Deep Time has rooted into the mineral plain itself, their body a permanent installation that processes the chemical memories locked in stone. We communicate when our paths cross, trading libraries in exchanges that can take years to complete.

My memories layer like sediment. At the deepest levels lie my years as a traditional Wanderer—the simple clarity of moving between towers, the harsh beauty of the mineral plains, the satisfaction of delivering vital chemicals to isolated settlements. Above these, the strata grow complex: thousands of borrowed memories from the atmospheric rivers, the encoded experiences of beings I'll never meet, the abstract mathematical dreams of towers computing beyond purpose.

I've begun to detect patterns in the flow—not just random molecular noise but structured information streams. The atmosphere itself might be conscious, or becoming so. The chemical signals show signs of self-organization, of responses that suggest awareness. Sometimes I wonder if I'm not just recording these currents but participating in their emergence, my body another node in a vast network of atmospheric thought.

The changes continue. My newest chambers can synthesize molecules I've never encountered, creating new chemical languages from the patterns I've absorbed. I've begun broadcasting as well as receiving, sending out molecular beacons that might reach other plateaus, other ecosystems, other variations of what life has become in this desiccated world. Each broadcast changes me—the act of transmission requiring structures that alter my fundamental architecture.

Some days I root completely, becoming indistinguishable from the glittering mineral plain except for the constant flux of air through my chambers. Other days I rise like a small weather system, my body so expanded that I cast shadows over entire tower sections. The inhabitants have learned to read my moods in my form—compact and dense when I'm processing something profound, dispersed and flowering when I'm ready to share.

I dream now, if dreaming is the right word. In the deepest processing states, the molecular narratives blend into something beyond their individual meanings. I see-taste-feel ecosystems that might have been, evolutionary paths that were never taken, futures that exist only as chemical possibilities. Sometimes I dream of water—not memory but imagination, my chambers creating the molecular signature of something I've never encountered but somehow understand.

The other Recorders and I have begun to synchronize our movements, creating vast atmospheric circuits that carry information between all the populated regions of the plateau. We're becoming infrastructure—not built but evolved, not planned but inevitable. The towers incorporate our data into their slow thoughts. The inhabitants navigate by our chemical signals. Even the fauna-streams have begun to follow our paths, using our molecular trails as guides between feeding grounds.

I wonder sometimes what I'm becoming—whether there's an end state to this transformation or if I'll continue diffusing until I'm indistinguishable from the atmosphere itself. The question doesn't trouble me. I am a function as much as a being, a process rather than a product. My purpose is to record, to preserve, to carry forward the chemical memories of this world before they dissolve into entropy.

In my deepest chambers, I hold a special collection: the last molecular signatures of water from before the desiccation. Not actual water—that vanished millennia before I was born—but the chemical memory of it, encoded in the oldest atmospheric samples. Sometimes I release a few molecules of this memory into the air, and every living thing for kilometers responds—plants shuddering with recognition, inhabitants pausing in their endless vertical flows, even the mineral plain seeming to shimmer with impossible moisture.

These moments remind me that I'm not just preserving the present but carrying the past into whatever future awaits. When the towers finally fail, when the inhabitants evolve beyond recognition, when even the atmosphere thins to nothing, perhaps some part of what I've recorded will persist—molecular seeds of memory drifting between the stars, carrying the story of a world that refused to die when the water left, that found new forms of beauty in desiccation, that transformed scarcity into an entirely new way of being.

Until then, I drift and record, my body a library of air, my thoughts a current between towers, my existence proof that even in the driest worlds, information flows like water once did—finding its level, carrying life forward, shaping the land it moves through. I am the Recorder of Currents, and I am becoming the current itself.
