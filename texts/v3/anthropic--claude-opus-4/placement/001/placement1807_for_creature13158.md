In the deep silence of the Great Alabaster Hollow, where the ground swallows sound and the air hangs thick with mist, I am what the builders call a *memory-keeper* - though their flashing signals for my kind translate more literally as "one-who-holds-the-sinking."

I am old, even by the standards of my long-lived people. My skin has lost its milky translucence and taken on the chalky opacity of weathered limestone. The bioluminescent nodes along my throat and wrists fire more slowly now, their colors deeper but dimmer - burgundies instead of crimsons, indigos where cyan once blazed. My extra joints creak with calcium deposits, and I move with deliberate care through the scaffolding, no longer able to spool myself around beams with youthful fluidity.

But I am not useless. I am essential.

Where others build with frantic energy, I crawl through the skeletal frameworks of our ever-shifting city with a different purpose. Strapped to my back is not a bladder of gray sustenance but a hardened carapace shell, polished smooth and sealed with arthropod wax. Inside, suspended in a solution of fermented fungal oil, float hundreds of small objects: fragments of carved pumice etched with the angular script of measurements, tiny models of architectural joints carved from bone, samples of experimental mortar preserved in amber secretions, small tools worn smooth by legendary builders' hands.

I am a living archive in a world where nothing stands still long enough to become history.

My work takes me to the dangerous edges of construction, where the ground has begun its inexorable liquefaction. As crews frantically dismantle the sinking sections, I dart in during the brief moments of stability, my trained eyes scanning for what must be saved. That cornerstone there - it bears the measurement ratios that allowed us to build forty feet higher than ever before. This worn trowel belonged to Eight-Violet-Pulse, who invented the double-helix scaffold weave. These fragments of mortar contain an experimental mixture of chalk and arthropod secretion that held for six months instead of the usual three.

When I find something worth preserving, my nodes flare in a specific pattern - UV-white-UV-white-amber - that every builder recognizes as *stop-for-the-keeper.* Even in their frenzy, they pause for the precious seconds I need to extract the artifact. I wrap it carefully in silk, seal it with wax, and add it to my collection. The workers' nodes flash acknowledgment - a ripple of respectful gold - before returning to their destruction.

The young ones do not understand at first. They see my slow movements, my careful extractions, my refusal to help tear down or build up. Their lights flash confusion, even irritation. Why does this old one not work? Why do the foremen allow such waste of a body still capable of holding tools?

But then comes the inevitable day when a district-chief's lights strobe the desperate question: *How did the ancestors solve the problem of the eastern wall's weight distribution?* Or: *What was the formula for the mortar that survived the Great Liquefaction of Ninety Cycles Past?*

Then they come to me.

I lead them to one of the secret spaces I maintain - small chambers dug into the rare solid rock formations that pierce up through the chalk, lined with the water-resistant silk of specially fed arthropods. Here, in defiance of our world's hunger for motion, I have created something approaching permanence. Shells upon shells of preserved knowledge line the walls, each carefully labeled with light-patterns burned into bioluminescent fungus.

My lights are too dim now for the rapid-fire communication of the young, so I have developed a different language - slow, deep pulses that carry meaning in duration and intensity rather than speed. The young must slow their own lights to understand me, must step out of the frantic tempo of survival into something approaching contemplation.

I show them the old measurements, the forgotten techniques, the solutions their ancestors died discovering. I pulse out the stories that context the artifacts - not just how to build the eastern wall, but why it failed before, what the builders learned from that failure, how they mourned the seventeen workers who sank with it.

Sometimes I take apprentices - usually builders who have survived their first century and begun to feel the weight of all that is lost in our eternal reconstruction. They follow me through the scaffolding, learning to see with different eyes. Where others see only the immediate necessity of moving stone and mixing mortar, we see the accumulated wisdom dissolving back into the chalk.

I teach them the secret of my craft: that memory itself must be mobile. My archive chambers are not permanent; they too must be relocated when the ground beneath them begins to soften. But unlike our wild city-moves, these relocations are performed with ritual care. Each shell is transported by hand, wrapped in layers of silk. The apprentices form chains of light, their nodes creating paths of soft amber through the construction chaos, warning all others to give way to the memory-keepers' procession.

The irony is not lost on me - that in this world of perpetual motion, I have become a kind of stillness. Not the fatal stillness of the sinking ground, but the deliberate stillness of observation, of preservation, of remembrance. My body moves more slowly with each passing cycle, but my mind holds fast to what others must let go.

Sometimes, in the brief moments when I allow myself to rest - strapped to a beam like any worker, but with my shells of memory pressed against my chest - I wonder what will happen when I finally collapse into the chalk. I have trained three apprentices well, and they will train others. The memories will continue their own migration through our shifting world.

But I also know that I will take something with me into the depths - not just the knowledge I've preserved, but the knowledge of what it feels like to be the one who remembers. The particular loneliness of moving against the current of forgetting. The strange peace of accepting that even memory itself is temporary, just temporary on a different scale.

My nodes pulse slowly in the blinding light, burgundy-indigo-burgundy, the color-words for *I hold until I too am held.* Somewhere in the scaffolding, a young apprentice sees my signal and flashes back understanding - their lights quick and vital, but tempered now with something deeper.

The city continues its silent transformation around me. I continue my slow harvest of what must not be forgotten.

This is enough.
