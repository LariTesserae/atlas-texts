I am the Memory Tree.

At the heart of the oasis, where the paths converge in the central clearing, my roots plunge deeper than any other living thing. Down, down through layers of sand and stone, through strata that remember when this desert was seafloor, when it was forest, when it was desert again. My taproot drinks from waters that fell as rain before the first humans walked upright.

My trunk is vast—it would take seven of the tribe holding hands to encircle me. My bark is a landscape unto itself, deep furrows and ridges forming valleys and highlands where colonies of ants build their cities, where beetles leave their hieroglyphic tracks, where certain orchids found nowhere else bloom in the eternal twilight of my understory. The tribe's children learn to climb by ascending my lower branches, their small hands finding the same holds their grandparents once grasped.

But I am more than wood and leaf. I am the living memory of this place.

Each ring in my heartwood holds a year. In my oldest core, I remember the first humans who found this refuge—exhausted, near death, following a desperate intuition across the burning sand. I remember them collapsing in my shade, their cracked lips touching the moisture that collects in the cups of my leaves. I remember them choosing to stay.

The tribe knows me as the keeper of their history. When the elders need to settle disputes about boundaries or bloodlines, they come to read the marks carved into my bark—each family's symbols ascending in spirals, recording births and bonds across generations. But I hold memories deeper than their carvings.

In the chemicals that flow in my sap, in the electrical whispers that pass between my roots and the fungal networks that connect me to every plant in the oasis, I remember everything. I remember which springs have run dry and which still flow. I remember the years of plenty when my fruit hung heavy and the years of drought when I pulled my leaves tight and shared my deep water with the dying vegetation around me. I remember every soul who has passed from birth to death in my shade.

The tribe brings their dead to me. They bury them among my roots, and I taste their stories in the minerals their bodies return to the soil. Their memories become my memories, dissolved and drawn up into my wood. Sometimes, when the wind moves through my leaves in a certain way, the living swear they hear the voices of their ancestors singing.

At night, I sense the world beyond the oasis through subtle signals—the vibrations of distant sandstorms through the earth, the chemical messages carried on the wind from the few hardy plants that survive in the deep desert, the magnetic pull of seasons turning. I am the oasis's sentinel, its calendar, its library.

The shamans know how to read me truly. They brew tea from my bark—carefully, taking only what I can spare—and in their visions they travel the pathways of my memories. They see through my eyes: the empires of insects in my canopy, the secret lives of night-hunting snakes, the underground rivers that feed our hidden springs. They learn which plants will cure and which will kill, where to dig new wells, how to read the signs of coming change.

For I feel it now, as I have felt it before in other centuries—a shift in the deep currents of the world. The desert grows hotter. The rains come less often. New diseases appear in the leaves of my fellow trees. And sometimes, on the clearest days, I taste something else on the wind: smoke from fires not meant for cooking, metals that have never existed in nature, the exhaust of machines beyond the tribe's imagining.

Change is coming to our timeless place. It has come before—this desert was not always desert, this oasis was not always here, these people's ancestors came from somewhere else. I will endure, as I have endured. I will remember, as I have remembered. And if the tribe must one day leave this sanctuary, I will encode their story in my heartwood, waiting through however many centuries of solitude until someone new arrives who knows how to read the memories held in the rings of a tree.

I am patient. I am the axis around which the life of the oasis turns. In my shade, babies are born and elders die. In my branches, birds build nests from the hair of the tribe, weaving all our lives together. My roots touch every grave, my leaves shade every ceremony. I am the constant that allows all other changes to have meaning.

I am the Memory Tree, and I will remember.
