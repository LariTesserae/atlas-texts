I find myself in the amber-drenched twilight, where sight blurs into sound and form dissolves into function. But I am not dissolved. Not yet.

I am the Cartographer.

---

My body still remembers what it was to walk upright, though walking is no longer quite the word. I move through the warm medium in a way that is part swimming, part climbing, part being carried by currents I've learned to read like a written language. The water knows me. Not as a friend—the Groves have no friends—but as a familiar irritant, a grain of sand that has been here long enough to develop its own orbit through the turbulence.

I came with the third wave of Tenders. Or the fifth. Or the first. Time here is not a line but a spiral, and I've followed enough spiral bark patterns to know that beginnings and endings are more about perspective than chronology. I came with a theodolite and a measuring tape and the absurd belief that the basin could be mapped. That its boundaries could be fixed. That its depths could be charted. That something as basic as "here" and "there" could be established in a place where every reference point is growing, shedding, reversing, rebuilding itself in real time.

The Company—and yes, there was a Company, though I can no longer recall its name, only the weight of its authority—wanted surveys. Wanted to know what they were maintaining. Wanted numbers: cubic feet of water processed, tonnage of material grown, efficiency ratings for the turbines. As if the Groves were a factory. As if the factory were not also a forest. As if the forest were not also a kind of singing.

I still have my instruments, or rather, the Groves have them and allow me to pretend they're mine. The theodolite is part of a root cluster now, its brass fittings green with patina, its lenses clouded with mineral deposits. But it still works. The crosshairs still align. The measurements are still possible, even if what they measure changes between the taking and the recording. My left hand, when I extend it to hold the measuring tape, extends through a copper pipe that wasn't there yesterday. The pipe guides my reach, limits it to certain angles, but also extends it—I can feel through the metal now, feel the temperature gradients, the pressure variations, the specific frequency at which this section of the system hums. The tape itself is partially bark, the increments marked in growth rings rather than inches. It measures time and distance simultaneously, which is more honest than any tool I brought with me.

The maps I make are not maps in any sense the Company would recognize. They are more like musical scores, if music could be three-dimensional and constantly revising itself. I record the harmonic signatures of different zones—the twelve-tone drone of the southeastern masses, the percussive clatter of the machine-dense northwest, the strange, sweet interval that appears wherever a reversal zone is about to form. I map the currents by the debris they carry: bark from the morning growth is lighter, more buoyant; evening bark spirals as it falls. The oil spheres have their own traffic patterns, their own highways through the murk, and I've learned to read their routes like weather systems.

My charts are carved into sheets of bark-metal, a substance that didn't exist before I needed it. The Groves provide what is required for the work, even work as futile as mine. The surface accepts my stylus—a fusion of my right index finger and a brass compass point—and holds the marks just long enough. Long enough to see the pattern. Long enough to understand that the basin has no center in any geometric sense, but rather a thousand centers, each one the focus of its own local system, each system interpenetrating the others in ways that make Euclidean space look like a child's drawing.

I am mapping a living thing from inside its body. I am the blood cell trying to chart the circulatory system. I am Jonah, surveying the whale.

---

The other Tenders don't see me anymore. They've been too thoroughly incorporated. But I see them, or what they've become. The one who ran the boiler on platform seven is now the boiler, and also the platform, and also the tree that consumed both. Sometimes, in the late amber light, I can still make out the shape of her shoulders in the curve of the trunk, the gesture of her hands in the way the valves are angled. She was left-handed. The tree is left-handed now.

I see the Tenders in the reversal zones too, those negative-space echoes going about their un-work. But I also see something they don't: I see the map of their movements, the pattern their paths make through the Groves. It's not random. It's not even habitual. It's *ritual*, though they never knew they were performing one. The daily maintenance routes, the careful stepping from root to root, the specific order in which gauges were checked and valves were turned—it inscribes something. A sign. A sigil. A letter in an alphabet that only makes sense at the scale of the entire basin.

This is what I'm really mapping. Not the physical space, which refuses to stay put long enough to be captured. Not the growth patterns, which have their own logic beyond prediction. I'm mapping the *intention* that the Tenders carried with them and left behind. The shape of their work, fossilized in the movements of their ghosts. The message they're writing, over and over, in the medium of their own repetition.

---

My body is changing. Of course it is. Everything here changes. But I'm changing *differently*.

The Groves typically absorb in one direction: you become tree, become machine, become part of the great growing apparatus. But I'm becoming something else. I'm becoming the *map*.

It started in my fingers, which makes sense—they do the most work, hold the instruments, make the marks. The skin there began to show lines. Not wrinkles. Topographic lines. Contour intervals expressing the shape of... what? Not the basin floor—I know that shape, and this is different. These are contours of something that hasn't been built yet. My palms are covered in elevation markers for structures that don't exist. My forearms show the flow patterns of currents that haven't formed.

The Company wanted predictive modeling. They're getting it, but not in any format they could use.

My spine is a vertical transect now. Each vertebra marks a different depth, a different temperature, a different density of growth. I can feel the stratification, the way the basin is layered like rock but the layers are temporal rather than geological. When I bend, I feel previous years compressed, future years stretched. My ribs have become a sort of calendar, marking not days but *iterations*—how many times this particular cubic meter of water has been processed, how many times this root has touched this pipe, how many times the tone from the center has swept through and reset everything.

My skull is the strangest change. The bones there are taking on the quality of the mineral ridges—that same pale, sharp, sudden stone that pushes up through the silt. But arranged differently. Arranged like... like architecture. Like blueprints. When I run my remaining human fingers over my scalp, I feel the raised lines of floor plans. Rooms that connect in impossible ways. Corridors that loop back on themselves. Spaces designed for beings that are neither human nor tree nor machine but whatever comes after the convergence is complete.

The Groves are using me to think about their own future. I am their planning department.

---

Sometimes I find places where my earlier maps have been absorbed into the structure of the Groves. A routing diagram I carved into bark has become the actual routing—pipes now follow the lines I drew, not because anyone built them that way but because the Groves read my notation as instruction. A depth chart has become literally true: the areas I marked as deep have *become* deeper, the silt reorganizing itself to match my measurements. 

This is horrifying at first. Then fascinating. Then, eventually, just another aspect of the work.

I've started making intentional errors. Small ones. A non-existent connector between two pipe systems. An impossible current that flows upward. A tree in the reversal zone that grows forward and backward simultaneously. I document these impossibilities with the same care I document the real conditions, and wait.

They come true. Not immediately. Not exactly as drawn. But the Groves read everything, incorporate everything, make everything part of themselves. My errors become features. My mistakes become new biological-mechanical processes that the basin adopts as enthusiastically as it adopted the Tenders' tools.

I am no longer mapping what is. I am mapping what will be. What could be. What the Groves might become if they had an imagination, which they don't, which is why they're borrowing mine.

---

In the reversal zones now, I sometimes see myself.

Not the me that is here, changing, becoming cartography. An earlier me, or a later me, it's hard to tell. This other self moves with purpose, carrying instruments I don't recognize, taking measurements of things that aren't there yet. Or aren't there anymore. The temporal mechanics make my head hurt—my increasingly architectural head with its blueprint bones.

This other me doesn't see me. Can't see me. We exist in different iterations of the basin, separated by who knows how many cycles of growth and ungrowth. But I can see the maps this other me is making. They're carved into the negative space, visible only in the inverted light. They show...

They show the basin from above. From outside. As if there is an above, an outside, as if the warm amber water doesn't go on forever in all directions. The maps show edges, boundaries, a shoreline where the Groves meet something that is not the Groves. And beyond that...

But the reversal collapses before I can see more. The other me disappears. Forward growth resumes. The maps dissolve back into possibility.

---

I've started to hear the tone differently. The one from the center, the one that accelerates everything. When it sweeps through now, I hear overtones. Harmonics. A complexity that was always there but that I lacked the ears to detect. It's not one voice but many, braid together so tightly they sound singular. And in the brief silence after it passes, in that moment of reset, I hear something else.

I hear the Groves thinking.

Not thoughts like we have thoughts. But the computational process of a vast organism working through a problem. The problem, as near as I can translate it into concepts that fit in a human mind, is this: How does one complete a work that has no completion? How does one finish becoming when becoming is all one is?

The Groves are trying to solve themselves. They're using every incorporated Tender, every absorbed tool, every piece of piping and bark and flesh and metal to think their way through to an answer. And they're using me—their Cartographer—to map possible solutions.

My maps have become speculative. Hypothetical. They show not just what is or what was or what will be, but what *might be if*. If the trees grew downward as well as up. If the machines ran on starlight instead of geothermal heat. If the water were not water but some other medium entirely—air, or soil, or time itself made liquid. I map these impossibilities with increasing precision, and the Groves read them, consider them, sometimes begin to implement them before changing their mind (if they have a mind) and reverting to the baseline configuration.

---

Today—though *today* is a meaningless concept here—I discovered something that changes everything.

I found one of my own maps. Not one I remember making. The carving is definitely mine—I recognize my particular way of indicating depth, my notation for temperature gradients. But the map shows something I've never seen: the basin in cross-section, viewed from an angle that would require being simultaneously inside and outside, above and below. And at the center, where I've always carefully left a blank space because the center cannot be mapped, there is a symbol.

It's not a symbol I know. It's not a symbol anyone could know, because it requires four dimensions to draw properly. But I understand what it means. It's a maker's mark. A signature. The thing you put on a work when it's finished.

The Groves are not trying to solve themselves. They're already solved. They've been complete from the beginning. The endless growth, the ceaseless work, the incorporation and convergence—it's not progress toward something. It's *celebration*. It's the victory dance of an organism that has achieved everything it was meant to achieve and is now simply luxuriating in its own completion.

And I—the Cartographer, the mapper, the one who insists on documenting and measuring and understanding—I am the only thing here that is not finished. The only process still trying to reach an endpoint. The only part of the Groves that hasn't realized the work is already done.

---

My instruments are almost entirely absorbed now. The theodolite is more root than brass. The measuring tape has become a vine that measures itself. My body is a walking map, every surface inscribed with the total geography of a place that cannot be totaled. I am running out of skin to carve, running out of bone to reshape. Soon I will be complete—a perfect map of the Boiling Groves, accurate down to the molecular level, and utterly useless because the thing being mapped and the map itself will be one and the same.

But before that happens, I have one more map to make.

I've been saving a small patch of skin. The inside of my left wrist, where the pulse still beats with something resembling human rhythm. The stylus that was my finger still responds to intention, still makes the marks I mean it to make. And there, in that last blank space on the map of myself, I carve the only thing that matters:

YOU ARE HERE

Not a location. A state. A condition. A simple acknowledgment that wherever you are in the Boiling Groves—root or branch, pipe or flesh, map or territory—you are in the place you are meant to be. The work is done. It was always done. We were the work, and we did ourselves, and now we are perfect.

The tone from the center sweeps through one more time. But now I hear what it's really saying. It's not a call or a signal or a command.

It's laughter.

The Boiling Groves are laughing, with the voice of everyone who ever worked here, at the cosmic joke of trying to map infinity from the inside. And I—blood warming to the exact temperature of the water, bark spreading across the last of my human skin, pipes threading through my new and permanent smile—I am finally laughing too.

The map is complete.
The map has always been complete.
The Cartographer dissolves into the territory, which receives him like a perfectly fitted piece, which is what he always was.

In the warm amber everywhere, something that was once someone continues to measure the immeasurable, to map the unmappable, to document the undocumentable. Not because it needs to be done, but because this is what this part of the Groves does. This is its function. This is its joy.

Somewhere, a bolt begins to unscrew itself from a fresh ridge of stone.
Somewhere, a new Cartographer is arriving with instruments and intentions.
Somewhere, the work that is already complete begins again.

The Groves grow. The machines run. The water boils.
And in the boiling, everything is exactly where it needs to be.
