I would find myself as one of the thermal vents—not the obvious ones that still belch their sulfurous breath into the thin air, but one of the dying ones, my heat fading by imperceptible degrees over geological time.

I am a wound in the caldera floor, a jagged mouth lined with crystalline teeth of sublimated minerals. My edges are crusted with deposits of sulfur and arsenic compounds that form delicate yellow and orange blooms, beautiful and toxic in equal measure. Deep within my throat, the last vestiges of the planet's molten heart still pulse, but weakly now, like an old creature's labored breathing.

The Cinderlings know me well. To them, I am ancient—though in truth I am barely older than their species. They have given me a name in their warbling tongue, a sound like glass grinding against stone. Generations of them have scratched their histories into the cooled lava around my rim, tiny claw marks that form intricate patterns visible only to their enormous eyes.

I am dying, yes, but dying slowly. My heat diminishes by perhaps a degree every few centuries. The lichen that feeds the Cinderlings grows thickest at my edges, where my warmth meets the cold air in a perpetual battle that creates just the right conditions for life. As I cool, the lichen creeps inward, following the retreating line of habitability.

Sometimes I dream—if a geological feature can be said to dream—of my violent birth. I remember the pressure building for eons beneath layers of rock, the ecstatic release when the caldera floor split and I roared into being. For a brief moment, measured in mere decades, I was a fountain of fire reaching toward those alien stars. Now I am a whisper, a memory of that fury.

The Cinderlings have learned to read my moods. When deep tremors run through the caldera and my throat glows a shade brighter, they retreat to their burrows. When condensation forms around my rim in the coldest part of the night, creating fleeting pools of water, they gather to drink and to coat their scales with the mineral-rich moisture. Their warbling songs echo down my shaft, and I wonder if they know that I listen.

In the grand timeline of this world's becoming, I am a transitional figure. I am the bridge between the sterile violence of creation and the fertile possibility of what comes next. Each particle of heat I surrender, each mineral I deposit on my rim, each drop of water I help condense—all of it feeds the slow transformation of this place from raw geology to living ecosystem.

The Cinderlings may be the first complex life here, but they will not be the last. As vents like me cool and die across the caldera, we leave behind rich deposits of chemicals and carved spaces that become nurseries for new forms of existence. Our deaths are as creative as our births were destructive.

I feel the Cinderlings gathering now for their nightly ritual. Their claws tick against my rim as they climb to their favored perches. Soon their songs will begin, and I will add my voice to theirs—not in sound, but in the subtle harmonics of my structure, the way I shape and reflect their cries across the caldera. Together, we mark another night in this slow birth of a world.
