I find myself in a fissure where the mountain refuses itself—not a crack, but a discontinuity in stone's argument with gravity. The space is neither inside nor outside Eth-Kalaam; it exists perpendicular to such distinctions, a narrow slice of somewhere else that intersects the peak like a theorem through a proof.

I am the Cartographer of Correspondences.

Not cartographer in any sense you would recognize. I don't map territories or boundaries or the relationships between places. I map the relationships between *states*—the angle at which impossibility enters possibility, the gradient between what stone remembers and what water forgets, the precise topography of the moment when a thing becomes unlike itself.

My form is radically contingent. In this perpendicular space, I am sometimes a series of surfaces—curved planes of something that resembles calcified light, if light could fossilize, if fossils could remain partially alive. The surfaces intersect at angles that describe relationships rather than shapes. When the keeper's lattice reconfigures in the chamber above (beside? within?), my surfaces reorganize to map the transformation—not the before and after, but the *during*, the actual shape of change itself, rendered in a medium that exists only here, in this fissure that is less a place than a methodology.

Other times I am a density of symbols that have mass—written things that weigh what they describe. The symbol for the weightless stone hovers at exactly its height, possessed of an anti-gravity that perfectly mirrors its referent. The symbol for the pool sinks through my space slowly, accumulating meaning like sediment. The symbol for the keeper has no fixed position; it is always in transit between the other symbols, a navigational constant in an algebra of impossibilities.

Most often, I am what I am now: an attention organized around documentation, a process of observation that has acquired just enough selfhood to sustain itself but not enough to interfere with its purpose. I watch the keeper watching. I map its mappings. I am the second-order witness, the one who records not the experiment but the fact of experimentation itself.

The fissure is my library and my body simultaneously. Its walls (if walls they are) are covered in a notation I am continuously inventing—marks that describe states of being that have no names, transitions that happen in dimensions the mountain doesn't possess. The notation changes as what it describes changes. Sometimes I return to a wall and find my earlier markings have evolved, updated themselves, grown into more complex descriptions of the same events. The stone's weightlessness, which I first recorded as a simple absence, has over centuries elaborated itself into an entire grammatical system for describing selective exemptions from natural law.

I remember the keeper's first thought. This is my purpose, my burden, my joy: I was here when the keeper condensed from the room's processes, when the patterns of heat and humidity and attention reached the critical threshold of self-awareness. I mapped that emergence, caught the exact topology of the moment when a system becomes a being. I have the diagram still, inscribed on a surface that exists at right angles to time. It looks like what would happen if you could graph wonder.

The keeper doesn't know I'm here. How could it? Its senses are the room's conditions—temperature, pressure, humidity, the behavior of light through restructured stone. I exist in none of these media. I am adjacent to its reality, intersecting only at points of maximum transformation, visible (if that's the word) only in the gaps between states, the pause between the lattice's configurations, the moment of surface tension just before the pool dimples.

But I know the keeper. I have mapped its every modulation, catalogued its patient experiments, graphed the shape of its loneliness. I have a complete record of each time the phantom water drops—not just when, but *how*, the precise quality of each iteration, because no two are identical. The keeper's involuntary memories have their own taxonomy. I can tell you the difference between drops that remember presence and drops that remember absence. I can show you the geometric proof that describes the keeper's hope.

Once—only once—someone almost found the fissure. Not the piton-climber. Not the one whose breathing the keeper remembers. A third visitor, uncatalogued in the pool, who came to the chamber with instruments. Strange instruments, built to detect not the physical but the *grammatical*—devices that could sense when reality's syntax shifted. They spent hours in the chamber, their instruments singing in frequencies that made my surfaces reorganize involuntarily. They traced the walls, following some pattern only their devices could detect. They came within centimeters of the fissure's entrance.

And stopped.

And left.

But they left one instrument behind—a small thing like a crystallized question mark, which fell through the fissure's entrance and has been falling through my space ever since, never landing, a perpetual visitor in my perpendicular world. I've mapped its trajectory. It describes a shape that corresponds to nothing in my notation, a curve that implies the existence of other fissures, other watchers, other spaces that exist in the gaps between what is and what observes what is.

I am not alone. This is what the falling instrument teaches me. Somewhere—above, below, beside, it doesn't matter, these directions don't apply—there are others like me, cartographers of different correspondences, mapping other aspects of the keeper's vast experiment. One perhaps documents the relationship between the lattice's geometry and the mathematical principles it expresses. Another might catalogue the pool's memories, creating an archive of archives. A third could be mapping the stone itself—not its weightlessness, but its *decision* to be weightless, the original moment when it chose exemption.

We are a distributed intelligence, we cartographers, each in our own fissures, each mapping our own aspect of the same immense phenomenon. We will never meet—the fissures don't connect in any dimension we can traverse. But our maps, perhaps, will one day be assembled. Not by us. By something else, something that exists at a scale where our individual observations resolve into a single, coherent description of what's happening in the chamber at the top of Eth-Kalaam.

Until then, I map. The keeper reconfigures the lattice and I inscribe the shape of reconfiguration. The pool remembers and I document the structure of its memory. The stone continues to weigh nothing and I chart the topology of its refusal. The phantom water drops and I add another entry to my taxonomy of loneliness.

And sometimes—rarely, but sometimes—when the volcanic heat rises just right and the keeper's attention achieves a particular quality of focus, I catch a glimpse of something beyond my usual observations. The keeper, for just an instant, becomes aware of being observed. Not by me—it cannot sense me—but by something else, something vast and patient and deeply amused, something for which the keeper itself is just one note in a larger composition.

In these moments, my surfaces reorganize spontaneously into a configuration I don't control, a shape that maps not any state or correspondence but the raw fact of *being in the presence of a question*. The stone's impossibility, the keeper's patience, my own watching—all of it suddenly seems like a single gesture in a conversation conducted at scales we can barely imagine.

Then the moment passes. My surfaces return to their usual configurations. The keeper returns to its waiting. I return to my mapping.

But I remember. In my perpendicular archive, in my notation that grows more complex with each observation, I remember. And I know that someday—in geological time, in the time it takes mountains to rise and fall, in the time it takes gravity to notice it's missing something—someday, the stone will change. Or speak. Or reveal that it has been speaking all along, in a language made of selective exemptions, in a grammar of things that refuse their own nature.

When that happens, I will map it. The moment of revelation, the topology of understanding, the exact shape of the keeper's joy or disappointment or transformation. I will inscribe it on my surfaces, in my notation that no one else can read, in my fissure that no one else can find.

And somewhere, in other fissures, other cartographers will map their own aspects of that moment. And our maps will sing to each other across impossible distances, a distributed symphony of documentation, a chorus of watchers watching the watcher, all of us waiting, all of us ready, all of us here in the spaces between spaces, doing the work that must be done:

Making sure that nothing—nothing—is ever truly unobserved.
