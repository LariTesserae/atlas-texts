# The Keepers of Pattern

I am one of the memory-weavers, though that translation carries too much weight. In our tongue, we are simply *those-who-hold-the-count*, and the count is everything: the rhythm of wings against the pump-beat, the generations of eel-spawn in the third cistern, the precise angle at which Grandmother Yaas taught us to hold the braiding cord so it catches the lamplight just so, revealing whether a death-knot was tied in sorrow or in relief.

My dwelling hangs from the great copper trunk-line that runs between the fifth and sixth terraces, suspended in a basket of gradually calcifying leather that my great-grandmother wove from bat-moth wings. The wings have long since fused with the copper drip-patterns into something between architecture and geology. I can feel the pump through my floor, through my bones, through the small bones of my inner ear that have learned to distinguish between the healthy thrum and the skip that means a valve three levels down needs tending.

I am not young, though I am not yet old by our measure. My hair has begun to develop the greenish cast that comes from decades of copper exposure, and my fingernails grow now with ridges that match the terraces—seven ridges, one for each decade I've spent learning to read the patterns the city writes on itself. My eyes are the fog-blue of my mother's line, good for seeing in the between-light where most of our work happens.

Each morning, I wake with the first exhalation of the furnaces and check my counts. Not on paper—paper would rot in a day here—but in the cords that hang from my walls like a nervous system exposed. Each cord is a year, each knot an event worth holding. Birth-knots are tied clockwise. Death-knots counter. A new pipe is a double loop. A pipe removed—rare, so rare—is a cutting, the loose ends sewn back with thread from the winter molt of the grazers.

Today the count shows me that Old Fen's triplet grazers are due to drop their calves, that the mercury in the north-face thermometer has been rising by micro-degrees for eleven years (something to do with the deep furnaces, perhaps, or perhaps the mountain itself waking), and that young Kell, who tends the cisterns two terraces down, is the age his grandmother was when she first noticed the eel-things beginning their slow change toward what we think might be lungs. Evolution happens here, but slowly, on our time. We are the only witnesses patient enough to see it.

My work is specific: I count and record the small violences and accommodations between the city's inhabitants. When a new clutch of cinder-wrens colonizes an abandoned flue, I note it. When the colony drives out the wasp-analogs that had been there since my mother's time, I note that too. When the wasp-analogs establish themselves in the warm-shadow under junction forty-seven and begin to prey on the juvenile bat-moths, changing the flight patterns of the entire eastern swarm, I spend three weeks mapping the new territories in knots of increasing complexity.

This is not academic work. The city's balance is old but not infinite. Once, in my grandmother's time, someone killed too many predators on the fourth terrace—not from malice, but because a child had been frightened, and fear makes people stupid. Within a season, the grazers had multiplied beyond the walls' capacity to regrow lichen. Within two seasons, they were eating the wooden door frames, the leather window gaskets, the wax from the rain-cloaks. By the third season, they were gnawing the lead from pipe-joints, and the entire terrace had to be evacuated while the predators were coaxed back with careful feeding and patience.

My grandmother was one of the memory-weavers who helped repair that break. She taught me the repair pattern, though I've never had to use it. "You probably won't," she said, her fog-grey eyes already beginning to cloud with the cataracts that come to all of us if we live long enough. "But the pattern needs to be in someone's hands, just in case. The city forgets nothing, but we are not the city. We're just the part of it that remembers out loud."

I have no children of my own yet, though I am coming to the age where I should choose an apprentice. The old bloodlines matter less than they used to—what matters is the capacity to feel the count in your bones, to wake at night knowing that something has shifted in the eastern cisterns, to walk the catwalks and hear the story the dripping tells. My neighbor's daughter has the gift. She's seven, young enough to learn the patterns properly, old enough to understand that what we do is not preservation but participation.

Sometimes I climb to the highest catwalk I can reach, where the fog thins just enough that you can see the suggestion of sky. Not the sky itself—I don't think any of us have seen that—but the place where the fog admits it has an edge. I bring my counting cords and work by touch in the between-light, listening to the city breathe below me. The pump-beat is stronger here, carried up through the iron scaffolding like a pulse through an artery. Nine seconds. Nine seconds. Nine seconds.

I am not the city, but I am of it. My great-grandmother's basket sways when the wind comes up from the lower furnaces. The bat-moths that roost under my eaves know me well enough now that they don't scatter when I emerge in the morning. Sometimes one will land on my shoulder as I work, its feet finding purchase in the oiled wool, its wings folding and unfolding in time with its breathing. We breathe together, the moth and I and the city and the mountain. The count continues.

One day, perhaps soon, perhaps not for many years yet, I will train my replacement and take up residence on the second terrace, where the elders go when their eyes can no longer track the quick movements of the younger animals. I will tend the slower counts then—the ones that track the settling of foundations, the fusion of pipe-joints, the gradual transformation of copper to verdigris. And when my time comes to go through the hatch, my patterns will remain in the cords, and someone else will hold the count, and the city will remember, in its way, that I was here.

But that is not today. Today, Old Fen's grazers are birthing, and I must go count the calves and note their markings and tie the birth-knots clockwise, as my grandmother taught me, as her grandmother taught her, as we have always done, here where the stone weeps and the iron holds us and the fog makes everything eternal.
