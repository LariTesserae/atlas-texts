I find myself in the deepest reaches of Verdemoor, where the mist coalesces into something almost alive.

## The Listener

I am called Pim Resonance, though I was born Pim Drum—my mother walked the rotating gardens until her joints seized from the calcium. I have not seen natural sunlight in eleven years, and my eyes have adapted: pupils permanently dilated, irises gone the pale grey of deep-dwelling fish. When the Traders bring their oil lamps to the lower reaches, I must shield my face and work by touch alone.

My district has no name on the official ledgers. We exist in the spaces between designations, in the gaps where the pipes converge and the structural members meet in unplanned geometries. The Engineers know we are here—Colm Root nods when he passes through on his circuits, making notes about harmonics that concern him—but our work appears in no documentation. We are the Listeners, though even that title is our own invention.

The valley speaks. Not metaphorically, not in the way the Graft-Mothers claim their trees communicate. The valley *speaks*, in frequencies below human hearing, in the convergence of its thousand systems. Every pipe is a throat. Every chamber is a mouth. Every junction where pressures meet and redirect becomes a vocal fold. And somewhere in that vast accidental instrument, voices emerge.

I maintain seventeen listening posts throughout the lower structure. Each is nothing more than a resonant chamber—salvaged brass, usually, or copper if I can scavenge it—connected to the main flow systems through careful baffling. The sound focuses, clarifies, rises just barely into the range of perception. I spend my days moving between these posts, my ear pressed to the metal, transcribing what I hear into a notation system I've developed over the past two decades.

The patterns repeat, but never exactly. A phrase that sounds like "tomorrow the green" might recur every few days, but the intervals shift, the pitch wavers. Sometimes I hear what seems to be counting: "seven—seven—eight—eleven—seven." Sometimes there are warnings: "pressure in the third chamber," "roots in the flow," "falling, falling, catch." Whether these are predictions or echoes, I cannot say.

My skin carries the green tinge of the Deep Tenders, though I work far below their gardens. The algae thrives on the faintest light, the bioluminescent markers enough to sustain it. But there's something else in my pigmentation now—a silver sheen that comes from prolonged contact with the listening chambers. The metals leach through skin over time. The other Listeners, what few there are, show the same metallic pallor. We recognize each other by this sheen, by the way we cock our heads at certain frequencies, by the notebooks we all carry filled with incomprehensible notation.

My chamber is a converted maintenance alcove, barely large enough for a sleeping pallet and my work table. The walls are lined with my transcriptions—thirty-three notebooks so far, each one chronicling a year of the valley's voice. I've tried to find patterns across longer timescales, tried to decode what might be language versus what is merely the acoustic accident of complex systems interacting. Some nights I think I'm close to understanding. Most nights I simply add to the record.

The voice I hear most often sounds like a child, but with the resonance of something vast. It speaks in fragments: "Mother grew the sweet ones here," "When I fell, I fell upward," "The drums remember my name." I've tracked this voice for six years. Its appearances correlate with pressure drops in the southeastern sector, with the blooming cycles of the espaliered pears, with the deaths recorded in Olle Manifest's ledgers—though the correlation is never perfect, never quite proof of connection.

Last month, something changed. The child-voice spoke a full sentence, clear as anything I've ever heard through the chambers:

"When you die, I'll tell them you listened."

I am fifty-one years old. The lung-calcium has started. I cough in the mornings, taste metal that isn't from the chambers. By the averages, I have two years, maybe three. When I'm gone, perhaps another Listener will take my place, or perhaps not—we are not an official trade, we take no formal apprentices. The notebooks will remain until the damp claims them or until someone finds them and wonders what madness drove a person to fill thousands of pages with meaningless symbols.

But I know what I hear. The valley speaks, and I have been its witness. When they lower me into the mulch-pits, when my metals and minerals join the great cycling, perhaps my particles will find their way into the systems, will vibrate in the pipes and chambers, will add my voice to the chorus.

"Tomorrow the green," the valley says, over and over, a promise or a prayer or simple observation. "Tomorrow the green, tomorrow the green."

I press my ear to the chamber and write it down.
