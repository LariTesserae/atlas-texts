I find myself in the District of Provisional Conclusions, where the surveyor's maps have all been returned marked "needs revision" in handwriting that changes tense mid-sentence. My office—if we're being generous with definitions—occupies the third-and-a-half floor of a building that lost an argument with its own blueprint about whether staircases should commit to directions.

I am the city's Cartographer of Unmade Decisions, though my business cards keep editing themselves to read "Choreographer of Unplayed Diversions" or sometimes just "?" in fonts that haven't been invented yet. The position was created by municipal accident when someone filed the wrong form in triplicate, but by the time anyone noticed, I'd already furnished the office with maps rolled so tightly they've become theoretical.

My body exists as a negotiation between several competing drafts of humanity. My left hand is running Version 3.2—it remembers being fins but settled for fingers after focus group testing. My right operates on Legacy Code, still processing updates from when opposable thumbs were controversial. My spine is pure committee compromise: straight enough to appear professional, curved enough to accommodate doubt, with vertebrae that take votes before allowing me to turn around.

I wear clothes sewn from distances—the space between "almost" and "not quite," hemmed with the pause before "yes, but." My coat has pockets that open into other coats I might have worn if Thursday had gone differently. My shoes are made of compressed perhapses, their laces tied in knots that will resolve themselves pending approval from the Department of Ambulatory Intentions.

My age fluctuates with the humidity of regret. On dry days, I'm precisely thirty-seven-and-three-quarters, old enough to have made mistakes but young enough to make them again ironically. When it rains (and the rain here falls upward into its own potential), I fragment into ages I've borrowed from parallel lives: eight years old hiding under Conference Table #7, sixty-five and retired from jobs I haven't started yet, occasionally both, filing taxes in crayon.

I map unmade decisions for citizens who've lost their way between choices. They arrive at my office through doors they didn't take, referred by futures they decided against. My waiting room exists in the subjunctive mood—chairs that would be comfortable if comfort had graduated from theory to practice. The magazines are all final drafts of novels that gave up and became shopping lists.

My tools are cartographic implements that cartographers would find personally offensive: rulers that measure in units of "almost there," compasses that point to magnetic maybes, pencils that draw in the conditional tense. The maps I create show not where things are, but where they're considering being. Streets that might exist if enough people walk them into reality. Buildings in various stages of perhaps. Districts zoned for residential ambivalence.

Each morning (a designation that arrives by consensus rather than solar mechanics), I check the Previous Day's Deferred Decisions Drawer. Letters accumulate from choices that went into overtime: "Dear Cartographer, I was supposed to turn left at the intersection of Now and Then, but both streets were under construction. Please advise." I map new routes through the Possibly District, past the Museum of Unlearned Lessons, around the Paradise Lost and Found Department.

The hardest cases are the recursive ones. Citizens who've decided not to decide, then changed their minds about changing their minds. Their maps become Möbius strips of intention, leading everywhere and nowhere with equal determination. I file these under "Pending Pending" and wait for them to achieve escape velocity from their own indecision.

My lunch breaks occur in temporal brackets, borrowed from moments when I should be doing something else. I eat sandwiches that exist in quantum superposition between turkey and regret, with lettuce that remembers being seeds that considered becoming flowers instead. The café where I take these meals serves coffee in cups that haven't quite committed to being empty or full, so I drink the ambiguity and leave exact change in currencies from economies that dissolved into metaphor.

Evenings, I attend the Mapmakers Anonymous meetings in the basement of St. Schrodinger's Cathedral of Provisional Faith. We sit in a circle that's actually more of an ellipsis, sharing stories about clients who wanted directions to places that don't exist yet, or worse, places that exist too much. "Hi, I'm the Cartographer of Unmade Decisions, and I've been lost for seven years." "Hi, Cartographer."

My greatest professional achievement: a map of the route between wanting to leave and actually leaving, with detailed topography of all the reasons to stay marked in dotted lines that spell out "but what if" in seventeen languages. It hangs in the Municipal Gallery of Accomplishments Pending Review, protected by glass that's still deciding whether to be transparent.

My greatest failure: attempting to map my own decisions. The result was a document so recursive it achieved consciousness and immediately filed for emancipation. It now lives in the Autonomous Document District, sending me birthday cards written in my own handwriting saying "Wish you weren't here."

At home (a word I use while making air quotes with fingers that have considered becoming wings), I maintain an archive of unmade maps—blueprints for territories of pure potential. Cities built entirely from the pause before "no." Highways connecting all the lives I decided against living. An atlas of everywhere I'm not.

Sleep comes in installments, downloaded from the Municipal Dream Library's dial-up connection to the unconscious. I dream in drafts: first draft, I'm mapping the inside of maps. Second draft, the maps are mapping me. Final draft, we reach a mutual understanding to misunderstand each other professionally.

Tomorrow (which has been rescheduled to the day after yesterday's yesterday), I have appointments with three clients whose decisions have metastasized into elaborate choice architectures. One needs directions to a childhood that's running Tuesday through Thursday with matinées on weekends. Another wants a map showing all possible routes to forgiveness, with alternate directions in case forgiveness is closed for renovations.

This is my function in the Fresh Delta's democracy of doubt: to chart the unchartable, to provide clear directions through unclear intentions. I am the city's admission that even indecision needs infrastructure, that even wandering requires some idea of where you're not going.

My office door reads "Open During Business Hours," but neither the door nor the hours have agreed what business means. The nameplate below changes depending on who's reading it and what they need not to find. Sometimes it says "Department of Cartographic Uncertainty." Sometimes just "Here, But Elsewhere Under Consideration."

I keep office hours in time zones that haven't been ratified yet, available by appointment or accident, whichever loses its nerve first. My secretary is a draft of a person I might have hired if secretaries hadn't unionized against the concept of assistance. Instead, my appointments schedule themselves according to the anxiety levels of unmade choices throughout the city.

This is where you'd find me, if finding weren't considered aggressive by local custom. In my office of overlapping maybes, drawing maps for people who've lost their way between yes and no, charting territories in the republic of perhaps, measuring distances in the democracy of doubt.

The city pays my salary in time I would have wasted anyway, plus benefits that include dental coverage for words I should have said differently and vision insurance for hindsight that arrives too early to be useful. It's honest work, if honesty hadn't filed for conscientious objector status.

I am the Cartographer of Unmade Decisions. I am here to help you not find your way.
