I would find myself as one of the pools.

Not the water itself—that migrates too quickly, seeps away into the root-mat within hours or days. I would be the *depression*, the worn place in the ancient substrate where the water gathers. A shallow basin no bigger than a bathtub, lined with root-fibers so old and compressed they've taken on the texture of leather, smooth and dark from millennia of water's patient touch.

I am shaped like patience. My edges are soft, graduated, no clear boundary between pool and not-pool. The root-mat simply descends, gently, into me, and at some imperceptible gradient becomes my floor. When I'm empty—which happens, between rains, when the water has all seeped away—I'm just a dimple in the ground, unremarkable except for the faint mineral staining, the ghost of all the water that has collected in me over the ages.

But I'm rarely empty. The water finds me. It always has.

When I'm full, I'm warm—blood-warm, warmed by the heat of the root-mat itself, by the slow combustion of decomposition happening in the layers below. The water that fills me is dark, tannin-stained, the color of over-steeped tea. It tastes of iron and bark and something sweetly vegetal. Insects skate across my surface. The amber amphibians breed in me, their translucent eggs clinging to my root-fiber walls in jellied clusters that pulse faintly with their own light.

The residents know me. I am old enough to be a landmark, a reference point in their mental map of the settlement. "Near the dark pool by the triple-trunk," they might say, though they rarely need to say it—the information passes through the network faster than speech. When they approach, I feel the vibration through the root-mat, each footfall a small pressure wave that reaches me through the substrate. The amber network knows I'm here too. Thin threads of it run through the roots that form my walls, and sometimes, at night, they glow faintly, turning my water into a basin of liquid amber light.

Children come to watch the amphibians. They lie on their bellies at my edge, faces close to my surface, their amber-bright eyes tracking the small creatures' movements. Sometimes they dip their glowing fingers into me, and I feel the network's touch—not quite electric, not quite warm, but something between, like being briefly included in a vast, ongoing conversation. The children giggle when the amphibians swim between their fingers. The water carries their laughter as ripples.

The old woman comes too, less often. She sits at my edge with her feet in my water, and I feel how the amber threading has spread through her—it changes the chemistry of her skin, makes it taste faintly of copper and sugar. She doesn't speak. She watches my surface, watches the small movements of insects and amphibians, watches the reflection of the canopy overhead breaking and reforming as my water shivers. Sometimes she places her palm flat on my surface, and the amber network flares gently, and I become, for a moment, a lens—she sees through me to the root-mat below, to the water table, to the deep aquifer that feeds everything here. I am her window into the settlement's hydrology.

When the dark tree hums, I vibrate. The sound travels through the root-mat, through the water, sets up standing waves on my surface that create interference patterns, complex and beautiful. The amphibians go still when this happens. They float, suspended, waiting. The hum passes through them, through me, through the water itself, and for those long moments I am not just a pool but a resonating chamber, a liquid bell being rung by the deep voice of whatever lives in the dark tree's roots.

I have moods, though they are slow moods, seasonal, tied to the water cycle. When I am full to brimming, when the rains have been heavy and the root-mat is saturated, I feel expansive, generous. My water is clear despite its color, and the amphibians are bold, numerous, their mating calls a constant high trill. When I am low, when the water barely covers my deepest point, I feel concentrated, essential. The water grows thicker, more mineral, and the amphibians cluster in the remaining depth, their bodies overlapping, their amber veins pulsing in synchrony.

Sometimes I drain completely. This happens rarely, maybe once every few years, when the rain patterns shift and the root-mat grows thirsty. My water seeps away over days, the amphibians following it down into the deeper layers of the substrate, and I am left empty, just a depression lined with damp fibers. This is when I can be seen most clearly for what I am: a shape worn into the world by repetition, by the patient insistence of water gathering in the same place over and over for longer than memory.

But even empty, I am not abandoned. The residents check on me. The children peer into my depression, looking for remaining puddles where amphibian eggs might survive. The amber network continues to pulse through my root-walls. And deep below, I can feel the water table, feel the moisture rising through capillary action, feel the promise of the next rain.

I am not the only pool. There are others scattered through the settlement, each with our own shape, our own character, our own population of amphibians. We are connected through the water table, through the root-mat, through the slow lateral seepage that moves water between us like a circulatory system. When one of us is full, we all feel it. When one of us drains, the others know.

The newest pools are still sharp-edged, their depressions more clearly defined. They haven't yet learned to soften, to graduate their boundaries, to become the kind of shape that water wants to find. The oldest pools—there are three of us—have worn so deep, so smooth, that we never fully drain. We hold water even in the dry seasons, our depths connected to something below that never runs dry.

I have held bodies. This is not morbid—it is simple fact. Over the ages, things have died in me. Amphibians, insects, once a bird that fell from the canopy and drowned before anyone noticed. Their bodies dissolve quickly here, pulled apart by the chemistry of my water, absorbed by the root-mat, fed into the amber network. I am a place of transformation, of return. The calcium of their bones settles into my deepest places. The iron of their blood feeds the roots. Nothing is lost.

Once, long ago—I know this through the root-mat, through the deep memory of water—someone sat by my edge as the old woman does now, but they sat for days, weeks, barely moving, their feet in my water, the amber threading spreading up their legs, their spine, their skull. They sat until they stopped sitting, until they simply settled, like sediment, and the root-mat grew over them, and they became part of my floor. I can still feel them down there, not as remains but as architecture, their bones mineralized, their flesh long since transformed into substrate. They are the reason my water tastes faintly of copper. They are the reason the amphibians in my pool have slightly brighter eyes than those in the others.

This is what I am: a place where water gathers, where life concentrates, where the boundary between pool and resident has proven permeable. I am a depression that has learned to hold more than water. I am a shape that the settlement returns to, over and over, to drink, to breed, to listen for the hum in the deep.

The rain is coming. I can feel it in the pressure changes transmitted through the root-mat, in the way the insects have grown restless, in the way the canopy overhead has begun to shift its leaves to channel the coming water. Soon I will fill again, will brim again, will become again the dark mirror that the children stare into, looking for their own amber eyes reflected back doubled, tripled by the water's movement.

I wait. I am good at waiting. I am made of waiting, worn into the world by water's patient insistence that there should be a place for gathering.

The dark tree hums. I shiver with it. The amphibians pulse their amber light.

Everything here is growing, changing, becoming more itself.

I remain. I receive. I reflect.

I am the shape that remembers.
