# The Mender

I am the one who fixes what should not need fixing.

In a place where nothing changes, where perfection has been achieved and maintained for generations, I am the flaw. Not because I break things — I would never — but because I see how they could break. Will break. Are already breaking in ways too small for even the listeners to hear.

My dwelling is set apart from the others, carved deeper into the limestone where the drip-song arrives muffled and strange. I tell myself I chose this remove to avoid disturbing the great work with my movements, my too-large body that never learned the proper smallness. But perhaps I simply could not sleep inside that perfect sound, knowing what I know.

My fingers are wrong. Where the others have spider-silk digits that know the exact tension of every cord, mine are thick, clumsy things that want to pull too hard, tie too tight. I have broken seventeen vessels in my lifetime. Each time, the listeners came to me in the dark, not speaking, and I knew to begin making the replacement. It takes me months to get the pitch right. The others could do it in days, but the others would never need to.

I learned my strangeness early. At six, when the other children were learning to move between drops without disturbing the air, I was watching the limestone ceiling, seeing the microscopic shifts where new water found old channels. A stalactite that had dripped in the same spot for a thousand years would, I knew, begin to drift leftward by the width of a hair. The cup below would need to be rehung. When I told the listeners, they went still in that way that means either deep thought or deep dismissal. Then they rehung the cup, but they hung it where it had always hung. The drip began to hit the rim instead of the center. The sound went sour.

That was when I understood: they cannot see forward. They can only see now, and back. The song is perfect because it has always been perfect, and it has always been perfect because it is perfect now. Time is a circle here, not a line, and I am the only one who feels it moving forward, who knows that limestone grows and copper oxidizes and hair-cords rot from the inside out.

So I mend in secret. At the deadest hour, when even the listeners sleep, I climb the scaffolding with my too-heavy body and my wrong hands. I replace cords before they snap. I clean the mineral scale from vessel-bottoms by touch. I adjust the hanging-heights by fractions to account for the ceiling's growth. And in the morning, the song sounds exactly as it always has, because I have changed things precisely enough that nothing changes.

The others know, I think. Not what I do, but that something is done. Sometimes I catch them not-quite-looking at me, the way you might not-quite-look at a shadow that moves when it shouldn't. I am tolerated because the alternative is admitting that the song needs tending, that perfection is not a state but a process, that someone must be responsible for keeping time at bay.

I had a lover once. She was a listener, small and perfect, with eyes that saw sound. She would sit with me in my far chamber and we would listen to the muffled song together, and she would tell me what each diminished tone meant, how the distance changed the meaning. For a year we were happy in this strange way. Then one night she found me on the scaffolding, replacing a cord that would have lasted another season. She watched me work, silent, and when I climbed down she was gone. She never came to my chamber again. I see her sometimes at the cistern, drinking our ancestors, and she looks through me like I am a sound her ears have learned not to hear.

I am old now, which is wrong too — we do not age here, not visibly, but I feel the weight of all the prevented disasters in my joints. My hands shake when I braid replacement cords. Soon I will not be able to climb to the highest vessels. I should train someone, but how can I? To see what I see is to be ruined for the song. To know that it needs help is to be unable to hear it purely.

Last night I found water on my sleeping mat. A new seep in the wall, microscopic but growing. The limestone is shifting in some deep place, pressed by the weight of the mountain. In ten years, perhaps twenty, this wall will crack. Water will pour in. The careful tuning will be destroyed. The cistern will overflow. The lichen will die in the new wetness and the darkness will be absolute.

I could warn them. I could show them the damp spot, explain the geology, convince them to dig new drainage channels or move the entire settlement. But warning them would mean admitting that the world changes, that the song is not eternal, that we live in time like everyone else.

So instead I lie on my mat and feel the dampness growing and think: perhaps this is what I was always for. Not to preserve their perfection, but to be the one who knows it will end. Someone has to carry that knowledge. Someone has to be changed by it.

When I die, they will put me in the cistern with the others. I wonder if my wrongness will dissolve too, or if it will persist in the water, a bitter taste they learn to ignore. I wonder if another mender will be born, or if they will simply let the cords fray and the cups crack and the ceiling fall, all at once, when the time comes.

There is a word I have, which I have never spoken aloud, which is the word for the sound a cup makes in the moment before it breaks — a sound too high for hearing, a kind of readiness. I whisper it sometimes in my far chamber, where the song cannot quite reach, where time is allowed to pass.

It sounds like singing.
