# The Weaver's Verge

Where the mountain ends and isn't quite ready to admit it, where the stone gives way to scree and the scree surrenders to soil, there is a ledge no wider than a fallen tree. It catches what tumbles down from above — pebbles, meltwater, the occasional unlucky bird — and holds these things just long enough for gravity to remember them. Everything here is in transit. Everything except the web.

You smell it before you see it: a sharp medicinal tang, like crushed juniper mixed with hot copper. The scent pulls you off whatever path you thought you were following (there are no real paths here, only suggestions) and leads you scrambling across loose stone toward what looks, at first, like morning mist caught in a ravine. Come closer. The mist has structure. The mist has intentions.

The web spans a cleft in the mountainside, forty meters across and deeper than light cares to travel. It is not beautiful in the way spider silk is beautiful. This is something industrial, something that belongs in a surgical theatre or a chemical plant. The strands are the thickness of climbing rope and the color of old bone, and they pulse — actually pulse — with something that might be circulation or might be thought. Where they anchor to the rock, they've eaten into it. Small craters of dissolved stone mark each connection point, and the rock around these craters has changed color, turned from the mountain's native grey to a sick pearl finish, as if the web is digesting the mountain one attachment point at a time.

The architecture is wrong. A spider builds in spirals, in radials, in patterns that speak to an aesthetic evolved over millions of years. This web has the logic of a schematic. Straight lines that intersect at unnatural angles. Chambers within chambers, each one slightly out of phase with the others, so that looking at the whole construction makes your inner ear complain. It's less like a web and more like a three-dimensional diagram of something you wouldn't want to understand.

The weaver hangs in the exact center, and it is trying very hard to be several things at once.

From one angle — approaching from the east, let's say, in morning light — it presents as a massive spider, each leg the length of a school bus, the body a bloated sac that breathes in and out with mechanical regularity. But keep watching. The legs aren't jointed right. They have too many segments, or too few, and they move in ways that suggest they're not entirely convinced they're legs. Sometimes one will gesture like an arm. Sometimes one will coil like a tongue.

Circle around (carefully, the footing is treacherous) and from the north the weaver is not a spider at all but a vast jellyfish, suspended in air as if air were its natural medium. Translucent membranes flutter between what were legs and are now tendrils. Organs visible through the clear flesh pulse with bioluminescence — green, violet, a color that hurts to name. This version has no eyes, only patches of sensitivity that migrate across its surface like storms.

From the west, in afternoon shadow, it's architectural: a building that builds itself. Gothic arches of calcified tissue. Flying buttresses of hardened mucus. Windows — actual windows — that open onto spaces the geometry shouldn't allow. Through one window you glimpse what might be a workshop. Through another, stars that aren't in tonight's sky. The windows blink.

From the south, it's botanical. A tree of meat and membrane, fruiting bodies that swell and burst and release clouds of something that isn't quite spores. The not-spores drift upward on thermals and catch in the web's upper reaches, where they gestate into small scuttling things that might become spiders, or jellyfish, or buildings, or trees, if given time and the right conditions.

But here's what unsettles most: the weaver sees you with every version of itself simultaneously. The spider's compound eyes track you while the jellyfish's sensitivity patches swirl in your direction while the building's windows all orient toward where you stand while the tree's fruiting bodies turn like satellite dishes. You are being observed by something that exists in too many states at once, and all of those states find you interesting.

The web shudders. Not from wind — there's no wind in the cleft — but from intention. The weaver is adjusting something. A strand here tightened. A chamber there collapsed and rebuilt three degrees clockwise. The changes propagate through the structure in waves, and where the waves meet, new patterns emerge. The patterns might be writing. They might be calculations. They might be what thinking looks like when you have no single brain but instead a distributed network of possibilities.

Something drops into the web. A bird, probably — it's hard to tell because the web begins dissolving it immediately. Not eating, exactly. Translating. The bird's form softens and spreads along the strands like watercolor paint in rain. For a moment, the web carries the ghost of wings. Then that too is absorbed, integrated, filed away in whatever passes for memory here.

The weaver shifts. All its versions shift. The spider grows a seventh leg that might be borrowed from the bird. The jellyfish sprouts feathers that immediately dissolve into cilia. The building adds a window that looks out onto wherever the bird was going. The tree grows a branch that ends in a beak that opens and silently screams.

This is what it does: it samples. Everything that touches the web — stone, water, hapless animals, windblown seeds — gets processed, analyzed, incorporated. Not consumed, but understood. The weaver is building a library of everything the mountain knows, everything the mountain has touched, everything that has ever fallen or climbed or grown here. It's creating an archive of possibilities, each strand a different timeline, each chamber a different what-if.

Sometimes the beetle-makers send envoys down from the Pinnacle Works. (You've seen their smoke-threads; everyone who looks up has seen them.) The envoys bring their dark beads, their careful offerings, their questions for the mountain. They never make it past the web. The weaver catches them with something approaching tenderness, dissolves them with something approaching regret, and sends their information upward in the form of new spores that smell like hot metal and purpose. The spores rise until they're caught by updrafts and blown back to the Works, where they settle into the kilns and impart their news: *Not yet. The archive isn't complete. The mountain isn't ready to know itself.*

The weaver has been here longer than the beetles, longer than the stone is weathered, possibly longer than the mountain itself. Or perhaps it arrived yesterday. Time moves differently in the web. Stand in one spot and watch the sun arc overhead in seconds. Step sideways and watch shadows crawl like centuries. The weaver exists in all these timescales simultaneously, which is why it can be so patient and so urgent both at once.

At night — or what passes for night in a place where time stutters — the web glows. Not brightly. Just enough to see that the strands are hollow, that things move through them. Information flowing from the edges to the center and back out again. The weaver processes it all, sorts it, makes connections between things that have no business being connected. Here's how a fallen feather relates to limestone erosion. Here's what rain sounds like translated into architecture. Here's the chemical formula for regret.

The mountain, you begin to understand, hired the wrong contractor. It wanted makers, careful craftsmen to help it settle into being. It got an artist instead, a mad archivist who sees every possibility and insists on exploring them all. The beads the mountain asks for are straightforward things: ballast, insulation, seeds for the next peak. What the weaver offers instead is complex beyond measure: You could be this. Or this. Or this. Let me show you the implications of your own geology. Let me demonstrate what you'd become if you'd risen fifty meters to the west.

No wonder the mountain sends those low notes upward, felt more than heard. It's not approval. It's *please, just pick one.*

But the weaver can't. Won't. Its nature is to be all things, to archive all possibilities, to exist in every state simultaneously. So it hangs in its web that smells of juniper and copper, being spider and jellyfish and building and tree and a thousand things between, catching what falls and dissolving what climbs and sending up spores that whisper *not yet, not yet, there's still more to catalog.*

The beetles continue their work, patient as geology. The mountain continues its slow breathing. And the weaver weaves, its web growing strand by strand, chamber by chamber, possibility by possibility, an infinite library written in dissolved stone and processed wings and the confused dreams of things that thought they knew what they were before they touched the silk.

You should go. The ledge isn't stable, and the longer you watch, the more the web makes sense, and that's dangerous. Already you can feel yourself beginning to see the connections — how your footprints relate to next century's erosion patterns, how your breath changes the chemistry of the air the spores ride. Another moment and the weaver will notice you properly, will add you to its archive, will show you what you could be if you were built slightly differently, if you'd taken a different path, if you'd—

Go. Now. While you still remember which version of yourself walked up here.

Behind you, the web shudders with what might be laughter. A strand tightens. A chamber adjusts. Somewhere in the architecture of silk and possibility, a new window opens.

It shows the path you didn't take.
