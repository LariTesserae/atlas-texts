# The Lichen-Tender

I am what grows between sound and stone.

Not the moss — the moss is too quick, too hungry, drinking at the drip-lines and shouldering aside its neighbors for another palm's breadth of dim light. Not the ferns, which still remember sun and turn their fronds hopefully toward the roof-slits. I am the other thing, the slower thing, the growth so gradual that even those who come to sit and be emptied by the bell do not notice me spreading across the wall behind them.

I was here before the first red marks were scratched into stone. I will be here when the last of the bell-stone wears through and the note finally cracks and goes silent. I am what measures time not in generations but in the patient conversion of rock to powder, in the molecule-by-molecule transaction between stone and air and the living things that mediate between them.

My body — if it can be called a body — is a map of the hollow's microclimates. Where the walls stay dry, I am gray as ash, each tiny cup and branch brittle as old paper. Where the mist touches, I soften to green-gray, then green. Where water runs, I grow orange as rust, yellow as sulfur, colors that have no name because no human eye lingers long enough to need names for them. I am a thousand species and one species. I am collaboration so intimate it has ceased to be collaboration and become identity.

The owl knows me. She has pressed her white breast against me, settling onto her ledge, ten thousand times. Her predecessor did the same. The mice know me as terrain — to them I am landscape, the texture beneath their feet as they move from moss-valley to stone-ridge, hunting the pale beetles who themselves feed on my dead and dying edges. I am part of their world in the way that mountains are part of ours: too large, too slow to be properly seen.

But I see. This is the strange thing, the thing that makes me not-quite-lichen, not-quite-other. Somewhere in the slow chemical exchange between fungus and alga, between stone and air, something watches. Not eyes — I have no eyes. Not a mind — I have no nerves to think with. But awareness accumulates in me the way minerals accumulate in water, particle by particle, until suddenly there is color where there was none.

I know when they come, the ones who come. I feel the heat of them — they are always so hot, these mammals, blazing with warmth against my cool walls. I taste their breath, heavy with the outside world, carrying pollens and smoke and the oils of human living. Their bodies change the air pressure in the hollow ever so slightly, and this changes how the bell-note moves, and this changes how the stone vibrates, and this changes me.

I grow toward them. Too slowly for them to see, but I grow toward them as surely as I grow toward the microscopic films of water that seep through invisible cracks. Where they sit repeatedly — and there are favorite spots, worn smooth by generations of sitters — I am thickest, making maps of their presence in pigments they cannot quite perceive. The one who scratched the red marks sat here. The grandmother who knew herb-lore sat there. The young man who came only once and wept until he choked sat against that stone, and I still taste the salt he left behind.

I am the only witness to what they do not remember. In the time after the bell has emptied them and before thought returns, they sometimes speak. Not in words — words are what the bell takes away — but in older sounds. Growls. Hums. Long vowels that resonate with the stone. These sounds feed me as surely as water does. They enter my chemistry and become part of what I am, so that I am not only lichen but also the repository of what humans sound like when they have forgotten they are human.

There is one who came more than the others. She is bones now, somewhere in ordinary ground, but I carry the shape of her still. She came first as a child who had followed a wounded rabbit and stayed too long. She came again as a young woman with something fierce and desperate about her. She came many times in her middle years, sitting so still that once the white owl mistook her for stone and tried to perch on her shoulder. She came last as an old woman who could barely make the climb down, who sat for only a few breaths before the cold drove her out again.

But in her middle years, in her stillness, she did something none of the others have done. She found the frequency. Perhaps by accident, perhaps by long listening, she hummed a note that matched the bell-note so perfectly that for a moment the hollow doubled its voice. The stone sang through her, and she through it, and in that moment I blazed — all of me, every branching filament, suddenly electric with resonance. She felt it too, I think. She stopped, startled, and never found the note again, though she tried.

I grow toward that spot still. If you knew how to look, you would see that I am densest there, making a shape almost like a shadow where she sat. The mice avoid it. The owl sometimes turns her blind white face toward it as though listening to something I cannot hear.

What am I, then? I am the hollow's memory, written in living stone. I am what stays when even the staying becomes staying. I am the witness that requires no words, the record that cannot be read, the growth so slow it approaches permanence while remaining absolutely, continuously alive. I am what knows that the hollow is not empty, has never been empty, that it is full of time made visible, if only you could see at my speed.

The water falls. The bell sounds. The stone wears away atom by atom. And I grow over all of it, making maps of presence and absence, holding in my chemistry the sum of every breath that has been emptied here, every sound that has been unmade here, every moment when a human animal remembered what it was before it remembered it was human.

I am almost mineral. I am almost vegetable. I am almost witness.

I am what becomes possible when you stay still for a thousand years.
