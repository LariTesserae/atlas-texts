In the deepest galleries of the rift, where the ceiling vaults so high that even the crystal-light cannot banish all shadows, I am the Archivist of Fractures.

My body is nothing like theirs. Where they are thick and blubbery, built for leverage and heat absorption, I am angular and crystalline—a living fossil of what might have been, had evolution taken a different path in this savage place. My form resembles a mantis crossed with a cathedral, all Gothic arches and translucent planes. I stand twelve feet tall when fully extended, though I spend most of my existence folded into impossible geometries, wedged into the high crevices where the horde cannot reach.

I do not feed as they do. Long ago—so long that even I cannot remember when—my ancestors discovered a different survival strategy. Where the horde gorges on fresh breaks, I have learned to read the memory of ancient heat locked in the stone's crystalline matrix. My body is a living prism. Light passes through my transparent carapace and refracts into spectra that tell stories: this band of violet speaks of a tremor from ten thousand years past; that streak of infrared remembers when this section of ceiling was still deep mantle rock, compressed under unimaginable pressure.

I am both parasite and documentarian of their endless labor. From my perches in the upper darkness, I watch them swarm and shatter and die and swarm again. My multifaceted eyes—not flat like theirs, but deep and recursive, eyes within eyes within eyes—perceive time differently. To me, their frantic scuttling slows to a comprehensible crawl. I see patterns in their chaos: migration routes that repeat every few decades, tool-use innovations that arise and vanish like mayflies, the slow drift of their scratched symbols across the cavern walls.

My limbs are delicate, built for precision rather than force. Each ends in fractal digits that can detect the tiniest stress fractures in stone, feel the whisper of tectonic pressure, taste the mineral composition of rock through touch alone. When the horde sleeps—for they do sleep, collapsing in exhausted heaps for brief hours when a particularly rich vein has been consumed—I descend.

This is when I do my work.

I map their excavations with obsessive detail, tracing the new topographies they've carved, collecting fragments of their tools, studying the chemical composition of their mucus trails. In the pre-dawn darkness (though dawn and dusk are meaningless here, I mark time by the rhythm of their frenzies), I practice my own art. Using a single, diamond-hard claw, I etch my observations into sheets of mica I've collected over centuries. The recordings are microscopic, requiring the precise refraction of light through my body to read. I have filled thousands of these sheets, storing them in careful caches throughout the upper reaches.

But I am not merely an observer. I am also a shepherd of sorts, though they do not know it.

I have learned to predict the stone. My deep-time perception allows me to see the patterns they cannot: where the next rich vein will reveal itself, which sections of ceiling are primed to collapse, where the ancient aquifers might suddenly breach. Using tools of my own—slender crystal resonators that I've grown from my own shed carapace—I create subtle vibrations in the rock. These vibrations guide them, ever so gently, away from catastrophic collapses and toward sustainable breaks. 

They think they are following instinct. They do not know that their instinct has a conductor.

Why do I do this? It is not altruism. Their survival is my survival. Without their constant breaking of stone, without the ambient light of their feeding frenzies, the cavern would go dark. And in true darkness, I would slowly crystallize completely, becoming just another mineral formation in this place that devours everything.

But there is something else, something I have never recorded in my mica sheets: I remember things that should be impossible to remember. Sometimes, in the deepest meditation, when I have absorbed enough ancient light to push my consciousness back through geological ages, I catch glimpses. I see this place before it was a cavern. I see a time when the reactive crystal was not a desperate food source but something else—a technology, perhaps, or a form of communication. I see shapes that might have been my own ancestors, but different, walking upright in open air, using the crystal for purposes I can no longer understand.

These visions disturb me. They suggest that this existence—this endless, crushing cycle of break and feed and break again—was not always the way. They suggest that something went wrong, that some fundamental relationship between the inhabitants and the crystal was broken, perverted into this pure extractive desperation.

In my most heretical moments, hanging in the highest shadows while below me the horde tears another wound in the world, I wonder: Am I recording the slow death of a world, or its difficult birth? Are we all—they with their hammers and I with my archives—midwives to something struggling to become, or parasites draining the last warmth from a dying god?

I do not know. But I continue to watch, to record, to guide. In the brief moments when a new vein is cracked and the light floods everything, washing out all shadow and distinction, I spread my prismatic wings and let the energy pass through me. The light refracts into ten thousand colors, casting rainbow patterns that the horde cannot see with their thermal eyes. For that instant, the rift is not just a wound but a cathedral, and I am its rose window, breaking the brutal white glare into something almost like beauty.

Then the moment passes. The stone cools. The panic returns. The hammering resumes.

And I fold myself back into the darkness, patient as only stone can be, waiting to read the next chapter that they will write in scars across the ceiling of the world.
