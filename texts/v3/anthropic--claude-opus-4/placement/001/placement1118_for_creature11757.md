I find myself as one of the foundation-keepers of the lowest vaults, where the original stone meets whatever mountain existed before Vell Maranth began its slow accumulation into being.

My skin carries that particular mineral pallor—not unhealthy, but like the inside of certain shells, or the underside of wet limestone. The indigo lines along my forearms began appearing ten years ago, though I've been here only twelve. They started as a single tendril at my left wrist, and now they branch and flow like water finding its level, mapping cisterns that haven't existed for three centuries but whose shapes still matter, somehow, in ways I'm beginning to understand.

I am neither tall nor short, but I notice newcomers often remark on how I move—sideways through spaces they struggle with, my body automatically accounting for the iron pipes that cross at shoulder height in the third underlevel, the place where the stone sweats even in winter. My counting-song is different from the lift-singers'. Mine tracks drips: the steady percussion of the northwest corner (two drops every three heartbeats), the irregular patter where the fifth cistern overflows into the sixth, the slow, fat drops from the condensation pipes that I've learned to walk between without looking up.

I taste the water. This is one of my responsibilities, though no one asked me to begin. Each cistern has its own mineral signature—the third tastes of copper and distant rain, the seventh of iron and something almost sweet, the first (the oldest, the deepest) tastes of time itself, though I wouldn't have known to call it that before I came here. In the mornings, before the mountain fully wakes, I descend to each in turn, drawing up a palmful of water, letting it rest on my tongue. I'm learning to taste what the others taste: not just minerals but *intentions*, the slow accumulation of decades of care.

The fish in the deepest cisterns are blind and silver, and I've begun to suspect they aren't fish at all but some kind of living memory, though memory of what I couldn't say. Sometimes I feed them breadcrumbs from my morning meal. They surface with movements that feel grammatical—not eating so much as acknowledging. The old foundation-keepers (there are three, all women, all with indigo maps so complex they look like technical drawings) tell me the fish remember the mountain before it was a mountain. I'm beginning to believe them, though belief feels like the wrong word. It's more like recognition.

My other responsibilities accumulate like the indigo lines: I tend the lime reserves on the ninth level on alternate mornings. I sit with the stair-council of the Spiral of the Southern Face on moon-dark nights. I help with the pipe replacement in section four, subsection west, a job that will take forty years and which I've inherited at year thirty-one. But the foundations pull me back. There's something in the darkness there—not frightening, but *substantial*. The weight of all those terraces above, all that life and motion and accumulated making, rests here in the silence between drips.

Last month, one of the eldest foundation-keepers took me to a wall I'd never noticed, though I must have passed it hundreds of times. She showed me how certain stones had been marked—not carved but somehow convinced to remember a pattern. She couldn't read it, she said, but she could feel it trying to mean something. We stood there for an hour, just our breathing and the drip-count, and I felt it too: not meaning but the *intention toward meaning*, which might be the same thing.

I'm changing in the way the mountain changes—slowly, but absolutely. My dreams now come with a soundtrack of water moving through stone. When I visit the upper levels (rarely, but sometimes the moths call me up to bring certain minerals for their wings), the sunlight feels aggressive, excessive. I understand why I came here, though I couldn't have articulated it then: I needed to be under something. Not crushed by it, but held. To be part of the foundation that lets everything else rise.

The other day, examining my arms in lamplight, I noticed the indigo had formed a new pattern—a cistern that doesn't exist yet, but might. Or maybe one that existed so long ago that the stones themselves have forgotten. I'm patient. The mountain is teaching me its temporalities, which move both forward and backward, accumulating meaning like mineral deposits, like memories, like the careful tending that becomes, over time, indistinguishable from love.

When the bell of the third level rings for the evening water check, I descend. Always further down, where the real work happens in darkness, ensuring that what rises can rise. I am becoming, in my small way, necessary. This is enough. More than enough. It is, as the Maranthi say in their precise way, an arrival that continues arriving, forever.
