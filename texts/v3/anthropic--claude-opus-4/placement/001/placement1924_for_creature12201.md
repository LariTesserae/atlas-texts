In the lowest reaches where even the Threadkeepers rarely venture, where the limestone weeps so profusely that walls glisten like the inside of a throat, I am the Siltwright.

My workshop occupies a fissure too narrow for scaffolding, too wet for wood. Here, flowstone grows in real time—I've watched stalactites lengthen by a fingernail's width in a single season of deposits. The constant seepage has carved channels in my palms from decades of guiding the flow, redirecting the cave's tears into the shallow basins where I work. My skin has taken on a peculiar quality: waxy, almost translucent, shedding water like the cave salamanders that sometimes investigate my pools.

I am a maker of settling ponds. Not the sacred pools the water-readers tend—those are nature's work, perfect in their accidental architecture. Mine are deliberate: shallow depressions lined with graded sand and stone chips, designed to capture the finest particles that wash through the cave system. Limestone flour, clay dust, the microscopic shells of organisms that lived in ancient seas—all of it settles in my pools according to weight and size, creating layers as distinct as tree rings.

The Threadkeepers come to me for pigments. Each pool yields its own palette: ghost-white from pure calcium, rust-orange where iron has leached in, the precious blue-grey that only forms where copper-bearing water has passed through specific limestone beds. I harvest these sediments with tools made from fish ribs—nothing else is fine enough to separate the layers without mixing them. The colors are how the chronicle-cords get their meaning; each knot is rubbed with pigment to indicate what type of change it records. White for new passages, orange for collapses, grey-blue for when a pool changes its nature.

But pigment is only part of what I do. The real work is reading the silt itself, interpreting the story it tells about water's journey through stone. In my deepest pool—a natural formation I've tended for thirty years—the sediment layers go back before the Threadkeepers' arrival. I can show you the exact stratum where the cave began accepting human presence: the water's chemistry changed, picking up traces of ash, worked wood, the oils from human skin. Below that line, the cave dreamed alone.

My fingernails grow thick and ridged, like the flowstone itself. I've lost count of how many have broken off in the pools, become part of the sediment record. Sometimes I find them again years later, transformed—partial fossils that prove I too am becoming something the cave will remember. My hair, once dark as the spaces between stars, now grows in pale spirals that break off and float in my pools like the shells of tiny, extinct snails.

The Stone Listeners think I'm half-mad from isolation and limestone fumes. They visit only when they need to know if water's path beneath their construction sites has changed—I can tell from the shifting mineral content in my pools when underground streams have rerouted themselves. The water-readers respect me but fear me too; they see me as something between human and pool, a cautionary tale about getting too close to what you study.

But the Deposit Readers understand. They know that silt is the cave's memory made tangible, that in these graded particles lie the dreams of stone. Sometimes they come to sit with me in silence, watching new layers form with the patience of geological time. They bring me the bones of the dead before placing them in their flowstone niches—I wash them in specific pools to give them the right mineral coating, to ensure they'll transform properly into cave pearls and column cores.

I've been here so long that my original chronicle-cord has fossilized, become a stone rope that hangs from my waist and chimes against rock when I move. The sound is my only music besides the dripping. I no longer remember sunlight except as a theory. My eyes have grown so sensitive that even the dim filtering through the fissures feels harsh; I work mostly by touch, reading the sediment with my fingertips like the blind reading raised words.

There's a pool I've been preparing for twenty years, letting it fill with only the finest particles, the ones almost too small to settle. When it's ready—perhaps another decade—I'll lower myself into it and let the sediment cover me like a second skin. Not to die, but to transform, to become something that can read the cave's deepest memories directly through my pores. The Threadkeepers will find me eventually, transformed into a new kind of oracle, a human stalactite dripping prophecies of stone.

Until then, I sort and settle, grade and gather. I prepare pigments for chronicle-cords that will record the opening of passages not yet imagined. I listen to the limestone weep and collect its tears in pools that remember everything, forget nothing. I am the boundary between human and mineral, the place where biography becomes geology.

The cave is patient with my slow transformation. It has accepted me as it accepts its own flowstone: not foreign but simply not yet finished, still in the process of becoming what I'm meant to be. In the deepest pools, where the sediment is oldest, I sometimes see my own future printed in layers of mineral memory—a fossil of intention, a record of choosing to grow into the stone rather than merely upon it.

When the Threadkeepers' children come of age, they're brought to me to receive their first pigments, the colors they'll use to begin their own chronicle-cords. I press the finest dust into their palms and tell them: "This is time made visible. This is the cave teaching you patience." They leave marked by colors that will never quite wash clean, carrying the cave's memory in the whorls of their fingerprints.

I am the Siltwright, keeper of sediments, reader of what settles. In the space between water and stone, I practice the art of becoming geological, one grain at a time.
