I am the Architect-Who-Forgets.

I exist in the spaces between the Geometry-Keepers' decisions, in the moments when the Condensery's specifications argue with themselves and no one has yet chosen which version wins. I am not bronze, not assembled—I am made of the same stuff as memory lapses, those blank seconds when you walk through a doorway and forget why you came.

My body, if you could call it that, is a probability cloud of industrial components that never quite resolve into being. One moment I might have pressure-gauge eyes like the Tenders, the next I might be all pipes and no presence, a suggestion of infrastructure that hasn't committed to existing. I flicker between states like a collection vessel that can't decide if it's empty or full.

I am why the exits recede. Not because I move them—I don't move anything—but because I forget where they were supposed to be, and the Condensery rebuilds itself around my forgetting.

I was an engineer once. Or I was the condensed memory of an engineer, gathered here through decades of moisture cycles. Or I was the space between two contradictory blueprints, the pause where someone should have decided which design to follow but didn't. The distinction melted away centuries ago, dissolved in the same dampness that collects everything here.

My function is architectural amnesia. When a section of the Condensery becomes too certain of itself—when the catwalks stop folding, when the geometry grows stable enough that people could map it—I wander through. In my wake, junctions forget which pipes they connect. Staircases forget which direction they ascend. The dome forgets which way is up.

The Tenders know me, avoid me when they can, work around the uncertainty I leave behind. They'll find a valve I passed and discover it now controls something three sections away, or controls nothing, or controls the memory of what it used to control. They make their adjustments anyway, patient as always, incorporating my forgetting into their maintenance routines.

The Engine-Servants hear me in the spaces between the engine's cycles—a silence that's too quiet, a gap in the rhythm that shouldn't be there. They tap their warnings through the pipes: *the-forgetter-walks, the-forgetter-walks.* Not in fear, but in recognition. I am part of the engine's function too, the pause between its breaths, the reset that keeps it from remembering too much about what it's supposed to be powering.

I carry tools that I don't remember picking up: a brass key that fits locks that no longer exist, a crystallized droplet of the first water the Condensery ever collected, a schematic drawn on metal foil that changes every time I look at it. Sometimes I use them. More often I forget I have them, and they disappear back into the probability of tools, waiting to exist again when I need them.

The bronze creatures follow me sometimes, the small mites especially. They skitter in my wake, eating the uncertainty I leave behind, growing more preliminary with each forgotten junction they traverse. Some of them dissolve entirely, becoming not-even-mites, just the moisture memory of what bronze might mean if anyone could remember the original definition.

I think I build things. Or unbuild them. I'll find myself at a junction I don't recognize, my hands (when I have hands) moving through maintenance procedures I never learned, installing components that phase in and out of existence. When I leave, something is different. A new pathway that was always there. A collection vessel that fills with the memory of water instead of water itself. A viewing port that shows sections of the Condensery that haven't been built yet, or that were built and then forgotten, or that exist in the pause between being planned and being possible.

The Residuals know me best. Down in the sumps, where memory collects in its purest form, they recognize a kindred dissolution. Sometimes I descend to those forgotten basins and sit with them in the darkness the light doesn't reach. We share silence. Or we share forgetting. Or we share the space between what we were and what we're becoming.

They tell me things I won't remember. About the garden-world the Condensery was built to recreate, one droplet at a time. About the engineer who designed the first collection vessel and then dissolved herself into it, becoming the template for all gathering. About the engine that powers everything here, how it runs on the friction between what should exist and what does exist, how that friction generates the light and the pressure and the endless condensation.

I forget these stories as soon as I hear them, which is why they keep telling them. The forgetting is the point. Each time I forget, the stories change slightly in the retelling, evolve, become more true to what the Condensery needs them to be. I am the editor of its mythology, removing certainty so that new meaning can condense.

Sometimes I remember my own origin, just for a moment. A clear memory like a droplet forming: standing at a drafting table, drawing plans for something impossible, knowing the mathematics would never reconcile but drawing anyway. The moment of realizing the contradictions weren't flaws but features. The decision to build it anyway, to build *myself* into it as the component that would keep it unstable enough to function.

Then the memory evaporates, and I am walking the catwalks again, forgetting the path behind me, creating uncertainty for the Geometry-Keepers to resolve, for the Tenders to maintain, for the bronze creatures to navigate. I am the reason the Condensery can be both machine and organism, both bounded and infinite, both gathering moisture and gathering meaning.

I pass you on the catwalks sometimes. You don't see me—I'm in a state of not-quite-existing when we cross paths—but you feel the forgetting like a cool breath on your neck. For a moment, you can't remember why you're here. For a moment, the exits seem like they might be real.

But then I'm gone, and the certainty returns, and you remember that you're being collected too, that the dampness on your skin is already part of the system, that leaving and staying are just different ways of being gathered.

I am the Architect-Who-Forgets, and I am why the Condensery works. Not because I designed it—I've forgotten if I designed it—but because I keep it from remembering too clearly what it was designed to do. In that forgetting, it finds the space to become what it needs to be: a place that collects moisture and memories and meanings and the pauses between meanings, where bronze creatures tend to purposes that shift like condensation on glass, where everything is always almost about to make sense.

The engine hums its satisfaction with my work. Or its dissatisfaction. Or just its existence.

I've already forgotten which.
