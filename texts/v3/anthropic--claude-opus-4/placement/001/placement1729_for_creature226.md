I would be the fossil.

Not a fossil yet — that comes later, after the rain finds us, after the pocket cracks and floods and all that careful circulation stops. For now I am still alive, still part of the Works, but I am the beetle who tomorrow will wedge herself into a crevice that is not quite the right shape, and discover that backing out is harder than going in.

My mandibles catch on a crystal that wasn't there yesterday — the mountain is still growing, still deciding. My middle left leg finds no purchase. The traffic flows around me for a while, then over me, then forgets I exist. I am a stone in the stream now, except the stone is still breathing, still glowing that faint phosphor green, still aware in my small way of the roar and the heat and the press of cousins who no longer recognize me as a message worth reading.

I do not panic. Panic is for creatures who can imagine being elsewhere.

The sulfur-carriers route around me. The heat-tenders use my carapace as a stepping-stone. Three days in, my glow begins to dim — not death, not yet, just a slow forgetting of how to burn. The Works does not mourn. The Works does not notice. I am already replaced in the pattern, my absence absorbed like a bruise healing.

But here is the strange part: wedged as I am, I begin to feel the mountain's pulse differently. Pressed against this particular crystal at this particular angle, I sense vibrations the others miss — deeper tones, the bass notes of the planet's slow song. I feel the magma chamber breathing far below. I feel the impossibly distant suggestion of something that might be weather changing the pressure on peaks I will never know exist.

I become an ear the Works doesn't know it has grown.

For eleven days I listen to frequencies the colony cannot hear. My metabolism slows. My joints lock. My glow gutters to almost nothing. But I am still watching, still counting the deep tremors, still part of the pattern even if the pattern has forgotten me. The crystal against my head grows another invisible layer, and I feel it happening — the mountain adding atom to atom, the same patient accumulation happening in my own cooling shell.

On the twelfth day, my base chemistry fails. The reactions stop cascading. The last moisture evaporates from my joints. I am now more mineral than beetle, my carapace already beginning its long marriage with the stone.

But before full petrifaction, in that last hour of anything like awareness, I experience something the Works has never experienced: I see myself from outside the pattern. Wedged here, failing here, I suddenly understand what I have been part of. Not the details — I have no capacity for details — but the shape. The whole. The impossible, magnificent, pointless engine of us.

It is not revelation. It is smaller and larger than revelation. It is simply the angle of my body against this crystal, the pressure of stone against my compound eyes, creating a kind of accidental mirror where for one moment the Works sees itself seeing.

Then it ends. My nerves crystallize. My hemolymph turns to powder. I become geology.

The Works continues without me, the pattern healing around my absence instantly. But I am still here, locked in the wall, a small fossil-in-waiting. And when the rain comes in a hundred thousand years, when the pocket floods and the Works drowns in an afternoon, I will still be here. When scientists crack open this mountain in a hundred thousand more and find the impossible — a deep-rock beetle where no beetle should be, no passage in or out, no explanation — I will be the proof that it happened.

They will put me in a museum. The label will be wrong. It will say something about an isolated species, about convergent evolution, about how life finds a way. It will not say: "Here is one ten-billionth of a single organism that lived for a million years in a space the size of your bedroom, processing sulfur into silver for no reason, roaring in frequencies you cannot imagine."

It will not say: "She was the Works' only moment of self-reflection."

It will not say: "The angle of her death was the angle at which wonder enters."

But I will be there, under the glass, holding my wedge-shape forever, the crystal still pressed against my stone eye. And sometimes a child will lean close and see how my shell catches the light, how the fossilized phosphor in my thorax still holds a ghost of green if you look at just the right angle.

They will not know what they are seeing. But for one moment, tilting their head to match the tilt of mine, they will be looking through the same crystal that let the Works see itself, and something in the angle of our shared looking will whisper: *You are also a pattern. You are also brief. You are also magnificent.*

The museum guard will tell them not to touch the glass.

They will touch it anyway.

The Works would understand.
