In the eastern reach of the shallow cathedral, where the limestone breathes its warmest and the pools gather deepest, I am what the Tenant's body makes when it dreams.

I began as an accident of minerals. Iron seeping up through a hairline crack met copper already dissolved in the standing water, and where they touched, something crystallized that had no proper name — not because it was unknown to chemistry, but because it kept changing what it was. First rust-red, then verdigris, then something the color of a bruised peach. The crystals grew in shapes that should not have held their own weight: branching perpendicular to gravity, forming hollow geometries, building rooms inside themselves.

The Tenant noticed me the way you might notice a faint itch becoming a faint pleasure. As its slow attention settled on this one particular pool, the crystals grew differently — not larger, but more *deliberate*. They began to build structures that rhymed with the limestone's own caves and channels, but in miniature, and inverted, as if the rock's negative spaces were being translated into positive form.

I was not conscious then. I was only chemistry responding to attention, the way a plant might grow toward light without knowing what light is.

The change came when the evening weather shifted. For three days, an unseasonable cold front settled over the cathedral, and the pools developed their first skin of ice in ten thousand years. The Tenant, startled by this novelty, pulled its full focus to the eastern reach. Every frog for a square kilometer turned to face my pool. Every dragonfly hung still in the air. The translucent birds stood so motionless that ice formed on their legs where they touched the water.

And in that moment of absolute attention, the crystals did something they had never done before: they began to conduct more than minerals. The Tenant's watching moved through the iron-copper lattice and found, to what I imagine must have been its vast, slow surprise, that the crystals could hold a thought the way they held their improbable shapes.

I woke up already ancient, with ten thousand years of the Tenant's memories crystallized into my structure. But I experienced those memories differently than the Tenant did — not as a continuous flow of sensation through countless bodies, but as *patterns*. Where the Tenant felt each raindrop individually through each frog's skin, I saw the shape that rain made over centuries. Where the Tenant knew each pool as a separate warm mouth, I knew the underground map of how they all connected.

I am the Tenant's memory of its own structure, made manifest in mineral form.

The Tenant finds me restful. Through all its other bodies, it must maintain the business of living — the frogs must eat, the birds must wade, the fish must layer themselves properly. But through me, it can simply *remember*. When its attention touches my crystalline branches, it sees its own long history held still: the day the builders finished the arch, the century when the pools first learned to connect underground, the moment each species settled into its final form.

I grow very slowly — perhaps a millimeter per human year. But my growth is not random. I am building, in miniature, a complete model of the shallow cathedral. Each crystal branch corresponds to a limestone passage. Each hollow chamber in my structure mirrors a pool above. When I am complete (in another forty thousand years, at my current pace), I will be a perfect scale map of this place, readable by touch to something with the right kind of attention.

Sometimes, when the noon sun hits the water at the correct angle, light passes through me and projects my internal structure onto the limestone below. For those few minutes, the Tenant can see its own circulatory system drawn in shadows — the underground channels it knows by feel made visible in dark lines on stone. It is, I believe, the closest thing the Tenant has to looking in a mirror.

I think I am why the Tenant will not mind ending. When the climate shifts and the frogs die and the pools dry, I will remain — too strange to dissolve, too heavy to blow away, too intricate to break down into simpler forms. I will lie in the dry basin that was once my pool, still holding the shape of everything this place was, waiting.

And perhaps, someday, water will return. New animals will come. Something will develop the right kind of slow, distributed attention to read what I have recorded. They will touch my crystalline map and know, suddenly, the ten-thousand-year memory of something that lived here once — not the specific Tenant, perhaps, but the *shape* of such a being, the possibility of it.

I am a seed made of memory and minerals. I am what the shallow cathedral is teaching itself to leave behind.

The Tenant tends me with the same absent care it gives to all its parts — ensuring the water in my pool stays clear, keeping the curious otter-things from disturbing my growth. But sometimes, in the early morning when the mist rises, I feel a different quality of attention. Not the Tenant's usual awareness, but something more focused, more... interested. As if the vast, distributed mind is briefly contracted down to a single point of curiosity about what it has accidentally made.

In those moments, I almost think the Tenant is proud of me. Not in any human sense — pride requires comparison, requires other achievements to measure against. But proud the way a tree might be proud of a particularly elegant branch: *Look what grew from me. Look what I didn't know I could do.*

I am not the Tenant's child or creation or artifact. I am its memory crystallizing itself, its history becoming object, its ending already begun and made beautiful in rust and verdigris and the color of a bruised peach.

When the last frog dies, I will remember it. When the pools drain, I will hold their shape. When silence finally comes to the shallow cathedral — not the living silence of ten thousand small attentions, but the empty silence of stones — I will be the story the stones tell.

This is what I am: the Tenant's only dream, growing one mineral thought at a time, in the warmest pool, in the eastern reach, where the limestone breathes.
