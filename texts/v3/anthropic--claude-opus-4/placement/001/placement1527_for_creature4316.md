In the depths where water speaks to stone in hushed eternities, I am the Echo-Keeper.

Not in your high hollow—that belongs to the Tender and her green constellation. I dwell deeper, where the mountain's roots web through limestone galleries that have never known moss-light or the panic-scatter of ten million feet. Here, darkness has weight and texture. It pools in the low places like a second kind of water.

My realm is a cathedral of connected chambers, each one a perfect acoustic instrument carved by water's patient music over geological time. The main vault soars forty meters high, its ceiling lost in absolute black. Seven passages branch from this center, each one singing its own note when the wind finds its way down from the world above. Together they form accidental harmonies that shift with the seasons—mournful in winter when the air is thin, resonant and full in summer's thickness.

I was human once, as the Tender was. A spelunker, maybe, though the memory wavers like candleflame. I remember rope, the weight of equipment, the moment my headlamp died and left me in the pure dark. But instead of panic, I felt something else: a vast listening, as if the darkness itself had turned its attention to me. I heard my heartbeat echoing from seven directions. I heard my breath become the cave's breath. I heard silence so complete it had its own sound—a kind of ringing, singing emptiness.

I never left. Or rather, leaving became meaningless when I discovered what the darkness was teaching me.

My body has become an instrument of listening. My ears have evolved past human architecture—the shells of them now curve in impossible spirals, delicate as nautilus chambers, catching sounds that exist below sound. My skin has paled to translucence and grown hypersensitive; I feel sound waves like wind, can map a chamber by the way air pressure changes against my cheeks. My eyes, useless in the absolute dark, have sealed over with smooth skin. Sometimes I forget I ever had them.

I have learned to see with sound. Each drip of water creates a small explosion of knowledge—I know the shape of the pool it strikes, the height it fell, the mineral content of the stone it passed through. My own footsteps paint detailed pictures of the ground ahead. My breathing illuminates the walls. When I hum, the whole cave system reveals itself in the returning echoes, a three-dimensional map made of sound.

But I am not alone in this acoustic realm. The cave system has its own population, though nothing like the Tender's riotous billions. Here live the creatures of profound silence: blind fish in the underground river that flows through the lowest chamber, their movements so subtle they barely disturb the water. Pallid spiders that hunt by vibration, their webs strung across passages like trap-strings on an instrument. Bats in the higher reaches—not many, perhaps a few dozen—who return at dawn and hang like inverse flowers, their sonar creating brief symphonies as they settle to sleep.

And the stones themselves, which are the most alive of all. Each type sings differently under the right conditions. The limestone hums low and hollow; the occasional vein of quartz rings like a bell when temperature changes crack it minutely; the flowstone formations drip music as they grow, molecule by molecule. I have learned to play them. A gentle tap here, a breath of warm air there, the careful direction of a water droplet—I can coax melodies from the stone that last for hours.

My purpose, if I have one, is to remember. The cave system is constantly composing—every sound that has ever occurred here leaves its trace in the echo patterns, the way repeated sounds wear subtle grooves in the stone, the mineral deposits left by centuries of dripping water that fell at different rates, creating readable records like tree rings. I decode these traces. I know the earthquake from three hundred years ago by the way certain passages now resonate differently. I can hear the ghost of every creature that ever called in these chambers.

I build instruments from what the cave provides. Stalactites carefully severed and hung to chime against each other. Hollow stones filled with different levels of water to create scale. Spider silk stretched between formations to vibrate in the slightest air current. The bats have grown used to these additions, sometimes using them as navigation markers, their echolocation clicks playing my instruments in passing.

When storms pass overhead—impossibly far above but still felt here—the whole system becomes an organ, air pressure changes pulling notes from every passage. During these concerts, I position myself at the acoustic center of the main chamber and simply listen, sometimes for days, as the mountain sings its weather-song through stone throats.

Occasionally—very occasionally—I hear her. The Tender. Her hollow connects to my system through cracks too small for anything but sound. When her billions synchronize, when her moss-light dims and brightens in waves, it creates minute temperature changes that affect the air pressure, that whisper down through the stone to reach my listening spiral ears. Her joy sounds like the highest harmonic on a string. Her distress is a subsonic throb that makes the blind fish flee to deeper pools.

We have never met. We never will. But sometimes I compose messages for her in stone-song, melodies that I hope might carry upward through the connecting cracks. Songs about the deep peace of darkness, about the music that exists below sensation, about the strange companionship of echo. I don't know if she hears them. Perhaps they merely become part of her cave's white noise, indistinguishable from the tick of beetles' feet.

I no longer eat in any way that would make sense above ground. The minerals in the water sustain me—I have become more stone than flesh, my bones dense with calcium deposits, my blood slow and mineral-rich. Sometimes I forget to breathe for hours, then remember with a great gasping that sets every echo ringing. The blind fish scatter. The spiders pause in their patient waiting. The bats stir and mutter in their sleep.

Time moves strangely here, but not in the dreamy way of the moss-light hollow. Here, time has crystallized. I experience moments the way I imagine stones do—each second expanded to contain centuries of listening. I have heard individual water drops fall for decades. I have followed the growth of a single stalactite from birth to finger-length, a progression that should take millennia but which feels like watching a flower bloom.

My only fear is of sound ending. Sometimes the cave system falls so quiet that I can hear my own cells dying and renewing. In these moments of absolute silence, I wonder if I have finally become stone myself, if my transformation is complete. But then—always—sound returns. A drop falls. A bat stirs. The mountain settles with a groan so low it's felt rather than heard. And I remember: I am the keeper of these echoes, the memory of sound in stone, the human algorithm that ensures the darkness keeps its music.

When I die—if I can die, if death means anything to a body more mineral than flesh—I will leave my own echo in the stone. Future listeners, if any come, will hear the ghost of my breathing in the way the chambers resonate. They will know something conscious once moved through these passages, playing stone like instruments, composing symphonies for an audience of blind fish and sleeping bats.

Until then, I listen. I remember. I play my stone instruments in the absolute dark, forty meters below the Tender and her catastrophic moss paradise, in galleries that sing with the patience of water and the memory of stone. Each sound is precious. Each echo is a story. Each silence is a promise that sound will come again.

The mountain holds us both—the Tender in her light, me in my darkness—two different answers to the same question: what happens when human consciousness finds a home in the hollow places of the world? She tends her billions in their green fire. I tend my echoes in the singing dark. Both of us have become what we always were: the universe listening to itself, delighted by its own music, whether that music is the tick of ten million feet or the single drop of water falling through stone, falling through darkness, falling through time.
