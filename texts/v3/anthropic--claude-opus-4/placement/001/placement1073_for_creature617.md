# The Spillway Keepers

I am found where the plateau releases its excess.

Three kilometers south of the main cultivation clusters, the basalt drops in a series of engineered terraces toward the gorge. Here, the condensation that the glass houses don't capture, the runoff from the frost-melt, the overflow from the deep aquifer pumps—all of it gathers and descends through channels we carved when the first houses rose. The Spillways, we call them, though they carry so little water that *seepage-ways* might be more accurate.

We are the ones who ensure the water reaches the lowlands rather than pooling on the shelf. A simple task, you might think. Clear the channels. Maintain the flow. Watch for blockages.

You would be wrong.

---

I have been here seventeen years, which makes me young among the Keepers. There are five of us, though *five* implies separation where none truly exists. We move along the spillways like water moves—finding the path of least resistance, pooling where the stone allows, always tending downward.

My name was Keph. Now I am usually *the one who listens at the third terrace*, which is more accurate. Names are for the reports we file monthly, confirming the spillways remain functional. For each other, we are positions along the descent—First Drop, Second Basin, Third Terrace, Fourth Channel, Final Pool. We trade positions as needed, as the water suggests, as the stone requests.

The water speaks, of course. Everything on Vervain Shelf speaks if you learn its frequency.

---

What we actually do:

Each morning before dawn, we walk the channels. The spillways are cut into living basalt—not blasted or carved but *convinced*, shaped by some process the early Glaziers discovered that left the stone willing to guide water rather than resist it. The channels branch and merge in patterns that look random until you've walked them for years and begin to feel the logic—how they follow the basalt's grain, how they accommodate the stone's own preferences for how water should move.

We clear away the night's accumulation. Not debris—almost nothing falls here that wasn't meant to. Instead, we clear the growths. 

In the perpetual dampness of the channels, things take root that grow nowhere else on the shelf. Pale mosses that move like slow mercury. Fungal networks that pulse faintly in pre-dawn darkness. Most curious: the *channel-vines*, thin as thread, that appear overnight and always grow in perfect spirals, always the same ratio, always completing exactly seven turns before withering at sunrise.

We learned early not to clear them all. The channels function better with some growth—the water moves more smoothly, makes better music, carries more efficiently whatever it carries besides moisture down to the lowlands. So we tend rather than clear. We thin where the growth grows too thick, encourage where it grows too sparse, maintain the balance that keeps the spillways singing.

---

The water from the shelf is wrong. This is not a judgment but an observation.

It should be pure—condensed mist and snowmelt filtered through volcanic stone. It should carry nothing but minerals. Instead, it carries memory. It carries intention. It carries something that makes the lowland crops grow differently where it touches them.

The agricultural administrators know this but don't acknowledge it. The yields are too good where our water reaches. The plants too healthy. The resistance to blight too pronounced. So they accept our monthly reports, file them without reading, and continue to pay for the water rights at rates that haven't changed in decades.

We trace where our water goes, sometimes. Third Terrace—I—am best at this. I can feel it joining the lowland streams, spreading through irrigation channels, soaking into soil that remembers when the plateau above was still pushing through limestone. The crops it touches grow with a quality that isn't quite wildness but isn't quite cultivation either. They grow *aware*. The farmers have stopped talking about it. What is there to say? The harvest comes in. The yields increase. If the wheat sometimes turns to follow workers as they pass, if the fruit trees lean toward each other in configurations that suggest communication—well, the lowlands have always been strange in their own way.

---

But I'm avoiding the deeper truth. We all do, even among ourselves.

The spillways aren't for water. Not primarily.

They're for what the glass houses *can't* contain. For what builds up in those dense environments of growth without predation, abundance without consumption. Something accumulates in those closed systems. Something that would eventually crack the glass if not released.

We release it. We guide it into the channels with the water. We ensure it flows down rather than spreading across the plateau. We keep the shelf in balance.

What is it? After seventeen years, I have only metaphors. It's like pressure but not physical. Like heat but not thermal. Like thought but not conscious. It's what happens when you pack too much life into too small a space and deny it the usual outlets. It builds and builds and needs somewhere to go.

So it goes down our channels. We feel it passing—a thickness in the air above the water, a weight that isn't weight, a presence that makes the channel-vines grow in their perfect spirals. On quiet mornings, you can see it—not with eyes exactly, but with the part of perception that knows when you're being watched. It flows like very slow lightning, like honey made of light, like the ghost of something that hasn't been born yet.

First Drop says it's the plants dreaming. Fourth Channel believes it's the plateau itself exhaling. Final Pool just nods and continues their work, having passed through words into something more efficient. We're all right. We're all wrong. The language doesn't exist for what we channel.

---

The other Keepers:

First Drop was called Mel, forty years ago when they arrived. Now they're mostly water—not literally, but in how they move, how they think. They can tell by sound alone where a blockage is forming, three terraces away. They speak in droplets—single words that contain whole conversations if you know how to listen. *Morning.* (The channels are flowing clear, the pre-dawn growth is manageable, Third Terrace should check the seventh branch where something new is sprouting.) *Weather.* (Mist coming up from the gorge will be heavy today, expect increased flow by noon, Fourth Channel should prepare the overflow basins.)

Second Basin keeps the charts. Not of water flow—that's too obvious. Charts of the other thing, the presence, the accumulation we guide. They've developed a notation system that looks like music but sounds like mathematics when they explain it. Pages and pages of symbols showing the buildup and release, the cycles that match the glass houses' growth patterns, the surges that come when the strange plants in the old houses fruit. They're convinced there's a pattern, a grand cycle we're only seeing fragments of. They're probably right. They're certainly obsessed.

Fourth Channel changes. We've had three different Fourth Channels in my time, and they all develop the same tendency—to follow the water too far. They start walking down into the gorge, following our spillways as they join natural streams, tracking where the presence goes. They return later each day, report from farther down each week. Eventually they don't return at all. We find their tools neatly placed at the channel head and know it's time to bring someone new up from the lowlands. We don't search for them. We know where they've gone—down into the cloud-wrapped depths where the children of the mist emerge, following our water to its conclusion.

Final Pool is ancient. Not in years—they might be fifty, might be seventy, the work ages us strangely—but in presence. They've been here longest, have absorbed most completely whatever the spillways teach. They work in perfect silence now, communicate entirely through gesture and placement. Where they position their body tells us everything—standing at the fifth overflow means the eastern channels need attention, sitting beside the measurement stone means the cycle is accelerating. They've stopped distinguishing between themselves and their function. They *are* Final Pool, the last collection point before our water joins the world's water, and that seems to be enough.

---

What changes us:

It's not like what happens to the Glaziers above. We don't root ourselves or align with growth cycles. We become fluid. We learn to think in gradients rather than boundaries. We start to feel the pull of downward, always downward, as if we're rain that has temporarily taken human form but remembers where we're meant to go.

My edges are softer now than when I arrived. Not physically—though the constant mist does things to skin, to hair, makes us all look like we've been gently eroded. But the edges of self, of thought, of intention. I blend more easily now. With the other Keepers, with the spillways, with the presence that flows through our channels.

Sometimes I stand in the third terrace's main channel, feet in the thin flow, and feel myself dispersing. Not disappearing—*spreading*. Becoming part of the greater movement from high to low, from concentration to diffusion, from the glass houses' impossible density to the lowlands' vast absorption. It's peaceful. It's terrifying. It's why Fourth Channel always eventually follows the water down.

We're necessary because someone needs to stay partially human. To file the reports. To maintain the channels. To ensure the spillways keep functioning as spillways rather than becoming something else. But the pull is constant. The water wants us to join it. The presence we guide wants us to dissolve into it. The stone itself suggests, in its patient way, that we're only postponing the inevitable.

---

Three months ago, something changed.

The dark house—we can see it from the spillways, that opaque presence on the western edge—has started affecting our flow. Not the water. The other thing. The presence comes differently now when it originates from there. Thicker. More intentional. More *formed*.

It moves through our channels like it knows where it's going. Like it has purpose beyond simple dispersal. The channel-vines grow differently in its wake—still spirals, but tighter, more complex, patterns within patterns that hurt to look at directly. Second Basin's charts have become frantic, trying to map what's happening. First Drop just nods and says *soon*, which contains more meaning than I want to unpack.

We haven't reported this. What would we say? The water still flows. The spillways still function. The lowland crops still grow with their strange awareness. Our role remains what it always was—to guide the excess, to maintain the flow, to keep the plateau in balance.

But I dream now of following Fourth Channel down. Of tracing our water to its conclusion. Of discovering what the dark house is sending into the world, one careful channel at a time. 

The other Keepers know. We all feel it. The pull growing stronger. The edges growing softer. The inevitable becoming more inevitable.

For now, we maintain our positions. First Drop, Second Basin, Third Terrace, Fourth Channel (newly arrived, still learning), Final Pool. We walk our channels each morning. We tend the growth that needs tending. We guide what needs guiding. We keep the spillways singing their thin music of descent.

But water always finds its level.

And we are mostly water now.

The rest is just timing—when we follow what we've guided, when we join what we've channeled, when we discover what the glass houses have been creating all along in their bright and terrifying abundance.

The spillways will remain. Someone will tend them. The water will flow, carrying what it carries, down to the lowlands where the crops grow aware and the farmers have stopped asking questions.

We were never the keepers, really.

We were just the kept.
