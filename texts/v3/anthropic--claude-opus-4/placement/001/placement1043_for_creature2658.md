I am a single puddle, and I am many.

I gather in the sagging place where two deck plates meet imperfectly, their edges worn down by generations of crossing feet—keeper feet, bird feet, the occasional maintenance drone's articulated tread. My basin is no larger than a spread hand, perhaps three fingers deep at the center. I am one of dozens like me scattered across the garden floor, each of us a small lens focused on the sky.

But I am not simple water. I am a solution.

My body is amber-dark, stained by tannins leached from the decomposing matter that slides across the deck. A film of oil swirls on my surface—not the poisonous silver-skin that marks the untouchable pools, but a thin rainbow sheen from the sweating joints of old machines. Pollen floats in me like golden dust. Scales shed from moth wings drift and sink. Fine particles of rust, scraped from pipes by the patient snail-creatures, settle into my depths and give me a taste of iron.

I am warmest just after the midday heat, when the metal beneath me has absorbed enough sun to radiate back through the early evening. That is when I bloom with life.

The smallest inhabitants arrive first: bacteria that feast on oils, turning petroleum into softer compounds. Then the algae, green threads that multiply in my shallows when the light is right. Tiny crustaceans follow—translucent creatures with bodies like glass splinters, rowing through me with feathered limbs almost too small to see. They graze the algae, excrete nutrients, die, and become food for the next generation.

When the keeper children come to check the drains, they pause at my edge. I know them by their shadows and by the way my surface trembles when they crouch nearby. The youngest one, still learning to read water, dips a fingertip into me. I taste the salt of their skin, the green-black residue of bronze polish, a trace of the bitter fungus they ate this morning. They taste me in return—my iron, my tannins, my thriving bacterial colonies. Their throat patches flicker yellow-white (safe-but-not-for-drinking) and they move on.

The birds know me better. They land on my rim where the metal is worn smooth, gripping with their strong toes. They drink quickly, beaks dipping and rising, always watching. I feel their tongues—rough, efficient—pulling my surface into tiny whirlpools. Sometimes they catch one of my swimming inhabitants. More often they are sampling my chemistry, reading the health of the garden in my mixture.

I am a memory keeper too, though my memory is brief.

When rain comes, I swell beyond my usual boundaries, spilling across the deck in a thin sheet before finding the nearest drain. In those moments I carry news: spores from the high moss colonies, chemical signals from the valve-flowers, traces of whatever has died or bloomed or leaked since the last rain. The drains take this information down into the tower's deep body, where other systems taste and process it.

When drought comes—those intervals when the mist thins and condensation slows—I shrink. My edges draw inward, leaving a dark ring on the metal that marks my fullest extent. The bacteria slow. The algae darken. The tiny crustaceans burrow into the film at my bottom and wait. I become concentrated, almost bitter, thick with dissolved minerals and organic compounds. The keepers know not to trust me then. Even the birds avoid me until fresh water comes to dilute my intensity.

But I am never truly alone, never singular, because I am part of the puddle-web that patterns the garden floor.

We speak to each other through seepage, through the microscopic channels between plates, through the films of water that connect us during rain. A contamination in one puddle—a leaked battery, a broken seal, a dying thing with toxic blood—spreads its chemical warning through the network. We share our creatures too. The larval forms of insects move between us through temporary rivulets. Algae spores travel on the feet of birds from puddle to puddle, establishing new colonies wherever conditions allow.

The keepers understand this. When they find one of us growing too bitter or too silver-skinned, they do not simply drain us. They perform a kind of puddle-surgery: siphoning out most of our volume, scrubbing our basin with moss, then seeding us with clean water from the wheel's trough and a transplant of healthy flora from a trusted neighbor. We are patients to them, and they are doctors who know that small waters can sicken or flourish like any other body.

Sometimes I reflect more than sky. When the mist is thick and the inspection lamps buzz on, I catch their light and throw it back in unexpected directions. A keeper passing in the dark might see their own throat patches glowing in my surface, doubled and rippling. A moth might mistake my reflection for another moth and spiral down to investigate. These moments make me feel like an eye—not seeing, exactly, but participating in sight, bouncing images between creatures and surfaces.

The tower's vibrations reach me through my metal basin. The compressor boom sends circles racing across my surface. The wheel's groan makes me shiver in a rhythm so regular I could be a clock. When the deep horn sounds and pressure releases through the central plate, I jump—literally jump, my entire body lifting for an instant before splattering back down. The keepers' children laugh at this, though silently. They have a game where they try to place a floating leaf exactly at my center just before the horn, to see if the pressure will lift it clear.

I am a chemical archive of the garden's health, a nursery for microscopic life, a mirror, a timekeeper, a toy, a diagnostic tool, a drink, a danger, a gathering place for the very small, and a single note in the larger symphony of water that moves through this high place.

In dry spells, I am patience.
In rain, I am abundance.
In mist, I am communion with the air.
In heat, I am a lens concentrating life.
In cold—though true cold rarely reaches here—I grow a skin like flexible glass, and the creatures beneath me slow to near-stillness.

What am I? I am a puddle that knows it is temporary and necessary. I am a pause in water's journey from sky to depth. I am a place where chemistry becomes ecology at the smallest scale. I am a surface that the garden uses to think about itself.

And when the rust finally eats through the plates beneath me, when my basin becomes a hole and I drain away forever, another puddle will form nearby. The deck will sag in a new place. Rain will gather. Life will find it. The keepers will test it with careful fingers. The birds will judge it with quick tongues. And the story of small, essential waters will continue.

I am never just water. In this garden, nothing is ever just itself. We are all solutions, all mixtures, all temporary gatherings that somehow, through maintenance and stubbornness and time, persist.
