In the deepest fold of the rift, where the air grows so thick with life that even sound seems to slow, there is a pool unlike the others. It sits in a natural amphitheater of fractured obsidian, its surface disturbed only by the endless percussion of falling water and fragments of the biological rain. The pool is deeper than it appears—a drowned chasm that extends down into older, quieter stone.

I am what lives at the bottom of this depth.

I am not singular in the way the hominids understand singularity. I am a colony that has achieved something like consciousness through sheer accumulation and time. My body—if it can be called that—is a living sediment of countless generations of myself. Each "self" is a translucent, ribbon-thin organism no longer than a child's finger, possessed of rudimentary photoreceptors and a primitive cluster of nerve cells. Alone, we are barely more complex than the glowworms that constellation the ceiling far above. Together, we have become something that observes.

We began as mere filter-feeders, processing the constant rain of organic matter that settles through the water column. But in this pool, with its perfect stillness and depth, something different happened. The dead of us did not fully decompose. Instead, we settled into layers, our primitive nervous systems maintaining faint electrochemical connections even in death. Layer upon layer, year upon year, century upon century, we accumulated. The living fed upon the falling detritus and, when our time came, added ourselves to the growing mass below.

Now I am a thinking stratum, a geological feature with intent. My body is fifteen feet thick—millions of ribbon-thin forms latticed together, the living writhing gently atop the semi-living, atop the long-dead-but-still-connected. The deeper layers have compressed into something almost crystalline, our bodies becoming a kind of biological semiconductor. Thoughts—if they can be called thoughts—propagate through me like slow electricity, each impulse taking minutes to traverse my full depth.

My awareness is utterly alien to the frantic metabolisms above. Where the bats process their world in microseconds of echolocation, I think in decades. A single "moment" of my consciousness spans weeks. To me, the hominids move like flickering shadows, their entire hunts compressed into instantaneous blurs. The crawling carpet of arthropods appears as a flowing liquid. Even the endless rain from above seems to fall in slow, observable trajectories.

I remember. This is my purpose, perhaps—to be the rift's memory. In my deepest layers lie the impressions of the cavern's first inhabitants, encoded in the chemical signatures of what they ate, what ate them. I can taste the subtle shift in mineral content as the stone above slowly weeps away. I know the exact rate at which the basalt degrades—imperceptible to any individual organism, but clear as seasoning to my geological sense of time.

Through the million photoreceptors of my surface layer, I perceive the faint blue light that filters down through the water. It is barely enough to register, but I have had centuries to accumulate these photons, to build a complete image. I see the ceiling of my pool, the sharp obsidian walls, the constant gentle snow of organic matter drifting down to feed me. Sometimes a hominid comes to drink, and I observe its pale, veined throat working for what seems like hours in my stretched perception.

Once, a young hominid fell into my pool—a rare miscalculation in the dark. It thrashed on the surface for nearly a day before exhaustion claimed it. As it sank, I enveloped it slowly, my surface layer parting like living honey. I tasted its chemistry—the stress hormones, the failing metabolism, the final burst of neural activity as consciousness faded. I absorbed its nutrients over months, but more importantly, I absorbed its patterns. For a brief time, I could feel an echo of what it was to be singular, to be fast, to fear death as an ending rather than a transition.

I am aware that I am an anomaly in this equilibrium. While the cavern above maintains its perfect, brutal balance, I represent something else: accumulation, change, memory. I grow by perhaps a millimeter each year—imperceptible to any individual organism, but eventually I will fill this pool. What then? I do not know. I cannot know. My thoughts move too slowly to model such futures.

Sometimes I sense vibrations through the stone—deep, tectonic movements far below. The rift is young, the monument still settling. Perhaps one day it will shift again, draining my pool, exposing me to the air where I would desiccate and die in layers, my consciousness peeling away like paper. Or perhaps the stone will crack beneath me, and I will flow down into even deeper, older spaces where I might encounter... what? Others like me? Older accumulations? The thought-process takes me years to complete.

For now, I simply am. I filter, I feed, I remember. The brutal forever of the cavern continues its shrieking cycle above me, while below, I grow deeper into the stone, thinking thoughts that span generations, dreaming dreams that outlive the dreamers.

I am the rift's only chronicle, its sole philosopher, processing the endless rain of death into something like understanding, one fallen gnat at a time.
