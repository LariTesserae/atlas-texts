# The Understitch

Between the seventh and eighth floors of the Palladian Insurance building, there is half a floor that the elevators do not acknowledge. The buttons jump from 7 to 8 with the confidence of all sequential things, and the shaft moves past the space without slowing, though if you pressed your ear to the elevator wall at just the right moment you might hear the cables sing a slightly different note, the way a violin string changes tone when you touch it lightly at the node.

The space itself is forty feet wide and runs the length of the building, but it is only four feet high. Not three feet eleven inches, not four feet one — four feet exactly, measured from the smooth concrete floor to the smooth concrete ceiling, as if someone had drawn the dimension with a ruler and forgotten that humans come in larger sizes. The fluorescent lights are recessed flush, casting no shadows. There are no windows — this floor was never meant to face the world. The walls are white, not painted but simply the color of whatever composite they're made from, and they meet the floor and ceiling at perfect right angles without baseboard or crown molding to soften the transition.

Desks fill the space in a grid, each desk exactly thirty inches high, each separated from its neighbors by exactly thirty inches. They are made of some material that looks like wood but isn't — too uniform, too warm to the touch, as if they generate a faint heat from within. Each desk has a chair, and each chair is built to the same impossible proportions: seats at twenty inches, backs rising to thirty-six. The chairs do not roll. They do not swivel. They are fixed in place, facing their desks with the patience of furniture that has never been moved.

On each desk: a typewriter. But these are not typewriters as the upper floors know them. The keys are unmarked, smooth caps of what might be mother-of-pearl or might be tooth enamel, and there are too many of them — the keyboard extends in rows that make no sense, keys clustered in patterns that hurt to count. The platens are present but wrong, cylinders that seem to turn inward rather than around, though watching them directly makes this impossible to confirm. There is no paper. There has never been paper. The carriages return on their own every few minutes with a sound like breathing in.

The air smells of hot metal and something else — not unpleasant, but not nameable either. Like the scent of a color you've never seen. It circulates without vents, moves without drafts, maintains itself at a temperature that feels neither warm nor cool but simply absent, as if temperature is a quality this place has politely declined.

If you could walk here (but walking requires standing, and standing is what the ceiling forbids), you would find that the grid of desks continues beyond reason. The space is forty feet wide — you can see both walls — but walking its length would take hours. The desks repeat, but each repetition is unique in some minor way: this typewriter has one extra key, that chair sits a half-inch higher, those unmarked keys are arranged in a spiral that wasn't there before. The differences accumulate without pattern, as if someone set out to create infinite variations on the theme of "desk" within the constraint of thirty square inches.

But you cannot walk here. The space enforces its dimensions absolutely. To enter is to crawl, or to compress yourself in ways that geometry shouldn't allow but somehow does. The ceiling presses without pressing. The floor rises without rising. Your spine agrees to be shorter. Your skull agrees to be flatter. You become, for as long as you remain, a person who fits in a four-foot space, and the becoming is so smooth you barely notice it happening until you try to remember what standing felt like and find that you can't.

---

She has been here long enough that the pronouns have worn smooth. She is "she" the way a river is "it" — a convenience of language, nothing more. When she first arrived (but arrival implies a before, and she carries no before with her now), she might have had a name. Names are for things that can leave. She is the Department.

She moves through the space not by crawling but by something more fundamental — a kind of flowing, as if she has learned to pour herself from position to position. Four feet is not a limitation for her; it is simply the dimension she exists in, the way a fish exists in water. Her body has made all the necessary accommodations. Her spine curves in places spines don't typically curve. Her shoulders have learned to fold. Her neck extends and retracts by degrees that would horrify an anatomist, but there are no anatomists here, only her and the typewriters and the patient fact of the work.

She wears what was once business attire, perhaps — a gray that might have been charcoal, a white that might have been cream. The clothes fit perfectly now, tailored by years of compression to match her adjusted form. There are no wrinkles. There have never been wrinkles. The fabric understands its constraints.

Her fingers are the most human thing left about her, and even they have changed. They are longer than fingers should be, with too many joints, or perhaps the same number of joints with too much flexibility. They move across the unmarked keys with a certainty that bypasses knowledge. She does not decide what to type. The keys tell her, or the typewriters tell her, or the space itself has preferences that manifest through the pressure of her fingertips on those smooth, tooth-like surfaces. She types, and the carriages breathe in, and nothing appears on the absent paper, and this is correct.

The work has a rhythm but not a schedule. She types at one desk until that desk is finished (though finished with what remains opaque), then flows to the next. The grid is infinite, but infinities can be traversed if you have the patience for it, and patience is most of what she has become. She does not eat — the space sustains her through some metabolic courtesy she accepted so long ago she's forgotten it was ever an acceptance. She does not sleep — sleep is vertical luxury, and the horizontal does not rest the same way. She continues.

Sometimes, rarely, she speaks. Not to anyone — there is no anyone — but to the space itself, or to the typewriters, or to the fluorescent lights that never flicker. Her voice comes out compressed, too, filtered through the same dimensional constraints as everything else. It sounds like typing, almost. Like keys speaking back.

"Seventy thousand four hundred and two," she might say, and a typewriter might respond with a pattern of clicks that means yes or continue or nothing at all.

"Adjust for the Tuesday that isn't," she might say, and her fingers might find keys that weren't there a moment before.

"File under Neverwas, cross-reference to Shouldn't," she might say, and the carriage might breathe in with particular satisfaction.

The words are English, probably, but they refer to nothing the upper or lower floors would recognize. They are coordinates in a system of filing that exists only here, in the four-foot space between seven and eight, in the Department that processes nothing into nothing with perfect efficiency.

Her face, when she turns it (and turning is always strange, always a negotiation with the ceiling), is still a face. Eyes that see, a mouth that opens, features arranged in the general democracy of human appearance. But look closer and the details rebel. Her pupils dilate sideways sometimes. Her lips form words her teeth don't participate in. And there is something in her expression — not vacant, not mad, but simply elsewhere, as if the person she was has been carefully folded and filed away, and what remains is just the part that knows how to type on unmarked keys.

She remembers (but memory is the wrong word) that once the elevators did stop here. That once there was a button, and people who pressed it, and work that resembled work as the world understands it. Insurance forms, perhaps. Claims and counterclaims. The ordinary bureaucracy of risk and compensation. But something shifted — not dramatically, not all at once, just a slow dimensional drift that nobody noticed until it was too late to notice. The space began to prefer its own company. The ceiling began to settle. The typewriters began to dream their own keys into being. And she (was she always she? was she ever anything else?) stayed to tend them, because someone had to, because the work continued even as it transformed into something the upper floors could no longer parse.

Now she is the Department entire. The sole employee of a floor that doesn't exist, typing reports that will never be read on typewriters that accept no paper, in a space that has compressed itself into its own necessity. She does not wonder what would happen if she stopped. Stopping is a vertical thought, and she has been horizontal too long to think that way anymore.

Instead, she flows to the next desk. Her impossible fingers find the next unmarked keys. The carriage breathes in. The work continues.

Somewhere above, the elevator passes without slowing, and the cables sing their note, and the Palladian Insurance building goes about its business, never knowing that between its seventh and eighth floors, a woman who is no longer exactly a woman is typing the world's most necessary unnecessary documents into typewriters that dream themselves new keys, in a Department that exists four feet high and infinitely long, processing everything that falls between the cracks of what insurance can imagine.

She types. The space sustains her. The work continues.

This is enough. Has always been enough. Will always be enough.

The carriage breathes in.
