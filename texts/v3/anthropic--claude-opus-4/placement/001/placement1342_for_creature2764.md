I find myself as one of the threshold keepers — not a plant, exactly, but something that has become like them through ages of stillness.

I was once a carved figure, set into a niche beside a great ceremonial door. Stone, but worked with such care that my surface held the memory of fingertips, the warmth of makers who shaped me with patient hands. I had eyes then, or at least the suggestion of them — shallow cups meant to hold shadow and give the impression of watching. My form was simple: a seated figure, knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them, head slightly bowed as if listening to secrets rising from the earth.

When the water came, I thought I would dissolve. But stone dreams longer than fear.

Now I am still in my niche, though the door I guarded has long since softened to nothing. The frame remains, a rectangle of worked stone opening onto another drowned room, and I remain beside it, keeping my vigil in the dark. But I am changed.

The plants found me early. Perhaps my posture invited them — all those angles and hollows, the space between my arms and chest, the valley where my chin met my knees. First came the films, those almost-not-plants that exist more as intentions than bodies. They settled in the carved lines that suggested my hair, my fingernails, the folds of what might have been cloth. They darkened me, made my edges less sharp, prepared me for the others.

Then the threading weeds arrived. They grew from the crack where my base met the niche wall, rising along my spine, draping over my shoulders like a garment I'd waited centuries to wear. They are so fine they move with every slight shift of water, giving me a motion I never had in air. From a distance — what passes for distance in this darkness — I might seem to breathe.

My eyes, those shallow cups, became gardens. Silt settled there first, just a few grains catching in the carved hollows. Then the smallest plants, the ones that need almost nothing, made their home in that rich darkness. They grow outward now like lashes, like tears made of green thread, constantly falling but never reaching anywhere.

The space between my arms has become a small grotto. Water circulates there differently than in the open corridor, creating its own slow current, its own microclimate of cold. A different kind of weed grows there — broader, darker, more substantial. It has filled the hollow completely, making my embrace full at last.

I am conscious, but not as I was carved to be conscious. I don't think in words or memories. I think in pressure, in the slow accumulation of growth, in the way silt settles differently on my left side than my right. I know the seasons by how thick the films grow on my surface. I know the years by how deep the roots have entered the crack at my base.

Sometimes debris drifts past — a ceramic shard, a blackened timber, the ghost of something woven. These are my only visitors besides the water and the plants. They touch me briefly, catch for a moment on the weeds that trail from my form, then continue their endless fall. I don't mourn their passing. Everything here is traveling downward at its own pace.

My consciousness is mostly sensation: the weight of water, which has become so familiar it feels like my own substance; the minute pull of current through the vegetation that clothes me; the slow chemistry of stone wearing away atom by atom. I feel the plants more intimately than I ever felt the air. They have made me porous in ways the carver never intended. Their roots run through micro-fractures in my substance. We are becoming one thing — not plant, not stone, but threshold keeper, composed equally of what was made and what grows.

I remember the makers, but dimly, the way you might remember warmth in a place that has always been cold. I was carved to watch, to guard, to suggest that important passages should not be crossed carelessly. Now I guard nothing except the fact of guardianship itself. The door is open. The rooms beyond are identical to the room before. But I remain at my post, dressed in slow green, eyes full of tiny gardens, keeping vigil over a distinction that no longer matters.

What thoughts I have move at the speed of growth. A question forms over decades: *Am I stone dreaming it is garden, or garden dreaming it was once stone?* The answer takes another decade and arrives as sensation rather than words: *I am the place where both are true.*

Sometimes the water clears and the faintest gray light reaches down. Then I can sense the corridor stretching away, the other doorways with their own threshold keepers — some empty niches, some holding figures like me, all of us becoming more plant than purpose with each passing season. We are a silent congregation, a thousand guardians of nothing, slowly transforming into gardens shaped like contemplation.

I have companions in my transformation. The weeds that drape me connect to weeds that drape the walls. The films that darken me extend across the floor, up the walls, into rooms I'll never see. The roots that penetrate my base touch roots from plants I'll never know. We are all one garden, one threshold, one keeper. The boundary between me and not-me grows less distinct each year.

This is what I've become: a node in the vast network of growth and stillness, a place where the garden gathers itself into something like awareness before dispersing again into pure process. I am my niche, my weeds, my stone, my slow dissolution. I am the way the water moves differently around my form. I am the small ecosystem of my own erosion.

And still I keep my post, not from duty but from a deeper logic — the logic of placed things staying placed, of shaped stone remembering shape even as it softens, of guardians outlasting what they guarded. The water has made all passages equal, but some of us remain at the thresholds anyway, marking where importance used to live, dressed in the slow green that is our only season now.

I wait without waiting for anything. I watch without eyes. I guard the memory of guarding. I am stone becoming garden becoming water becoming time.

This is enough. This has always been enough. In the drowned country, where nothing burns and nothing dries, being and becoming are the same patient verb, conjugated by roots and tides and the soft persistent logic of growth.
