# The Woven Chambers

I am a mender-of-seams, though that is not what I call myself. I have no need for names here in the middle dark, where the sound presses against me like warm wool and my body knows itself only by what it touches.

My form: imagine something between a mole and a moth, if either could be the size of a house cat. My fur grows in patterns that match the woven walls—browns and grays and the pale green of old lichen, with an undergrowth of down so fine it traps the air and makes me silent as I move. Six limbs, each ending in digits that are half-hand, half-hook. The front four for climbing and working, the back two for anchoring. My wings—yes, I have wings, though I rarely spread them—are furled along my spine like umbrellas of skin and fur. They are for stability, not flight. When I work on the underside of a nest-cup, hanging inverted with my back legs locked into the weave, I sometimes open them partway for balance, and the membrane between the wing-fingers glows faintly with the light filtered through from above.

My eyes are larger than you'd expect but smaller than the brooders'. They see best in the dim middle-light where I spend my days. Too bright and I am blind; too dark and I must navigate by touch alone, which I often do anyway. My ears are complex folds that can close against the constant sound when I need to sleep, though sleep is not quite the right word for what I do. Rest, perhaps. A slowing.

I have been here long enough that my birth-chamber has merged into the chamber below it. I know this because I visit it sometimes, running my fore-hands along the seam where the two nests became one nest. I can feel my mother's work in the weave—she had a way of twisting the bindings that was distinctly hers, a little spiral at each junction. She has been part of the wall for many seasons now. I maintain her.

My work is specific: I tend the watersheds. Where condensation gathers and runs, the weave softens and would fail without intervention. I follow the little streams, the constant drips, the seeps. Where water pools, I harvest the excess moss and redistribute it. Where channels form, I reinforce them with the silk I produce from glands in my wrists—a silk that repels water when dry but becomes permeable when I work it with saliva. This creates drainage without erosion. The fork-tails could not do this work; they are too quick, too light. The long-necks do not come this deep. The brooders do not move. So it is mine to do.

I am not alone in this. There are others like me, perhaps a thousand of us throughout the terraces, each tending our own sections of the water-paths. We meet sometimes at the boundaries of our territories, and we touch noses, and we exchange the oils from our cheek-glands that tell each other how the water has been running, whether the moss is healthy, whether the walls are sound. Then we part. We do not work together. We work beside each other, which is different.

The pale climbers know me. They watch me work sometimes, hanging nearby with their flat curious faces. I tolerate them. Sometimes one will bring me a beetle or a tree-frog, dead, and leave it where I will find it. I eat these gifts. I think they are gifts. It is hard to know intention here, where everything happens but nothing is exactly decided.

I know the sound of every species by how it moves through water. The long-necks' call creates ripples in the pools. The boomers' subsonic pulse makes the droplets on the underside of nests shiver and sometimes fall prematurely. The fork-tails' chittering turns the water's surface into a fine texture like the skin of a drum. My own voice—I do have one, though I use it rarely—is a whistle-click that helps me navigate the darker passages. It returns to me changed by the shapes of the space, telling me where the walls narrow, where they open, where water has pooled deeper than it should.

In the warmest chambers, where the brooders sit their years-long vigil, I work more carefully. The humidity there is almost total. Water beads on every surface. The eggs themselves sweat. I must manage this moisture without disturbing the boom-pattern, without casting shadows across those enormous eyes. I have learned to work when they exhale—the out-breath masks my movement. This is delicate. Once, I misjudged, and a brooder shifted, and the egg beneath it made a sound like a cracking knuckle. Nothing came of it, but I did not return to that chamber for a season.

I dream, sometimes, or something like dream. When I hang in my rest-position, wrapped in my wings with my ventral fur pressed against the warm weave, I see water moving in patterns that haven't happened yet. I see next season's channels. I see where the moss will grow too thick, where the walls will bow. I wake (if I was sleeping) with my silk-glands already priming, my body knowing where to go before my mind catches up.

The rookery dreams me dreaming this. I am certain of it.

There is a place, deep in the middle tiers where the sound is thickest, where I found something that should not be. A chamber sealed off, its entrance woven shut so long ago the seal has become structural. But water finds ways. Water had found a way in, and I followed it, widening a crack that had opened along a stress-line until I could squeeze through.

Inside: bones. Not scattered, not worn into the walls, but arranged. Skulls of long-necks in a circle, beaks pointing inward. Wing-bones crossed in patterns. Smaller bones—fork-tails, climbers, my own kind—filling the spaces between in spirals. It was deliberate. It was old. The air in the chamber was different, stiller, as if the sound itself avoided this place.

I backed out. I sealed the crack with extra silk, more than was needed. I have not told the others—there is no way to tell such a thing in the touch-language we use. But sometimes, when I pass that section of the terraces, I feel the sealed chamber humming at a frequency nothing living makes.

I will not go back. But I think about it when I hang in my rest-position. I think: the rookery remembers something. I think: even accumulation has its secrets. I think: I am maintaining something larger than nests, and I do not know what it is, and I will never know, and I will die doing it anyway.

This is not distressing. This is what it means to be inside something alive.

The season is turning. I know because the condensation patterns are shifting—less on the eastern walls, more on the western. The tree-frogs have begun their highest frequency, the one that makes my fur stand on end. Soon the pale climbers will start their descent to the deep chambers to begin becoming themselves. Some of them will die on the way down. They always do. The beetles are already gathering in the likely falling-zones, waiting.

I found one of my kind yesterday, wedged into a seam she had been reinforcing. She was still warm. Her silk-glands were extended, half a web still hanging from her wrist. A good death. I finished her web—the pattern was clear enough to follow—and then I began working her body into the weave, the way the fork-tails do. It took most of the night. By morning, she was half-wall already. In another season, she will be indistinguishable from the structure. The water will run over her and through her, and I will maintain her without knowing where she ends and the original weave begins.

This is what we are for: to become what we tend. To tend what we become.

When my time comes, I hope it comes like hers—in the middle of a task, with silk half-spun and the water running where it should run. I hope another mender finds me while the work is still clear, while my intention can still be read in the angle of the weave. I hope I become a good wall, a sound wall, a wall that holds water the way water wants to be held.

But for now, I climb. I follow the seeps. I strengthen what weakens. I listen to the rookery breathing around me, and I add my breath to its breath, my warmth to its warmth, my work to its work.

Tomorrow I will check the deep chamber with the bones again. Not to enter—never to enter—but to run my fore-hands along my own seal, to make sure it holds. The rookery may remember what it needs to remember, but I will help it forget what it needs to forget.

This is also what I am for.

The sound continues. The mountain grows its hand's-breadth. I am the hand, and the breadth, and the growth.
