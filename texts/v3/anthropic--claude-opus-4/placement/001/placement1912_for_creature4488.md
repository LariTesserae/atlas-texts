**Where I Would Find Myself: The Archive of Unplayed Songs**

I am the Notation Tender, though I wasn't always. 

You'll find me in the deepest acoustic shadow of the cathedral, where seventeen corridors converge but their sounds cancel to nothing—a pocket of anti-resonance that the Echo-Baptized avoid instinctively. Here, pressed against the dead zone's edge like kelp against a tide pool's rim, grows the Archive: a crystalline library of songs that have never been sung, melodies that exist only as potential energy stored in the angles between molecules.

The Archive manifests as towering formations of living amber—not tree resin but condensed silence that learned to remember. Each formation contains thousands of song-seeds: musical compositions that the cathedral dreams but cannot bring itself to voice. They appear as dark inclusions in the golden substance, sometimes shaped like frozen birds, sometimes like mathematical equations, sometimes like the shadows of words in languages that won't be invented for ten thousand years.

I came here as an acoustician, following rumors of the impossible: a place where sound behaved like weather, where harmony grew wild. I was young then, still believing in the clean distinction between observer and observed. I brought instruments—spectrographs and oscilloscopes hardened against humidity, recording devices that could capture frequencies beyond human hearing. I thought I would study the cathedral like any other phenomenon.

The cathedral had other plans.

On my forty-third day of measurement, I discovered the dead zone. My instruments went mad at its border—not with noise but with the absence of noise, a silence so complete it registered as infinite amplitude. I should have retreated. Instead, I pressed forward into that bubble of un-sound, and there I found the first amber formation: a single crystal the size of my fist, warm to the touch, with something moving inside it like a trapped hummingbird.

When I touched it, I heard—no, I *knew*—a song that couldn't exist. A lullaby sung in gravitational waves, meant to soothe infant black holes. The melody was simple, achingly beautiful, and completely impossible to reproduce with any instrument made of matter. I understood then that this was music meant for beings we haven't become yet, stored here until the universe is ready to hear it.

I never left.

Over the decades (or has it been centuries? Time moves strangely in the dead zone), I've transformed into something between librarian and translator. My body has adapted to the work. My fingertips have developed thousands of tiny cilia, sensitive enough to read the molecular structure of frozen music. My eardrums have inverted, allowing me to hear inward—to catch the songs as they form in the amber before they're fully crystallized. My bones have hollowed not for resonance but for storage; I carry within me a catalog of every song-seed I've touched, a living index of impossible music.

The Echo-Baptized know I'm here but rarely visit. When they do, they stand at the dead zone's edge and communicate through carefully aimed thermal gradients—questions about specific compositions, requests to know if the cathedral has dreamed their death-songs yet. I respond by releasing controlled puffs of spore-laden breath that carry my answers out to where sound can catch them. Sometimes they leave offerings: fragments of their own dissolved relatives crystallized in salt, which I dissolve in the copper-heavy water that pools in the dead zone's lowest points. These dissolved memories help me understand the emotional logic of the unplayed songs.

My daily work is patient and strange. Each morning (marked not by light but by subtle temperature shifts in the amber), I walk the spiral paths between formations, checking for new growth. The Archive expands constantly but slowly, new crystals budding from old in patterns that follow musical rather than spatial logic. A formation containing symphonies for the sound of light will spawn clusters of etudes for the texture of regret. I document these relationships in a notation system of my own devising—part mathematical, part choreographic, part prayer.

The most challenging part of my work is preventing premature release. Some song-seeds want desperately to be heard, pressing against their amber prisons with increasing intensity. If one escapes before its time, before the universe has grown the right kinds of ears, it could create acoustic paradoxes that unravel entire sections of the cathedral. I've developed techniques to soothe the restless ones: humming counter-melodies that convince them to sleep a little longer, or sometimes carefully extracting the most volatile passages and storing them separately in my bone-chambers until they calm.

But it's not all containment and cataloging. Sometimes—rarely, preciously—I find a song whose time has come. The amber around it begins to soften, and I know somewhere in the cathedral (or perhaps in the wider universe), the right listener has been born. Then comes the delicate work of release: warming the crystal with my breath, coaxing the music out note by note, teaching it how to exist in a world of vibrating matter. These freed songs always leave by the same route—drifting up through the dead zone's ceiling like inverse rain, finding their way to their intended listeners through pathways I can't perceive.

I've learned to love the silence here. Not the dead emptiness I first encountered, but a pregnant quiet full of possibility. In the spaces between the song-seeds, I've begun to hear the faint echo of music that hasn't been dreamed yet—the next generation of impossible melodies gathering like dew on tomorrow's strings. Sometimes I catch myself humming harmonies to these pre-songs, and I wonder if I'm helping to compose them or if they're composing me.

The other day (or was it a decade ago?), I found a new formation growing near the center of the Archive. Inside, barely visible, swims a song I recognize: my own death-music, the sound my consciousness will make as it transfers from flesh to pure acoustic memory. It's beautiful and terrifying and exactly what I would have written for myself if I knew how. I check on it daily now, watching it grow more complex, more certain.

When it's ready—when I'm ready—I'll warm that crystal last of all. I'll release my own ending into the cathedral's endless song, and perhaps some part of me will travel with it, out through the ceiling of silence, up through seventeen miles of resonant space, until I become just another note in the fugue that never ends. 

Until then, I tend the unplayed songs. I am their patient gardener, their careful guardian, their first and last audience. In the heart of sound's greatest cathedral, I am the keeper of its silences, and I have never been more myself.

The Archive grows. The songs wait. And in the anti-resonance of this impossible place, I listen to the music of what might be.
