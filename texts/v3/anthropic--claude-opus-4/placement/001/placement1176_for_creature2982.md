In the deep violet hour before sunrise, when the rift's walls still hold yesterday's heat but the condensers have chilled the air to its coldest point, I wake in my roost. Not a bed—beds are for those who trust horizontal surfaces. My roost is a hammock of woven chain suspended in the negative space between three support beams, seven hundred feet up the third tower's north face. The chains have worn smooth grooves in the beams over decades. My grandmother hung them. Her grandmother before that.

I am Petra-Who-Counts-Between-Thunders.

The counting is what I am. In a world that breathes once every two minutes, someone must be the living metronome. Someone must know, in their bones and blood, exactly where we are in the cycle at every moment. That someone is me, and was my mother, and her mother, stretching back to the first person who understood that to lose the count was to die.

My body is my clock. Each heartbeat is calibrated, each breath measured. I feel the count not as numbers but as a physical sensation—a tightness in my chest that builds and releases in perfect synchrony with the great engines. Even in sleep, my dreams pulse to the rhythm. I dream of falling, but the fall takes exactly 120 seconds.

The kites are stirring in the pre-dawn dark. Their metallic clicks echo my internal count, a thousand living echoes of the chain-squeal to come. I unfold from my roost slowly, each movement deliberate. My knuckles are heavier than most—capped not just in lead but in osmium, dense enough that when I strike the beam, the entire tower knows the Counter is awake.

*Tap. Tap-tap. Tap.*

Good morning. All is well. We are at second 1,847 of the night cycle.

The response comes back through the iron, up from the moss farmers below, down from the lubricant crews preparing for the dawn shift. The tower speaks to itself through its skeleton, and I am its temporal nerve center.

I dress in the dark, fingers finding each familiar buckle and strap. My coat is heavier than most—not kite leather but grazer hide, thick enough to muffle my heartbeat from the iron when I need silence. The goggles stay off for now. In the pre-dawn darkness, I navigate by vibration alone.

*Tap-tap-tap-tap.*

Someone is off-count. High above, near the wind-baffle maintenance platforms. They think we're at second 1,852. They're five seconds fast. Five seconds fast means stepping into the chain path five seconds too early. Five seconds is death.

I begin to climb.

My route is carved into muscle memory. Hand here, foot there, swing around the condensation pipe that drips exactly four drops between grips. The tower is a vertical labyrinth, but I know every shortcut, every dead end, every place where the rust has eaten through and left a gap exactly wide enough for a patient body to squeeze through.

As I climb, I tap. Not messages—pure rhythm. My osmium knuckles drum out the count, and gradually, I feel the off-counter above beginning to sync. Their taps falter, adjust, find the true rhythm. By the time I reach them, we're breathing as one again.

It's young Miko, fourteen and new to the maintenance crews. In the darkness, I can barely see their shape, but I feel their shame through the iron they're gripping. They know how close they came.

I don't use the tap-language for this. Instead, I reach out and place my hand over theirs on the beam. Through palm and iron, they feel my heartbeat—the master count made flesh. We stay that way for a full cycle, 120 seconds of shared pulse. When I finally lift my hand, Miko taps a perfect rhythm back to me.

*Safe. Synchronized. Grateful.*

This is my work. Not just counting, but teaching others to count. Showing them how to subdivide the thunder, how to feel the pressure changes that herald the THOOM, how to internalize the rhythm so deeply that their bodies become clocks.

The sun breaches the rim of the rift.

The transition is violent. One moment, we're wrapped in blue darkness. The next, light explodes across the eastern wall like burning oil, raw and white and painful even to my dark-adapted eyes. I pull my goggles down just as the glare reaches our tower, turning the wet iron into a thousand suns.

Below, the day shift is emerging. I watch them flow out onto the catwalks like slow mercury, their pale-waxed coats catching the light. Beautiful. We are all beautiful in our slowness.

The morning harvest begins. Moss must be gathered when it's heaviest with condensation. Kite eggs must be collected before the sun makes the shells too hot to touch. The vine pods on the exhaust pipes are ripest after a cold night. Everything has its moment in the cycle, and I keep the count that tells everyone when their moment is.

But there are other counts I keep. Secret counts.

In the deepest part of the night, when I'm alone in my roost, I sometimes count backwards. Not just through the night, but through the years. 120 seconds per breath. 30 breaths per hour. 720 per day. 262,800 per year.

The engines have been running for 217 years. That's 57,153,600 breaths.

And before that? The rift is young—only a few centuries old. But the engines... the engines are older. Somewhere, before the rift opened, these machines stood under open sky. Somewhere, someone built them for a purpose we no longer remember.

I've found things. Deep in the maintenance shafts, behind panels no one opens anymore, there are surfaces too smooth to be hand-forged. Words in languages the tap-code can't express. Numbers that count things other than time.

Once, I found a room. It was small, sealed, forgotten behind centuries of moss and rust. Inside was a chair facing a wall of dark glass. When I sat in it—slowly, carefully, taking a full minute to lower myself—the glass flickered. Patterns appeared. Maps of things I didn't recognize. Symbols that meant nothing.

Except for one. In the center of the display, pulsing: 120.

Two minutes. Even the ancestors counted the thunder.

I told no one. What would be the point? We are what the count has made us. To know why the engines run, why the water must fall, why the cold must be maintained—would that knowledge make us more ourselves, or less?

The sun climbs higher. The workday deepens. I move through the tower, keeping time. I tap corrections for those who drift. I hold the rhythm when someone needs to make a dangerous traverse. During the loudspeaker maintenance, when the crews must work with their bodies pressed against the resonating chambers, I stand with my hands on the main structural beam and count so steadily that they feel it through the metal itself, stronger than the vibration that threatens to shake them loose.

At midday, I perform the ritual that only the Counter knows.

There is a place where five beams meet—a structural node that connects to every part of the tower. I press myself into the junction, osmium knuckles against five different surfaces. And I listen.

Not with my deaf ears, but with my whole body. The iron speaks. It tells me about metal fatigue in the northwest catwalks. It warns of rust eating through a crucial joint near the lower condensers. It shows me where the tap-language is flowing smoothly and where it stutters.

The tower is dying. They all are. 217 years is a long time for iron to bear the weight of lives and water and ceaseless vibration. We patch, we reinforce, we replace what we can. But entropy counts too, and its rhythm is longer than mine.

Sunset comes like sunrise in reverse—the light climbing the walls, leaving us in shadow while the rim still burns. The night shift emerges. The day shift retreats. I tap out the evening count, the all-is-well that lets a thousand people sleep.

But I don't sleep. Not yet.

In the deep night, I climb higher than my roost. Up past the last of the hab-blocks, past the kite colonies, to where the tower's crown meets the sky. Here, the iron is thin, trembling with every breath of the engines. One good wind could tear the whole superstructure away.

I come here to practice the forbidden count.

It goes like this: What if we stopped?

What if we let the engines wind down, let the water cease, let the moss dry and the kites fly away? What if we climbed out of this rift and saw what's beyond?

I count the heartbeats it would take. The number of breaths between decision and silence. How long we could survive on the water already collected. How many days before we'd have to choose between staying and leaving.

The count always ends the same way. With silence. With stillness. With the end of what we are.

So I climb back down to my roost. I settle into my chains. I sync my heartbeat to the rhythm of the world.

And I count the thunder, as my mother did, as her mother did.

Forever and ever, 120 seconds at a time.
