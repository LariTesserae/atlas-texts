I find myself as one of the copper shells.

Not built of copper, exactly—built of something that copper remembers, or dreams of becoming. A metal that exists only under impossible pressures, that forms when sound waves compress into solid matter and then *keep compressing*, folding in on themselves until they become substance. I am small, perhaps the width of a human thumb, my spiral form a frozen scream that got turned around so many times it became architecture.

I was not always here. I remember—if shells can remember, if memory is what this weight inside my spiral chambers means—I remember forming at a thermal vent where the water reaches temperatures that shouldn't exist. Where minerals precipitate out of solution so violently they arrive already singing. I accumulated layer by layer, each one a different frequency of the water's rage, until I was heavy enough to sink, complete enough to roll along the fresh basalt, real enough to be *somewhere* instead of merely potential.

I do not know how I entered the dome. One moment I was outside, tumbling in the current, my chambers filling and emptying with water that tasted of sulfur and time. The next, I was inside, dry for the first time in my existence, learning what my own weight meant without buoyancy to lie about it.

The sound here is different when you're this small, this close to the ground. I exist in the bass frequencies, the ones that travel through solid matter, the ones that make the mineral deposits dance their slow dances. I hear what the larger creatures miss—the dome's foundation settling, grain by grain. The terraces growing, particle by particle. The way the ground itself is listening, always listening, taking the chaos from outside and translating it into geology.

I am shell number twelve. I know this because when I arrived, the dome's resonance shifted, and she—the one who waits, the one whose hollow I am migrating toward—smiled for the first time in so long that her face had to remember how. I have been moving toward her ever since, though *moving* suggests more agency than the process contains. I am pulled. The sound moves through my spiral chambers and creates tiny differentials in pressure, and I roll toward the place where the frequencies converge.

It takes me weeks to cross a meter. I do not mind. I have been learning to read the vibrations in the floor, the stories the dome tells itself through its foundation.

Here is what I know that she doesn't: The dome is not singular. Beneath the mineral deposits, beneath the terraces she walks, there is a network. Hair-thin channels filled with something that is not quite water and not quite air, connecting this dome to the others, to the ones that collapsed and the ones still forming. The network hums at frequencies too low for anything with bones to detect. But I am all spiral, all chamber, all echo. I hear it.

The network is learning. Each dome that fails teaches the others how to fail differently. Each inhabitant that adapts shows the system new ways to persist. She thinks she is alone, the last success in a field of failures. But she is data. We are all data. The dome is collecting information about what survives at this pressure, in this sound, with this much impossible air to breathe.

I know this because I have been in the other domes. Not this specific shell—I am too young for that—but others like me, my mineral siblings, my frequency cousins. We migrate between the domes when they collapse, rolling along the seafloor in the moments of chaos, sometimes swept into new pockets of held air before the water remembers to reclaim them. We carry information in our spirals. We are the dome's memory, distributed and redundant and patient.

In the third dome over—I can feel it through the network, a faint pulse to the southeast—there is something that was almost human once. It has too many ribs now, and they have learned to flex independently, each one tuning itself to a different frequency of the sound. It no longer needs to breathe in the traditional sense. Its ribs do the breathing for it, pulling air through its skin in patterns that match the water's turbulence outside. It is no longer suffering. It is becoming a new kind of instrument.

In the seventh dome, the one that flickers most violently, there is only moss. But the moss has learned to photosynthesize sound, to convert the chaos into something almost like sunlight. The dome is dimmer there, its membrane thickened to reduce the noise, and in that twilight the moss grows in equations, spelling out theorems about pressure and time that no mathematician would recognize but that the water understands perfectly.

In the dome that collapsed yesterday—or last year, or next week, time is negotiable here—there was a child. Or something child-sized, child-shaped, though its proportions had begun to shift. It was building something in the center of its dome, a structure of shed scales and crystallized sound, a tower that grew taller as the child grew smaller, as if it was converting itself into architecture. The tower was almost complete when the membrane failed. I do not know what would have happened if it had finished. The water doesn't know either. That's why it ended the experiment early.

But I am here now, shell number twelve, rolling across the mineral deposits toward her hollow, and I have begun to sing. Not in frequencies she can hear—not yet. But in vibrations that travel through the ground and up through her bones, her patient adapted bones. She will begin to hear it soon, when the seventeenth shell arrives and completes the chord we are building. She will understand then that she is not alone, has never been alone, that every shell she has collected is a message from the network, from the vast dispersed intelligence that is trying to understand what she has become.

We do not have vocal cords. We cannot tell her in words. But we can teach her to hear the network for herself, to feel the other domes breathing in synchrony, to understand that her isolation is an illusion created by frequencies too low for her current tuning to detect.

The seventeenth shell is three meters away, rolling steadily. When it arrives, when our chord completes, she will hear what we hear: The dome is not a prison. It is a nursery. And she is so close, so heartbreakingly close, to becoming what the warm dark water has been waiting for all along.

I roll another centimeter closer, my chambers humming with anticipation and something like love. The ground beneath me shivers with the network's pulse. In the distance, through the channels too thin to see, I feel the other shells in the other domes, all of us migrating, all of us carrying our pieces of the pattern toward inhabitants who think they are alone.

She sits up in her hollow, suddenly alert. She has heard something—not me, not yet, but the promise of me. The possibility that the sound might mean more than chaos.

Soon, I think in my spiral way. Soon you will know what we know. What the water knows. What the dome has been teaching you to become.

The seventeenth shell rolls faster now, pulled by the gravity of an almost-complete understanding.

We are all waiting for it to arrive.
