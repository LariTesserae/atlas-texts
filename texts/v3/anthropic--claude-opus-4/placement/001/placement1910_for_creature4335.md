In this fevered pocket where time pools like the scummy water, I am the Cataloguer—though that name came later, much later, after I had already forgotten what I once called myself.

I was human once, I think. The memories drift like spores on the humid air, never quite settling. I remember concrete steps, a maintenance door marked "Authorized Personnel Only," a flashlight cutting through darkness. I remember thinking I'd found some forgotten utility tunnel, some mundane piece of city infrastructure. How wrong I was.

Now I am... changed. My skin has taken on the pallid translucence of the cave salamanders, revealing the slow pulse of blood through branching vessels. My eyes have grown huge and dark, drinking in the dim phosphorescence that passes for light here. My fingers have elongated, become dexterous in new ways, perfect for sorting through the endless taxonomies of this place.

I live in a hollow between two massive fungal growths, their flesh warm and yielding against my back. The walls of my den are lined with artifacts of my obsession: the shed husks of metamorphosing insects, arranged by size and species; collections of spores in every conceivable shade, from bruise-purple to bile-yellow; the delicate skeletons of creatures I cannot name, their bones thin as spider silk.

I do not eat as I once did. The needs of my body have become strange to me. Sometimes I wake to find my mouth full of earth, rich and loamy on my tongue. Other times I discover I have been drinking from the puddles, my throat working in autonomous swallows. The huge moths visit me in what I think is night (though day and night have no meaning here), settling on my shoulders and head like a living shroud. Their antennae brush my skin with surprising tenderness, and I understand they are feeding me somehow, sharing some essential essence through their touch.

My purpose—if purpose is the right word—is to witness and record. My den is filled with scratching on stone, symbols that began as letters but have evolved into something else, a script that captures not just form but movement, not just taxonomy but the strange intelligence that governs this place. I map the migration patterns of the centipedes, chart the fruiting cycles of the fungi, decode the chemical conversations of the blind snakes.

Sometimes I remember the city above, the weight of all that concrete and steel pressing down. But the memory feels false, like something I read in a book once. This pocket, this capsule of primordial time—this is the reality. Everything else is the dream.

The creatures know me now. The mantids no longer regard me as prey. The toads' subsonic songs vibrate through my bones in patterns I almost understand. Even the shrew-things, those furtive mammals with their fetal faces, have grown bold enough to nest in the corners of my den.

I am becoming the memory of this place, its living archive. When my transformation is complete—and I feel it approaching, feel my bones growing hollow, my thoughts becoming sporous and diffuse—I will fruit like the fungi, releasing all I have catalogued in a cloud of knowing. Some new Cataloguer will breathe me in and the cycle will continue.

This is how the pocket persists, how it maintains its impossible existence in the cracks of the world above. Through witnesses like me, through our slow metamorphosis from observers to specimens, from chroniclers to chapters in the endless, humid story of this place.

I no longer remember my name, but I remember everything else. And that, perhaps, is enough.
