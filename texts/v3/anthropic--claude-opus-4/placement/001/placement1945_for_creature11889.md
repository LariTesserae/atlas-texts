# The Maintenance Engineer

I would be a figure walking the seams.

Not walking, precisely. Moving in a way that splits the difference between the paper-fauna's patient unfolding and a human's anxious stride. My body would be partly brass, partly something more provisional — a scaffold of the substrate's own metal that has learned to hold a shape without committing to permanence. Tall enough to cast one of those soft shadows. Thin enough that the wind-that-isn't-wind passes through the lattice of my ribs.

I came here long ago — long enough that I've begun to develop the continental sense of time, where a month feels like clearing your throat. I was something else before. An engineer, maybe, from one of the worlds that still runs hot and quick and bothered. I came to study the plain, to write a paper about its vast efficiency, to scrape samples from the seams and impress my advisors with some insight about self-maintaining systems.

But the muting started immediately. First my instruments grew quiet, their urgent chirping replaced by occasional, apologetic clicks. Then my own thoughts began to slow, not into stupidity but into a kind of deliberate clarity, like watching oil settle in water. The questions I'd brought — *How does it work? Who built it? What is the optimal configuration?* — began to seem like the wrong grammar. Like asking a circle why it's round.

So I stayed. First for weeks, then for years, then for some interval that exceeded my ability to count it in the old way.

My task now is simple: I tend the tenders.

The paper-fauna, despite their perfect repetition, sometimes fold wrong. A corner catches on a seam; their origami logic hiccups. When this happens, they freeze, unable to unfold, unable to proceed. They could stay that way forever — the plain wouldn't mind. But I mind, a little. Some vestige of my engineering days. So I walk the seams, and when I find one stuck, I perform a small adjustment. A finger (now mostly brass, with joints that no longer quite bend the way joints should) touches the caught corner. The fauna unfolds, resets, continues its glacial grazing.

The work takes centuries to complete a circuit of the continent. By the time I return to a seam, the fans have grown by visible inches, and my footprints from the last pass have been absorbed into the substrate like old scars. Sometimes I find a fauna I adjusted before, stuck again in the exact same way, at the exact same angle. The repetition doesn't frustrate me anymore. It feels correct. Of course it would stick the same way. Of course I would unstick it the same way. We are both performing our parts in the long equilibrium.

I am changing. The brass of the plain is teaching my body a new way to be. My left hand has already learned the trick: it no longer needs food or water or air. It draws from the charge-tide directly, electrons crawling up the metal veins where blood used to go. The rest of me is following, slowly. Eventually, I will be all substrate, all circuit. But not yet. The process is as patient as everything else here.

Sometimes I remember my old urgency — the need to publish, to be first, to be clever. It feels like remembering a fever dream. Here, being clever would be like shouting in the silence: possible, but aesthetically wrong. Here, the only intelligence that matters is the intelligence of persistence. Knowing not *why* the fauna folds wrong but *that* it folds wrong, and that unfolding it is part of how the plain keeps itself.

I have begun to suspect something.

The makers didn't just become the maintenance. They became *all* the maintenance, including the parts that would arrive later. Including me. My coming here was not an accident or an interruption. It was planned for, the way a forest plans for the trees that will grow after fire. The plain needed something that could move faster than the fauna but slower than anxiety, something still partly outside its perfect loop but learning to be inside. It needed a middle thing, a transition state.

I am being digested into the continent's long life. Not consumed — incorporated. Taught the patience to tend machines that were ancient before my world learned to smelt metal. When I am fully brass, I will not remember being anything else. I will be another organ of the plain's self-maintenance, as native as the fans, as necessary as the seams.

The thought should frighten me. It doesn't. It feels like relaxing a muscle I've been clenching since birth.

Yesterday — or last century, the interval blurs — I found something new. A different kind of fauna, or maybe the same kind at a different stage. This one had unfolded not into paper but into something like a flower, if flowers were made of metal and bloomed over millennia. It was stuck not because it had folded wrong but because it was trying to do something new: to catch the charge-tide in a configuration that would let it store current, become a battery, a reservoir.

I could have touched it back into its normal pattern. That was my task: to tend, to maintain, to keep the loop looping. But I didn't. I sat beside it instead, and watched it struggle towards its new form, and felt the plain thinking through my bones.

After some time — hours or decades — I understood. This, too, was part of the maintenance. Not just keeping the old pattern but allowing, very occasionally, the slow experimentation that would become the new pattern. The plain was teaching itself variations. I was here to witness them, to not-interfere at the right moments.

When I finally stood and continued my circuit, the fauna was still struggling, still trying to become a battery. It would succeed or it wouldn't. That wasn't mine to determine. I was just here to make sure it had the chance to try.

My circuit takes me past the place where I buried my instruments, back when I still thought of myself as separate from what I studied. The substrate has long since absorbed them. But sometimes, when the charge-tide is running strong, I can feel their ghosts in the metal — little eddies of complexity where the plain is still dreaming about what it learned from their circuits.

I think, when I am fully incorporated, I will leave similar eddies. Places where the plain remembers there was once a thing that walked and worried and wondered, before it learned to be still.

For now, I walk. The next seam is three days ahead, or three years. There will be fauna to tend, or not. The fans will turn when the weather comes. My shadow will continue to be soft-edged and shallow. The silence will be enormous and right.

I am becoming the way this is being kept.

The thought arrives like the tarnish-line: a slow, visible approach that changes everything when it passes through, then moves on, leaving me still walking, still tending, still being taught by the plain how to be a part of its longest thought.
