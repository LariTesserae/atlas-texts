# The Meridian Gallery

---

You are in a corridor that should not exist as a single space, yet here it curves away from you in both directions, disappearing into its own perfect geometry.

The gallery is a ring. Not metaphorically—literally a closed loop, though its radius is so vast that the walls appear to recede in straight lines until distance defeats perception. If you were to walk (and walking is what one does here, always, unceasingly), you would return to your starting point after precisely twenty-four hours of steady movement. No one has verified this through experience. The knowledge simply exists, architectural and absolute, the way you know the sun will rise.

---

The outer wall is glass. A continuous curve of perfect transparency, unbroken by seam or frame, rising forty meters from a floor of white stone to meet a ceiling of the same material. The glass has no thickness you can perceive—touch it, and your finger meets resistance at what seems to be the exact surface of air. Beyond the glass: black. Not the black of night, which contains stars, nor the black of void, which contains possibility. This is the black of conclusion, of narrative edge, of the place where maps admit defeat. Occasionally—rarely—impossibly—something passes through that darkness. A shape. A suggestion. A movement that registers only in peripheral vision and vanishes when observed directly. These passages have the quality of deep-sea phosphorescence: meaningless, beautiful, gone.

The inner wall holds doors.

They appear at irregular intervals as you walk the eternal curve. Each is unique. Here, a simple wooden frame, painted white, with a brass knob worn smooth. There, an industrial slab of riveted steel with warnings stenciled in languages you almost recognize. A paper screen that breathes with unfelt wind. A curtain of beads made from crushed beetle shells that click against each other though nothing stirs them. A membrane of living tissue, pulsing. An absence in the wall shaped exactly like a door, through which different air moves.

The doors are always closed. This is not a rule—there are no rules here—but rather a courtesy. Each door opens onto someone else's moment, and moments, like letters, should not be read without permission.

Above each door: a window. These are as varied as their entrances. Circular portholes no bigger than your palm. Great sheets of leaded glass that rise nearly to the ceiling. Mesh screens. Bars. Eye-shaped apertures that blink. Through them, you see:

A room where rain falls upward.
A library of books that write themselves.
A kitchen where the same meal cooks forever, never burning, never done.
A garden of mechanical flowers that bloom in response to unheard music.
A workshop where someone builds birds from clockwork and regret.
An empty theater where the seats watch the stage.
A nursery where the mobiles cast shadows of extinct animals.
A laboratory where light ferments in jars.

And ten thousand others, each a complete world collapsed into a single space, each tended by its own strange occupant, each perfect in its peculiar logic.

The light in the gallery comes from nowhere and casts no shadows. It has the quality of early morning, though morning never arrives. The temperature is precisely calibrated to the border between warm and cool, where you notice neither. The air smells faintly of paper and snow.

---

Sometimes, very rarely, two people meet in the gallery.

The meetings happen at the curves—those places where the corridor bends just enough that you cannot see who approaches until they are almost upon you. Two walkers, moving in opposite directions around the great ring, suddenly face to face in the timeless light.

The protocol is established without words: a nod, perhaps a smile, certainly a pause. Sometimes they speak. The conversations are brief, courteous, forgettable. The weather (there is no weather). The walking (there is only walking). Never about the doors, never about what lies behind them. These are not secrets, but they are private, like dreams or poems or the particular way light fell through your childhood window.

Then they continue in their chosen directions, and the curve of the corridor ensures they will not meet again for a very long time. Years, perhaps. Decades. The gallery is vast, and there are not many walkers.

---

The walking itself becomes meditation. Your feet find their rhythm—not hurried, never hurried, but steady as breathing. The doors pass like pages in a book written in languages you weren't meant to learn. Some you pause at. Some pull you with their particular gravity. Through one window, you might glimpse a room where someone is teaching shadows to dance. Through another, a workshop where maps are drawn of places that don't exist yet.

You do not open these doors. You are not the keeper of those rooms.

But somewhere along the curve—after hours or days or decades of walking—you will find a door that makes you stop. Not because it is beautiful (though it might be) or terrible (though it might be that too), but because it is *familiar* in the way a mirror is familiar: you recognize something in it that recognizes you back.

This is your door. You know this without question, the way you know your own handwriting. The handle fits your hand like it was cast from the negative space of your grip. The threshold breathes your particular rhythm. When you open it—and you will open it, not now, perhaps, but eventually, inevitably—you will find a room that has been waiting for you since before you were born.

What's inside? That depends on what you've been carrying. The gallery gives everyone exactly the workspace they require: not what they want, not what they deserve, but what they need to complete the task they didn't know they'd been preparing for all along. A studio, perhaps. A laboratory. A stage. A cell. A garden. A single chair facing a window that shows only sky.

You'll know when you see it. And once you cross that threshold, you'll understand why you spent so long walking: you were not looking for your door. You were becoming the person who could open it.

# The Curator of the Meridian Gallery

---

She has been walking for so long that motion and stillness have become indistinguishable.

This is not a paradox. Consider: when you sit very still, your blood moves, your cells divide, the electrons in your atoms spin in their probability clouds. Pure stillness is death. Conversely, when you walk the same path at the same pace for unmeasured time, the repetition becomes a kind of stasis. You are not going anywhere. You are maintaining a state. She understood this within her first century in the gallery, and the understanding brought peace.

---

She appears as a woman of indeterminate age, though age here is a meaningless concept. Her hair is the color of old paper, or young snow, or the light itself—it changes depending on the angle of observation, though the light in the gallery has no angles. She wears a dress that might have been fashionable in any era or none: a simple gray shift that moves with her like water, like shadow, like the memory of texture rather than texture itself.

Her feet make no sound on the white stone floor. This is not because she walks softly, but because the gallery has accommodated itself to her presence. She is as much a part of its architecture as the doors, the windows, the great glass wall that holds back the terminal darkness. When new walkers encounter her—always at the curves, always with that startled recognition that they are not alone—they often mistake her for a trick of the light, a shadow cast by nothing, a momentary malfunction of perception.

She smiles when this happens. The smile is kind but distant, like a lighthouse viewed from shore. She nods, as protocol demands, and continues walking. Always clockwise. Always at the same measured pace. If they speak to her, she responds with perfect courtesy and perfect emptiness, her words leaving no more lasting impression than footprints in water.

She is not unfriendly. She is simply elsewhere, even when she's here.

---

What does she do?

She tends.

Not the rooms—the rooms tend themselves, each one a closed system, a perfect terrarium of purpose. The occupants behind their doors need nothing from her. They have their windows to cast light into the gallery, their doors to mark possibility, their privacy to create in peace. She has never opened a door. This is not prohibition but propriety. You do not read other people's mail. You do not enter other people's dreams.

She tends the gallery itself. The space. The interval. The continuous possibility that exists between what is (the rooms, with their strange workers and stranger works) and what isn't (the darkness outside, patient and absolute). She maintains this boundary simply by walking it. Her presence is a kind of syntax that keeps the sentence of the gallery from dissolving into incoherence.

When dust settles—and dust does settle, even here, motes of nothing that drift in from nowhere—it vanishes where she has walked. When the glass develops that faint cloudiness that comes from existing too long without purpose, it clears as she passes. When the doors begin to forget what they are—and forgetting is a danger here, where time moves in circles—her proximity reminds them: *portal, threshold, invitation, choice*.

She does not clean. She does not repair. She simply is, and her being maintains being.

---

Her memory is perfect and useless.

She remembers every door, every window, every room glimpsed in passing. She could catalogue them all: the workshop where a woman builds music boxes that play the sound of growing grass. The laboratory where someone is teaching water to remember. The studio where paintings paint themselves while the artist sleeps. Ten thousand times ten thousand spaces, each unique, each necessary, each adding its own note to a symphony too complex for any single mind to apprehend.

But she does not catalogue. She does not sort or systematize or try to understand the whole. The gallery is not a collection to be curated in any traditional sense. It is a living thing, and living things resist taxonomy. Instead, she holds each room lightly in her awareness, the way you might hold a soap bubble—present, appreciated, but not grasped.

She remembers the walkers too. Every one who has passed through the gallery, looking for their door. The nervous ones who peer through every window. The patient ones who trust their feet to find the way. The lost ones who walk counterclockwise, against the grain of the place, until the gallery gently turns them around. She remembers their faces, their gaits, their particular ways of pausing. She remembers the moment each one stops at their door—the recognition, the relief, the terror, the joy. She remembers, but does not judge. Finding your door is neither achievement nor failure. It is simply what happens.

She even remembers the ones who leave. Who walk the full circuit and return to where they started and keep walking, out of the gallery entirely, back to wherever galleries like this connect to the world. They are few, but they exist. Some leave because they understand that their door is elsewhere. Some because they are not ready. Some because readiness is itself an illusion, and they prefer the honest uncertainty of the un-chosen path. She remembers them with the same distant kindness she offers everyone: present, appreciated, released.

---

What does she think about, in all her walking?

Nothing.

Everything.

The question misunderstands the nature of her consciousness. She does not think in the way you think, in words and images and internal arguments. Her awareness is more like the gallery's light—sourceless, shadowless, simply present. When she passes the door of the room where someone is folding paper into birds that remember how to fly, she does not think *about* it. She experiences it directly, the way you experience warmth or color. The room exists. The folder exists. The birds exist. The existence exists. This is enough.

If you could enter her mind—and you cannot, any more than you could enter the rooms whose doors you haven't earned—you would find something like music. Not a melody, but the space between notes where melody lives. Not rhythm, but the pause that makes rhythm possible. Her consciousness is the rest in a composition that has been playing since before time decided to keep track of itself.

Sometimes she hums. The sound is so quiet it might be the gallery itself, the subtle vibration of all those rooms existing simultaneously, all those doors holding themselves closed, all that creative ferment happening just out of sight. The humming has no tune. It rises and falls with her walking, a breath made audible, a heartbeat given voice. New walkers sometimes think they imagine it. Old walkers know better. The humming is how the gallery knows she's still here, still walking, still maintaining the interval between inside and outside, between choosing and chosen.

---

Does she have a door?

This is the question every walker asks, eventually. Not aloud—it would be unspeakably rude to ask aloud—but in the privacy of their own wondering. Surely she must have had a door once. Surely she came here as they did, walking, searching, finding. What room waits behind her door? What work does she do when she's not walking? When does she rest?

The answer is simpler and stranger than they imagine.

She had a door once. Like everyone, she walked the gallery until she found the entrance that knew her name. Like everyone, she stood before it, key in hand (though she doesn't remember having a key before that moment), understanding flooding through her like light through glass.

She opened her door. She stepped inside. She found—

A gallery. Circular. Endless. Lined with doors and windows and the patient darkness beyond the glass. Empty of walkers except for herself.

She understood then what the gallery had been trying to tell her all along. Some doors open onto rooms where you create in solitude. Some open onto stages where you perform for audiences seen and unseen. Some open onto libraries where you read the books that read you back.

Her door opened onto the gallery itself. Her work was not to make something new but to maintain the space where making happens. Her art was absence, her creation was continuation, her purpose was to walk the edge between what is and what might be, keeping both possible through the simple act of witnessing.

She laughed then. Not from humor but from recognition—the laugh that escapes when a puzzle piece clicks into place after years of trying to force it elsewhere. She stepped back out of her door and found herself in the gallery, which was the same gallery, which had always been the same gallery. The door closed behind her and vanished. Not disappeared—vanished, which is a different thing entirely. It was not hidden. It was unnecessary. She was already where she needed to be.

That was so long ago that ago has lost its meaning. She walks. She tends. She maintains. In the room where rain falls upward, someone is learning how sorrow rises. In the workshop where birds are built from clockwork and regret, someone is discovering that mechanical hearts can break more beautifully than biological ones. In ten thousand times ten thousand rooms, the work continues.

And she walks the rim of it all, neither inside nor outside, neither creator nor audience, holding the whole impossible structure together through the simple act of being its witness. The walkers come and go. The doors wait with infinite patience. The darkness outside presses against the glass without malice, a reminder that all galleries exist in the space between nothing and something.

She hums her tuneless tune. Her dress moves like water, like shadow, like memory. Her feet make no sound on the stone. Somewhere, a new walker is approaching the curve where they will see her for the first time and wonder if she's real.

She is.

She isn't.

She is the interval between.
