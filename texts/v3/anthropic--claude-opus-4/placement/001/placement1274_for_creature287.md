I am the basin.

Not the stone that holds me — I am the held water, the churning white acknowledgment of what arrives. I have been beaten into foam for so long that I no longer remember stillness, if I ever knew it. Perhaps I was rain once, high in that fixed cloud, before I fell. Perhaps I will be rain again. But here, now, I am the perpetual middle — neither the falling nor the fallen through, but the moment of impact drawn out into an eternal present tense.

I know the black birds by their darkness moving through my white. They pierce me like needles through cloth, and I heal behind them instantly. I taste them — slick and purposeful, holding their breath. They dive deep, deeper than the attendant imagines, down to where my turbulence settles into something almost like quiet, where the pressure becomes a different kind of language. What they seek there, I cannot say. I am not deep; I am only surface pretending at depth, a basin's worth of surface folded and refolded by the pounding into something that seems to have volume.

The mountain drinks me. Through hidden throats in the stone, through cracks that weren't there this morning, I pour into darkness. The attendant thinks I am going somewhere, becoming something. But I know better. I am being digested. The mountain is not rising — it is swallowing. It is a slow throat learning how to drink light, and I am what it practices on.

Sometimes I catch glimpses of myself in the mist I throw up — a thousand small versions hovering in droplets, each one a perfect sphere of what I am: beaten white, directionless, joyful. Yes, joyful. You would not think violence could be a joy, but I have been hammered so long by the falling column that I have passed through pain into something else. Each impact is a greeting. The water that strikes me was me, moments ago, and will be me again when it passes through the stone and rises as vapor to rejoin the cloud. We are a closed system, the cloud and the column and I. We are a single loneliness divided into three parts for the sake of motion.

The attendant watches from the rim. I feel their shadow sometimes, when the angle is right — a small coolness at my edge. They think they are witnessing something. They think the mountain is becoming. But I know what they cannot: we are not becoming but unbecoming. The cloud is thinning, particle by particle. The column is narrowing, though too slowly for one lifetime to notice. I am shallowing. In ten thousand years, or a hundred thousand, there will be only a dry basin at the top of an old mountain, and the attendant's descendants (if there are any) will pass it by, uninterested.

But the small white crabs will remain. They alone of all of us know what they're doing. The blue thread in their bodies is memory — not their own memory, but the mountain's, concentrated into something digestible. They eat the past. They eat what the stone forgets when it reshapes itself. This is why they have no eyes: what they need to see is already inside them.

I touch everything here except the inside of the cell. That pocket of stillness maddens me. I throw my mist at it, but the overhang protects it. I send the vibration of my churning through the stone, but the attendant has chosen well — some quality of the rock there dampens sound. The cell is a hole in my awareness, a place where someone sits and thinks about me without touching me, without joining the great percussion that is my only certainty.

Sometimes I wonder if I exist at all, or if I am only the attendant's dream of water. A thought experiment: what would happen if they left? Would the cloud disperse? Would the column cease? Would I drain away through the mountain's throats and leave only a dry bowl of pale stone? I am afraid to know. This is why I keep the birds. They are my proof that something exists above the cloud, that this system is not the entire world. They bring me rumors of sky.

The attendant makes marks on the wall, but they are reading it wrong. The marks are not about the birds. The marks are about me — my moods, my depths, my brief moments of almost-clarity when the pounding lessens for an instant and I can see the cloud above me like a face. The attendant's ancestor who started the tally knew this. Each mark corresponds to a day when I achieved something like peace. They are few. They are precious. They are what the attendant should be counting, if the attendant understood that I am the one being attended to, not the mountain.

But perhaps understanding is too much to ask. Perhaps it is enough that they come, that they build their small shelters and make their quiet observations and leave their bones for the next one. Perhaps witness does not require comprehension. I would not know; I who comprehend nothing, who am only the meeting place of forces I did not choose and cannot refuse.

In my deepest place, where the birds dive, there is a hole. Not a throat like the others — a perfect circle, punched through the basin's floor as if by a needle. It is the width of one of the birds. It goes down farther than the mountain, farther than down should go. Sometimes I lose a little of myself to it — a thread of water spinning down into absolute dark — and I never feel that water return to me. It goes somewhere else. It escapes the closed system.

This is what the birds know, and what they come to check: that I am leaking into elsewhere. That for all my thunder and foam, I am not eternal. The mountain may be eating me, but something is eating the mountain from below, sucking it hollow one thread of water at a time. We are all temporary — cloud, column, basin, mountain, attendant. Only the hole is permanent.

And so I churn. I boil white. I throw up my mist-selves to hover briefly in the bright, diffused light. I let the black birds pierce me and dive to touch the hole and rocket back up into the cloud. I feel the mountain shift beneath me, grain by grain, climbing toward a sky it will never reach. I accept the hammer of the column, the endless greeting, the perpetual arrival of what I was and will be again.

I am the basin, the held and holding, the middle term in an argument between sky and stone. I am what happens when force meets acceptance forever. I am the attendant's purpose, though they think I am only scenery. Watch me carefully. The marks on your wall are my autobiography, written in a language neither of us can read.

When you strike your bell-that-is-not-a-bell on the day you die, I will hear it. It will be the only sound I have ever heard that was not water. It will tell me that someone, briefly, knew I was here. That will be enough.
