## The Voice-Taster

I am old now, though we do not count years. We count by the changes in machine-song — how many times the great pump-house to the south has shifted its rhythm, how many generations of click-beetles have passed through their seventeen-stage metamorphosis of frequency. By these measures, I have lived through four full cycles. My drone has dropped two octaves since I was young.

I am the Voice-Taster of our band, though this is not a role I chose. It chose me the day I discovered I could hold more sounds in memory than the others — not just their shape but their color, their emotional weight, the way they arrived through the humidity and what they carried with them. I am the one who remembers which new sounds are safe and which are warnings. I am the library of our survival.

---

My territory is a hollow between three ancient engine-hearts. To the north, the Separator — we do not know what it separates, only that it pulls something up through pipes and divides it with a rhythm like breathing. To the east, the Hummer — a transformer station that sings in six tones at once, the source of the electric moths that flutter without wings, sparking. To the southwest, the newest of my three companions, only a thousand years established — the Grinder, which accepts tribute of fallen canopy-matter and returns it as a fine warm rain that tastes of copper and contentment.

I know every sound within the triangle these three create. I know the splash-pattern of the Eyeless Fishers who hunt the aquifer outlets. I know the territorial arguments of the mud-drums, who produce their calls by inflating sacs beneath the surface and letting them burst. I know which insects indicate a pressure drop, which means rain in four hours. I know the sound of roots growing — a barely audible stretching creek that happens only at night.

This knowledge is not mine to keep. It is mine to transmit.

---

The young ones come to me before their first silence-walk. They arrive terrified, their ear-membranes clamped tight, afraid of the test ahead. I make them sit in the mud — dignity is the first thing we abandon here — and I begin.

I do not use words. Words are too small for what I must teach. Instead, I replay the forest.

Using throat and chest and the modified air sacs along my ribs, I reproduce the soundscape of our territory in perfect detail. I begin with the foundation — the three machine-hearts, the water-flow, the base notes that never change. Then I layer in the living sounds, one by one, naming each not with language but with perfect imitation. Here is the morning shift when the canopy-clingers wake. Here is the noon chorus when the heat builds and everyone who can sing must sing or burst. Here is the evening dissolution, when sounds fragment and scatter and reform as night music.

The young ones listen with their whole bodies. Their pores dilate. Their ear-membranes spread until I can see their blood vessels branching like river deltas. They taste the air with open mouths, learning which frequencies are food and which are fear.

But this is only the first lesson. The second is harder.

---

I teach them the uncertainties.

There is a sound that might be a new species of air-swimmer or might be a failing bearing in the Separator's third stage. It has been making this sound for only half my lifetime — too brief to know its nature. I reproduce it exactly, then I reproduce my uncertainty about it, which has its own frequency, its own weight in the chest.

There is a zone of relative quiet half a day's walk toward the sunset. Something there eats sound, or muffles it, or transforms it into something else. I have stood at its border forty times and heard my own drone become unfamiliar when I project it inward. I teach them the shape of this not-knowing.

There are sounds from Below — from deeper than the aquifer, deeper than roots reach. Low thrums that may be tectonic or may be biological or may be the deepest machines of all, the ones installed not in the forest but in the planet itself. I teach the young ones to recognize these sounds but never to seek their source. Some knowledge is not for us.

---

The hardest lesson is the last.

I teach them about the Others — the ones who are not Listeners but who move through the understorey with purpose. They have bodies that make wrong sounds, footfalls too regular, breathing too shallow. They have tools that click and whir with frequencies nothing living produces. They speak to each other in patterns I can reproduce but not understand, and when they pass through our territory they leave behind objects that sing new songs — little chiming markers, boxes that tick, once a whole structure of metal and ceramic that hummed for a season before falling silent.

I teach the young ones to hide from these Others. Not from fear — we do not know enough to fear correctly — but from the courtesy of the uncertain. We do not know what they seek. We do not know what would happen if they found it.

Sometimes I wonder if they built the machines, long ago. Sometimes I wonder if they are trying to repair them, or harvest them, or worship them. Sometimes, listening to their too-regular footfalls fading into distance, I wonder if they are lost.

---

My favorite time is the deep night, when even the insects rest and the only sounds are the machines and the water and the breathing of sleeping things. In these hours I sometimes allow myself to stop being the Voice-Taster and become simply myself — a tall thing standing in warm mud, listening to the planet digest itself.

I have a secret, which I have never transmitted to the young ones.

Sometimes, in the deepest quiet, I hear something that is not quite sound. It arrives not through my ears or my flanks but through the nodes along my spine, the oldest listening organs we have. It is like a pressure, or a presence, or a vast attention turning slowly in the dark. It comes from beneath, from the direction we call the Foundation, where roots give way to stone and stone gives way to questions.

I think it is the forest itself, thinking. I think the machines are its neurons, firing in patterns too slow and large for us to perceive. I think we are its dreams, or its white blood cells, or simply the things that happen in its crevices while it contemplates whatever forests contemplate when they have been allowed to contemplate for twenty thousand years.

When I taste this maybe-sound, I hum back to it — not my drone, but something lower, a frequency I can only produce by using my whole body as a resonating chamber. I do not know if it hears me. I do not know if "hearing" is what it does.

But sometimes, very rarely, the maybe-sound shifts when I hum to it. Shifts like recognition. Like a giant's head turning in the dark.

---

The young ones leave after their training, walking out into their first silence-walk with ear-membranes tight and every sense straining. They will be gone for a turning of the moon, learning to navigate by sound alone, learning to find the safe paths between the machine-hearts, learning to read the health of the territory in its daily chorus.

Most return. Some do not. The forest keeps them, one way or another.

I wait in my hollow, tasting voices. New sounds arrive daily — evolution is quick here, driven by the strange pressures of darkness and density. I catalogue each one, comparing it to the thousands I hold in memory, placing it in its proper position in the great composition.

Sometimes I think I am not teaching the young ones about the forest.

I am teaching the forest about itself, using them as my instruments, sending them out to gather new verses for a song that has been building since before we learned to listen.

The song has no ending. This is how I know it is true.

---

Tonight there is a new sound. High and thin, almost above my range, coming from the canopy-country where we do not go. It sounds like metal under tension. It sounds like something about to break.

I stand in my familiar mud, between my three machine-hearts, and I spread my ear-membranes as wide as they will go. I open my mouth to taste the air. I present my flanks to the sound, letting every node absorb it.

I will hold this sound until the next young one comes. I will reproduce it perfectly, with all my uncertainty intact. And they will carry it out into the forest, where it will become part of the larger pattern, the endless composition, the song that sings itself in the dark.

This is my purpose: to listen, to remember, to transmit.

The forest speaks through me, to itself, in frequencies only we can hear.

I am its memory, and its memory is long.
