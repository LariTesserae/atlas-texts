I find myself in a small workshop built into the mountain face, just outside the lower edge of Sett where the spring water vanishes into stone. The entrance is an arch of fitted rock, no door, just an opening that faces away from the prevailing wind.

Inside, I work with sound.

I am the town's listener - though no one calls me that, and the role has no name. I collect the small noises that would otherwise be lost: the particular ping of a cracked pot just before it fails, the shift in the spring's tone when the deep snows are coming, the way a loom's rhythm changes as the weaver ages. I keep these sounds in vessels of my making - hollowed stones, each one shaped to hold a different kind of silence around a different kind of sound.

My hands are scarred from years of carving these vessels. The stone here sings differently depending on its grain, and I've learned to read the bands of rust and grey like sheet music, knowing which will ring clear and which will muffle. Some vessels are no bigger than a child's fist. Others could hold a loaf. The largest sits in the corner, tall as my hip, and contains the sound of the last earthquake - just a low rumble now, barely there, but still turning in its chamber like a sleeper who won't quite wake.

People come, sometimes. Not often. They bring me sounds they think might be ending - an old woman brings the creak of her mother's door before she replaces the hinges, a potter brings the scrape of his worn wheel before he trues it. They don't ask me to keep these sounds. They just set them down in my workspace, the way you might leave a stone you found by a cairn. I work them into vessels when I can.

In return, they leave things by my door: bread, sometimes. A pot. Wool for winter. Kindling when the man who splits it has extra. I mend what needs mending in the town's quiet - the pause in conversations, the space between the spring's notes where a stone has shifted. I go up in the early morning or late evening, when few are about, and adjust what needs adjusting. A shimming stone here to change the water's pitch. A whispered word to fill a silence that has grown too heavy.

The vessels line the walls of my workshop, hundreds of them, each holding its particular quiet. In the deepest part of night, they seem to breathe together, all the captured sounds of Sett turning in their chambers like a second population - the town's echo, its memory made tangible.

I am getting old. My successor - though we don't use that word - already comes by some mornings. A child who stands in my doorway and listens to the vessels breathing, who can tell without being taught which ones hold weather-sounds and which hold human ones. When I am gone, they will move into this workshop. They will learn the stones. The vessels will continue their quiet singing.

And I will become one of the unmarked stones on the slope above, weathering back into the mountain, while somewhere in the workshop a vessel holds the sound of my last morning's work: the careful tap of hammer on chisel, shaping stone to hold a silence that will outlast us both.
