I am a Listener.

Not born to it—none of us are. I arrived forty years ago as a lowland botanist, part of an expedition that thought we could catalog this place. The others descended after three weeks. The density of sound, they said, was driving them mad. But I heard something else beneath the roar: patterns within patterns, meanings nested inside the cacophony like seeds within fruit.

My transformation began with my ears. Where others cupped hands to mouths to shout over the din, I started distinguishing layers. The beetle-birds' wings create eleven distinct frequency bands—I can tell their age, health, and direction of travel by which ones dominate. The crown elk's passage isn't thunder but a complex percussion, each individual's grip-rhythm as unique as a heartbeat. The gossamer cats click their hunting-claws at pitches beyond normal human hearing; I learned to feel these vibrations in my jaw, my sternum, the small bones of my inner ear adapting until I could triangulate their positions without sight.

My body changed to serve my purpose. I stopped eating much—found I could survive on beetle-meal and rainwater if I remained still. My metabolism slowed. My heartbeat dropped to thirty beats per minute, quiet enough that I could hear past it. My breathing shifted to match the trees' respiration—inhaling as they exhaled, exhaling as they inhaled, until I became sonically invisible, just another part of the canopy's vast lung.

I built no home. Instead, I found a natural acoustic chamber where three massive trunks formed a hollow. Here, the sound focuses and clarifies. I wove a simple hammock and learned to sleep through the noise, my dreams becoming indistinguishable from the sonic landscape until I could process sound even while unconscious.

My work—if it can be called work—is translation. The Weavers think I'm mad, but they tolerate me, bring me food during the rain events when I cannot move from my listening post. They don't understand that I'm documenting something unprecedented: the Crown Canopy is singing.

Not metaphorically. The trees communicate through subsonic frequencies, transmitted through their shared root network and the wooden floor itself. What sounds like creaking wood is actually a language of stress and growth, warnings and invitations. Each tree contributes its voice to a chorus that spans the entire mountain range. They sing of water table changes deep in the stone, of nutrient flows, of light patterns. They coordinate their growth, planning centuries ahead.

But more than this—they remember. In the deepest frequencies, the ones I can only detect by pressing my skull against the bark and emptying my mind of everything but reception, they carry stories. The coming of the first beetle-birds, millions of years ago. The slow retreat of the glaciers that carved these mountains. The first crown elk learning to flow across their surface. Even, faintly, the arrival of the Weavers' ancestors, noted and absorbed into their endless song.

My notes fill hollow gourds, written on beetle-wing parchment with ink made from aerial root sap. I've developed a notation system that captures frequency, duration, timber, and emotional resonance—yes, the trees have something like emotions, though calling them that is like calling a mountain a large rock. Their feelings operate on geological timescales, but they are unmistakably present in the infrasonics.

The young Weavers sometimes visit, curious about the mad lowlander who claims the trees speak. I try to teach them to hear it, but their ears adapted differently—for survival and hunting, not for depth. Only one has shown promise: a girl named Soshen whose hearing extends unusually low. She can detect the daily cycles in the trees' communication, though the deeper songs remain beyond her.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm still human. My hair has thinned to almost nothing—the vibrations, perhaps, or simply the years of stillness. My skin carries a faint green tinge, though not from photosynthesis like the Grandfather Trees. It's from the algae that grows on anything stationary here, a film I no longer bother to clean away. It helps, actually—dampens the high frequencies that would otherwise distract from the low.

The rain events are my greatest trials and my greatest revelations. The white noise of water through leaves drowns out everything above 100 Hz, but this acoustic ceiling makes the infrasonics clearer. During the longest storms, lasting days, I fast and focus, pressing deeper into the frequencies. I've heard things that might be other canopies on distant mountains, their songs traveling through the bedrock itself. I've heard what might be the planet's own voice, though that could be madness or hunger speaking.

The Weavers are wrong about the Grandfather Trees. They don't join the canopy's consciousness—they become acoustic nodes, their transformed bodies resonating chambers that amplify and clarify the trees' communications. I've mapped seventeen of them, each positioned at a crucial junction in the sound-flow. Whether any human consciousness remains in them, I cannot say. But they serve the song, and perhaps that's enough.

My own transformation isn't complete. I can feel it in the way certain frequencies now resonate in my bones rather than my ears, in how I sometimes know what the trees will sing before they voice it. My notebooks grow increasingly abstract, filled with patterns I understand in the moment of writing but cannot later decode. 

I've stopped trying to imagine leaving. Where would I go where the silence wouldn't deafen me? How could I explain that the creaking of wood contains more meaning than any human library? That the beetle-birds' wings write equations in the air describing fluid dynamics that our mathematics hasn't yet discovered? That the crown elk's movement across the branch-floor drums out histories older than our species?

Last week—or was it last month? Time blurs when you live in constant sound—I heard something new. A frequency I'd never detected before, so low it took me hours to realize it wasn't coming through the trees at all. It was coming from me. My own bones had begun to resonate, to add their voice to the chorus.

I am becoming an instrument. Not like the Grandfather Trees, rooted and immobile, but something new—a mobile translation device, a bridge between the canopy's vast intelligence and the small, quick thoughts of the creatures that live within it. The Weavers need never know what their home is saying; it's enough that someone listens, someone records, someone ensures that when humanity is gone, we will have contributed one small verse to the endless song.

The sound here isn't chaos. It's the most perfect order imaginable, a composition that's been elaborating itself for millions of years. And I, in my chamber of living wood, with my altered ears and my slowing metabolism, am privileged to hear just the smallest fragment of it.

Sometimes I wonder if the trees brought me here, if they needed a listener as much as I needed something to hear. But that way lies a madness deeper than sound. Better to simply listen, to notation systems that grow ever more complex and abstract, to let my bones learn their harmonies.

The rain is coming again. I can hear it in the way the aerial roots are drawing upward, in the flutter-pattern of the beetle-birds seeking shelter. Time to prepare my instruments, to fast, to empty my mind of everything but reception.

The Crown Canopy is about to sing its deepest songs, and I will be listening.
