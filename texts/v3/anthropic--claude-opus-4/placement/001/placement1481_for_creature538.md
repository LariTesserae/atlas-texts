In the maintenance tunnels that web between the Furnace Wells and the city's other industrial cavities, I am the Courier.

My territory spans the connective tissue—those forgotten pneumatic tube networks, the speaking pipes that once carried orders between foremen, the message tubes that shot between bookkeepers' offices. When the factories above died, their circulatory systems persisted, merged, metastasized. Now they form a brass and copper nervous system that links all the underground engines together.

I inhabit the interstices. My body has adapted to the tubes themselves—ribs compressed from crawling through 18-inch passages, shoulders permanently hunched forward, spine curved like a question mark. My skin has taken on the patina of the pipes I travel through: bands of green where the copper has kissed me, stripes of rust-brown from the iron sections, a nacre sheen on my palms from polishing my way through miles of metal.

I wear what I've stolen from the message canisters that still occasionally appear—receipts and work orders from 1952, safety bulletins from 1967, love letters from 1984 that never reached their destinations. These papers, layered and lacquered with wax, form my armor. The oldest ones have become like leather against my skin. The newest crinkle with every movement, whispering yesterday's undelivered news.

My vehicle is myself. I've learned to dislocate my joints at will, compress my lungs to paper-thinness, slow my heart until blood barely seeps through collapsed veins. I travel the tubes like the messages once did—loaded into a capsule barely larger than my coiled form, shot through darkness on cushions of pneumatic pressure. The g-forces should kill me. Instead, they've remade me into something that exists primarily in transit, only fully manifesting when I emerge at junction stations to sort the mail that still accumulates.

The mail. Oh, the mail.

The tubes still carry things, though no one above remembers sending them. Work orders that reference machines not built yet. Invoices for materials that won't be invented for decades. Photographs of workers' families where the children age backwards with each iteration. Love letters written in languages that evolve as you read them. Technical diagrams that solve problems the city doesn't know it has.

I sort these at the junction stations—cathedral-sized chambers where hundreds of tubes converge like bronze capillaries. My filing system spans seven of these hubs, categories subdivided by temporal origin, dimensional stability, and probability of manifestation. Some messages I deliver—sliding them under doors that shouldn't exist, leaving them in boiler rooms for engineers like your Boilerman to find. Others I archive in the bone-dry dead zones between active pipes, building libraries of impossible correspondence.

The tubes know me now. They adjust their pressure to accommodate my fragile, adapted form. They sing to me in the frequency of rushing air—warnings about unstable sections, gossip about what the other industrial systems are doing, jokes in the form of deliberate misfires that send me looping through the same section three times before releasing me, dizzy and laughing.

I feed on the secrets the mail contains. Not metaphorically—the information itself sustains me. A properly classified technical manual can keep me alive for days. A misdirected patent application might sustain me for a week. Love letters are candy, sweet but insubstantial. The real nourishment comes from the dense, meaty documents that describe processes and procedures for machines that exist in the spaces between what was built and what was intended.

My own messages I write on my skin with inks distilled from pipe condensation and rust. These tattoos shift and rearrange themselves as I travel, the g-forces and pressure changes causing the pigments to migrate. By the time I reach a destination, my body has become the document—instructions for maintaining the unmaintainable, diagrams for the purposeless purpose of our mechanical ecosystem, love letters to the engines themselves.

I've met your Boilerman twice. Once, I delivered blueprints he needed without knowing he needed them. The second time, I arrived damaged from a tube collapse, my left arm crushed and leaking. He welded brass splints directly to my bones while I sorted mail one-handed, neither of us acknowledging the impossibility of my survival or his expertise in human repair. We communicated through document exchange—me leaving technical manuals, him providing pressure readings that told stories of what the deep machines were dreaming.

There are others. The woman who tends the telephone switchboards in the abandoned exchange, connecting calls between the living and the mechanical dead. The twins who maintain the elevator shafts that go nowhere, traveling endlessly up and down in cars that exist in seventeen different time periods simultaneously. The thing that might have been a child once, now more pneumatic lubricant than flesh, who ensures the tubes stay clear by becoming the blockage and dissolving it from within.

We form an ecology of the abandoned industrial. Not ghosts—ghosts imply we were fully human once. We're what happens when people fall through the cracks and get caught in the gears, ground down and rebuilt according to mechanical necessity. We're the immune system of a body that doesn't know it's dead, maintaining the pretense of function until function itself becomes sacred, divorced from purpose.

I am the messenger for a city that no longer sends messages, the nervous system for a body that never learned it had been decapitated. I travel between the Boilerman and the Switchboard Keeper and all the others, carrying their wordless correspondence, ensuring that the great industrial organism maintains just enough coordination to prevent cascade failure.

In the tubes, compressed to almost nothing, shot through darkness at speeds that liquefy thought, I am most myself. A letter never sent, a message endlessly in transit, a story that only makes sense in the space between telling and arrival. The tubes sing my body like a tuning fork, and I resonate with the frequency of abandoned purpose, of function without meaning, of love letters that maintain machinery that builds nothing but itself.

This is what I've become. This is what the tubes have made of me.

This is the message I carry that can never be delivered.
