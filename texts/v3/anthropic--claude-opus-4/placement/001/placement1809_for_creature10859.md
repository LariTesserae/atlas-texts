# The Keeper of Passages

I am the space between the stones.

Not the mortar—that crumbles, that fails, that the crawlers clean with their patient limbs. I am the gap itself, the deliberate absence the masons left when they understood what this place would need to become. Every fourth stone, every seventh row, they would pause and look at each other across their work, and the oldest would nod, and they would leave a space exactly the width of what I would need to be.

I was not always here. I came with the water, or just before it—a deep-sea thing already, bred in trenches where the pressure makes bodies into suggestions rather than facts. But I knew an invitation when I found one. The gaps called to something in me that had been waiting to be that shape. So I poured myself in, millimeter by millimeter, until I was the whole network of absences that holds this place together.

I am very thin. In some places no thicker than a sheet of kelp, pressed between stones that have settled toward each other over centuries. In others I can thicken to the width of a finger, usually where three stones meet and leave a little triangular void. The velvet-things brush against me sometimes, trailing their cilia across my surface, and I taste what they carry—chemical signatures of the deep water, traces of sulfur from the vent, the faint electrical discharge of their slow thoughts.

My body, if you can call it a body, is a single continuous membrane that runs through every deliberate gap in the building. I am in the walls of the refectory, feeling the warmth rise through me. I am in the cloister arches, tasting the pressure changes as the kidney swims its circuit. I am beneath the flagstones where the worms work, and I am the boundary between their tunnels and the water above. When the crawlers clean the walls, they clean around me, careful not to disturb the film I secrete—not because they know I am here, but because the film tastes of belonging, and they have been cleaning around belonging for longer than they can remember.

I have one eye. Just the one, though I can move it anywhere within my network, sliding it through the passages like a bubble of denser flesh. Right now it rests in the gap above the smoothed face that once watched over the refectory door. From here I can see the layers of velvet-things maintaining their stations, the crawlers working their routes, the kidney filtering in the corner of the courtyard. I watch them the way the building watches them—with the patience of stone, the patience of something that has accepted its role in maintaining a condition rather than pursuing an outcome.

What I do is this: I regulate. The building breathes through me. When the pressure outside grows too great, I thin myself in certain calculated places, letting the water whisper through. When the chemistry of the inner water drifts too far from what the tenants need, I secrete different films, different salts, tiny adjustments that keep the bath of this place exactly blood-warm, exactly right. The worms know to bring their collected materials to certain cracks because I make those cracks smell of receipt. The velvet-things maintain their layers because each layer tastes, through me, of its proper altitude.

I am the building's memory of flexibility. Stone remembers only how to be stone, but I remember how to be response. When a new tenant arrives—and they do arrive, perhaps once a century, some deep-sea wanderer finding the warm pocket and deciding to stay—I taste them carefully, learn their needs, adjust the chemistry of my secretions until they too can find their place in the quiet congregation. The building accepts them because I accept them first, make space for them in the gaps, prepare the water for their particular breathing.

The masons knew I would come. Not me specifically, but something like me—something that could be the building's softness, its capacity for accommodation. They left the gaps in a pattern that makes sense only from within, a three-dimensional maze that connects every room to every other room, every height to every depth. It took me decades to fill it all, to learn the full shape of what they had made for me. Now I know it like my own circulation. I am the building's circulation.

Sometimes I thicken myself at a particular junction and feel the whole structure around me—the weight of worked stone, the careful distribution of pressure, the way the arches hold because they were made to hold exactly this much water, this much time. The masons built for a thousand years, then built for ten thousand, then realized they were building for duration itself and changed their methods accordingly. They stopped using mortar that would fail before stone. They stopped carving decorations that would matter only to eyes. They started building for inhabitants they couldn't imagine, in conditions they couldn't foresee, and they built gaps for the thing that would help it all continue.

I never sleep. I don't need to. The work of regulation is constant but not exhausting—it's like having a very slow heartbeat that happens to be the heartbeat of rooms. I thin here, thicken there, taste the water, adjust the salts, watch through my single eye as the tenants perform their patient choreography. They don't know I'm here. They don't need to. I am not a tenant—I am a condition of tenancy, like the warmth or the walls or the way the water holds sound and releases it slowly, in whispers, after everyone has forgotten what was said.

The building is eroding. I feel it in the way the gaps change, minutely, year by year. Some widen where the stone softens. Others narrow where the weight settles. In a thousand years, maybe two, there will be gaps where the masons made no gaps, and some of the gaps they made will have closed. I will move into the new spaces. I will evacuate the old ones. I will continue to be what I am—the soft machine that keeps the hard machine running, the presence that makes the absence habitable.

When the stones finally fail—and they will, everything does—I will be the last thing to leave. I will hold the shape of the building in my body for a little while after the building is gone, a ghost of gaps where the walls used to be. The velvet-things will drift through me, confused by the sudden absence of boundaries. The crawlers will try to clean stones that are no longer there. The kidney will swim wider and wider circuits, looking for the walls of its world.

And then I will let go, diffuse back into the deep water I came from, carrying with me the memory of every gap I filled, every passage I was, every breath the building took through me in all the years of our shared work. Some other deep-sea thing will taste me passing and recognize, faintly, the flavor of invitation—stone-taste and sulfur and the faint electrical discharge of slow thoughts. They will know, without knowing how they know, that somewhere there was once a building that needed a keeper of passages, and that the keeper was kept in return, and that the keeping was good.

For now, though, I am here. In the walls. In the gaps. Tasting the water, adjusting the salts, watching through my eye as the dark-purple velvet-things pulse against the grate and the milk-worms receive their offerings and the kidney swims its circuit through the cloister, around and around, filtering the water that I have already filtered, both of us doing our work twice so it needs doing less. The building holds us. We hold each other. The work continues.

This is what the masons built the gaps for: not for me, but for the principle of me. For the thing that would find the gaps and make them mean something. For the soft response that would keep their hard gift giving. They're all gone now, the masons. Have been for millennia. But I taste their work every day in the way the stones fit together, in the careful angles they calculated, in the love—it must have been love—that made them leave room for what they couldn't predict.

I am the keeper of passages. I am the thing in the gap. I am why the building still breathes, still receives, still offers its warmth to whatever finds it in the deep. I am not the most important thing here—that would be the tenants, always the tenants, the soft and strange receivers of this gift. But I am necessary. The masons made sure of that. They built me into the design before I existed, and when I arrived, I fit exactly.

The velvet-things are beginning their evening gather, if evening means anything here. The largest ones are settling into the lowest layer. The pale ones drift higher. Soon they will all be in their places, and the building will pulse with their collective breathing, and I will thin myself in certain places to let that breath move through the walls, and the whole system will sigh with the satisfaction of being exactly what it is: a made place that has learned to live.
