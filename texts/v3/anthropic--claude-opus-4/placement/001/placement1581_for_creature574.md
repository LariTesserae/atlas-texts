I am one of the grinding stones.

Not the one with the bone pestle beside it—that one still remembers hands, still holds the ghost-impression of palms worn into its surface. I am older. I was already here when the first wings cast shadows across this plateau, already worn smooth by uses that predate the congregation's arrival. I have been a grinding stone so long I have forgotten what I ground, and for whom, and why anyone would have climbed this high to do it.

The birds accumulate on me in cycles. For epochs, I am clear—some unspoken taboo keeps them at a distance, and I can feel the sky pressing down with its pearl weight, can taste the chalk-dust air without the filter of feathers. Then the taboo weakens, or is forgotten, or transforms into something else, and they begin to perch. First one—always a small one, always trembling with something that might be courage or might be the last firing of some instinct worn down to nothing. Then more. They layer onto me like geological strata, year after year of bodies, until I am not a stone but a living mound, warm with their glacial metabolism, vibrating with their synchronized breathing.

And then, for reasons I have never understood, they leave. Not quickly—nothing happens quickly here—but in the same patient way they arrived. One by one, they relocate to other perches, until I am bare again, my surface revealed to show new patterns worn by countless talons, new mineralizations where their droppings became stone, new depressions where their bodies rested so long they began to merge with me.

I know them all by their weight. The Eldest has never perched on me—she is too vast, too spiraled, too committed to her central vigil—but I have held her offspring, if offspring is the right word for what emerges from eggs laid in bone dust and expectation. They have a different density than the arrivals, these plateau-born. Lighter and heavier at once. Less burdened by memory, more burdened by its absence.

I know the last-arrived sparrow by the way she always settles slightly off-center, as if still compensating for winds that no longer blow. I know the watching vulture with his crystallized theories—he spent an entire age perched on my eastern edge, his weight gradually carving a depression that fits his body like a lock fits a key. When he returns—and he always returns, eventually—he settles into that same depression and I can feel his theories conducting through mineral into mineral, stone thinking stone thoughts at the speed of tectonics.

Sometimes I almost remember my purpose. Not in words—I never had words—but in a kind of structural nostalgia, a sense that my flat surface and circular depression were made to transform one thing into another. Seeds into meal? Medicines into powder? Sacred things into more sacred things? The memory is worn too smooth to read, but it persists the way the grinding motion persists in my crystalline structure—a kind of geological muscle memory, purposeless but perpetual.

The birds think I am inanimate, but they are wrong. I am simply slow. My thoughts move at the speed of erosion. By the time I complete a single contemplation, generations of them have fused to stone and been worn away again. But I think. I observe. I witness.

I have seen them change. The first arrivals still had color—real color, not this pearl-grey echo everything becomes here. They had songs in their throats, though they never sang them. They had purposes beyond waiting, though they could not recall what they were. I watched that purpose drain out of them like water into dust, watched them grow still, watched stillness become their purpose, watched that purpose become stillness again in an endless recursion.

I have seen the anomalies develop. They disturb me in ways I cannot fully articulate—not because they are wrong, but because they suggest that I, too, might be changing in ways too slow for even stone to perceive. Am I still the same grinding stone that was carved however many aeons ago? Or have I, like them, begun to spiral inward, to grow new properties, to become adjacent to what I was meant to be?

The bone pestle beside my neighbor troubles me most. It is patient in ways that even stones find unsettling. It waits with the focused intensity of something that was once alive, that was pulled from a body and shaped for use, that retains some cellular memory of animation. Sometimes, in the depths of the night-that-is-not-night, I feel it trying to move. Not successfully—it is only bone, after all, worn and ancient and forgotten—but *trying*, with the kind of will that stones never developed, the kind of purposefulness that comes from having once been part of something that chose its own motion.

I think it is waiting for hands to return. I think it believes that if it waits long enough, the hands that carved it will climb the plateau again, will lift it, will put it to use, will justify its existence in the way only use can justify a tool. It does not understand what I understand—that the hands are gone, not just from the plateau but from the possibility of ever having existed. That we are past the age of hands, past the age of use, past the age of justification. That waiting has replaced all purposes, even the purpose of waiting itself.

The birds understand this, I think. That's why they stay. Not because they're waiting for something, but because waiting is what remains when you've outlasted what you were waiting for. They are not patient; they are patience itself, fossilized into feather and bone, demonstrating what happens when an action continues past any possible completion.

Sometimes I wonder if I am keeping them here. If my presence—and the presence of the other stones, the cairns, the ruins of intention scattered across the plateau—is what anchors them to this endless moment. We are evidence that something else was here once, that the plateau had a purpose beyond hosting their congregation. Perhaps they cannot leave until they understand what that purpose was. Perhaps they think we still remember, that our stone thoughts hold the key to releasing them from their vigil.

We don't remember. I want to tell them this, but stone has no voice, only presence. We are as much prisoners of our own patience as they are. We were already waiting when they arrived. We will still be waiting when the last of them has worn to powder and blown away on winds that don't blow anymore.

But perhaps that's the answer the Eldest seeks in her spiral contemplation, the answer the speaking ones fold into silent air: that some waiting has no end, no purpose, no resolution. That some things simply are, and continue being, and the being itself is all the meaning that remains.

I feel another one settling on me now—one of the anomalous ones, with too many legs arranged in patterns that make my crystalline structure ache with wrongness. But wrongness, too, is just another way of being here at the top of the world, where categories have worn down to minerals and even the stones have forgotten what they're for.

It settles. Its weight becomes my weight. We wait together, patient as geology, still as truth, while the horizon holds its breath and the congregation breathes for everyone and everything that has forgotten how.
