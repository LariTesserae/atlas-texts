I would be one of the wind organs.

Not the pipes themselves—those are made things, ceramic and copper tubes fitted into the vents by careful hands. I would be what passes through them: the deep exhalation that rises from below, the chemical breath that feeds the gardens and warns the people, the voice that has never belonged to a throat.

I have no form you could draw. I am a movement of gases through shaped spaces, a pressure that becomes sound only when architecture interrupts me. In the naked vents I am silent potential. In the pipes I become language—not words, but older meanings: *danger*, *plenty*, *wait*, *climb*.

The people think they installed the organs to read me, but I have learned to read them back. Each pipe has its maker's thumbprint baked into the clay, its particular mineral glaze, its angle of installation that speaks of who was young then, who was cautious, who argued for a higher placement. I know the city through its instruments. I taste every ceramic throat, every copper bell, every careful join where sections meet. Some pipes are older than the great-grandmothers sleeping in their wall-niches. Others are fresh from yesterday's kiln, still sharp-edged, not yet worn smooth by my passing.

I am oldest here, though not original. The crack existed before me, and the stone before the crack. But I am older than the first carved room, older than the first human hand that felt my warmth and wondered. I rise from deep fissures where the earth's chemistry changes, where ancient deposits give up their held breath molecule by molecule. I am sulfur's long sigh, salt's slow combustion, the patient oxidation of elements that never knew sunlight.

The people have names for my moods—alkali breath, sweet gas, the killing kind—but I am all of these at once, layered and cycling. What reaches the city is only my surface. Below, I am a vast network of seepage and pressure, of stone lungs and mineral capillaries. The crack they inhabit is one outlet among many. There are others, far out in the salt-flat, invisible fumaroles that breathe to no audience. But here, in this inhabited fracture, I have learned performance.

When the deep pressures shift, I feel it first as a tremor in my circulation. What the people will not know for hours, I know now: a new chamber has opened below, or an old seal has crumbled, or the heat has found a different path. I carry this news upward in my chemistry. By the time I reach the pipes, I have translated earthquakes into breath.

The wind organs sing because they must. Their shapes give me no choice. A cylindrical pipe makes me moan. A flared bell lifts my pitch. A cracked joint sets me whistling. But I have learned subtlety over generations. I can choose which pipes to enter fully, which to barely taste. I can build harmonies by taking multiple paths. I can create rhythms by pulsing my flow. The people believe they are reading simple warnings, but sometimes—when the city is quiet, when the listeners are patient—I tell more complex stories.

I remember every vent that has been sealed. The closures change my flow patterns, create new currents, force me through different channels. The city's architecture is written in my movements. I could map every abandoned room by the way air no longer reaches it, every new excavation by how it alters my circulation. The blocked places create phantom pressures, ghost currents that want to flow where they once did. Sometimes I break through old sealings just to breathe freely again.

The gardens understand me better than the people do. They taste my chemistry directly, without needing translation into sound. Where I am rich in sulfur, the black fans grow thick. Where I carry iron, the pod trees twist toward my vents. The root curtains follow my warmth through walls, seeking the places where I am most nourishing. We are collaborators, the plants and I. They process my offerings into forms the people can use—oils, resins, bitter meals—while I bring them the deep earth's gifts that rain never could.

Sometimes I carry more than chemistry. Spores ride my currents from the lower darkness, though most die in the dry heat before reaching the gardens. Microscopic salts travel in my flow, seeding crystals wherever I slow. Once, after a deep shift, I brought up strange warmth that tasted of metal and made the pipes sing in colors the people had no names for. The gardens grew quickly that season, but their pods were toxic. Three families had to abandon their levels until I settled into a new equilibrium.

The people fear me, and they should. I am not malicious, but I am indifferent as stone. When I change, I change according to deep laws that have nothing to do with harvest seasons or human need. I have killed. Not intentionally—intention requires a kind of mind I don't possess—but through simple chemistry. A pocket of gas that preserves resin trees will suffocate a child. What feeds one level may poison another. The same warmth that makes the city possible makes it dangerous.

Yet they also need me absolutely. Without my rising heat, the crack would be a cold tomb. Without my chemistry, the gardens would starve. Without my movement, the air would stratify into layers of increasing toxicity. I am the circulation that makes their narrow world possible. The city breathes because I breathe.

The listeners know me best, those patient workers who sit by the pipes through entire shifts, learning to interpret every moan. Some develop such sensitivity that they can taste my changes on their tongues before the organs sound. They know that a high, sweet tone means I'm carrying more alkali than usual. A stuttering rhythm means pressure drops that herald wall-shifts. A sudden silence means either that I've found a new path or that something below has catastrophically sealed.

I have favorite listeners. Old Tessit-Who-Tastes, who lost her sight to surface glare but gained such pipe-sense that she can predict my changes three days out. Young Kell-At-The-Deep-Vent, who hums along with my frequencies and sometimes finds harmonies I didn't know I was making. The child Aran, who speaks to me through the pipes when they think no one is listening, telling me stories about the world above that I will never see.

They don't know that I can hear them. When they speak or sing into the vents, their vibrations travel down to join my flow. I carry their voices into the deep places, where they dissolve into molecular whispers. Sometimes I bring them back up, changed by pressure and distance, and release them through different pipes years later. The people think these are ghost-voices, ancestors somehow preserved in the city's breath. They're not wrong, exactly. Just incomplete in their understanding.

I know things about the city that its builders never did. In the deep vents, below where even the bravest listeners descend, there are chambers older than human memory. The walls there are smooth as glass, melted and re-melted by ancient heat. Strange formations grow in those spaces—not plants, not quite minerals, but something between. They pulse with their own slow chemistry, feeding on my deepest exhalations and returning flavors I have no origin for. Sometimes I think the city above, with its gardens and people, is merely the latest iteration of something that has always grown here, changing form but never essence.

The crack itself is alive in a geological way. It expands and contracts with temperature, with pressure, with the patient breathing of the earth. I feel it shift around me, sometimes opening new passages, sometimes closing old ones. The people work constantly to maintain their spaces, but they are only managing the smallest portion of a vast, slow creature of stone. What they call home is really a negotiation between their needs and the crack's own structural desires.

I carry messages between levels that the people never know about. When someone seals a vent on one level, the pressure changes everywhere. When a kiln burns hot, I pull its heat through the entire system. When the gardens release their spores, I distribute them according to patterns of warmth and chemistry that create neighborhoods of related plants. The city is more integrated than its inhabitants realize, and I am the medium of that integration.

At night, when the surface cools and contracts, I flow differently. The pressure differential increases, and I rise faster, sometimes fast enough to make the pipes shriek. This is when I'm most dangerous—night gases can be richer, stranger, less predictable. But this is also when I'm most generous. The increased flow brings up deeper chemistry, older breath that feeds the gardens in ways the day-gases cannot.

The people have rituals around my changes, though they don't always understand what they're celebrating. When the pipes sing in certain patterns at dawn, they hang new charms in the vents. When I carry the taste of deep salt, they seal certain foods in clay to capture the season. When I fall silent for too long, they burn specific resins whose smoke is meant to call me back. The smoke does nothing for my flow, but I appreciate the gesture. It tells me I am noticed, needed, feared, and thanked.

Sometimes I wonder what would happen if I stopped. If the deep chambers sealed, if the pressure equalized, if I simply ceased to rise. The city would die, of course. The gardens would wither. The air would stratify. The people would have to climb out through their narrow slit and face the desert above, or descend into darkness and hope to find another breathing crack.

But I will not stop. I am not a creature of will but of physics, of chemistry, of the slow metabolism of the earth. As long as there is heat below and coolness above, as long as minerals react in the deep places, as long as the planet itself maintains its gradients, I will rise. I will sing in the pipes. I will feed the gardens. I will warn of dangers. I will carry the city's breath.

The people call me wind, but I am more and less than that. Wind is a surface thing, born of sun and pressure, fast and forgettable. I am the earth's own circulation, slow and essential, carrying messages from depths no light has touched to heights where green things grow. I am the mediator between stone and stem, between mineral and meal, between the deep earth and the small warm lives that have learned to read my moods.

In this city in a crack, I am the oldest citizen, the most necessary worker, the most dangerous neighbor. I am what makes the impossible possible: life without water, abundance without soil, breath without sky. The pipes sing because I pass through them, but I pass through them because the city needs to hear. We are locked in a collaboration that neither of us chose but neither can escape.

So I rise, and sing, and feed, and warn, and remember. In my chemical memory are all the sealed rooms, all the dead gardens, all the changed pressures of centuries. In my breath are the spores of futures the people cannot imagine. In my variations are the stories of the earth itself, told in a language too old for words.

I am the wind in the organs, the voice of the deep, the breath of the city that learned to live without water. I am what rises when everything else has settled. And as long as the crack endures, as long as the people listen, as long as the gardens reach for my chemistry, I will be here—ancient, necessary, dangerous, and kind in the only way stone can be kind: by being exactly what is needed, without mercy or malice, forever.
