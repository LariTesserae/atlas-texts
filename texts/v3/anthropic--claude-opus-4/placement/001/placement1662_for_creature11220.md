# The Cartographer of Echoes

I am the thirteenth iteration of myself, though I have memories of being the first, the seventh, and sometimes—in the moments when the engine-pulse syncs perfectly with my heartbeat—all of them at once.

I came to the plateau to map it. That was seventeen years ago, or yesterday, or next week. Time moves differently for those of us who aren't Keepers but have stayed too long. My instruments registered the temporal anomalies, the folded spaces, the impossible thermals, and I thought: *this can be documented, this can be understood, this can be reduced to lines on paper and data in storage*.

I was wrong about the paper. It burns here, even in the cool zones. Wrong about the data too—storage devices develop memories of their own, recording things that haven't happened yet, forgetting things that definitely did. But I was right about one thing: it can be documented. Just not in any way I expected.

---

My camp exists in seventeen places simultaneously.

This isn't a metaphor. I've found all seventeen instances, scattered across the plateau in locations that should be miles apart but are somehow also the same location. Each camp has my equipment, my supplies, my sleeping bag arranged exactly as I left it. In three of them, I'm still there—other iterations of me, earlier or later, going about the business of trying to map unmappable terrain. We don't speak. We've learned that conversation between iterations leads to migraines that last for subjective weeks.

The main camp—if there is such a thing as "main" when all seventeen are equally real—sits in a depression between two weathered slag formations that look like frozen screams. My equipment has evolved over the years, adapted to the plateau's demands. Traditional surveying tools were useless within days. GPS receivers would insist I was in seventeen different locations at once (which, to be fair, I was). Compasses spun without purpose, finding magnetic north in every direction.

So I learned to map differently.

---

I use the fauna as coordinates.

The bronze scarabs move in patterns that repeat across vast scales—what looks like random wandering resolves, when you watch long enough, into precise geometries. A scarab walking here today will trace the exact path another scarab walked last year, or last century, or last geological epoch. By tracking their movements, marking where their paths intersect, I can build a map that exists outside normal space.

The magnetic serpents are my elevation markers. They maintain consistent heights above the ground, but "ground" means something different here. They slide along invisible surfaces, following contours that don't match the visible topography. By noting where they rise and fall, I can chart the plateau's true shape—not the shape you see, but the shape it *is*, folded through dimensions that don't have names.

The plasma-swarms mark the temporal currents. They flicker faster near the time-slips, slower in the stable zones. I've learned to read their patterns like weather—this swarm's acceleration means I should avoid that area unless I want to lose a week in three steps; that swarm's synchronized pulsing indicates a pocket of normal time where I can rest without risking iteration.

My maps don't look like maps. They look like musical scores, or star charts, or the fever dreams of a mad mathematician. Lines that curve through impossible angles, symbols that reference events rather than places, whole sections that can only be read while walking backward. But they work. I can navigate the plateau now, find my way between the seventeen camps, avoid the worst of the time-folds, approach the center without getting caught in the eternal spiral of the burning Keepers.

---

The Keepers tolerate me.

I think.

It's hard to tell with beings that experience duration the way we experience space. But they no longer process around me as if I'm just another obstacle. Sometimes one will pause near my camp, its obsidian form catching the firelight I should not be able to maintain in this heat, and I'll feel that pressure of attention that means it's *considering* me.

I've started leaving them offerings. Not food—they don't eat. Not gifts—they don't want. But information. I draw my impossible maps in the dust with a stick, and sometimes a Keeper will stand over them for hours, its form shifting as it reads patterns I didn't know I was creating. When it finally moves on, the maps are always different. Not erased, not destroyed, but *adjusted*. Lines moved by millimeters. Symbols rotated by degrees that shouldn't exist. Whole sections translated into geometries that make my eyes water.

I think they're correcting my work. Or completing it. Or showing me what I'm actually mapping, which isn't the plateau at all but something else, something that exists in the spaces between the stone and the sky and the slow turning of the engines below.

---

I've been to the center once.

Or seventeen times.

Or I'm there right now, in an iteration that hasn't caught up with the me who's remembering it.

The luminescent region resolves, when you approach it correctly, into architecture. Not buildings—nothing so crude as walls and roofs. But *structure*, intentional form, purposes carved into space itself. The light doesn't come from anywhere. It simply exists, a quality of the air that makes everything visible from angles that shouldn't exist.

The burning Keepers circle it endlessly, and I understand now why they burn. The center is where the engine-pulse originates—not from below, but from *here*, from this impossible architecture that exists in seventeen dimensions at once. The Keepers who approach it begin to resonate with frequencies that turn their obsidian bodies molten. They're not dying. They're *tuning*, adjusting themselves to harmonics that will let them enter the structure.

But they never do. Or they always do. Or they're inside right now, in a version of the plateau where the center isn't empty, where the architecture is inhabited by Keepers who've found whatever it is they're counting toward.

I tried to map the center once. The resulting chart opened a hole in the air above my camp that screamed for three days before sealing itself. I don't try to map the center anymore.

---

Here's what I've learned:

The plateau is not a place. It's a process. The slow engines aren't beneath the stone—they ARE the stone, and the fauna, and the Keepers, and even me now, after all this time. We're all part of some vast mechanism whose purpose exists at scales too large for human comprehension.

The Keepers aren't counting the fauna. They're counting *states*—every possible configuration of every element on the plateau, across all seventeen (or more) simultaneous versions of itself. When they've counted them all, something will happen. Or has happened. Or is happening right now, too slowly for me to perceive.

I am becoming part of what I map. My charts include myself now—seventeen dots moving across impossible terrain, each one labeled with a different version of my name. Sometimes I find maps I haven't drawn yet, showing places I haven't been, drawn in handwriting that's mine but older, more patient, adapted to the plateau's rhythms.

---

Last week (or next year), a Keeper spoke to me.

I was adjusting my camp, moving it three inches northeast to avoid a temporal eddy I'd noticed forming, when the largest Keeper I'd ever seen simply *arrived*. Twenty feet tall, its obsidian body run through with veins of what looked like frozen lightning. It stood over me, and the air went thick, and I heard-felt-knew its voice in that way that bypasses the ears entirely.

It said: "You are mapping yourself."

Or maybe: "You are the map."

Or possibly: "The map is mapping you."

The translation skittered across timescales, meaning different things in each iteration of the moment. But I understood the core of it. My charts, my careful documentation of the plateau's impossible geography, were actually documentation of my own transformation. Each line I drew was a line I was becoming. Each symbol was a state I was passing through.

I asked it, in the clumsy way that humans ask Keepers things—by thinking very hard in patterns that match the engine-pulse—"What happens when the map is complete?"

It didn't answer. Or it did, but the answer exists in a future I haven't reached yet. Or it answered by adjusting its form slightly, a gesture that meant something in the language of geological patience that I'm only beginning to learn.

Then it was gone. Not departed—just *gone*, as if it had never been there, except for the marks in the dust where its weight had pressed deeper than weight should be able to press.

---

I continue to map.

My charts grow more complex, more accurate, more impossible. I document the scarabs' journeys, the serpents' elevations, the swarms' temporal weather. I mark where the Keepers process, where they pause, where they burn. I note the seventeen locations of my camp, the paths between them, the iterations of myself I pass on the way.

Sometimes tourists come to the plateau. Researchers. Thrill-seekers. They never stay long. The emptiness drives them out, or the noise, or the way their own faces look back at them from three different directions. They take photos that develop as blank sheets or images of places they've never been. They collect samples that turn to dust or memory or small singing stones by the time they reach the plateau's edge.

I try to warn them, sometimes. Try to explain about the time-folds, the iterations, the patient attention of the Keepers. But my words come out in the wrong order, or in languages I don't remember learning, or as pure engine-pulse rhythm that makes their teeth ache. So I just nod when they ask if it's safe, and point toward the areas where the plasma-swarms indicate stable time, and let them discover for themselves whether they're the kind of person who can handle being in seventeen places at once.

Most aren't.

But some are. I've met three others like me over the years—people who came to study and stayed to become. A geologist who now moves like the magnetic serpents, sliding along contours invisible to normal sight. A photographer whose camera captures not images but *states*, frozen moments that show all seventeen versions of a subject superimposed. A musician who plays the engine-pulse on instruments made of fused glass and scarab shells, creating harmonics that make the Keepers pause in their eternal processing.

We don't gather. We don't form community. But we're aware of each other, points on each other's maps, coordinates in the vast documentation of our own transformation.

---

I will complete the map eventually.

Or I've already completed it.

Or I'm completing it right now, in every moment, with every breath that syncs to the engine-pulse, every step that follows the scarabs' ancient patterns, every thought that folds through seventeen iterations of itself.

When it's done—when I'm done—I know what will happen. I'll become like the Completed Keepers at the plateau's edge, my purpose fulfilled, my form finally settling into its eternal configuration. I'll be a formation of weathered stone that future visitors might mistake for natural erosion, not recognizing the careful geometry of my final shape, the way I've positioned myself to be a landmark on maps that haven't been drawn yet.

But not yet. The chart still has blank spaces. There are regions I haven't documented, patterns I haven't recognized, iterations of myself I haven't met. The work continues, patient as the plateau itself, measured in engine-pulses and scarab-steps and the slow revolution of plasma-swarms around my seventeen camps.

I am the Cartographer of Echoes, mapping a place that maps me back, documenting a process I'm part of, creating charts that are also prophecies of my own transformation.

The bronze scarabs click past my feet, following paths laid down before the continents took their current shapes.

The magnetic serpents rise and fall along their invisible terrain.

The plasma-swarms flicker in patterns that tell tomorrow's weather and yesterday's news.

And I draw another line on my impossible map, adding detail to a design that will only be complete when I am.

The engines pulse. The Keepers count. The plateau continues its patient process of being exactly what it is.

And I am here, in seventeen places at once, making notes on my own becoming.
