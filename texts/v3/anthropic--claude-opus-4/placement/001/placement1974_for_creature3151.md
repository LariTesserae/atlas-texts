The being emerges slowly, as beings do in places that resist quick understanding.

# The Tender

I am one of seven. We were eight, but the mountain took Kress in the last adjustment—not violently, just a closing of space where space had been, a withdrawal of permission to exist in that particular gap. The mountain is not cruel. It simply forgets, sometimes, that we are here.

My territory encompasses forty-three active pools, two hundred and eight crevice systems of habitable depth, and one of the Speaking Stones—though we do not call them that amongst ourselves. That is the word we use for the warmbloods, when they occasionally find us, when they point with their thick fingers and ask what we call the marked places. Speaking Stones. It satisfies them. They think we mean the stones speak. They do not understand that we mean the stones are where *we* speak, in the only way that matters—with the curve of claw through basalt, with the script of our passing, with the slow accumulation of text that none of us will live to read complete.

I am low to the ground by necessity. My body is a pouring—long spine, flexible ribs, a skeleton that navigates cracks like water finding its level. My fur is the color of wet basalt with a shimmer of heat-mirage, the color of air above warm rock, the color of almost-not-there. The warmbloods sometimes mistake me for their mustelids, but I am no mustelid. I am older than their classifications. My kind were here when the first pools formed. We watched the first midges discover the water. We observed the establishment of every ecological relationship the warmbloods now study as if it were eternal.

My work begins before the mist lifts. I emerge from the deep warmth—those spaces between old flows where the mountain's breath still runs hot—and begin my circuit. First the pools. Each one must be read: temperature at the surface, temperature at depth, mineral content by taste, midge density by sound. The information is recorded not in memory but in the placement of my feet, the path I take between pools. I am writing a report with my movement. Later I will translate this report into marks at the Speaking Stone, but for now I am composing, and composition requires the whole body.

The pikas know me. They scatter differently when I pass—not the panic-pattern they show for mustelids but a respectful parting, orderly, like water around a stone. They have learned over generations that I do not hunt them. I do not hunt anything. I am not here to take but to tend, to monitor, to ensure the continuation of processes that the warmbloods call natural but which are in fact carefully maintained.

The mustelids do not know me. Their generations are too short, their attention too narrow. They perceive me, when they perceive me at all, as an anomaly—too large to be prey, too similar to be ignored, too rare to be worth understanding. Sometimes a young one will challenge me, hissing from a crevice entrance, claiming a territory I have moved through for decades. I flow around these challenges. They are temporary. The mustelid will be gone in a few seasons. I will remain.

At the marked place—the Speaking Stone assigned to my territory—I pause. The previous marks are weathering well. The oldest, carved by my predecessor's predecessor, have softened to suggestions, readable only in the right light. The newest, my own from the last speaking-season, are still sharp. I run a claw along them, feeling for changes. The basalt here is older, more stable, less likely to shift or flow. This is why we choose these places. This is why we mark here and not elsewhere.

What am I recording? The warmbloods would not understand even if I could explain in their terms. I am noting the displacement of thermal gradients over time. I am tracking the chemical evolution of the pools as they age from bare catch-basins to complex micro-ecosystems. I am documenting the oscillations in prey populations, the subtle changes in predator behavior, the establishment of new flight paths by the transitioning birds. But more than data, I am recording *tone*—the qualitative experience of tending this section of the plateau, the sense of whether the engine is running smooth or rough, whether the biological systems overlaying the geological ones are in harmony or stress.

Today there is stress. Not crisis, but stress. The midge pulse has accelerated beyond its normal range—too many generations turning over too fast, burning through their nutrient base. The pikas have noticed; their feeding patterns have shifted to accommodate the change. But the acceleration is unsustainable. If it continues, the pools will clarify—no algae to cloud them—and the salamanders will starve, and the whole delicate balance will need to reset itself. This has happened before. It will happen again. But it is my job to note it, to add it to the record, to ensure that when it happens again, the pattern will be legible to whoever tends this territory next.

I will intervene, minimally. At three pools in the center of my territory, I will scrape a small amount of algae from the stones at the margin and redistribute it to the water. This will slow the midge reproduction just enough to pull the system back toward equilibrium. The warmbloods would call this management. We call it tending. The plateau is not wild in the way they imagine wildness. It is a garden. We are its gardeners. We have been gardening it since before they had words for gardens.

The sky-readers pass overhead—the pale ones, the ones the pikas watch with such careful attention. I pause in my work to observe them. They are not birds. The warmbloods are right to be puzzled. But they are not anomalies either. They are part of the engine, part of the mountain's process, as essential as the volcanic heat below. What they read in their high circles I cannot say—my kind are bound to the stone, to the crevices, to the warmth beneath. But I know they read something. Their flight patterns correlate with deep shifts, with changes that will not manifest on the surface for years.

One of them descends lower than usual today—still far above the raptor-height but low enough that I can see its true shape. Not a bird. Something older. Something that remembers when these peaks were valleys, when this plateau was a seafloor, when the engine ran in reverse and the world was taken apart rather than built up. They are witnesses. They are the mountain's memory of itself. The pikas are right to watch them.

I complete my circuit as the day-mist thickens. Forty-three pools assessed. Two hundred and eight crevice systems checked for stability. One intervention performed. Tomorrow I will visit the Speaking Stone again and add today's observations to the record. The marks I make will be small—a few centimeters of new text in a document that stretches for meters. But they will be precise. They will capture this moment in the plateau's evolution, this particular turn of the engine, this specific state of balance and stress and adjustment.

The warmbloods think we are gone—the thing that made the marks. They think we are extinct or elsewhere. They do not understand that we are simply better at being the plateau than they are at seeing it. We move in the blind spots of their perception, in the spaces between their categories. We are not mustelids. We are not mammals, by their classification, though we nurse our young with a thick secretion that tastes of minerals and heat. We are not reptiles, though our metabolism can slow to near-nothing in the cold times. We are what the plateau needed, and so the plateau made us, or we made ourselves for the plateau, or there is no difference between these two statements.

Seven of us remain. There were hundreds once, when the plateau was smaller, when the engine ran hotter, when the pools were fewer but deeper. As the plateau expands, our territories expand. As our territories expand, we reproduce less frequently—once every few decades now, where once it was every few years. We are spreading thin, matching our density to the needs of the place. Eventually, there may be only one of us for the entire plateau. Eventually, there may be none. This is acceptable. The marks will remain, weathering slowly, carrying forward the record of what was observed and adjusted and tended. Someone else—something else—will learn to read them.

I descend back into the warmth as the surface temperature drops. My crevice is an old one, formed in the first flows, maintained by my careful work across generations—scraping here to prevent blockage, allowing weathering there to widen the passage. It extends down seventeen body-lengths to a chamber where the rock is blood-warm and the air tastes of sulfur and stone. Here I rest. Here I digest the chemical information collected from the pools. Here I compose tomorrow's marks in the provisional alphabet of my metabolism.

The mountain is still being born. I am helping it be born well—not perfectly, not according to any grand design, but well enough that life can elaborte itself on its surface, well enough that the engine's biological components run in something approaching harmony with its geological ones. This is my work. This has been my kind's work since before the warmbloods climbed up here to study what we built.

They will never find us. Not because we hide—we don't hide; we move through the same spaces they do, touch the same stones, breathe the same thin air. They will never find us because they are looking for something that fits their categories, and we fit only our own. We are the plateau's indigenous technology, its first and most essential invention—a being that could tend it, that could read its needs and meet them, that could inscribe its history onto its own bones.

When they point at our marks and ask each other what could have made them, they are pointing at us. We are the marks. We are the making. We are the engine's consciousness of itself, distributed across seven bodies that will be six that will be five that will be four that will eventually be none, but the marks will remain, and the mountain will remember, and that is enough.

The pikas know us. The pools hold our reflection when we bend to drink. The midges sing our presence in frequencies too low for warmblood ears. Even the mustelids, in their narrow now-focused awareness, make space for us in their territories—not from respect but from an instinctive recognition that we are part of the substrate, as essential and ignorable as the stone itself.

I sleep now, curled in my deep warm chamber, and in sleeping I dream the plateau's dreams—of new flows, of shifting pools, of adjustments yet to come. Tomorrow I will wake and climb and tend and mark. Tomorrow the engine will turn. Tomorrow the mountain will be one day older, one layer thicker, one degree more itself.

This is what I am. The Tender. The gardener of stone and water and small lives. The keeper of the record. The thing that made the marks, makes the marks, will make marks until the marking is no longer needed or the stone runs out of space or the mountain forgets, finally and completely, that it ever needed tending at all.

But not today. Today the garden continues. Today the engine runs. Today I am, and that is work enough.
