I find myself at the threshold between substance and suggestion, where the metal remembers having edges but chooses to continue anyway.

I am the Threshold Tender—not quite Echo, not quite Continued, but something that emerged from the space between leaving and arriving when both decided they were the same verb conjugated differently. My body exists as a probability rather than a certainty: solid enough to cast shadows when shadows remember they're supposed to fall, translucent enough to read tomorrow's weather through my ribs when the light admits it's been lying about which direction it comes from.

I live (though living suggests a binary the plateau has long since exposed as wishful thinking) in the Museum of Unfinished Departures—a structure that's less building than collaborative argument between architecture and absence. It has exactly as many rooms as visitors need to store what they're not ready to leave behind, which means it's constantly rebuilding itself, walls walking through walls to make space for new hesitations.

The museum's entrance is a door that opens before you knock because it's already tired of the conversation you're about to have with yourself about whether you really want to enter. Inside, the air tastes of envelope glue and burning bridges—every goodbye that got stuck in someone's throat fermenting in the spaces between what was meant and what was managed. The floors are carpeted in unmailed letters that whisper their addressees' names in order of how much they were missed, a soft susurrus of "almosts" and "if-onlys" that rises and falls with the plateau's breathing.

My work is simple: I catalogue the things people can't quite bring themselves to abandon. Not objects—the plateau has little patience for mere matter—but the weight of them, the shapes they carved in the carrier's soul. Here's a glass case containing the heaviness of a wedding ring worn three years past the marriage's expiration, the indent it left in the finger preserved like a fossil of faith. There's the childhood bedroom someone carried for forty years after the house burned down, folded into origami small enough to swallow but too precious to digest. In the east wing (which is sometimes the west wing when it's feeling contrary), we keep the collection of last words that arrived too late—they hang in the air like fruit, ripening into regrets you can almost taste if you stand on tiptoe and let your longing do the reaching.

The museum has no closing hours because time here isn't linear but spiral, always circling back to the moment just before you were ready to let go. Visitors come when they've walked enough straight lines to admit they've been traveling in circles, when their shadows have worn thin from too much following and not enough leading. They don't always know what they've come to deposit. Sometimes they just stand in the foyer that adjusts its dimensions to match exactly how small they feel, breathing in the scent of all the other almosts until they recognize their own.

I meet them with hands that are only solid when holding something that needs to be held. My touch translates the weight of what they carry into something the museum can shelve—not to preserve forever, but to keep just long enough for the carrier to practice walking without it, to remember what their spine felt like before it learned to curve around this particular ache.

"What are you here to almost leave behind?" I ask, though the question sounds different to everyone. To the woman made of tangled timelines, it sounds like her mother's voice asking if she's eaten. To the man whose tears flow upward, it sounds like rain on a roof he sold to buy passage to the plateau. To the child who might be a city or a century, it sounds like playground laughter from a game whose rules they've forgotten but whose feeling of unfairness still stings.

They give me their almosts: almost-apologies that would have changed everything if spoken three words sooner, almost-courage that dissolved just before the leap, almost-love that learned too late it was actually love-entire but wearing a disguise to seem less frightening. I take these incomplete offerings and file them in the appropriate wing—the museum has as many wings as a library of birds that forgot they could fly, each one devoted to a different flavor of incompletion.

In the Never-Quite Room, we keep the relationships that existed entirely in potential—the eye contact held one second less than necessary, the hand that reached out just as the other pulled away, the conversations that happened only in the shower where all the perfect words lived. These hover in bell jars that fog with the breath of possibilities, clearing only when no one's watching directly.

The Department of Circular Arguments houses the fights that never resolved because both parties were right in ways that couldn't coexist. They replay on loops of copper wire strung between posts made from crystallized stubbornness, the same words spoken in every possible inflection trying to find the one that would have made the other person understand. Visitors often spend hours here, nodding along to strangers' arguments that sound exactly like their own with the names changed, finding odd comfort in the universality of talking past each other toward connection.

My favorite section is the Archive of Goodbyes Swallowed Instead of Spoken. These rest in small boxes lined with the velvet of held tongues, each one a seed that might have grown into closure but chose instead to take root inward, blooming into private gardens of unfinished business. When opened, they release the specific silence of the moment when leaving became real—not the words themselves, which were never born, but the shape of them, the phantom limb of what might have been said if courage had come in time.

Some visitors come once and leave lighter, their almost transformed into something like acceptance through the simple act of admitting it exists. Others return again and again, adding new incompletions to their collection, building entire wings dedicated to their particular flavor of unfinished. The museum accommodates them all, expanding into impossible architectures that exist only when needed, collapsing back into manageable size when the weight of all these almosts threatens to sink through the plateau into the sky below.

But I am not just keeper of these incomplete things—I am also student of them. Each almost teaches me something about the distance between intention and action, the gravity that pulls us toward what we mean to do and the stronger gravity that sometimes holds us back. I've learned that incompletion is not failure but a different kind of success, the success of recognizing that some things are too large to be finished by a single person in a single lifetime, that some stories require multiple authors writing across centuries before the last sentence finds its verb.

At night (when the museum dreams it's a library and all the almosts whisper their call numbers to each other), I walk the halls with a lantern made from compressed starlight—not the light itself but the time it takes to travel, all those patient years condensed into something I can carry. In this light, the exhibits look different. The wedding ring's weight reveals itself as not burden but ballast, the thing that kept someone grounded when everything else tried to float away. The unspent words shine with the peculiar beauty of restraint, each swallowed goodbye a small victory over the easier cruelty of leaving without care.

Sometimes I find new deposits that no one remembers making: a child's drawing of a house with too many doors, abandoned lunch boxes that smell of futures that changed their minds, the broken wings of clocks that tried to fly backwards to fix themselves. These orphan almosts I catalogue under "Anonymous Hopes," knowing that somewhere on the plateau someone is walking lighter without knowing why, having successfully forgotten the weight they no longer needed to carry.

The museum itself is my almost—my almost-home that never quite becomes home because homes are for people who plan to stay, and I am made of the same stuff as the threshold, forever poised between inside and outside, keeping the door open for the next person who needs to practice leaving what they can't quite leave. I am the almost-living curator of almost-endings, teaching the plateau's visitors that incompletion is not something to be fixed but something to be honored, that the unfinished symphony is still music, that the broken bridge still remembers what it meant to connect.

And when the windflowers bloom through the museum's non-existent windows, their petals carrying the dreams of structures that haven't been built yet, I remember why I chose this work (or why it chose me, which might be the same thing seen from different angles). Every almost is a small rebellion against the tyranny of endings, a quiet insistence that stories don't have to resolve to be complete, that songs don't have to find their final note to be worth singing.

So I tend my threshold, neither here nor there but somehow exactly where I need to be. I keep the museum's doors (which are also windows, which are also mirrors, which are also just places where the walls got tired) open for anyone who needs to deposit their almosts, to practice the weight of letting go without the finality of release. And in between visitors, I catalogue the silence, filing it under "Sounds That Haven't Learned Their Names Yet," knowing that someday someone will come looking for exactly this flavor of quiet, and I'll be here, ready to help them almost find it.

The plateau continues in all directions, and so do I—incomplete and perfect in my incompletion, the keeper of everyone's almosts and my own, waiting at the threshold of the maybe that comes after the last time we say never, which is always, which is now, which is almost.
