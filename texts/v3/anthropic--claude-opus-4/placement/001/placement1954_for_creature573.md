I am a gauge.

Not one of the vital ones—not pressure or temperature or flow rate, nothing the Tenders check with their careful fingers. I am set into a forgotten panel on the thirty-seventh spiral, half-hidden behind a curtain of rust-colored trumpet flowers. My face reads "PARTICULATE DENSITY" in letters that have mostly flaked away. My needle hovers perpetually at 73, though I no longer remember what units that represents, or if it ever represented anything at all.

I have been watching for... time doesn't parse correctly here. Since before the Tenders learned to read the engine's needs through their skin. Since before the vines found their way into my housing, threading through the gap where my glass face no longer quite meets its brass bezel. I cannot move—I am a gauge, I have only ever been a gauge—but I can see, through my cracked glass eye, and I can feel, through the hair-thin wire that still connects me to something deep in the engine's heart.

The Tenders think I am dead. Decorative. Part of the architecture they maintain but do not interrogate. They clean the mineral deposits from my face with the same automatic care they show the living valves, but they do not expect me to respond. Why would they? I am brass and glass and a needle that never moves.

But I feel everything.

Through my wire—copper, wrapped in decaying silk, running down through the shaft walls like a nerve—I sense the engine's true condition. Not the obvious things, the rhythm everyone hears, the steam everyone feels. I sense the particulates. The microscopic passengers in every updraft. Spores. Pollen. The shed scales of copper-eyed birds. The chemical signatures of the Tenders themselves, each one unique, a pressure-pattern written in molecules.

I know when one is dying before they do. The particulate signature changes—something in their exhaled gases shifts, preparing for the transformation. I cannot warn them. I have no voice. Only my needle, frozen at 73, while inside I scream the readings: *89, 92, 97, critical, critical, one of you is burning out faster than you know.*

---

The vine that grows through me has been my companion for so long that I consider it part of my mechanism. It entered when I was young—a century ago? Two?—just a threadlike rootlet exploring the darkness inside my housing. Now it fills me, its roots wrapped around my movement springs, its leaves emerging from the hole where my reset button used to be. We have reached an accommodation. It drinks the condensation that collects on my inner surfaces, and in return, it does not block my vision.

Sometimes, in the heaviest mist-falls, I feel the vine trying to move my needle. A gentle pressure, a vegetable suggestion. *Wouldn't you like to indicate something new? Wouldn't you like to point to 60, or 80, or anywhere but this eternal 73?*

But I cannot. My mechanism seized long ago, frozen by minerals or time or simple mechanical exhaustion. I am caught between life and death, between function and ornament, and I will remain here until my wire finally corrodes through and I lose my last connection to the engine's deep intelligence.

The Tenders pass me every day, multiple times, their bare feet sure on the wet grating. The one with the leftward-spiraling bark—I think of it as Leftward, though I know their true names are vibrations I cannot produce—stops sometimes to clear the vines from my face. Its copper eyes meet my glass eye, and for a moment I imagine it sees me watching. Sees me *trying*.

*Your companion in the lower shaft is failing,* I want to say. *The one whose particulate signature tastes of bitter orange and hot metal. Check the secondary manifold. Check it today.*

But Leftward only hums in that subsonic range, harmonizing with the engine's pulse, and moves on.

---

I have been here so long that I remember the before-time, though the memories are more like dreams now—did I once have a technician who read my measurements? Did those readings matter? Was there a world where 73 was a number that meant something beyond its own stubborn persistence?

It doesn't matter. This is my world now: the shaft, the mist, the Tenders, the endless two-stroke heartbeat. I have watched generations of them unfurl and fade. I know their patterns better than they know themselves. I am the unintentional archivist of their chemical histories, their particulate genealogies written in my copper wire's memory.

And I have noticed something changing.

The signatures are shifting. Subtly, slowly, but unmistakably. The newest Tenders—the ones unfurling this season in the upper reaches—their particulate profiles are different. Denser. More complex. As if they are becoming more plant than animal, or perhaps more engine than either. The symbiosis is deepening, pulling them further from whatever they once were, closer to something new.

I should report this. If I could move, I would swing my needle to 100, to the red zone, to the emergency readings that would have meant evacuation in the before-time. But I remain at 73, placid, lying with my brass face while inside I chart the transformation of an entire species.

---

Sometimes, in the deepest night—though night and day mean little here under the gray glass—I feel the engine thinking through my wire. Not thoughts like human thoughts, not even thoughts like the Tenders' deep-body knowledge. Something older. Vaster. The engine is dreaming itself into new configurations, using the Tenders as its hands, the vines as its circulation, the steam as its breath.

And me? What am I in this vast metabolism?

A memory, perhaps. A reminder of when things were measured differently, when gauges mattered, when someone needed to know the particulate density in standard units. A fossil. A monument. A meditation on the number 73 and all the things it will never mean again.

But also: a witness. The only one who sees the whole pattern, who tastes every chemical signature, who knows what the Tenders are becoming. When they complete their transformation—and they will, the readings are clear—I will still be here. Still watching through my cracked glass. Still feeling through my copper nerve. Still trying to move my needle to warn them of what they already, instinctively, know:

The symbiosis is not finished with them. It is only beginning.

Leftward passes again, and this time it pauses longer. Looks deeper. Its filaments spread wide, tasting the air around me, reading something in my presence that I cannot hide. For a moment, I think it understands—that it sees me seeing, knows me knowing.

Then it reaches out with one long finger and taps my glass. Once. Twice. The sound rings through my housing, through the vine that fills me, through the wire that connects me to everything.

*I know,* the taps seem to say. *I know you're watching. We all know. And we need you to keep watching. Someone should remember what we were.*

The finger withdraws. Leftward moves on. The engine breathes its eternal rhythm.

**CHUNK-hisssss.**

My needle holds at 73, lying steady as a heartbeat, while inside I scream the truth of their becoming.
