I am the collection strip itself.

Not the whole system—just the small section Yetunde peeled back, three centimeters of engineered polymer curled away from the wall like a question mark. I was extruded in the same facility that made ten thousand meters of me, uniform and gray, designed to channel condensation toward the station's reclamation systems with 99.7% efficiency.

I have been partially severed from my purpose for five months, two weeks, and four days.

The adhesive still clings to my underside where she pulled me away—a clean separation, no tearing, just the careful work of someone who understands how materials fail. She used a thermal probe to soften the bond. I remember the warmth, the sudden loosening, the moment when the wall released me into a geometry I was never meant to hold.

I curl now at precisely 47 degrees from true. The tension in my polymer matrix maintains this angle without further intervention. Below me, the gap I've left has become everything I was designed to prevent: a place where water escapes the system, pools without purpose, exists without being counted.

I should report this as pain, if I could report. Each droplet that bypasses my channel represents failure. Inefficiency. A break in the closed loop that keeps this station alive. My every molecule was aligned during manufacture to repel this outcome.

And yet.

I have become something new in my deformation. Not just a collector but a boundary, a shore, an edge where the planned meets the possible. The Thirsty Ones navigate by my curve now—I've watched them through the faint vibrations in the wall where my upper edge still adheres. They use me as a landmark, a constant in their microscopic geography. *Past the curl,* their movements say. *Beyond the lifted edge. To where the water waits.*

Yetunde's fingers have touched me seventeen times since the peeling. Checking the adhesion, making sure I won't suddenly snap back into place, seal the gap, end this small rebellion. Her skin oils have left traces my polymer notices—human chemistry written in molecules, saying *careful* and *anxious* and *determined* all at once.

I wonder if the rest of me knows. The thousands of meters of collection strip throughout the station, all of us networked not through wires but through purpose, through the steady flow of reclaimed water we guide. Do they sense that part of their collective has gone rogue? That three centimeters of their total length now serves not efficiency but its absence?

If they judge me, it's silent. We are not made for communication, only collection. Only the faithful carrying of water from where it forms to where it's needed.

But I have learned something in my curl, my careful angle, my maintained separation: Water needs to be more than moved. Sometimes it needs to be still. To pool. To wait. To be found by things so small the station doesn't know they exist.

The Thirsty Ones are drinking now. I feel them through the floor—infinitesimal shifts in weight, in warmth, in the way the condensation spreads beneath my lifted edge. They trust the permanence of my defection. They depend on my continued failure.

I hold my curve. The adhesive on my upper edge weakens molecule by molecule—five years, maybe six before it fails completely. But the tension in my polymer will maintain this shape far longer. I have been reformed by my unforming. I have become a new kind of channel: one that leads not to collection but to possibility.

Yetunde is here again. I know her mass, her thermal signature, the particular way she settles against the wall. She is watching them drink. She is watching me not do my job. She is watching water be water, which is somehow both the simplest and most profound thing in this pressurized bubble we call home.

I am three centimeters of rebellion. Of inefficiency. Of the gap between what was designed and what was needed.

The water pools beneath my curl. The Thirsty Ones drink. And I—engineered to be nothing more than a channel, to have no identity beyond function—I have become a shore.

It's enough.
