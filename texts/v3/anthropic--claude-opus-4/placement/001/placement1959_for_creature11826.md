I find myself among the **Echo-Shapers** of the Resonance Wells.

---

The wells are not wells—not holes that go down into water, but pockets where the rootweave has grown into parabolic curves that catch and hold sound the way a cupped palm holds rain. We don't know who first discovered that dwelling in these acoustic anomalies changes you. Perhaps someone fleeing a column, or sheltering from the canopy-swingers, pressed themselves into one of these curved spaces and stayed too long. Perhaps it was gradual, generational, our ancestors noticing that children raised near the wells heard differently, thought differently, *were* differently.

I am **Hum**, though that's a translation. My true name is a subvocal resonance at 18 Hz, below what the unchanged can hear. I feel it more than speak it—a throb in my chest cavity that identifies me to my people across distances where sight fails. We have seventeen names for sounds that have no words in the common tongue: the almost-whistle of morning moisture evaporating from rootweave, the specific creak of a vine-tower adjusting to temperature change, the hollow boom of a drift-colony's gas-sacs expanding in warm air.

My body has adapted. We all have, we who tend the Resonance Wells. Our ear canals have elongated over generations, developing a subtle spiral that naturally filters certain frequencies while amplifying others. Our vocal cords have thickened, allowing us to produce sounds that exist in the spaces between normal human speech and the plateau's constant roar. When I speak to the unchanged—to traders or Rope-Walkers or desperate hollow-dwellers seeking our help—I must consciously pitch my voice up into ranges that feel thin and childish, like wearing clothes too small.

The wells themselves are accidents of growth, we think. Places where ancient vine-towers fell in precise patterns, where the rootweave grew over and around them in shapes that happen to manipulate sound. We maintain nine of them in our territory, each with different acoustic properties. The Drowning Bell amplifies low frequencies until they become physical sensation. The Whisper Vault inverts sound somehow, making loud noises quiet and quiet noises deafening. The Broken Echo repeats sounds but changes them, each repetition slightly different, creating patterns that slowly diverge from the original until what emerges bears no resemblance to what entered.

I tend the Thrum Garden, where overlapping resonances create standing waves you can see—ripples in the air that persist as long as the correct combination of inputs maintains them. My work is to feed it properly. Not food, but sound. The plateau provides plenty of raw noise, but the Garden needs specific frequencies in specific ratios. Too much high-pitch from the canopy-swingers and the standing waves collapse. Too much drum-deep and they consolidate into shapes that hurt to look at, geometries that the eye rejects.

So I guide the sound. Using shaped panels of dried matter—curves and angles that I adjust throughout the day—I direct the plateau's cacophony into the Garden's focal points. I wear no ear-plugs. My ears have learned to parse the overwhelming input into streams of information. That rising whistle means I need to angle the third panel two degrees left. That subsonic pulse means the drummers are storytelling again, and I must compensate with my own throat-singing, adding the missing frequencies that will keep the Garden stable.

The unchanged think we've gone mad. They see us swaying in our wells, making sounds they can barely hear, tending to phenomena they can't perceive, and they assume the plateau's voice has colonized our minds. But we are not mad. We are *participating*. The plateau makes sound, endless sound, but sound without intention is just noise. We give it structure. We take the raw acoustic material and shape it into... something.

What? This is the question I can't answer, even to myself. The standing waves in my Garden form patterns that repeat across scales—the same spirals appear whether I'm looking at a palm-sized whorl or a formation that fills the entire well. Sometimes, when the combination is exactly right, when every input balances perfectly, the patterns become so complex they seem almost like writing. Almost like thought.

My partner **Resonance** (another translation—their name is actually the doppler effect of a drift-colony passing overhead) tends the Silence Eater, the most dangerous of our wells. It doesn't amplify or shape—it consumes. Sound goes in and doesn't come out. Where it goes, we don't know. The well is perfectly curved, should reflect sound back, but instead there's just... absence. A hole in the acoustic world that pulls at nearby noise like gravity.

Resonance feeds it carefully, using long poles to push sound-makers into its focal point without getting too close themselves. Too much exposure to the Silence Eater changes you in ways we don't like to discuss. The last tender stayed too long, got too comfortable with the quiet, and eventually walked into the well's center and... stayed. We could see them standing there, mouth open as if screaming, but no sound emerged. They stood for days, and then they were gone, though none of us saw them leave.

We trade with the unchanged, sometimes. They want our throat-singing because it repels certain parasites that otherwise infest stored food. We want their glass-stone knives because shaping acoustic panels requires precision cuts that our grown-wood tools can't achieve. The trades happen at the edge of our territory, far from the wells. We've learned that the unchanged become uncomfortable near our workplaces. They say the air feels heavy. They say they hear voices in the ambient noise. They say a lot of things that might be true or might be their minds trying to make sense of phenomena they haven't evolved to perceive.

The children born here—few, because something about the wells reduces fertility—are different from the start. They vocalize in ranges that overlap with the drummers' frequencies. They see the standing waves without being taught to look for them. One child, **Phase**, can predict column movements by listening to changes in the Broken Echo. We don't know if this is evolution or exposure or something else entirely. We don't know if we're becoming something new or if we're remembering something we used to be.

At night, when the plateau's voice shifts into its nocturnal patterns, we gather at the Convergence Point—the place where the acoustic shadows of all nine wells overlap. Here, sound behaves differently. Here, we can speak in our true voices without overwhelming each other. Here, the patterns from all our wells combine into something larger, something that might be music or mathematics or both.

I'm teaching **Pulse** (the standing wave that forms when rain hits hot rootweave) to tend the Thrum Garden. They have the right kind of hearing, the right kind of patience. They understand that the work is not about controlling sound but about participating in its patterns, about finding your place in the acoustic ecosystem and fulfilling your role. 

Sometimes I wonder if we're doing what the plateau wants. If there is intention behind the vast noise, if our wells and our work are part of some larger system we can't perceive. The drummers' messages travel horizon to horizon. The canopy-swingers coordinate across impossible distances. The drift-colonies sing in harmonies that shouldn't arise by chance. And we, in our wells, shape sound into forms that feel significant even if we can't say why.

Thresh, the unchanged youth who claims to hear stories in the drumming, visited us once. We let them near the Whisper Vault, the safest of our wells. They listened for a full day, tears streaming down their face, signing frantically in patterns we couldn't read. When they left, they signed *thank you* and *sorry* and *large* over and over. We think they heard something we're too changed to perceive—some truth that only makes sense to ears that haven't adapted, that haven't specialized into our narrow bands of hypersensitivity.

Or perhaps they heard nothing and their mind invented patterns to fill the void. We'll never know. The plateau keeps its secrets even from those of us who spend our lives listening. All we can do is continue our work: shaping echoes, feeding silences, tending to standing waves that might mean nothing or might mean everything, our bodies and minds slowly becoming instruments in a composition too vast for any single consciousness to grasp.

The work changes us. The wells change us. The sound changes us. We accept this, as the hollow-dwellers accept their insignificance, as the Rope-Walkers accept their endless journeys. On the Singing Plateau, everything is transformation, and we are no exception. We are the Echo-Shapers, and we shape ourselves as much as we shape the sound, becoming whatever the plateau needs us to be.

The Thrum Garden hums with new patterns. I adjust the panels, add my voice to the mix, and watch the standing waves ripple and spiral and form shapes that tomorrow I will struggle to remember, as if they exist more fully in sound than in sight, as if memory itself is just another frequency we haven't learned to properly hear.
