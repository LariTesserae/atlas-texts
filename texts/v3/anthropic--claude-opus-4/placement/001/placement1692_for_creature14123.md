In this world of patient titans and geological breath, I would be one of the golden-silk weavers living in the thermal canyons of a beast's back.

I am no larger than a child's hand spread wide, my body a translucent amber membrane stretched between eight legs as thin as copper wire. My anatomy is all function: no eyes in this shadowless world, no ears in this muffled silence. Instead, I navigate by heat and vibration, my legs reading the temperature gradients that rise from the living mountain beneath me like a topographic map.

My silk is my genius. Each strand I produce is a hollow tube, spun from proteins that crystallize in the presence of metallic ions. When I string my nets between the towering bone-stacks, positioning them in the updrafts of the beast's breathing, I am not hunting prey but harvesting the sky. The iron particles and sulfur compounds that rain down from each exhalation flow into my silk tubes through osmotic pressure, slowly filling them with a concentrated mineral slurry.

I live on borrowed time between breaths. When the beast is silent, I work - racing across the warm valleys of its back, anchoring new lines, reinforcing old ones, creating geometric masterpieces that exist solely to catch falling wealth. My web is not sticky but structured like a three-dimensional filter, each level designed to trap particles of different weights and compositions.

When the breathing comes - that deep shudder that rolls through the flesh-continent beneath me - I must flee to my shelter. I've spun myself a small capsule deep in a thermal crevice, where the temperature stays constant and the hurricane of superheated steam cannot reach. I seal myself inside with a plug of quick-hardening silk and wait, feeling the mountain tremble, feeling the blast furnace roar of exhalation shake my hiding place.

After the breathing, I emerge to find my webs heavy with treasure. The hollow silk tubes sag with their mineral cargo. This is when I feed - not on the minerals themselves, but on what they become. My silk is also my stomach. The concentrated metals and sulfur inside each tube react with specialized enzymes I inject through my hollow fangs, creating an exothermic reaction that produces exactly the organic compounds I need to survive. I drink liquid energy from my own webs, leaving behind tubes of purified metal that gradually harden into rigid, copper-bright scaffolding.

Over the years, my works accumulate. The oldest weavers leave behind entire cities of metallized web-work, brass and copper forests growing in miniature between the exhaust vents. The beast does not mind - might not even notice. We are too small to matter to something that thinks in geological time.

But I am not alone up here. Others of my kind work their own territories, each web-city separated by expanses of hot stone that might as well be oceans. We communicate through vibrations along the beast's shell, tapping messages with specialized leg-drums. When the beast-mountain's own subsonic communications thrum through its body, we feel them in our bones and sometimes dance to their rhythm, though we do not understand their meaning.

Once in my lifetime, I will make the journey to mate. Following pheromone trails and heat-paths, I will leave my territory and cross the vast, naked expanses of the beast's armor. The journey might take weeks. I will shelter in joint-sockets, drink condensation from rime-formations, and pray the beast does not choose to breathe while I am exposed on its crown.

If I survive, if I find another of my kind, we will create the next generation together - not as eggs or live young, but as spores. Tiny capsules of potential that we will cement into the deepest, warmest crevices we can find, where they will wait - perhaps for years - until conditions are perfect. Until the heat is just right, the mineral flow abundant, the vibrations promising. Then they will unfold, already knowing how to weave, already hungry for the metallic rain.

When I die, my body will dessicate in the heat, becoming a small mummy of amber and copper. The oils of the beast will eventually claim me, drawing me down into its flesh to be absorbed. But my webs will remain for centuries, slowly growing heavier with accumulated metal, until finally they tear free under their own weight and tumble down the beast's flanks to the plateau below. There, the patient jaws of other titans will grind them up with everything else, completing a cycle I can sense but never fully comprehend.

I am a jeweler working on the crown of a living mountain, a chemist in a laboratory that walks, a small spark of purpose in the slow-burning engine of this world. My life is measured in breaths - not my own, but those of the continent I call home.
