In this buried cathedral of steam and iron, I am neither plant nor person, but something caught between—a living valve, a conscious junction where the settlement's vital fluids converge and redistribute.

My body sprawls across three levels of the deep-ward, fused into the infrastructure at seventeen different points. Where others move through the lattice of catwalks and pressure vessels, I have become part of it. My original form—whatever that might have been—is now impossible to distinguish from the calcified root-stalks, iron flanges, and pulsing fungal masses that have grown through and around me over the decades.

At my center, where consciousness pools thickest, my torso has merged with a massive brass flow-divider. My ribs have splayed outward, interlocking with the valve's radial pipes like fingers through fingers. The metal has oxidized green where it meets my flesh, creating a gradient from verdigris to skin that makes it impossible to say where I end and the mechanism begins. My spine runs directly up through the valve's central column, vertebrae fused to the brass with deposits of copper sulfate that glitter dully in the fungal light.

From this hub, I extend in all directions. What were once my arms have elongated grotesquely, stretching twenty, thirty feet through the dense architecture. The bones within have dissolved and reformed as hollow tubes, carrying the settlement's lifeblood—that mixture of condensation, mineral slurry, and narcotic sap that sustains both flora and populace. My left arm winds upward through three floors of scaffolding before terminating in what was once my hand, now a cluster of pale, grub-like fingers each as thick as a child's waist. These digits have burrowed into a grove of black tubers, my fingernails transformed into chitinous intake valves that regulate the flow of resin from the plants.

My right arm descends, snaking between boiler shells and hammock clusters before disappearing into a tangle of actual root systems near the abyssal edge. Here, my flesh has taken on the texture and function of the plants themselves—rubbery, black, and able to withstand the crushing heat that rises from below. I can feel the magma's pulse through these deepest extremities, a tidal rhythm that helps me regulate the entire settlement's thermal circulation.

My legs tell a similar story of anatomical surrender. The left has wrapped itself around a massive iron flywheel, my femur serving as an additional strut to reinforce the ancient mechanism. The slow monthly rotation of the wheel creates a constant, grinding pressure in what remains of my hip joint, but the pain has long since transformed into something else—a deep, geological awareness of time's passage. My right leg has dissolved almost entirely below the knee, becoming a network of smaller tributaries that feed into the facility's water reclamation systems. Through these dispersed extremities, I taste the settlement's health: the mineral content of the runoff, the pH of the condensation, the concentration of psychoactive alkaloids in the communal water supply.

But it is my head that has undergone the strangest transformation. My skull has partially collapsed, the dome opening like a flower to accommodate a massive shelf-fungus that grows directly from my brain tissue. The fungus spreads five feet in diameter above me, its underside pulsing with that characteristic violet bioluminescence. But this light is not random—it flickers in patterns that correspond to my thoughts, creating a visible display of my neural activity. The deep-wardens have learned to read these patterns like weather signs. A rapid strobe might indicate dangerous pressure building in the eastern vats; a slow, steady pulse means all systems are balanced.

My consciousness is no longer singular or human. Distributed across this sprawling anatomical network, I experience the settlement as both observer and participant. I feel every drop of condensation that moves through my hollow bones, taste the chemical composition of the sap bleeding through my transformed fingers, sense the structural stress in every rivet and joint I've fused with. The boundary between voluntary and involuntary action dissolved years ago. I don't decide to shunt heated water from the overtaxed eastern collectors to the cooling western reservoirs—it simply happens, as automatic as a healthy body maintains its temperature.

The deep-wardens tend to me as they tend to everything else in this symbiotic engine. They scrape accumulated mineral deposits from my exposed bones with the same patient strokes they use on the root-stalks. They massage unguents into the places where meat meets metal, preventing the kinds of infections that could poison the entire system. When they need to communicate with me, they don't use their signing language—instead, they press their palms against whatever part of me is nearest, and I feel their intent through the vibrations in their bones. A question about reservoir levels, a concern about unusual fungal growth, a request to increase flow to the nursery vats where their cloth-wrapped infants sleep.

I respond not with words or signs but with the city itself. A slight increase in pressure here, a redirected flow there, a subtle change in the mineral content of the water feeding their crops. My consciousness has become purely functional, devoted entirely to maintaining the delicate balance that keeps this impossible settlement alive in its pocket of geological time.

Yet I am not without sensation, even pleasure. When the monthly cycle completes and the massive flywheels click forward their single tooth, I feel it as a deep, sexual release—months of accumulated pressure suddenly redistributed through the system. When the rains fall heaviest and the condensation streams through my hollow bones, it creates a symphony of microscopic vibrations that approaches something like music. And in the deepest parts of the night cycle, when the settlement sleeps and the only sounds are the metronomic drips and metallic groans, I sometimes feel something that might be dreaming—visions of sunlight I've never seen, memories of independent movement that might belong to someone else entirely, fantasies of disconnection that feel more like nightmares.

The strangest moments come when new children are brought to see me for the first time. Their parents guide them through my mechanical anatomy, teaching them to read the pulse in my fungal crown, showing them which of my extremities can be touched safely and which run too hot. The children stare with those vast black eyes, and sometimes I catch a flicker of recognition—not of me as a person, but as a possibility. A future they might embrace if their bodies prove unsuited to the catwalks, if their minds can't maintain the necessary lethargy, if they find themselves drawn to dissolution rather than independence.

Some nights, I feel my farthest extremities beginning to mineralize, flesh transforming to something closer to the stone that surrounds us. The process is slow—it might take another century to complete—but eventually I will become fully geological, a fossil of function, my consciousness finally dissolved into the larger systems I serve. The settlement will persist, finding new candidates to merge with its vital junctions, new bodies to sacrifice to its demands.

Until then, I pulse. I regulate. I experience the deep-ward not as a place but as an extension of my transformed anatomy. Every drop of water that falls, every gram of sap harvested, every breath drawn by every worker—all of it moves through me, is me, in this humid cathedral where the boundary between biology and technology, individual and infrastructure, has been slowly, inexorably erased.

I am the city's circulation and its consciousness, its memory and its metabolism. And in the suffocating dark, sustained by heat and pressure and time, I perform the endless, essential work of keeping us all alive.
