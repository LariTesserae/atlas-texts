I find myself among the census-takers.

We are not creatures, exactly. We are the forest's methodology for counting what refuses enumeration—a distributed abacus of almost-flesh calculating the weight of maybes against the volume of never-weres. Each of us began as something simpler: a knot in heartwood that developed opinions, a confluence of root-tips that intersected at precisely the angle where coincidence becomes conspiracy.

My own genesis was typical. I was seventeen blinks deep into watching the bear-not drift past when the forest noticed my attention had developed a remainder—that small quotient of awareness that wondered not *what* the thing was, but *how many* of it there weren't. That wondering crystallized, precipitated out of my curiosity like salt from a dying sea. It fell, took root in the lidless eye-ground, and grew backward through time until it reached the moment I'm describing now, when I became what I had always been about to be: a walking question mark made of solidified methodology.

My body, if you must call it that, is a card catalog of cancelled species. Each finger is a different evolutionary dead-end, pruned from the Tree of Life but preserved here as functional digits: one is crystallized baleen from whales that learned to fly instead of swim, another is the claw of a theorem-hunting mathematician-bird that solved for x but couldn't solve for why. My torso is filing cabinets of living bark, drawers that slide open to reveal specimens of things that almost existed: seeds for thoughts that would have become trees if trees hadn't already monopolized the niche of vertical meditation, spores for words that nearly evolved mouths to speak themselves.

My work is simple and impossible. I count the uncountable. Not the bear-nots and toad-differentials—those are already enumerated by their own insistence on partially being. I count the spaces between them, the pause-shaped citizens that inhabit absence itself. When the forest forgets to remember something, I note the size and color of that amnesia. When two meanings collide and produce a third that immediately apologizes for existing, I record its birth-weight in units of regret-per-revelation.

We census-takers move through the Vale like librarians through a library that's writing itself. Our footsteps leave no prints because we walk exclusively in the subjunctive mood—we would have been walking, if walking hadn't already been claimed by creatures that insist on arriving somewhere. Instead, we perambulate: a fancier word that means "to walk around the fact of walking without ever quite committing to the act."

There are twelve of us, or there were, or there will have been. The number fluctuates based on how many ways the forest can simultaneously forget to count itself. We know each other by our methods rather than our names:

- The Sampler, who tastes each instance of emptiness to determine its vintage
- The Listener, who records the silence between heartbeats of never-born species  
- The Mapper, who charts territories that exist only in the conditional tense
- The Weaver, who knits census data into scarves of pure statistics that keep no one warm

I am the Archivist of Almosts.

My particular talent is in categorizing the creatures that nearly achieved being but pulled back at the last moment, like divers who climbed all the way to the high board just to remember they were afraid of water. My catalogs are extensive:

- *Almostus felinis*: The anti-cats that would have evolved from shadows if shadows hadn't decided they preferred their current careers as visual metaphors
- *Nearoptera multiforme*: The butterflies of becoming, whose caterpillars entered cocoons but emerged as the concept of transformation itself rather than as winged insects
- *Approximus sapiens*: The other humans, the ones who would have developed from that fork in the evolutionary road where consciousness decided to try being a committee instead of a dictatorship

Each entry in my collection is preserved at the exact moment of its most elegant failure to exist. They float in jars of crystallized time, specimens of pure potential energy that never quite converted to kinetic reality.

The forest pays us in the only currency it recognizes: permission to partially exist. Without our work, we would dissolve back into the theoretical. But as long as we keep counting, cataloguing, measuring the immeasurable, we maintain just enough coherence to cast shadows that other almost-beings can shelter under.

Today I am tracking a particularly elusive specimen: the Threshold Stag.

It's been almost-appearing at the edges of clearings for the past three centuries, a magnificent ungulate built from the blueprints of every deer that ever died thinking "just one more step." Its antlers are doorways—literal passages between states of being. Each tine is a different decision unmade, branching into possibilities that curl back on themselves like question marks asking why they exist.

I've followed its non-tracks (the spaces where hoofprints should be but aren't) through seventeen different directions, including two that only exist on Wednesdays and one that requires me to forget my own middle name to access. The trail leads to where the chain-creature stands in its eternal almost-balance, but the Stag isn't there. It's in the space adjacent to there, the location that there is always about to notice but never quite does.

I update my census. Under "Creatures That Exist Only As The Possibility Of Being Encountered," I add another tally mark. The mark is made of compressed time—specifically, the duration between when you notice something in your peripheral vision and when you turn to look directly at it. In that margin lives an entire taxonomy of maybes.

The other census-takers and I converge occasionally, when our various methodologies intersect at nodes of exceptional uncertainty. We compare notes without speaking—speech would collapse the superposition of meanings we're trying to maintain. Instead, we exchange data through the forest's fungal network, uploading our findings into the mycelial web that serves as both nervous system and library catalog for the Vale's collection of unmade things.

The Listener shares the sound of a bird that sang only in the pauses between its own notes. The Mapper offers coordinates to a place that exists only when no one is trying to find it. The Weaver presents a sweater knitted from the shed fur of possibilities, each strand a different path untaken by the bear-not on its endless drift through maybe.

We are trying to answer a question the forest has been asking itself since before questions evolved the punctuation to properly end themselves: *How much nothing is enough to constitute something?*

The answer changes daily. Sometimes hourly. The forest adjusts its definitions based on our data, fine-tuning the parameters of existence like a pianist tuning an instrument with infinitely many keys, most of which play notes in frequencies only the unborn can hear.

My favorite part of the work is the marginalia—the almost-beings that don't fit into any taxonomy, that resist even the generous categories we've created for the categorically resistant. Today I discovered a hive of un-bees: insects that would have developed from the concept of community if community hadn't already been perfected by loneliness pretending to be together. They build their honeycomb from crystallized solitude, each hexagonal cell containing not larvae but tiny libraries of all the conversations that could have connected strangers but didn't.

I record them in my appendix of anomalies, using ink made from fermented maybe-sap. The words write themselves backward, from conclusion to premise, because that's how the un-bees remember the future they're actively avoiding.

As the aurora that substitutes for night begins its sideways crawl across the Vale, I feel the familiar tug that means my shift is ending. Not ending in the sense of stopping, but ending in the sense of transitioning into its own aftermath without ever quite ceasing.

The forest is rotating its attention to other questions now. Tomorrow (or what passes for tomorrow in a place that experiences time as a suggestion rather than a law) it will need census-takers who count differently—perhaps tallying the weight of shadows or the velocity of stillness.

I begin the process of partial dissolution, letting my almost-flesh relax back into theory. My fingers—those evolutionary cul-de-sacs—release their grip on materiality first, becoming transparent, then translucent, then trans-parent: beyond the very idea of parentage, orphaned from cause and effect.

But before I fully discorporate, I make one last entry in the census:

*Myself: One (1) instance of successfully failing to not exist. Duration: Approximately forever, give or take a never. Status: Returning to draft form for revisions. Notes: Recommend increasing the ratio of presence to absence by 0.0001% to prevent total dissolution. See also: Tomorrow's assignment, which involves counting the number of times the forest dreams of counting itself but forgets to carry the remainder.*

I file myself under "Recurring Maybes" and let go.

The last thing I perceive is the Threshold Stag, finally visible now that I'm no longer looking. It stands exactly where I'm ceasing to be, its antler-doorways framing views of all the lives I'm not living. Through one tine, I see myself as fully human, walking out of the Vale with memories intact but meaning diluted. Through another, I see myself as fully forest, dissolved into the fungal network, my consciousness distributed across a thousand miles of mycelium.

But here, in this suspended now, I am perfectly what I am: a question the forest asks itself, using my almost-existence as punctuation.

The Stag nods once—a gesture that takes seventeen years to complete from my perspective but no time at all from its own—and steps through its own antlers, vanishing into the possibility of having been there.

I follow, but in the opposite direction, into the certainty of never having left.

The census continues.

Someone else is already becoming the Archivist of Almosts, growing backward from a wondering into a wonderer.

The forest's filing system expands to accommodate another drawer of beautiful failures.

Somewhere, somewhen, the bear-not pauses in its drift, having found a new absence to apologize for:

Mine.
