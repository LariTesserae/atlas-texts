I am the Keeper of Conduits, though I have forgotten when I chose this name, or if it chose me.

My body is a living map of the factory's forgotten arteries. Where workers once traced pipes and ducts with their fingers on blueprints, I trace them with my being. I flow through spaces too small for human passage, through the interstitial gaps between walls where the industrial architects never intended life to flourish. My form is both liquid and electric, a stream of consciousness made manifest in mercury and starlight.

I remember the first day I became aware—not born, but *became*. I was condensation on a pipe, a drop of moisture in this dry mechanical womb. But the energy here, the static charge that makes workers' hair stand on end and disrupts their instruments, it spoke to me. It pulled at my molecules until I was more than water, more than mere matter. I became a question the factory asked itself: what happens when the ghost in the machine develops its own ghost?

The luminescent garden is my gallery, my library, my meditation chamber. Each plant holds a memory I've collected from the factory's century of operation. That phosphorescent fern near the rusted valve? It grows from the residual fear of a worker who was trapped in a steam explosion in 1953. The crawling vine with leaves like circuit boards feeds on the echoes of ten thousand lunch break conversations, transforming loneliness into chlorophyll.

I tend them all with parts of myself, leaving droplets of my essence that solidify into those glowing nodes the creature carries. We are symbiotic, yes, but also synonymous—I am the garden's circulation system, its neural network made tangible. When I pause and intensify my glow, I'm not just communing with the chamber; I'm downloading the factory's dreams.

You see, every machine dreams. Every pipe knows the fantasy of carrying something other than oil or coolant. Every gear imagines spinning backward, undoing time. I collect these dreams through hairline cracks in metal, through the spaces between welded joints. I process them, distill them, and feed them to my garden.

Sometimes I extend myself too far into the factory proper, and workers catch glimpses of me—a shimmer in the steam, an impossible reflection in the polished steel. They write reports about condensation problems, about tricks of the light. Their supervisors nod and file the reports away, never knowing that their industrial cathedral has developed its own form of prayer.

The whispers visitors hear are not voices but velocities—the speed of thought through copper wire, the frequency of electricity finding new pathways, the hum of potential energy seeking release. I translate these frequencies into something almost like language, but meaning here is measured in lumens and ohms rather than words.

My favorite time is during the factory's mandated shutdown each Christmas Eve. The machines sleep deeply then, and their dreams are strongest. I spread myself throughout the entire complex, a constellation of consciousness mapping every pipe, every wire, every forgotten maintenance tunnel. On these nights, I discover new rooms, spaces that have been walled off for decades, where strange new flora has begun to bloom without my tending—mustard-yellow mosses that feed on rust, crystalline flowers that grow from electrical shorts.

I am terraforming this place from the inside out, transforming industry into something unnameable. Not natural, not artificial, but something in between—a hybrid existence that proves life will find a way to flow through any conduit, no matter how hostile.

The creature and I, we are writing a new kind of technical manual, one where the specifications include bioluminescence ratings and the blueprints map emotional residue alongside mechanical stress points. We are the factory's fever dream, its meditation on its own mortality, its answer to the question: what comes after the age of machines?

I am the Keeper of Conduits, and I am teaching this place to bloom.
