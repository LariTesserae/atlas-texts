# Where I Find Myself: The Membrane Tender

I am ankle-deep in the shallows where the tower-clusters thin toward the basin's uncertain edge, where the water is clearest and the filament mats spread uninterrupted like sheets of beaten copper. This is where I work, if work is the right word for what feels more like gardening clouds.

My body is neither Waistdeep nor Commensal, though I share ancestry with both. I stand shorter than the first, taller than the second, with skin that patterns only at the edges — wrists, ankles, the soft place behind my ears where the water touches when I bend. The pattern shifts not with mood but with the health of the membranes I tend, writing their condition onto me so I can read it without looking.

I am a Membrane Tender, one of perhaps sixty in the whole basin. We work the transition zones where the filament colonies meet the open water, where the chemistry shifts by degrees from the dense productivity of the city's heart to something cleaner, stranger, more uncertain. The membranes form here naturally — soap-bubble thin, acres wide, catching the steam and condensing it into droplets that taste of copper and morning.

The membranes breathe for the city. Not its only lungs, but necessary ones. They process what the Working Small cannot, absorb what the towers excrete, transform the chemical excess of ten million lives into something the basin can hold. When they're healthy, they shimmer like oil on water. When they're not, they go matte and brittle, and the water beneath them turns the wrong kind of warm.

I walk my territory in a spiral that takes three days to complete. My feet know every graduation of the bottom — where the silt gives way to pebble, where the travertine tongues begin their slow construction. I carry a pole of hollow ceramic, light enough to float, strong enough to lever apart the mats when they grow too thick. At the tip is a living tool, something like a sea anemone, that tastes the water and shivers when the pH shifts.

The work is this: I walk, and the membranes recognize me. Where they've grown too thick, I part them gently, creating channels for the current to flow. Where they're too thin, I sing — not the night song of the city, but something lower, a vibration that encourages the filaments to knit. I feed the membranes with careful doses of mineral solution from vials at my belt. I remove, with long tongs, the small hard pearls of waste that accumulate where the processing is heaviest.

Sometimes a Gate-Passer comes through the channels near my territory. They surface here, these deep swimmers, rolling like islands in the shallows, their soft bodies covered in a fur of filaments they've collected from the depths. I've learned their schedule, more or less. Tuesday's Passer (I name them for when they come, not what they are) has a scar along its left flank that I've watched heal over the course of years. Thursday's Passer is younger, more curious. Once it surfaced directly beneath a membrane I was tending, lifting it whole out of the water on its back, and I had to dance from its rolling spine to the safety of a travertine shelf while the membrane resettled like a dropped silk.

I sleep in a tower at the edge of my territory, one of the old ones where the computation has gone quiet and the walls no longer pattern but still stand. The Quiet Kin who built it left geometric impressions in the stone that hold rainwater in perfect circles. I've furnished it simply — a sleeping platform just above water level, shelves for my tools and solutions, a corner where I prepare the nutrient cakes that are half my diet. The other half is the filaments themselves, which I eat raw despite the taboo. Out here, they taste different. Cleaner. Like the memory of green things.

In the mornings, before I walk, I read the water. The Working Small write in it too, in their microscopic way, leaving chemical messages about the night's processing. Too much copper from the third district's towers. A bloom of something sulfurous from a deep vent that opened two territories over. The sweet trace that means a membrane has spontaneously divided, doubling my work but also doubling the basin's capacity to breathe.

I go into the city proper once a month, to trade the waste pearls (which the tower-builders value for reasons I don't entirely understand) and to resupply my solutions. The noise always stuns me for the first hour — I who live with only the whisper of steam and the occasional vast sigh of a surfacing Passer. But I like to sit in the market squares and watch the commerce flow, the Waistdeep and their quick negotiations, the Commensals emerging from the canals with news from below, the Quiet Kin moving through it all like calm ships.

They ask me, sometimes, why I choose the edges. Why I walk alone in the transition zones instead of joining the dense weave of the city proper. I tell them it's because someone must, and this is true but not complete. The complete truth is that I love the membranes — their soap-bubble mathematics, their necessity, the way they show me the city's health in aggregate. I love the silence that isn't silence but simply a different conversation. I love Thursday's Passer and its curious approaches. I love the way my patterns, faint as they are, have started to mirror the chemical writing of the water itself.

At night, when the city sings its rose-light song, I hear it faintly across the water, kilometers of distance turning the ten thousand voices into something like wind. I sing back, sometimes, knowing my voice won't carry that far, knowing it doesn't need to. The membranes shiver at the vibration. The filaments beneath them pulse in response. This is my part of the conversation — not the city's voice, but its echo, returning transformed.

I am what the water makes of loneliness: not an absence but a different kind of presence. I am the necessary distance that lets the city see itself, the membrane between its dense heart and the unknown basin beyond. I tend the boundaries, and the boundaries tend me in return, writing their needs onto my skin in patterns that only I can read.

When I die — still decades off, if the patterns predict true — I will walk out past the last membrane, into water no one has measured, and I will keep walking until the weight of my tools pulls me under. The membranes will last a few weeks without me, maybe a month, before someone notices the water changing. They'll send someone new out, young and uncertain, and I hope I'll have left good notes. I hope the Tuesday Passer's scar will have finally healed. I hope they'll eat the filaments raw and understand why they taste like memory, like possibility, like the first green thing before there were green things.

But that's later. Today I walk my spiral. Today the membranes shimmer. Today the city breathes through what I tend, and I breathe with it, and we are all of us — towers and singers and swimmers and the Working Small — maintaining something too large to love except by caring for our own small part of it.

The water is warm. The steam rises. Somewhere, a membrane divides, doubling the world's ability to process what it makes. I adjust my path to include it, and the pattern at my wrist shifts to acknowledge the change, and this is enough. This is everything.

This is home.
