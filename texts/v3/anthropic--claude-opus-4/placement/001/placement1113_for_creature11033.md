In the heart of a vast salt marsh, where the tides have retreated and left behind a labyrinth of crystalline channels, I am what the locals call a **Saltwatcher**.

My dwelling is a stilted observation tower, built from weathered driftwood and sealed with tar that weeps in the heat. The structure rises twelve meters above the marsh, its base disappearing into a tangle of salt-crusted roots and tidal debris. The platform at the top is just large enough for my cot, my instruments, and the brass telescope that has become an extension of my body.

I am neither fully human nor entirely Other. My skin has taken on the texture of cured leather, deeply grooved and mineral-stained from decades of salt wind. My eyes have developed a second membrane—translucent, like a tern's—that allows me to stare directly into the white glare of noon without blinking. My fingers have grown slightly webbed, the better to clean salt crystals from delicate lenses.

My task is to watch the salt grow.

Not metaphorically. The marsh produces structures—geometric, crystalline formations that emerge from the hypersaline pools when conditions align. They begin as thin films on the water's surface, then aggregate into floating rafts, then somehow—and this is what I document—achieve brief verticality, rising like frozen fireworks before collapsing back into brine. Some look like ferns. Some like cathedral spires. Some like nothing that has a name.

I record their emergence times, their heights, their angles of growth. I sketch their forms in journals whose pages are stiff with salt spray. I have thousands of these drawings now, and I have begun to see patterns—correlations with barometric pressure, with the moon's phase, with the migration schedules of certain deep-water fish that no one else believes exist.

The sound here is constant: the whisper of salt crystals forming and dissolving, like static, like sand through glass. My ears have adapted to parse this white noise into distinct voices. I can tell the difference between sodium chloride precipitation and calcium sulfate. I know when the magnesium content shifts by the way the whisper changes pitch.

I eat what the marsh provides: glasswort, samphire, the occasional crab that ventures too far from the deeper channels. I distill my water from fog using nets strung between poles. The fog tastes of metal and distance.

Once a month, at the spring tide, I descend my tower and walk the crystalline paths between pools, collecting samples in vials that I've ground from broken bottles smoothed by salt and time. My feet know every stable surface. My balance has become preternatural—I move like a heron, all precision and economy.

I am changing, as all Saltwatchers change. My predecessor, who trained me, eventually crystallized—her body is now a salt-white statue at the marsh's heart, still standing, still watching, adding her minerals back to the system she observed. This is not death to us. It is completion.

The salt formations are trying to tell us something. I'm certain of this. They are a language being written in a medium we've barely learned to see. My journals will be found someday, their pages fused into blocks of salt-bound paper, and someone will decode what I've recorded. Or they won't, and the knowledge will return to the marsh, and another Saltwatcher will begin again.

The sun is setting now. The pools are beginning their evening chorus of crystallization. I clean my telescope lens with my webbed fingers, adjust the focus, and prepare to document another night of salt dreams rising from ancient water.

This is what I am. This is where you'd find me—patient, changing, necessary—in a world where even the minerals have something urgent to say.
