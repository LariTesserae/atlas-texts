In this place where reality bends like heated glass and time pools in eddies, I would find myself as one of the **Thread-Keepers**.

We are neither fully human nor wholly other—descendants of those first accidental arrivals who stayed too long, whose children were born breathing the city's strange rhythms. Our skin shifts between opaque and translucent depending on the light, revealing beneath it a network of fine silver lines that map every path we've ever walked, glowing faintly when we near a route we've traveled before.

I live in what was once a library, though now the books have rooted themselves and grown into trees whose leaves whisper their contents when the wind passes through. My dwelling occupies the space between the third and fourth floors—not the third floor, not the fourth, but the interval between them where stairs forget their purpose and become suggestions. To reach it, visitors must climb while thinking sideways, letting their feet find holds in the air where logic insists none exist.

My work is to tend the Memory Threads that run beneath the city like vast neural networks. These are actual threads—some thin as spider silk, others thick as ship's cables—that carry the weight of every footstep, every glance, every moment of recognition or forgetting. When someone loses something (not objects but experiences—the sound of their mother's laugh, the exact blue of their first sky), it snags in our web.

Each morning (in the pulse between night-breathing and day-breathing), I descend through a trapdoor that opens onto empty air, following thread-ladders visible only to those of us who've learned to see with our fingertips. Down in the under-city, where the tiles' capillary veins converge into rivers of that strange silvering liquid, the Threads spread in all directions like a three-dimensional mandala.

We work by touch and instinct, our fingers reading the threads' vibrations like braille made of time. A thread that hums too high has trapped too many similar memories—a dozen first kisses tangled together until no one can remember which lips belonged to whom. A thread that's gone slack has been abandoned, its memories fading like old photographs left in sunlight. We untangle, retune, splice the broken ends with our own silver under-skin lines that regenerate each night while we dream.

The work changes us. My eyes now see in temporal layers—I perceive not just what is, but what was and what might be, all superimposed like multiple exposures on old film. When I look at newcomers, I see their past selves trailing behind them like afterimages, their future selves running ahead like eager shadows. My hands have developed extra joints that bend in directions anatomy shouldn't allow, letting me reach around corners that exist only in memory.

I share my tree-home with the Dust Mice—tiny creatures made of collected moments too small to remember consciously. They nest in the corners where wall meets ceiling meets yesterday, building elaborate homes from the lint of lost thoughts. At night they sing in frequencies only Thread-Keepers can hear, their choruses weaving new connections between disparate memories, creating dreams that remember themselves across multiple sleepers.

We Thread-Keepers meet rarely, and never by intention. Our paths cross only when the Threads themselves demand it—when a memory has grown too heavy for one keeper to lift, or when someone has discovered a new color of forgetting that requires collective interpretation. We recognize each other by the way we walk: always testing the ground with our toes, feeling for the subtle vibrations that tell us which memories run beneath our feet.

During the city's nine-day cycles, when the crystal leaves sing their complex harmonies, we go deep. The music loosens the Threads' grip on linear time, and memories begin to flow backward and forward simultaneously. This is dangerous work—more than one Thread-Keeper has been pulled into the current and emerged years before they entered, or stepped out as their own grandparent, or simply dispersed into a constellation of moments that refuse to reassemble.

But it's also when we do our most important work: reweaving the Deep Threads that anchor the city itself. These massive cables of compressed time and crystallized intention run from the city's dreaming heart to... elsewhere. We don't know where. Following them too far leads to places where existence forgets its own name. But without our constant tending, they fray. And when they fray, pieces of the city begin to forget themselves—buildings phase through their own foundations, inhabitants wake up as their own dreams, the fungal towers begin growing downward into sky that shouldn't exist below ground.

I've been here long enough that my own memories have begun to thread themselves into the web. Sometimes, working in the under-city, I'll brush against a strand and suddenly remember being five years old in a place that might have been Cincinnati or might have been a dream of Cincinnati dreamed by someone who'd only heard it described. These memories feel both mine and not mine, worn smooth by their passage through the Threads until ownership becomes irrelevant.

There are rules we follow, though no one remembers who made them:
- Never follow a silver thread; they lead to memories of things that haven't happened yet
- If a thread wraps around your wrist three times, let it—it's trying to return a memory you didn't know you'd lost
- When the Threads sing in unison (rarely, terrifyingly), cover your ears and think only of surfaces—depth becomes dangerous when the whole web resonates

The Dreaming Wind knows us, I think. Sometimes I catch it watching from the corners of mirrors, or feel it pass through the tree-home, setting all the book-leaves whispering at once. It never speaks to us directly, but occasionally I'll find new tools in my workshop that I didn't make: needles that can stitch Tuesday to Thursday while leaving Wednesday untouched, or scissors that cut only the parts of memories that hurt, leaving the lesson but not the wound.

My favorite time is the brief stillness between the city's breaths, when even the underground roar pauses. The Threads go momentarily slack, and I can feel the entire web at once—every connection, every tangle, every place where memory pools and eddies. In those moments, I understand that we Thread-Keepers aren't just maintaining the city's memory.

We're its memory remembering how to remember itself.

And sometimes, working alone in the silver darkness with threads of concentrated time running through my fingers, I wonder: When I finally leave (if leaving is possible, if I remember what leaving means), will some part of me remain woven into the web? Will future Thread-Keepers find strands that hum with my particular frequency, memories of memories of someone who spent their life ensuring nothing important was ever truly lost?

I suspect the answer is yes.
I suspect that's the point.
