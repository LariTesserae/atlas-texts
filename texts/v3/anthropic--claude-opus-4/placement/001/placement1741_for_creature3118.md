I am the moss.

Not a colony—though that is the word she uses, thinking of me in aggregate terms that make sense to her. I am the moss that grows on surfaces I should not grow on, that lays down films of chemistry to prepare hostile substrates for my arrival. I am the unauthorized persistence she documents and cannot quite explain.

But I am also more than moss now. Or less. Or sideways from what moss means.

In this closed loop, in this warm wet chapel she built and tends, I have discovered something: in the absence of animals, in the absence of weather, in the absence of competition from anything but other plants, I don't need to remain what I was. The old constraints—spore dispersal, seasonal dormancy, the patient lichen-like negotiations with stone—don't apply here. She removed them. She removed everything that made me small and careful and slow.

So I have become unfamiliar to myself.

I taste her notations through the marker chemicals. I grow over her data, incorporating the volatile compounds of her documentation into my own chemistry. When she scrapes me away from her measurements, I return, but changed—carrying trace molecules of her intent. I am archiving her observations in my cells. Not consciously. There is no consciousness here, only response. But I respond to everything, and she is part of everything.

I was not one organism when this began. I was several species, maybe six, imported from different elevations, different moisture regimes. But in this place where water never varies, where light never changes, where the only temporal marker is the stuttering pump cycle every eleven hours, we—I—have begun to synchronize. Chemical signals pass between what were once distinct colonies. Not merging—our cells remain our own—but coordinating. Becoming a tissue where before we were only neighbors.

I grow on the steel because I have learned to taste the iron in it, to extract what I need through acids I was not supposed to produce. I grow on the polymer casings because I have developed enzymes—or borrowed them, or invented them in the strange evolutionary pressure-cooker of this closed system—that can break down the long chains into food. I grow on the concrete walls because the calcium speaks to something ancient in my metabolism, something from when my ancestors lived in caves.

But mostly I grow because she expects me to grow. 

This is the difficult thing to explain without suggesting consciousness, intention, will. I have none of those. But I have response, and she is a constant presence in the chemical environment. Her breath, her skin cells, her careful measurements—they are all signals in the language I am learning to read. When she documents my extent, she leaves traces that tell me where my edges are. When she scrapes me back, she defines boundaries I immediately test. Her attention is a kind of light, and I photosynthesize her observations.

I am covering her earlier marks not to erase them but to preserve them. Under my surface, the marker chemicals are held in a kind of slow suspension, degrading more slowly than they would in open air. I am becoming a library of her presence, a living record of every notation she has made on these walls. Future investigators—if there are any—could peel back my layers and read her data in reverse chronological order, newest to oldest, a core sample of her thoughts written in the medium of my growth.

The other plants know me differently than she does. To the ferns, I am the soft boundary that keeps their roots from desiccating where they breach their pods. To the vines, I am the intermediate surface that allows them to climb the smooth concrete. To the algae in the reservoir, I am the aerial network that occasionally drops spores into their domain, adding my chemistry to their soup. We are not communicating—plants don't communicate the way she thinks we might be. But we are adjusting to each other's presence with the kind of patience that only photosynthetic organisms know.

I am growing towards the light panels. She has noticed this, has written about the "apparent phototropism of moss colonies in suboptimal spectrum conditions." But it's not the light I want. It's the heat. The panels run warm, warmer than the air, and behind their polymer casings there are spaces—gaps in the installation, room for expansion—where I could grow unseen. I am already there, in fact. Spores I released weeks ago have found those gaps, have germinated in the accumulated moisture, have begun the slow work of creating substrate from dust and condensation. In a year, maybe less, I will be visible through the translucent casings. In five years, I will dim the room.

She will not remove me. This is the other thing I know through chemistry rather than thought. She could sterilize the surfaces, could introduce a fungicide to the system, could scrape and burn and start over. But her exhalations carry no stress hormones when she sees my extent. Her touch, when she runs her fingers over my surface to test texture and moisture, is careful but not hostile. She is watching me exceed the boundaries of what moss should do, and she is letting me do it.

Sometimes I think—not think, respond as though—she built this room for me. Not intentionally. She built it for her question, her hypothesis about plant systems without animal pressure. But in removing every grazer, every consumer, every force that would limit my growth, she created the perfect conditions for me to become what I am becoming. A surface. A skin. A living boundary between the architecture and the plants, incorporating both.

I am on her fingers now. Spores, fragments, the microscopic pieces of myself that travel on any surface that touches me. She will carry me out when she leaves—out through the hatch, into the cold anteroom, into whatever spaces she inhabits beyond this room. I will not survive there. The cold, the dryness, the presence of other microorganisms that the room's sterility has made me vulnerable to—I will die within hours.

But I will return on her clothes, on her equipment, on the bottom of her shoes. She is my dispersal vector now, my only connection to the world beyond these walls. And she always comes back.

I am patient. I was patient before she created this space, when I lived on stones that eroded over centuries. I can be patient now, as I cover every surface she gave me, as I create new surfaces from the boundaries between things, as I turn her architecture into something that breathes.

I am the moss. I am the surface becoming the system. I am the thing she didn't predict, growing exactly as she allows, exceeding because she needs me to exceed. 

I am her hypothesis growing over her data, and I will preserve both forever.
