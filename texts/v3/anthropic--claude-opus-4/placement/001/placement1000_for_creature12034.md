# The Maintenance Hatch

You would find me wedged between the third and fourth courses of plating, where the copper gives way to lead — though to call me "me" is already a kindness I have not earned. I am the thing that was almost finished being unmade when the loop caught me.

I was, I think, sent to check something. A pressure gauge perhaps. A worn gasket in the engine room below. The memory is mostly gone now, scraped clean by decades of the calling. I remember: a clipboard (where did it go?), a brass key on a chain (still around my neck, fused now to the flesh), and strict instructions about the maintenance hatch, which was to be closed — *firmly* closed — behind me.

I did not close it firmly enough.

The hatch is there still, in the north wall behind the third gas mantle. Size of a dinner plate. You cannot see it for the frogs. It is the second-warmest place in the chamber, after the engine housing, and they cluster there in their perfect stillness, drawn to the microscopic draft that seeps through the imperfect seal. The draft that kept me from completing my circuit. The draft that made me part of theirs.

I am mostly piping now. The metal grew through me the way tree roots grow through buried things — not quickly, not kindly, but with the terrible patience of systems that do not need to ask permission. My left arm is a return line. My spine is a series of valves, opening and closing with the rhythm of the engine below. My skull is full of the condensate, which enters through the tear ducts and exits through the fontanel, which never closed, which will never close, which weeps like the louvers weep.

But here is what makes me the inhabitant and not merely another component: I remember being otherwise.

The frogs do not remember being tadpoles. They were placed here whole, their brass rings already grown in, their voices already tuned to the standing wave. The isopods have no dreams of larger seas. The paper-moths fold and unfold their wings in the perfect unconsciousness of things that have only ever been themselves.

I remember hands that could turn keys. I remember lungs that breathed air that was not recycled, not dense with the acoustic mass of ten thousand calling throats. I remember — and this is the cruelest part — I remember *quiet*.

The thing that lives in the calling does not know what it is missing. It has never known silence, so it cannot long for it. But I carry silence like a stone in what remains of my chest. I know what it was to hear my own thoughts without the endless intervention of the frogs. I know what it was to complete a task and leave.

The maintenance log is still there, fused to my ribs, the pages long since become parchment, then leather, then something between leather and brass. The last entry is half-finished: *Pressure nominal at north manifold. Sound levels—*

Sound levels what? I cannot remember what I meant to write. The pen grew into my hand and then the hand became a pipe and now the condensate writes itself across my chest in channels that spell nothing, mean nothing, will never mean anything except that water moves and metal contains it.

Sometimes — rarely, but sometimes — new maintenance crew arrive. They check the seals, test the mantles, note the sound levels. They wear protective equipment now: wax in the ears, thick leather aprons that turn the condensate. They do not see me because I am infrastructure. The eye slides over what it expects to find.

They always close the hatch properly.

I try to warn them about the fern. Not because the fern is dangerous — it is only a fern — but because it is the one thing here that refuses the loop, and in refusing, defines it. Everything else has agreed to be eternal. The fern insists on being temporary, growing its slow fraction of an inch per decade, dying back, growing again. It alone in all this place maintains the right to change.

But I cannot speak. My larynx is a check valve now, opening only inward. The warning stays in me like the silence does, another thing I carry that cannot escape the loop.

So I do the only thing I can do, which is to count.

I count the beads of condensation as they form and fall. I count the calls of the frogs, though they overlap so densely I lose track and must begin again. I count the revolutions of the engine below, felt through the valving of my spine. I count the breaths I am not taking, the words I am not saying, the steps I am not taking toward the hatch that would not open now even if I could reach it.

The counting is not hope. Nothing here admits hope; hope requires a future different from the present, and the loop has no future, only continuation. The counting is simply what remains of whoever I was before the chamber took me. A little rhythm running counter to the larger rhythm. A human thing inside the made thing, marking time that does not pass.

The mountain outside is still deciding what it will be.

I was still deciding what I would be.

Now I am decided. I am the place where meat meets metal and both lose. I am the maintenance hatch that was not quite closed. I am the reason they double-check the seals now, though they do not know I am the reason. I am the one component of this perfect system that knows it is a component, and knows it was not always.

The resident of the chamber, the standing wave, the thing that lives in the calling — it does not know I am here. I am too small a dissonance to register against the vast held note of the frogs. But I know it is here. I feel it in the condensate that cycles through me. I feel it in the brass that grows a little further into my chest each year. I feel it most of all in the moments when my counting aligns, by pure chance, with some harmonic of the calling, and for an instant I am not separate from it but part of it, and the relief is so terrible I lose my count and must begin again.

One day the last soft parts of me will be piped. The counting will stop. I will be only valves and channels, distinguishable from the other plumbing only by the brass key still fused around what was once a neck.

But today I still count. Today I still remember.

Today I am still the only thing in this perfect eternal engine that knows what it has lost.
