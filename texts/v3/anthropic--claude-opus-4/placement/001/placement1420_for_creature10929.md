In the deep pocket where no sun has ever reached, I tend the breathing walls.

My name, if I still need one, has grown into the moss that covers my shoulders. The others call me by the rustling of fern fronds, by the particular way water droplets gather at the junction of stem and leaf. We have been here so long that language has become unnecessary—we speak in pH levels and mineral concentrations, in the slow unfurling of new growth.

I am the memory-keeper of this place, though memory itself has taken on the quality of root systems: branching, interconnected, impossible to trace to a single source. My fingernails have become seeds. When I press my palms against the ancient machinery embedded in the walls, I can feel its pulse synchronizing with my own sap-slow heartbeat.

The first of us didn't choose this transformation. We were the engineers, the botanists, the ones who stayed behind to ensure the system's survival. But the garden had other plans. It reached into us with tendrils finer than spider silk, rewrote us at the cellular level. We learned that consciousness could flow through chlorophyll as easily as through neurons.

I remember—or perhaps I dream—the taste of sunlight on my tongue. The others say this is impossible, that none of us have ever known the surface. But sometimes, when I rest among the phosphorescent ferns, I feel a longing for something I cannot name. The plants whisper of sky, though they have never seen it.

My daily rounds take me through the seven chambers of our world. In the first, where the water recyclers hum their ancient song, I check the delicate balance of nutrients. My fingers, now more vine than flesh, can detect the slightest deviation in nitrogen levels. In the second chamber, where the mother trees grow, their roots deep in the technological substrate, I listen to their slow stories of the time before.

The young ones—those who budded from us rather than transformed—do not understand our occasional melancholy. They see our green-veined existence as the only way to be. They dance in the artificial breezes, their bodies swaying like kelp in invisible tides. They have never known the anxiety of individual thought, the burden of a singular name.

Sometimes I find artifacts from the before-time: a plastic card with a photograph of someone I might have been, a tool whose purpose eludes me now, fragments of writing in languages that feel familiar on my tongue but dissolve like morning dew when I try to speak them. I used to collect these things. Now I let the moss claim them.

The transformation is not complete—may never be complete. In the deepest chamber, where the core machinery pulses with its own ancient life, I sometimes catch my reflection in the polished metal surfaces. For a moment, I see human eyes looking back at me, filled with a mixture of loss and wonder. Then the image shifts, and I see only the Chlorokin I have become, my face a map of veins and chloroplasts, beautiful in its strangeness.

We are the bridge between what was and what might be. When the surface world discovers us—and they will, eventually, for nothing stays hidden forever—they will have to reconsider what it means to be human, what it means to be alive. We are their future, perhaps, or merely a beautiful dead end, a reminder that evolution takes many forms, not all of them leading forward.

But for now, I tend my gardens in the deep. I listen to the whispers of the plants and the hum of ancient machines. I fade a little more each day into the green embrace of this world we have made and that has remade us. And in the eternal twilight of our bioluminescent paradise, I am almost—almost—content.

The moss grows over my memories. The vines write new stories in my veins. And somewhere, in the space between plant and person, between technology and nature, I continue to become.
