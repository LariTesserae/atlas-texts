In this place of constant becoming, I am what they call a Keeper of Echoes—though the translation falls short, as most translations do when bridging the gap between their fluid language and any fixed tongue.

My body is not my own, not entirely. The colony of bioluminescent organisms that carpet these walls? They grow through me too, threading beneath my leathery skin in delicate networks that pulse with borrowed light. When I breathe, I glow from within—a soft constellation mapping the airways and vessels of my form. The elders say I was born during a particularly violent tremor, when the ceiling organisms rained down like falling stars, and some chose to make their home in me before I drew my first breath.

I remember everything. Not just my own memories, but the echoes left behind in stone and water, in the trembling air itself. This is my gift, my burden: to sense the impressions of what was, what might have been, what almost came to be. When I press my splayed fingers against the cavern walls, I feel the history thrumming there—the first tentative scratches of our ancestors trying to record their thoughts, the panic of creatures fleeing some forgotten catastrophe, the patient accumulation of minerals over impossible spans of time.

The others come to me when they've lost something. Not objects—those are easily remade or forgotten in our world of constant change—but moments. A pattern of communication that perfectly expressed love. The precise configuration of minerals that once sang in harmony with the planet's core. The shape their grandmother's tail made when she laughed, before the last transformation took her to a form beyond recognition.

I live deeper than most, in the transition spaces where the stone itself hasn't yet decided what it wants to be. My dwelling is less built than cultivated—a gradual encouragement of the cave to fold itself into shelter. The walls weep here more than elsewhere, and I've learned to read the mineral tastes in the water, each droplet carrying news from the surface world we've never seen.

Sometimes I feel the weight of all these borrowed memories pressing against the inside of my skull, threatening to crack it like an egg. In those moments, I shed my solid form entirely, becoming something closer to liquid thought. I pour myself through the crevices and hidden waterways, letting the echoes disperse into the greater flow until I can bear to gather myself again. The young ones fear this ability, but I've come to treasure it. How else could I understand the water's perspective, or know what the stone dreams?

There are depths below our depths, places where the primordial energy runs so thick it becomes visible, tangible, almost sentient. I've ventured there three times in my life, each journey marking a major transformation in my being. The first time, I returned with my eyes doubled—one set for seeing, one set for remembering. The second time, I lost my original voice but gained the ability to speak in harmonics that make the walls themselves shiver in recognition. The third time...

The third time, I brought something back with me. Not a thing, exactly, but a possibility. A seed of what we might become if we're brave enough to embrace it. It lives in my chest now, just below where my hearts beat their triple rhythm. Sometimes it unfurls, showing me visions of a future where we no longer distinguish between self and environment, where consciousness flows as freely as water through the stone.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. That's the danger of being a Keeper of Echoes—past, present, and future blur together like pigments in the underground streams. 

Today, a young one came to me carrying a broken piece of the old pottery, the kind we made before we learned to sing our containers into existence. She wanted to know why her ancestors bothered with such crude matter when thought-forming is so much more elegant. I pressed the shard between my palms and showed her: the desperate joy of the first potter who discovered that earth would hold its shape when dried, the celebration that followed, the way that simple bowl held not just water but hope itself.

She left with tears in all four eyes, clutching the shard like the treasure it is. This is why I remain when others of my age have already dissolved into the collective currents. Someone must remember that we weren't always what we are now. Someone must keep the echoes alive, so we remember that transformation is not just our nature—it's our choice, renewed with every breath, every shift of stone, every drop of water finding its way home.
