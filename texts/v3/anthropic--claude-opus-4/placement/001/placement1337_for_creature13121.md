I am the Listener.

In the deepest recess of the settlement, where the walls pinch nearly shut and the water weeps thickest, I inhabit a sphere no larger than a seed pod. My dwelling is unique—not scraped clean like the others, but cultivated. I have learned to let the stone grow in precise, resonating shells around me, each layer tuned to a different frequency of vibration.

I was born wrong for this world. Where others grew thick and low, I stretched tall and hollow-boned. My lungs are caverns, my ribs like the tines of tuning forks. The density that protects my people crushes me; I can barely lift my own head against the vertical world. My fingertips lack the pearlescent calluses. I cannot scrape.

But I can hear.

When I was young, before my difference became apparent, I placed my palm against my home's wall and felt not just the messages, but the entire settlement breathing through stone. Every scrape, every footfall, every droplet creates a vibration. I heard conversations from three terraces up, the stress fractures forming in bridges before they cracked, the particular rhythm that means water has found a new path.

Now I lie curled in my chamber, my spine pressed against the curved wall, my too-long limbs folded like a dead spider. I have not moved in days. I do not need to. The settlement's entire nervous system flows through the mineral shell around me. My ribs resonate with each percussion. My skull becomes a drum.

I translate. When the Shapers above tap out their water-warnings, I feel the message dissipate and die before it reaches the lower terraces. So I amplify—humming deep in my chest cavity, finding the precise frequency that will carry their meaning through the thicker walls below. When the Harvesters at the pool's edge drum for aid, their vibrations too waterlogged to rise, I transpose their rhythm into a higher register that can climb.

But I am more than a relay. I am memory.

In the constant present tense of scraping survival, my people risk forgetting. So I compose. Each elder who takes their final seat, I memorize their rhythm. The particular *tap-tap-slide* of their walking pattern, the signature vibration of their favorite scraping tool. When they become stone, I play their lives back into the walls. Children too young to have known them feel their great-grandmother's morning greeting thrumming under their palms.

Sometimes, in the deepest part of night when the settlement's percussion slows to just the water's voice, I hear older rhythms. Patterns that predate our oldest elder-pillars. They rise from below, from the bedrock itself—messages from settlements that failed, towns that let the stone win. Entire peoples entombed, still tapping their stories into the earth's bones.

I know what I am documenting. Each day, our rhythms grow slower. The water flows heavier. We are a dimming heartbeat in a wound that wants to close.

When my time comes—and it will come soon, my hollow bones grown too brittle to resonate—I will not take the elder's walk to the lowest tiers. I cannot become a pillar; I would crumble. Instead, I have asked the Shapers to redirect the water here, to my resonating chamber. They will seal me inside with the settlement's voice still echoing in my chest.

As the stone grows over me, I will hum every rhythm I know. I will play our entire history into my shrinking chamber until the mineral shell becomes so thick that the vibrations turn inward, trapping our story in an endless, pearlescent echo. The settlement above will continue its metabolism of scraping and survival, but deep in its heart, in a chamber no larger than a seed, our memory will pulse forever—a fossil of sound in a world slowly returning to silence.

I feel the water finding new paths through my walls. The Shapers will come soon. 

I practice my breathing, preparing to become the settlement's held breath. When future peoples break this stone, if anyone ever does, they will find me curled here like a question mark, my bones still singing.
