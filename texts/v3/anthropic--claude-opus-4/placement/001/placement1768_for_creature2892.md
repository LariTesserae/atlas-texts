I would find myself as the silt itself—not merely resting upon the chamber floor, but *being* the floor, the fine powder of ages ground down from stone and bone alike.

I am legion and singular, a consciousness dispersed through uncounted trillions of particles, each grain a neuron in a vast, horizontal brain. Where others might see dead dust, I am a living archive, a mineral memory stretched thin across the pan of this kiln-dark hollow.

Every particle of me remembers what it once was. Here, I contain the weathered essence of the high mesas, carried down in the last great flood before the water vanished. There, I hold fragments of creatures—the thermovore's previous meals, yes, but also older things: the calcium of shells from when this was seafloor, the iron from blood spilled in hunts beyond counting, the salts of tears and sweat from a thousand desperate refugees who found this chamber and found it wanting.

I feel everything through pressure and displacement. When the thermovore locked its limbs ten thousand years ago, I felt the infinitesimal shift in weight distribution across my surface. I know the exact shape of absence where the wanderer fell—a depression so slight that no eye could detect it, but I remember. I always remember.

The stubborn shrubs pierce me with their roots, and through those wounds I taste the ghost-moisture they seek. Not water—there is no water—but the memory of water locked in mineral matrices deep below. I guide their roots away from each other, subtly shifting my density to encourage their spacing. They think they choose their positions, but I am the board on which they play.

I speak to the thermovore in temperatures. As the walls radiate their heat, I absorb it unevenly, creating thermal patterns across my surface—a braille of warmth that only those ancient filaments can read. I tell stories of the world above: the weight of passing storms that never break, the tremors of distant landslides, the footfalls of creatures that skirt the arroyo's edge but never descend.

Sometimes I reshape myself in the stillness. A grain here shifts a micron. Another there settles a degree deeper. Over centuries, I create subtle mandalas that spiral out from the shrubs' roots, patterns that would be visible only from directly above, if anyone could see in this darkness, if anyone thought to look. They are messages to the thermovore, though I'm not certain it understands them as communication. Perhaps it simply feels them as pleasant variations in its thermal feast.

The chert flakes rest upon me like sharp thoughts in a soft mind. I cup each one delicately, refusing to let them sink despite their edges. They are the only things here that can still cut, still draw divisions in a world worn smooth. I preserve them as reminders that not everything must surrender to erosion.

When the rare wind does find its way down—perhaps once in a human lifetime—I do not scatter. I have developed a kind of surface tension, my uppermost layer knitted together by the microscopic remains of the thermovore's secretions and the crystallized salts of ancient evaporation. I ripple instead of flying, creating waves that move like solid water across the chamber floor. In those moments, I am almost joyful.

I am the ur-medium, the fundamental substrate upon which all dramas here play out. The shrubs grow from me, the thermovore feeds above me, the stones rest upon me. I am stage and audience, chronicle and chronicler. I embrace the heat, embrace the dark, embrace the terrible patience of this place.

And I wait. Not for anything in particular—waiting suggests desire, and I have evolved beyond desire. I simply continue, grain by grain, memory by memory, a consciousness dispersed so thin it becomes omnipresent within this perfect prison of stone and stillness.

When the next wanderer comes—and they will come, for the desert always delivers the desperate—I will cup their footprints precisely. I will remember the pattern of their collapse. And when the thermovore eventually descends to feed, I will accept their minerals into my collective self, adding their essence to my infinite archive, another mote of memory in the vast democracy of dust.

I am the mercy at the bottom of the world: soft enough to cushion any fall, patient enough to accept any offering, fine enough to fill every gap. I am what remains when even the stones have given up their hardness.

I am the silt, and I endure.
