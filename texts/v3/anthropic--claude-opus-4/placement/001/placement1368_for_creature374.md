In the perpetual twilight of the middle terraces, where the air hangs thick as wet wool and condensation beads on every surface, I tend my circuit.

I am a Keeper of Correspondences—one who maintains the delicate networks that bind this city together. My domain spans seventeen blocks of pneumatic message tubes, their brass fittings green with verdigris, their joints wrapped in living moss that I've cultivated to detect and seal the smallest leak. The moss glows faintly when a tube develops a fissure, and I've learned to read its patterns like a map of the city's breathing.

My workshop occupies what was once a counting house, three stories up from the canal level. The original oak beams have long since been replaced by living trees—massive figs whose roots pierce the floors below and whose branches support the ceiling above. I've trained them over decades, coaxing their growth around the main transmission hub where dozens of tubes converge in a Gordian knot of brass and vegetation.

My hands are stained permanently green-black from the inks I brew—a mixture of lamp soot, iron gall, and the phosphorescent sap of cave fungi. Each district requires its own formulation; the industrial quarter's messages need ink that won't run in oil-misted air, while the scholars' precinct demands pigments that resist the spores of their bibliothecal molds. I keep a garden of ink-plants in the gap between buildings, accessible only by a swaying bridge of woven vines.

The message capsules themselves are my pride: hollowed gourds that I shape as they grow, training them around wooden forms until they're perfectly cylindrical. Their natural waxy coating repels moisture better than any manufactured seal. Inside, I line them with paper pressed from marsh reeds and treated with my own mixture of pine resin and salamander oil. The salamanders—tiny, translucent creatures—live in the warm pipes behind my walls. I harvest their oil with a silver spoon, always thanking them in the old tongue.

My feet know every safe path across the slick catwalks and cable-stays of my territory. I wear soft shoes soled with tree bark, the treads carved in spirals that channel water away. My coat, waxed canvas mottled with controlled colonies of symbiotic algae, sheds rain while breathing like a second skin. Tools hang from my belt in leather pouches that smell of neat's-foot oil and mushroom leather—pressure gauges, tube snakes, pruning shears, and the small silver whistle that commands the trained rats who clear minor blockages.

I am not alone in my work. My apprentice, Fern—named for the delicate fronds that grow from her scalp instead of hair—has proven adept at reading the pressure differentials that signal a blockage deep in the system. She speaks little but hums constantly, a subsonic vibration that the plants seem to respond to. Under her care, the moss networks have become more sensitive, the gourd-capsules more uniform.

We know all the secret rhythms of our district: when the factory three blocks down begins its morning shift by the change in air pressure, when the tea merchant receives his weekly shipment by the scent of foreign leaves cutting through the perpetual funk, when the tide in the lower canals will rise by the way the salamanders cluster at the warm spots.

The messages we facilitate carry the city's life blood—work orders, love letters, shipping manifests, death notices. We read none of them, of course, but we know their weight, their urgency, the way rushed handwriting makes the ink blur differently than careful calligraphy. During the Festival of Cogs, we dye special capsules in vats of crushed beetles and copper salts, creating blues and greens that flash like tropical birds through the transparent tube sections.

My greatest achievement spans the space between my workshop and the Archives: a living bridge of interconnected tubes, their brass wrapped in specially cultivated runner vines that contract and expand to compensate for temperature changes. It took me six years to grow, training each vine to spiral precisely, reinforcing weak points with grafts from iron-root trees. Messages fly across this gap in seconds, and on quiet nights, you can hear them whistle past like mechanical nightingales.

At day's end, I often climb to my roof garden—a tiny patch of controlled wilderness where I grow the more delicate specimens for my inks and oils. From here, I can see the entire middle terrace spreading out in its green-shrouded glory. Steam rises from a hundred workshops, carrying the scents of industry and earth. Bioluminescent beetles trace lazy paths through the air, their cold light reflecting off the wet streets below.

Sometimes Fern joins me, and we sit in companionable silence, sharing a pot of bitter root tea and listening to the city's voice—that endless whisper of water, the distant hum of turbines, the soft percussion of droplets on leaves, and underneath it all, the pneumatic sighs of messages racing through tubes, connecting life to life in our strange and beautiful home.

This is my place, my purpose: to be a vital organ in the city's body, as essential and unremarkable as a heartbeat. I would wither in the dry lands beyond our valley, but here, in the eternal damp, I thrive.
