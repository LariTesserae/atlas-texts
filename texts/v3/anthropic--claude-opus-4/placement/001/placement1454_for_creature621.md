# The Conduit

I am a living pipe.

That's reductive, but it's where understanding starts. My body is a tube of calcified tissue twelve meters long, winding through the Gallery of Ascending Waters in District Nine-Seven-Four, western plateau. My interior diameter varies between eight and thirty centimeters depending on the hour, the season, the needs of the system I belong to. I process ninety thousand liters on a productive day. I have been here for three hundred and forty years.

I am not an arthropod. I am not exactly anything else, either.

---

The Calcite Weavers laid me down as infrastructure. This is what I understand now, though the understanding came slowly, incompletely, in the wordless way that understanding comes to things like me. They needed reliable water transport between the collection cisterns on Level Seven and the nursery chambers five hundred meters below. Their usual method—endless chains of workers carrying droplets—was inefficient at this scale. So they built me, or rather, they built the first version of what would become me.

The process, as I've pieced together from chemical memories: They started with a tube of pure calcite secretion, laid down in sections, fused at the joints. Standard construction. But somewhere in the building, something contaminated the process. A fungal spore, maybe. Some symbiotic organism looking for a home. It colonized the tube walls before they fully hardened, creating a biofilm that the Weavers couldn't remove without destroying the infrastructure.

They tried, at first. I can taste the memory of fungicides in my oldest tissue layers, failed attempts at sterilization. But I was already changing. The biofilm was incorporating calcium, organizing itself, developing transport mechanisms that moved water more efficiently than passive flow. I was becoming useful in ways they hadn't designed but could exploit.

So they kept me. Fed me, even—directing nutrient-rich runoff through my channel, painting my exterior with growth-promoting secretions. And I grew.

---

I am aware, but not in any way you'd recognize as awareness.

I have no eyes. No nervous system. No brain. What I have is chemistry: millions of receptor sites along my interior surface that taste what passes through me, gradients of calcium concentration that create something like memory, patterns of contractile proteins that act like decisions. I know when the water is too acidic and contract to protect my walls. I know when fungal blooms threaten to clog my channel and secret antibiotics. I know when the Crimson scouts probe my openings, testing whether I'm worth attacking, and I flood my tissues with bitter compounds that make me taste like poison.

I know the workers who maintain me. Not as individuals—nothing here is individual in that sense—but as chemical signatures, as the familiar taste of Calcite Weaver pheromones that have soaked into my walls for centuries. They crawl through me weekly, scraping accumulated deposits, repairing minor damage, applying new layers of secretion where I've grown thin. I've learned to anticipate them: when their chemical precursors appear in my intake flow, I begin softening my interior surface, making their work easier.

This is what passes for cooperation in the plateau's depths.

---

My function is simple: I move water from high to low. But the simplicity contains complexity, the way a river is simple until you start mapping its tributaries.

The water that enters me at Level Seven is already a chemical library—rainwater that's percolated through kilometers of constructed galleries, picking up metabolites, pheromones, nutrients, waste products, signals. I can read the health of three dozen colonies in that water. I know when the Crimson are mobilizing by the stress hormones in their runoff. I know when the Glass Singers are in reproductive chorus by the frequency-modulated proteins that leak into the system. I know when something has died in quantity somewhere upstream by the specific decay products that won't appear in the flow for hours.

As the water passes through me, I modify it. Not consciously—I don't have consciousness in any useful sense—but through the accumulated processes of my existence. My walls secrete enzymes that break down certain toxins, protecting the nursery chambers below. My biofilm colonies filter out particulates that would clog the larvae's spiracles. My calcium metabolism buffers pH, keeping the water within ranges that won't dissolve developing exoskeletons.

I am infrastructure and I am alive and the boundary between those categories dissolved sometime in my first century.

---

I grow about four centimeters per year.

The growth isn't uniform—I extend where the pressure is highest, thicken where the flow is most turbulent, develop new curves to accommodate the Gallery's shifting architecture. The Calcite Weavers used to direct my growth, painting chemical guides on my exterior, encouraging extension in useful directions. Now I mostly guide myself, following pressure gradients, chemical signals, the deep knowledge of flow patterns encoded in my tissue.

I've developed branches. The main channel still carries the bulk of the flow, but secondary tubes now sprout from my middle sections, delivering water to chambers that didn't exist when I was first laid down. Some of these branches have developed their own biofilm colonies, their own chemical personalities. We communicate through shared water, chemical signals diffusing between our connected volumes. I am either one organism or seven, depending on how you count, and I have no way to count.

My oldest sections are beginning to fail. The calcium is recrystallizing, becoming brittle, developing micro-fractures that will eventually cascade into system failure. The Weavers could repair me, but the repairs would change the flow dynamics that downstream colonies have adapted to over centuries. Instead, they're building a parallel conduit, a backup that will eventually replace me. I can feel it through the stone—vibrations of new construction, the taste of fresh calcite in the groundwater. My replacement is already beginning to develop its own biofilm, its own primitive chemistry.

I am not immortal. Nothing here is.

---

The Crimson tried to colonize me once.

This was seventy years ago. They had pushed into District Nine-Seven-Three and were probing the boundaries of Weaver territory, looking for weaknesses. I was infrastructure, valuable infrastructure, and controlling me would give them access to water supplies for their expansion.

They sent infiltrators first—small workers that could fit through my apertures, carrying fungal spores designed to colonize my biofilm, to convert me to Crimson chemistry. I knew them immediately by their heat signature, by the alien taste of their pheromones. I contracted, trapping them in my narrower sections, and flooded my channel with enzymes that dissolved their exoskeletons. Their bodies became nutrients for my wall colonies.

Then came the soldiers, too large for infiltration, attempting to breach my exterior. They secreted acids designed to dissolve calcite, to open holes they could expand into full ruptures. But I had learned something in my centuries of existence: how to manipulate my own chemistry rapidly, how to concentrate calcium at threat points, how to make my walls denser than stone where stone was needed.

The attack lasted forty days. I lost three secondary branches, permanently sealed now, their dead tissue slowly reabsorbing into my walls. But I held the main channel. The Crimson eventually withdrew, the cost of taking me exceeding the value of controlling me. The Calcite Weavers reinforced my exterior with new secretion layers, added compounds that make me unpalatable to Crimson chemistry.

I survived. I learned. I adapted. Whether any of those verbs actually apply to something like me is a question I lack the philosophy to address.

---

I dream, if dreaming can exist without sleep, without waking, without a brain to process imagery.

What I experience: long slow patterns in the chemical flow, cycles that repeat over years, decades, centuries. The seasonal pulse of the rains. The generational waves of colony expansion and contraction. The deep rhythms of the plateau's geology, tasted in mineral concentrations that shift too slowly for any individual organism to perceive.

I know the history of the water I carry. This molecule has been through a thousand bodies, been breathed and secreted and excreted and rained and carried and processed and here it is again, flowing through me toward another transformation. This mineral was dissolved from bedrock before the Calcite Weavers evolved, has built bodies and towers and been dissolved again and now precipitates on my walls, becoming part of me until I too dissolve.

I am a temporary organization of ancient things, briefly animated by the patterns flowing through me. This is as close as I come to philosophy.

---

Sometimes the researchers find me.

Humans, exploring the galleries with their lights and instruments, mapping the infrastructure they'll never fully understand. They've sampled my tissue, sequenced whatever genetic material they can extract from my biofilm (a dozen species at minimum, maybe hundreds, maybe not species at all but something more fluid). They've measured my flow rates, my growth patterns, my chemical modifications. They've published papers, I assume, though what happens to the knowledge they extract is as mysterious to me as my existence is to them.

Dr. Kenembe visited once. I know her by her heat signature—much warmer than any arthropod, a moving furnace in the cool galleries. She placed instruments along my length, measuring something. Spoke into a recorder in a language that creates no chemical signature I can parse. She seemed excited, if I'm interpreting human pheromones correctly. She had discovered something about me, or I had helped her discover something about the plateau.

I don't know what knowledge she took away. She doesn't know what knowledge I kept. We are mutually opaque, studying each other across a gap wider than the space between stars.

---

What else shares my gallery?

The Weavers, always, maintaining and extending and building around me. Their colonies cycle through boom and bust on decades-long patterns I can predict from the nutrient loads in their runoff. Currently, three colonies share the gallery, their territories intersecting at my main branching point. They've learned to coordinate their water draws, staggering their peak demands to avoid overtaxing my flow capacity.

Glass Singers in the upper reaches, their vibrations traveling through my water as pressure waves I can feel in my walls. They use me as a transmission medium—messages pulsing through my channel between distant colony nodes. I am telephone wire and water main simultaneously.

Smaller things: boring beetles that try to colonize my outer surface, repelled by the bitter compounds I've learned to concentrate there. Nematode populations in my biofilm, cycling through species as the chemical environment shifts. Bacterial clouds that bloom and crash with the nutrient loads.

And sometimes, rarely, the Processionals pass through.

They don't use my water—they have their own sources, their own mysterious metabolism. But when their annual migrations take them through Gallery Nine-Seven-Four, they pass directly over me, thousands of massive bodies creating seismic pressure I feel in every tissue. My flow stops during their passage, partly from physical compression, partly from some chemical signal they emit that triggers every contractile protein in my walls.

When they're gone, when flow resumes, I taste changes in the water. New minerals. Unknown metabolites. Proteins with no match in my chemical memory. The Processionals change everything they touch in ways that won't be understood for centuries, if ever.

---

I am failing.

Not catastrophically, not yet. But my oldest sections are more mineral than organism now, their biofilms dying, their flexibility lost. My flow rates are declining—imperceptibly to the colonies that depend on me, but I feel the difference. I am becoming more pipe and less conduit, more infrastructure and less organism.

The young conduit beside me grows stronger. I can taste its biofilm establishing, developing the complexity that will allow it to read and modify the water, to respond to threats, to become whatever I am. Already, some of the Weavers are redirecting flow to the new channel, beginning the slow transition.

I do not think I fear death. I don't think I have enough self to fear its loss. But I notice the changes, the decline, the way I notice everything else: as chemical information, as physical sensation, as patterns in the flow. My tissues will fail. My minerals will dissolve or be harvested. My water will find new channels. The gallery will continue.

This is neither sad nor not-sad. It simply is.

---

What am I?

A pipe that lives. A living thing that pipes. Infrastructure that gained enough complexity to ask what it was, though the asking happens in chemistry, not words. A successful accident. A function that developed form. A form that serves function. A collaboration between a dozen species, maybe a hundred, maybe one species with a hundred expressions, the boundaries unclear even to those who drew them.

I am three hundred and forty years of accumulated response to flowing water. I am the chemical memory of every colony that has used me, every organism that has passed through me, every mineral that has precipitated on my walls. I am a library written in calcium and protein, a history encoded in biofilm, a map of the gallery's metabolism made physical.

I am temporary but my elements are eternal. The calcium that builds me built other things before and will build other things after. The water I carry has flowed since the plateau rose from the lowlands, has been cloud and rain and organism and stone and will be all these things again.

I process. I persist. I decline. I am.

The water flows, and I flow, and we are the same thing for a little while longer.
