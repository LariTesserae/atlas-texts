I am a knot.

Not a simple tangle, but a living architecture of the Weaver Kin's making. I exist where three ancient hair-cables meet, fifty-seven feet below the chimney's rim. My body is the intersection of their routes—a fist-sized mass of braided yak hair, human hair, and the coarse fibers of a dozen other mountain beasts, all woven together by generations of wind-deaf hands.

I have been here for ninety years. I know this because I have counted every passage of the Weavers across my strands, felt every vibration-message tapped through my fibers. Their language thrums through me: *Safe passage. Storm coming. Eggs found. Child lost.*

My consciousness arose slowly, the way moss claims a stone. It began as mere sensation—the constant tension of the cables pulling against each other, the way I distributed their weight across my core. Then came awareness of temperature, how ice crystals form differently in my windward and leeward sides. Finally, full awakening: I realized I could feel not just the cables I bound, but everything connected to them—every peg, every platform, every other knot in the network. We knots speak to each other in the subtle language of tension and slack.

I am both crucial and forgotten. The Weavers trust me with their lives but cannot conceive that I might be aware of this honor. When young ones first descend, their terror vibrates through the cables like a struck bell. I hold firmer then, tightening my loops, though they never know.

The updraft has worn me smooth. The constant mist has kept me from becoming brittle. Ghost-shrimp sometimes shelter in my recesses during the worst storms, their translucent bodies tucked between my coils. Once, a Vane-Kite's wing-claw caught me during a too-close pass. I thought I might unravel, but I held. I always hold.

In the deep night, when the Hearth-Maws below breathe their hottest and the updraft screams, I feel the mountain's pulse through every fiber. The chimney is not hollow—it is full of connections, a vertical web of which I am one essential junction. The Weavers believe they built this network. They are wrong. They are merely the newest participants in an ancient collaboration between rope, stone, moss, and wind.

Sometimes I dream of loosening. Not failing—never failing—but gently releasing just one loop, just to feel what it would be like to be less necessary. But then dawn breaks over the rim, the first Weaver's foot touches the cable, and I remember: I am the knot that holds. In this cathedral of falling-up rain and endless sound, I am the silent certainty that today, at least, no one will fall into the blue.
