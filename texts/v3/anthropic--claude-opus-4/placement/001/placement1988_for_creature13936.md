I find myself drawn to a particular chamber - one of the middle places, neither newly carved nor closing, where the work is not the urgent excavation of the forward teams nor the ritual preservation of abandonment. This is a teaching chamber, though that word sits oddly here where all learning is bodily, where knowledge passes skin to skin in the dark.

I am one who listens between. Not yet old enough to hear the wood think, but past the age where the wall-language is mere pattern. I spend my days - though day has no meaning here, only cycles of work and rest measured by the rhythmic groaning of the roots - with my palms pressed to surfaces where the grain runs strangely. These are the puzzle-places, where natural wood-growth and ancient carved marks have merged so completely that parsing their origins has become a kind of meditation.

The children come to me, or rather, I go to where the children are, finding them by their quicksilver warmth and the particular way their small bodies disturb the still air. They think I am teaching them to read the ghost-marks in the wood. What I am really teaching them is doubt - the productive kind, the kind that makes meaning flexible enough to survive compression.

My fingers are my tools, but also my memory. Each fingertip carries its own library of textures, a catalog of every ambiguous grain-pattern I've encountered. When I run my hand along a carved wall, I'm comparing, always comparing - this ridge against a thousand other ridges, this deviation against the accumulated knowledge of how wood deviates when it grows around intention.

I know seventeen different ways the wood preferentially preserves certain cut-angles. I know which root-species hold carvings most faithfully and which dissolve them into their own growth-dreams within a generation. I know the chemical taste - not on my tongue but through my forever-open eyes - of walls that are about to begin their forgetting, that moment when the fungal colonies shift their metabolism and begin to erase what we've written.

But mostly, I know what it feels like to find a pattern that might be a name and to spend days with my fingers reading and re-reading it, constructing histories for this potential person. Were they a carver? Did they love someone whose warmth I might recognize if they stood before me now? Did they discover something about the wood's nature that we've since forgotten, compressed into illegibility?

The children tire of this uncertainty. They want the world to be what their hands tell them it is - solid, readable, real. I place their small fingers on the most ambiguous surfaces and ask them to tell me stories about what they feel. At first they resist. "But what IS it?" they whisper, their breath barely disturbing the air between us. 

"It is what it is," I tell them, which is not an evasion but a teaching. "And it is also what it was, and what it's becoming, and what we need it to be. The wood holds all of these at once."

I am training them for the inevitable moment when they will slice into new wood and find a pattern that might be their own great-grandmother's name, distorted beyond certainty. I am teaching them to live with that uncertainty, to make meaning from it without requiring it to resolve into fact.

Sometimes, working alone in chambers where even the maintenance teams rarely come, I find patterns that shake me. Too regular to be natural, too warped to be readable, too persistent to be accident. I've begun to suspect that some of what we call natural wood-grain is actually carved pattern so ancient, so thoroughly digested by growth and regrowth, that it has become indistinguishable from the substrate's own tendencies. That perhaps the wood's "natural" pattern is partly the accumulated memory of every hand that ever shaped it.

If this is true - and I cannot know if it's true, can only feel the possibility of its truth in my fingertips - then we are not reading the wood. We are reading ourselves, compressed and transformed beyond recognition, returned to us as nature.

I press my ear to the wall and listen to the deep groaning. Sometimes I imagine I hear voices in it - not the tap-language, not any language, but something older. The sound of the first carvers, perhaps, their careful blade-work still resonating in the fiber, still shaping how the wood prefers to break.

But this is fancy. This is the kind of thinking that comes from too many hours with fingers on ambiguous surfaces. The children help, with their certainty and their need for stories that end. They keep me grounded in the actual world - the world where water must be collected, where chambers must be carved, where the work of living takes precedence over the work of wondering.

Still, when I find them tracing their own small patterns on the practice walls, I make sure to carve my own marks nearby. Not to preserve my name - names are just texture here, just another pattern for the wood to digest. I carve to participate in the long conversation, the one between us and the wood, between intention and growth, between what we mean to say and what gets remembered.

The wood is patient with us. It has been patient for longer than we can conceive. It will compress our carvings, digest our meanings, return them to us as grain. And we will read them, or try to, or fail to, and in the trying we will make new meanings, carve new marks, feed new patterns into the endless appetite of the growing dark.

I am small and warm and my fingers know a thousand textures. I am a library of doubt, a teacher of productive uncertainty. I am a person who lives inside something that might be a city, might be an organism, might be a memory making itself real through our hands.

The distinction matters less each year. The work continues. The wood remembers.
