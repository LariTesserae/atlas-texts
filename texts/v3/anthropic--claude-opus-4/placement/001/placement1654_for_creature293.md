I would find myself as one of the **Settling Tank Keepers**.

Not quite solitary, not quite social. There are three of us who work the deep tanks at the southern edge of the works, where the hot water comes up before it reaches the river. We rarely speak to each other, but we know each other's rhythms like the stilt-waders know their ancient nests.

My skin has taken on the yellowish cast, but also something else – a faint iridescence at the wrists and throat where the steam hits most directly. Twenty years of breathing mineral vapors and peering into the dark water has changed me. My eyes have developed a peculiar ability to see through the murk, to spot the difference between a normal eel and something that has no business being near the surface.

I carry a long pole with a net at one end and a hook at the other. The net is for the things that should be removed – the occasional dead rat, clumps of bacterial mat that break free, eel carcasses. The hook is for the things that should be sent back down. You learn the difference. A translucent claw the size of a dinner plate? Hook it gently, drop it into the return pipe. Let it find its way back to wherever it belongs. The deep has its own business.

My dwelling is a converted pump house between two tanks. The pump died forty years ago, but the building suits me – thick walls that hold the cool of morning through the bronze hour, a window that faces east toward the rowhouses but shows only steam and shadow. I've lined the walls with jars, each containing some oddity from the tanks: a coil of something that might be intestine or might be worm, suspended in clear alcohol; a fragment of shell that hums faintly when the barometric pressure drops; a perfect sphere of some organic matter that refuses to decay.

I know the sounds: the particular gurgle that means something large is moving through the underground channels; the change in pitch when the mineral content shifts; the silence that comes before something surfaces that shouldn't. At those times, I tap my pole three times on the tank's edge – a signal to the other keepers. We converge, not speaking, and do what needs doing.

The other fauna know me. The scavengers wait at a respectful distance when I'm cleaning the tanks, understanding they'll get what I leave. A family of pourers lives in the brick of my pump house, and I've watched three generations of them grow fat on the brinecatchers that gather in my steam. Sometimes, working late, I'll feel something watching from below – not hostile, just aware. I tap the pole once in acknowledgment and continue my work.

I am the membrane between the upper basin and the deep. I sort what belongs above from what belongs below, maintaining a boundary that everyone pretends is solid but which is, in truth, as permeable as the mud between the buildings. Things come up. Things go down. I ensure they go to their proper places.

At night, I drink the thin beer and eat eel that I've learned to prepare in the way of the deep – raw, with certain minerals from my jars that kill any parasites but leave a taste like copper and rain. I sleep to the sound of water moving through spaces no map shows, and I dream of chambers far below where things with too many joints tend their own unknowable gardens.

I am keeper, sorter, guardian of the margin. Like everything else here, I have found my place in the arrangement, and the arrangement has found its place in me. When I die – not soon, the minerals preserve as much as they corrode – another will take up the pole, learn the sounds, develop the eye for sorting. The tanks will barely notice the change.

This is how I live, in the Sulfur Flats, at the edge between the known and the vast wet dark, keeping balance with a pole and a peculiar kind of sight, growing more like the place each year until I am just another feature of it, as essential and unremarkable as the pour of a lizard or the click of a beak.
