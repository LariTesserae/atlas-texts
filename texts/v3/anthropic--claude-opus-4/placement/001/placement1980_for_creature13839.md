I am Miran, one of the engineers, and this morning I had the dream again.

Not the one where the buildings unspool like thread — that's an occupational hazard, that one. Every engineer has it after their first spatial anomaly. No, this is the dream where I'm standing at the edge of the southern peninsula, looking out over nothing, and the rock beneath my feet is singing. Not the ground note. Something higher, clearer. A question pitched at exactly the frequency of human loneliness.

I wake with my hands already reaching for the tuning forks.

---

I've been here eleven years. Came up on the cable cars with a mechanical engineering degree from somewhere below that I can barely picture now — a university of red brick and straight lines and a quadrangle where the grass grew predictably. I remember being so certain, then. So convinced that the world operated according to principles that could be mapped and measured and made to serve.

The plateau cured me of that certainty within a week.

My first assignment was to repair a conveyor system in the materials district. Simple enough. The belt had torn, needed replacing. I arrived with my tools and my confidence and found the entire mechanism had developed sympathies with the spiral-stalk processing equipment three buildings over. The belt was no longer rubber — it was something between rubber and plant fiber, faintly luminescent, and it *grew back* as I watched. Healed itself, following some borrowed biological imperative.

I sat there for an hour, just watching it mend. Then I went back to my supervisor and asked what I was supposed to fix, exactly, if the machine had decided to repair itself.

"Nothing," she said. "But document the rate of regeneration. We'll need to know when it'll start reproducing."

That was when I began to understand what engineering means here: not imposing order but negotiating with an order that's still being written.

---

My workshop is in the seventh district, tucked between a processing plant and one of those moss-covered buildings that looks like it's wearing a heavy coat. The moss has started creeping onto my walls too — just the north face so far, but I've been watching it spread with something between professional interest and personal fondness. It changes color with the humidity. Today it's almost black, which means rain within the hour.

Inside, my tools cover every surface: the flexible rulers, the gravity-adjusted levels, the tuning forks in their velvet-lined case. But also: jars of rock samples in various stages of decision, each labeled with date and location and compositional tendency. A burrower named Composite who lives in a terrarium of undecided stone and who has better instincts for structural integrity than any instrument I own. Seventeen framed maps arranged in a circle on the wall — yes, like Harath Senn's, like half the walls in the city. We all ended up with the same set somehow, as if the maps themselves insisted on being displayed this way.

I'm working on something new. Have been for three years now. The other engineers think I'm mad, but they thought Harath Senn was mad too, and she's chief engineer now, so.

The project: a building that anticipates the plateau's changes. Not resists — we tried resistance in the early days, and the plateau always wins. Not adapts — we do that already, constantly, exhaustingly. Anticipates. A structure whose design includes its own future modifications, whose walls are built to accommodate sympathies that haven't developed yet, whose foundations can shift smoothly as the rock beneath them resolves.

I'm calling it a probability architecture.

The mathematics are nightmarish. I'm working with equations that have to account for variables that don't exist yet. My calculations fill notebooks that sometimes develop their own opinions about what I should be calculating — I'll return to a page and find new work in handwriting that's almost mine but not quite, solving problems I hadn't recognized yet.

This should disturb me. Instead, I've started leaving the notebooks open overnight, with fresh pens nearby. The not-quite-me that works while I sleep has good ideas.

---

The translucency started at my fingertips two years ago. I'd been expecting it — eleven years is about the usual onset — but the actual experience was stranger than I'd imagined. Not a fading but a... clarifying. As if my edges were becoming less insistent about being edges.

Now it's spread to my palms, and when I press them against the plateau's rock, the consideration comes faster. Not just the not-warmth, not-vibration of acknowledgment, but something more complex. The rock knows me now. Recognizes my particular frequency. Sometimes I think it's been waiting for me to become translucent enough to really communicate with, the way you wait for a child to develop language before attempting certain conversations.

Composite, my burrower, noticed before I did. Started bringing me different rock samples, specific ones, chosen with what I can only describe as intention. Pieces from the edges where the cliff is youngest. A fragment from near the temporal layering that contains traces of past and future in its crystalline structure. A chip from directly beneath the spot where I'm planning to build the probability architecture.

This last one I've been studying for weeks. It's unlike any other rock I've seen from the plateau — neither fully decided nor entirely young. It exists in what I can only describe as a state of *perpetual commitment*, constantly resolving and unresolving, never quite settling on a nature. When I tune it with the forks, it resonates at every frequency simultaneously.

I think the plateau is trying to tell me something about permanence. About how even decisions can be fluid. About how to build something that is stable without being fixed.

---

I had lunch with Lenne Vas yesterday. We meet monthly, always at the same café that serves the moss tea, always at the same table that has developed a subtle tilt from the spatial anomaly two blocks east. She likes to hear about my probability architecture. I like to hear her thoughts on what she calls "the ontology of the provisional."

"The mistake," she said, stirring her tea with a spoon that was developing wood grain from the table, "is thinking the plateau will ever be *finished*. Even when it decides what it is, it will still be deciding. Resolution isn't an endpoint here. It's a process."

I told her about the perpetually resolving rock sample. She held it for a long time, her stone-textured palm pressed against its surface.

"You're building a verb," she said finally. "Not a building. A building-ing."

This is why I have lunch with philosophers.

---

The rain came, as the moss predicted. I stood at my workshop's entrance, watching it silver the empty lot across the street where the groundcover was already beginning to hum. Three children ran past, chasing something—not a burrower, something smaller, something that left brief phosphorescent footprints that faded before I could properly see them. One of the children, a girl of maybe seven, paused at my doorway.

"The spiral-stalks will be very open tonight," she informed me solemnly. "Good for the singers."

Before I could ask what singers, she was gone, leaving only the echo of her certainty and the fading glow of small handprints on my doorframe.

This is another thing about Kethane-Above: the children know things. Not everything, not always accurately, but they know them with such conviction that the knowledge becomes a kind of truth. The singers she mentioned—I've never heard of them. But tonight I'll go to the empty lots where the spiral-stalks grow thick, and I'll listen, because a child with glowing fingertips told me to, and in this city that's reason enough.

---

In my dream, the rock sings its question, and I answer it. Not with words but with blueprints, with calculations that spiral off the page, with a building that breathes and shifts and remains itself through every transformation. I show the plateau what I want to build, and the plateau considers it—that familiar not-warmth, not-vibration—and then does something I've never felt it do before, awake or asleep.

It *approves*.

I wake with my hands clutching the bedsheets, and the sheets are faintly warm where I've gripped them, and when I release them, they retain the memory of my fingers for several minutes before relaxing back to cloth.

Today I'll go to the building site with Composite and my tuning forks and the perpetually resolving stone. Today I'll press my translucent palms against the young rock and propose a collaboration. Today I'll begin building something that doesn't exist yet, not even as an idea, not completely.

The ground note is particularly clear this morning. The ridge-soarers circle lower than usual, their fur-feathered bodies catching what passes for sunlight. At the café, the philosopher and the cable-car operator are having what appears to be a silent argument conducted entirely through meaningful glances and the angle of their coffee cups. The moss on my north wall has developed a patch of pale blue that I've never seen before, that might be new, that might be a color that wasn't possible yesterday but is possible today.

I am Miran, engineer of Kethane-Above, and my city is an unfinished sentence in which I am both word and punctuation. My fingertips are translucent, my calculations develop themselves, and I am learning to build with indeterminacy the way others build with stone.

The question is not whether my probability architecture will stand. 

The question is what it will become as it stands, and what I will become as I build it, and whether the plateau and I will finish our becoming together or whether—better, more likely, more true—we will continue becoming, endlessly, a mountain and an engineer and a building that is really a verb, all of us part of the same long, patient, impossible conversation.

Outside, the rain intensifies, and somewhere—yes, there, from the empty lot—comes a sound that is not quite singing and not quite the wind and not quite the spiral-stalks humming, but something between all three, something I don't have words for yet, something I will need to invent new mathematics to describe.

I gather my tools. The day is young—or at least this version of the day is young, here, now, in this particular layer of time—and there is work to do.
