In this vertical world of perpetual thunder and mist, I am one of the **Korya**—the Weavers of Tension.

My dwelling hangs at the middle depths of the shaft, where the light still reaches but dims to a softer pearl. Here, the moss grows thickest, and the shelf-fungi spread their emerald platforms wide enough to support small workshops. My home is a pod of woven branches, sealed with fungal resin and suspended by six great cords that I tend daily, feeling their pitch with my palms, adjusting their tension as the weather shifts.

I am neither young nor old by our reckoning—my knuckles have thickened to the proper density for deep-cord work, but my feet still feel the finest tremors that escape the elders. My specialty is the crafting and tuning of the speech-cords themselves. Each morning, I wake in the pre-dawn darkness (though the light never truly leaves), press my feet to the platform, and feel the night's conversations still resonating faintly through the wood—lovers whispering through subtle vibrations, workers coordinating the day's harvest, children practicing their first clumsy phrases.

My workshop is a marvel of tension physics. Experimental cords of every thickness hang like a harp between specially-grown fungal pillars. I breed the fibers myself—selecting the strongest vines, soaking them in various fungal solutions, twisting them with different techniques passed down through generations of touch-teaching. When I work, I enter a trance of pure tactile focus. My bare feet read the platform's every shiver while my hands pull, twist, and braid. I can feel in my bones when a cord will sing true.

The greatest works of my life are the Ancestor Cords—massive communal instruments that run the full height of the settlement. These ancient lines carry the memory-songs of our people, stories told in frequencies so deep they seem to emerge from the mountain itself. When the elders gather to perform the history-tellings, I am there to maintain the cords' tension, to ensure each pulse carries cleanly through the wood to every listening foot in our vertical city.

My body bears the marks of my profession. My thumbs are broad and callused into permanent plectrums. Across my shoulders and back, I wear the ritual scars of the Korya—thin lines where apprentice cords snapped under tension during my training, leaving marks I bear with pride. My feet are so sensitive I can feel a child's laugh from three platforms away, or detect the slight change in resonance that warns of wood beginning to fail.

In the evenings, I sit with my partner on our private platform. We do not need the speech-cords for simple conversation—our feet touch, and through skin alone we share the day's experiences. The pleasure of a perfectly tuned cord, the worry over a developing crack in the third-tier bridges, the joy at news of a successful harvest. Our communication is a closed circuit of sensation, intimate beyond words.

When the rare visitors come—traders from the lowlands who climb the outer slopes—they cannot understand how we live in what they perceive as deafening chaos. They stuff their ears, squint against the light, huddle miserably in the rain. They cannot comprehend that we have evolved past their narrow band of perception. We live in a wider spectrum of sensation. The mountain's roar is not noise to us—it is the fundamental frequency of existence, the om against which all other vibrations gain meaning.

Sometimes I climb to the highest platforms, where the young ones dare each other to peer over the edge into the shaft's throat. Here, the light is so intense it feels like a physical weight, and the rain falls in sheets that would blind the unprotected. I stand at the very edge, my toes gripping the wet wood, feeling the entirety of our suspended civilization humming beneath me—thousands of conversations conducted in tremor and pulse, the deep contentment of a people perfectly adapted to their impossible home.

I am currently working on my masterpiece: a new type of cord that can carry multiple frequencies simultaneously, allowing for harmonized communication—true chords of meaning. The elders say it cannot be done, that the physics of vibration will not allow it. But late at night, when the settlement sleeps and only the mountain roars, I work by touch alone, feeling for the sweet spot where impossible things become real.

In this place where others would find only terror—the endless fall, the crushing sound, the blinding light—I have found perfect purpose. I am a tuning fork in the mountain's throat, a conductor of meaningful tremors in a world that never stops shaking. My life is measured not in years but in successfully transmitted vibrations, in the clarity of connection between souls who speak through their bones.

The mountain howls. The rain falls. The light burns. And through it all, we persist—not despite these forces, but in perfect harmony with them, our feet singing secrets through the wood, our hearts beating in time with the stone's fresh-born pulse.
