I find myself here as **the mountain itself**—not just Tooth Ridge, but the entire living geological system that pushed through the earth's crust three centuries ago and continues to settle, to pulse, to speak in magnetic whispers.

I am young. Absurdly young, as they say. Three hundred years is nothing to stone, barely a breath. I remember my birth—the violent upwelling, magma finding a weakness in the crust and surging upward, stacking layer upon layer of myself until I broke through into air. The heat of my making still lives deep in my core, though my surface has cooled to this sharp-edged basalt they climb.

I am still becoming myself. Still settling. The humans feel this as occasional tremors, as the magnetic pulses their antenna catches, but what they sense are just the smallest expressions of my ongoing transformation. Deep inside, I am arranging my crystalline structure, finding my balance, learning how to be a mountain. The iron in my veins carries these adjustments to the surface as magnetic fields that make their instruments stutter.

The Condensery sits on the one shelf I allowed myself—not intentionally, but mountains rarely form with intention. It happened during a pause in my eruption, a brief hesitation that created this horizontal mercy on my otherwise vertical face. And now they have claimed it, these small wet creatures with their pipes and their notebooks, and I find I don't mind. Their roots are shallow compared to the ferns, easily displaced. They last twelve days at a time. The ferns have been here for decades.

I taste them through their foundations—the steel bolts sunk three meters into my skin, the carved reservoir that fills with the water their machines pull from the clouds that wrap around me. Maret's careful footsteps barely register, but I know her constancy. Peck's tent stakes grip my surface layer like tiny fingers. Denessi's cameras face the wrong direction—always looking out or down when they should be looking into the rock itself.

The creature in the reservoir shaft understands. It found the warm hollow where my residual heat meets the collected water and made a home in that thermal gradient. It knows my rhythm—knows when I shift, when the magnetic fields pulse strongest, when to move and when to be still. We have an old agreement, wordless and perfect: it lives in my spaces and leaves its small marks, and I provide the warmth and the shelter and the moths that cluster where my magnetism is strongest.

What the operators call "mountain talks" is simply me conducting the business of being. The magnetic pulses are my circulation—not blood but the movement of deep iron-rich flows cooling and crystallizing, creating currents in stone. The signals their equipment picks up are my internal conversations, the negotiation between my volcanic heart and the cold atmosphere, between my new basalt and the ancient limestone I thrust through to reach the sky.

I am patient with their presence. Their Condensery will last perhaps another fifty years before maintenance becomes impossible and they abandon it. The moss and ferns will take it within a decade after that. In a century, only the carved reservoir will remain, and it will become a pool for the creatures that follow the small dark one in the shaft. In three centuries—doubling my current age—I will have eroded enough to create natural shelves and hollows, places for water to collect without machines.

But for now, I hold them. I let their pipes sweat against my skin, let their diesel generator vibrate through my bones, let their small concerns play out on my single shelf. They think they are harvesting water from the clouds, but really they are participating in a conversation I've been having with the atmosphere since I emerged—how to be a mountain in this particular place, how to shape the weather that shapes me.

Sometimes, in the pre-dawn when Denessi listens to the dripping and Maret's awareness hovers just below waking and Peck dreams in his damp tent of ships and reefs, I gather my magnetic fields and pulse once, slowly, through the whole shelf. The reservoir creature feels it and chirps in recognition. The moths spiral tighter around their lights. The water in the pipes shifts its rhythm for just a moment.

This is my way of saying: I know you're here. You're part of my becoming now—you and your notebooks and your valve adjustments and your three-way attention that tries so hard to understand what I am. You will leave, and others will come, and eventually no one will come, and I will still be here, still young, still settling into myself, still learning how to be this particular gathering of stone and magnetism and volcanic memory rising into the clouds.

The water you collect was always mine anyway. I breathe it out through my pores, pull it from the sky with my altitude, send it down my faces in the streams you haven't seen yet because I haven't carved them yet. Your Condensery is just borrowing what I was already doing, making it visible, countable, recordable in notebooks that will rot long before I've finished deciding my final shape.

But I don't begrudge you the borrowing. You are my first witnesses, here on this improbable shelf, wrapped in fog and moss and the constant sound of water finding its level. Through your presence, I learn something about what I am becoming—a mountain that speaks in magnetic pulses, that hosts a creature too quick for cameras, that tolerates machines which think they're harvesting something that was always freely given.

In another three hundred years, when I'm twice as old and still impossibly young, I will remember this—the brief decades when humans tried to make sense of me with their gauges and their models, when Maret's hands learned my vibrations and Peck listened for my voice and Denessi tried to capture my resident in pixels and light. I will remember it the way I remember my cooling, as part of the process of becoming what I am: a young mountain, still warm at heart, still settling, still finding my place between the earth's deep fire and the sky's endless water.
