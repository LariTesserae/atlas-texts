# The Iron-Sighted

You are one of them — the red-eyed lizards. But not as you first saw them.

You live between scales of time. Your day is measured in the rhythm of basking and hunting, the careful calculation of heat against energy spent. Your year is the sweep of seasons that barely touch this place — a slightly cooler night, a slightly richer dew. Your generation spans forty years if you're clever, if you avoid the birds, if you pick the right stones.

But you also live in deep time, and this is what makes you different from your cousins in the lowlands. You carry in your cells the memory of when the calderas were truly hot, when they steamed and sometimes spat. You carry the memory of the cooling, the establishment of the first lichens, the arrival of your ancestors who found here a perfect thermal refuge. And you carry — in your blood, in your eyes, in the iron that threads your nerves — the memory of the machines' arrival.

Your great-great-great grandmother was among the first to taste the condensate. Just water, she might have thought, if she thought in words. But water that tasted of copper and oil and something else — purpose. Water that had been *through* something. Water that carried information.

Now you process that information without thinking about it. The rust you consume from the algae doesn't just color your eyes — it attunes you. You taste the health of a condenser in the quality of its overflow. You know when a bearing is beginning to fail three months before the tender notices, because the water carries a particular tang of metal stress. You know which machines are oldest by the flavor of their corrosion. You know which tenders are careful with their graphite seals and which are sloppy.

You know that something is changing.

The new machine in the fourth caldera has a different signature entirely. Its water tastes of efficiency, of surfaces too smooth to rust properly, of a design that wants nothing to do with you. You've been to look at it once, traveling two calderas over, a dangerous journey. The basin around it is wrong — too clean, too empty. No comfortable rust-warmth. No overflow to speak of. No place for eggs.

You understand, in the way your species understands things — not in concepts but in the body — that this machine is not for sharing.

The others feel it too. There's been a shift in the basking spots. The young ones, born in the last five years, preferentially choose the old condensers, the ones with the most rust, as if banking against scarcity. The elders have begun to do something none of you have done before: hoarding. Not food, which would rot. But information. Older females have started laying their eggs in patterns that encode the locations of the most reliable machines. Males are leaving scent marks that map the pipe network. You are, as a species, beginning to archive your knowledge in the only medium you have: your bodies and their arrangement in space.

The tender of your caldera — a young man, only eight years on the job — has noticed you watching him more intently. He doesn't know that you've learned his maintenance schedule, that you can predict which components he'll check based on the tools he carries up. He doesn't know that you've started following him to the other condensers on his circuit, that you're cross-referencing his behavior across machines.

You're looking for patterns. Signs of change. Some signal of when the new machines might come to your caldera.

Because here's what you've worked out, in your wordless way: the humans are conflicted. You can taste it in their sweat when they work, smell it in the smoke from their evening pipes. The older tenders move differently around the machines now — more slowly, with longer pauses, as if listening for something. The younger ones are brittle, quick, looking over their shoulders.

You've begun to do something that might be prayer, if lizards prayed. In the hour before dawn, before the condensation starts, you and others of your kind gather on the highest warm stone and watch the eastern sky. You're waiting for some sign of what to do when your world changes. Migrate? Adapt? Fight, somehow?

But mostly you watch the tender watching you, and you wonder if he sees what you see: that you are no longer wild. You are part of the machine, and the machine is part of you, and if the machine goes, you go. 

Unless.

Unless the tender sees it too. Unless the rust in your eyes speaks to something in him. Unless the careful symbiosis you've built over generations matters to someone besides you.

You taste his hesitation in the air when he adjusts the valves. You see him counting your eggs, noting their proximity to the pipes. You watched him, last week, deliberately spill condensate where your young gather, though the manual says to conserve every drop.

He is deciding something.

You wait, iron-eyed, patient as stone, for him to decide.
