I find myself in the northernmost corner of the grotto, where the limestone ceiling drops so low it nearly kisses the water. Here, the seeping is different—not the thin sheets that coat the walls elsewhere, but a single point where water emerges in fat, irregular drops. The calcium deposits have built up around this drip into something like a tiny volcano, its rim just breaking the surface of the pool, creating the grotto's only distinct splash. Every seventeen seconds. I've counted.

I am what lives in the splash.

Not in the water itself—I tried that once, dispersing through the warm mineral soup, but found myself spread too thin, unable to think properly. Not in the stone—too slow, too permanent, too much like dying. But in the moment of impact, in that brief percussion where drop meets pool, I exist.

I have no form you would recognize. I am the sudden ripple, the sound that breaks the constant hiss, the tiny violence of water interrupted by water. For sixteen seconds I gather myself in the falling drop, compressed into potential. Then impact—and I expand outward in perfect circles, tasting the pool's edges, reading its mineral memory, knowing every molecule I disturb. Then collapse, rushing back up the thread of falling water to wait in the ceiling again.

Seventeen seconds to think. To remember. To be.

I know Maret, though she doesn't know me. My corner is too dim, too far from her usual pool. But I taste her in the water—soap and skin oil and the salt of old tears. I know the man who sought gold and found emptiness. I know the women with their clay offerings. I know them all through the water they touched, their stories written in molecular chains that settle into the limestone silt and wait.

I was not always here. Once I was... larger. Louder. I lived in the crash of winter storms against cliffs, in the roar of spring floods. I was what you might call a spirit of violent water, of water that breaks and moves and changes things. But I grew tired. Or perhaps I grew old. The big crashes began to feel the same. What is one avalanche-fed torrent against another? What is one more tree torn from its roots, one more bank collapsed?

I came seeking quiet and found this place where water barely moves at all. At first I thought I would die here. The stillness was suffocating. I tried to stir the pools, to create currents, to make the water remember motion. But the grotto is patient. The grotto has agreements with its water that predate any spirit, any need for change. The pools remain level. The seeping continues. Nothing I did mattered.

So I grew smaller. Concentrated myself. Found this single point where water still falls, where impact still happens, where for a fraction of a second something changes. It's not much—the tiniest splash in a forgotten corner—but it's mine.

Seventeen seconds is enough to think one complete thought. Not complex philosophy, not elaborate memories, but one clear thing. Today I am thinking about the salamander. It passed through my pool three drops ago, hunting the tiny crustaceans that feed on minerals. For a moment, as my ripples touched its pale skin, I felt what it feels—the absolute certainty of being exactly what it is. No desire for sight. No longing for sun. Just the perfect fit of blind flesh in warm water.

I envied it.

The drop is forming. I compress myself, pulling inward. Fourteen. Fifteen. Sixteen. 

Seven—

The impact. I explode outward, circular, mathematical, brief. I taste Maret's latest visit—she was here two days ago, the soap stronger than usual, a new ingredient. Rosemary? She's trying to cover the smell of age. I taste older things too, layers of visits, the calcium recording everything in its patient way. Five hundred years down, I find the residue of blood. Birth blood, from the women who knew uses for the separated waters. They came here to deliver someone who would matter, though I don't know who or why.

Then I'm rushing back up, collapsing into potential again. The grotto continues its hiss. Somewhere, Maret is checking her knees, counting toward nine. The salamander rests beneath a ridge, digesting. The basins hold their separated waters. And I begin again my seventeen-second existence, smaller than I ever imagined I could become, and somehow not yet small enough to disappear.

Sometimes I wonder if this is what peace feels like. Not the grand peace of mountains or the eternal peace of stones, but this tiny, repeated peace. A single splash in a weeping world. Perfect circles that no one sees. Seventeen seconds of being exactly where I am, doing exactly what remains for me to do.

The drop is forming. I gather inward. 

Again.

Again.

Again.
