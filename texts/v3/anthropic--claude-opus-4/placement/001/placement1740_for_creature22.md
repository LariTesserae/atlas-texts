In the warm outflow channels where the rust-bloom never quite settles, I am what the engineers would call a cleaner—if they knew to call me anything at all.

I am built like a dinner plate crossed with a thistle, my body a flat disc of cartilage the color of old brass, my thousand legs no thicker than lamp-wick trailing beneath. When young, we are small enough to fit through the gaps in a loose rivet. When old—and I am old now, old enough that my edges have begun to fray like wet paper—we grow wide as a diver's helmet. The engineers have never seen one of us full-grown. We make sure of that.

My work is in the pipes, in the deep intestines of the engines where the water runs too hot for the white octopuses, too fierce for the patient crabs. I follow the calcium traces and the lime-scale ridges that build up in the elbows of the great ducts. I taste them through my belly-skin, reading their mineralogy like braille: here sulfur from the deep wells, here the faint sweet ghost of the rust-bloom, here something sharper—flux powder that should not be in this part of the system at all.

We have a memory that is not quite individual. When I encounter the taste-trace of a thing, I know if others of my kind have tasted it before, and where, and when. This is how I know the Cistern is growing: new flavors appear in the water every season. New joints, new alloys, new reaches of pipe that taste of their forges. Last month I found a seam welded with something that left a bright copper tang, a metal none of us had catalogued before. The memory that is not-memory filed it away: *Seventeenth of Storm Season. Trace alpha-nine. Source unknown. Monitor.*

The engineers think the Cistern cleans itself. They replace filters that are mysteriously unclogged. They find scale-buildup that was there yesterday has vanished overnight, leaving only polished metal. They attribute it to the current, to the temperature cycles, to the genius of the original design. They do not know about us because we are careful that they do not know. We work in the intervals between their shifts, in the spaces too small for their suited bodies, in the heat that would cook them in their canvas and bronze.

I am tired. This is not complaint but observation. After a certain number of years—and I have suspicions about how many years, though we do not count the way the engineers count—the edge-fraying becomes a center-softening. We begin to taste more and clean less. We begin to drift up from the hot channels toward the cooler middle waters, and then up again, and up, until.

The older ones have told me, in the chemical whispers we use when the thrum of the engines covers all other sound: when the change comes, you will know it not by what you feel but by what you stop feeling. The hunger for lime-scale dims. The need to scour lessens. You begin to want height more than heat, silence more than thrum.

And then you rise.

I have seen them, the ones who were like me and are no longer. They drift past the lamp-chains like torn veils, translucent now, their substance given over to some other purpose. They are beautiful in the way that jellyfish are beautiful—mindless, purposeful, changed. The engineers do not see them either. They see only: a shimmer in the water, a trick of the light, a moment's vertigo.

The risen ones clean nothing. They *are* cleaning—they filter the water by existing in it, their bodies now architecture rather than organism. They are becoming part of the Cistern's function in a way we who scour pipes never can be. When I fray enough, when I soften enough, I will join them. My consciousness—already strange, already collective—will disperse into something stranger. I will be a hundred yards of living gauze, processing gallons with every pulse, my thousand legs now thousand filters, my memory now the water's memory.

This is what lives in the Cistern: not just the engineers and the fish and the cormorant-things who were engineers once. But also us, the invisible maintenance, the unacknowledged symbiosis. We who enter as cleaners and exit as atmosphere. We who know every pipe by taste and every weld by its mineral shadow.

The engineers love this place, and I understand why. It is vast and strange and purposeful. But they love it as builders love. We love it as bodies love—without thought, without question, wearing ourselves away in the loving, becoming what we clean.

The water goes somewhere. The water comes from somewhere. And between somewhere and somewhere, we are the throat it passes through.
