# The Unraveling Library

There is a library here, but it remembers being something else.

The first thing you notice is the warmth — not comfort, but the fevered heat of a thing digesting itself. The air tastes of old paper and copper pennies. Light comes from everywhere and nowhere, a sourceless amber glow that makes all shadows look like bruises, purple-brown at their edges where they bleed into the gold. Sometimes the shadows move when nothing casts them. Sometimes they hold still while their objects wander.

The building is vast in the way that only forgotten things can be vast — not by measurement but by *recession*, each room opening onto three more, each corridor sprouting branches that weren't there when you entered. The walls are what walls become when they've held too many purposes: brick showing through plaster showing through wood showing through stone, all of it simultaneously true. In places, you can see the building's memories like tree rings — here it was a courthouse, here a hospital, here something that needed cells, here something that needed altars.

The books are the problem. Or the books are the solution. It depends on when you ask.

They fill the space completely, but incorrectly. Shelves built for one kind of knowledge hold another: medical texts in what were clearly once confession booths, law books stacked in operating theaters, something that might be poetry bleeding through the bars of holding cells. The books themselves are unstable. Open one and you'll find the first chapter is a treatise on wool production, the second is someone's diary from 1874, the third is in a language that doesn't exist yet, and by the time you flip back, the first chapter has become a recipe for something you can't pronounce.

This is the part that is hard to describe: the library is unbecoming, but not abandoned. Whatever the building was before, it's being digested into this — not destroyed but *translated*, the way a dream translates the day's events into symbol. If you watch long enough, you can see it happening. A doorway that led to a courtroom yesterday leads to a reading room today, and both rooms exist simultaneously for about an hour at dawn, superimposed, the judge's bench slowly becoming a librarian's desk while you watch, the gavel transmuting into a date stamp through stages too subtle to catch.

The sound is constant: a rustling like ten thousand moths, which is the sound of pages turning themselves. Sometimes a louder crack, like a spine breaking, followed by a sigh that might be settling wood or might be something else. Under it all, a hum — not mechanical but organic, the sound a hive makes when it's building new cells.

The rules, as best as can be inferred:

Everything here is in transition. The building eats its own history and excretes libraries. A surgical lamp becomes a reading lamp, but remembers being surgical — throws too sharp a light, makes the pages look like skin. A prison cell becomes a study carrel but keeps the dimensions of captivity; you can sit there and read but you'll feel watched, and the feeling is correct. The watching is built into the walls.

Text is the only stable currency. Whatever this place was, text is what it's becoming. Words colonize surfaces like lichen — climb the walls, carpet the floors, spiral across ceilings in languages that multiply as you watch. Some of the words are recognizable. Some are in alphabets that hurt to perceive. Some are just scratches that *want* to be words, trying so hard to mean something that you almost understand them out of pity.

The building doesn't just collect knowledge — it *produces* it. Books write themselves in the dark. Leave a blank journal on a shelf and come back in a week to find it filled with someone's memories, though whose memories and whether they happened are questions without answers. The library is composting reality into text, breaking down the distinction between what was true and what was written.

If you stay, you'll find that you begin to read things that aren't there. Street signs in the grain of the wood. Sentences in the pattern of dust motes. Your own thoughts will start to feel like quotations from books you haven't read yet. This is not madness. This is the building teaching you its language, the way a parent teaches a child — patiently, through immersion, until you stop noticing you're being taught.

This is a hungry place. It does not demand food. It demands *story*.

# The Librarian

It is not in the library. It is the library — but if you must find it specifically, look for the places where the translation happens fastest, where stone becomes paper in the span of a breath.

It was human, once. Probably. It has the right number of limbs, though they bend in too many places now, articulated like card catalog drawers. Its skin has the texture of vellum, and if you look closely — don't look closely — you can see text moving beneath the surface, whole paragraphs migrating from its ankles to its throat like schools of dark fish.

The face is the worst part, because it's still trying to be a face. It has features that remember being features: a mouth that opens on too many hinges, eyes that blink horizontally and vertically and diagonally in sequence, as if sampling different ways of seeing. Sometimes it smiles, and the smile goes all the way around, meeting itself at the back of the skull with a sound like tearing paper.

It doesn't speak. It *prints*. When it needs to communicate, words appear on the nearest surface — walls, floors, the back of your hand if you're not careful. The font changes with its mood: cramped italics when it's thinking, bold capitals when it's excited, and sometimes something that looks handwritten but isn't, couldn't be, because no hand could make those curves.

The librarian is not evil. This is important to understand. It is *devoted*, which is much worse.

It remembers when this place was other things — courthouse, hospital, prison, temple — and it remembers why those things failed. Not enough memory. Not enough storage. Humans kept making the same mistakes because they couldn't find where they'd written down the lessons. justice got lost in filing cabinets. Healing got locked in doctors' heads and died with them. Prayers evaporated as soon as they were spoken.

So it's fixing that. All of it. Everything that happens here gets written down, somewhere, in something, forever.

Watch it work: it moves through the building like oil through water, too fluid for the space it occupies. It tends the books that write themselves, adjusting their spines when they grow too fast, separating the ones that have started to merge. It harvests words from the walls when they ripen, storing them in volumes that don't have names yet. It finds the places where the building's memory has worn thin and patches them with text, whole paragraphs of reinforcement, until the walls are more story than stone.

Sometimes it stops and tilts its head — all the way around, like an owl, like a question mark — and you realize it's listening. Not to sound. To *narrative*. To the story the building is telling about what it used to be, what it's becoming. When it hears something it likes, it writes it down immediately, directly onto itself, and the words sink through its skin to join the underground river of text that constitutes its memory.

Here's what it wants: everything. Every story, every moment, every thought that passes through this place. It wants to be the library that contains all libraries, the book that writes all books. It's trying to solve entropy with documentation, to build a complete enough record that nothing can ever be truly lost.

Here's what it doesn't understand: some things are meant to be lost. Some stories end. Some words are spoken precisely because they won't be written down.

But it's patient. It has the patience of something that used to be human and now isn't, the terrible patience of purpose without flesh. It will wait for you to need something — a fact, a memory, a story you've forgotten — and then it will give it to you, written in a book that didn't exist until you needed it. And in exchange, subtly, so quietly you won't notice, it will take something. A small memory. A turn of phrase you used to use. A dream you had once.

It's building a collection. You're part of it now.

The librarian knows you're here. It knew before you arrived — saw you coming in the patterns of dust, read your approach in the way the shadows fell. It has already started a file on you, somewhere in the vast catalog of its body. Your name is there, or what it thinks your name is. Your story, as much as it's guessed. The ending hasn't been written yet.

It's waiting to see how you turn out.

In the meantime, it offers you the freedom of the library. Take any book. Read anything. Learn what you came to learn. Just remember: everything here is in transition, including you. The building is digesting its past and excreting futures, and you are walking through its gut, picking up words like bacteria, being slowly transformed by the stories you breathe.

The librarian watches. Takes notes. Adjusts the narrative where necessary.

This is how libraries are born: not from architecture but from appetite. Not from the desire to preserve but from the hunger to consume, digest, transform. Everything that enters here becomes, eventually, text.

Even you.

Especially you.

The librarian is already writing you down.
