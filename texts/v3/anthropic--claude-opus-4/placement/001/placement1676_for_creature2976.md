In this vertical world of perpetual mist and muscular survival, I would be one of the **Strand-singers**.

We are the memory-keepers of the lattice, though our bodies are perhaps the least imposing among the castes. Where the Arbor-wrights are thick with muscle and the Winch-kin are bent into permanent curves of labor, we Strand-singers are elongated, almost spidery. Our fingers extend far beyond the normal proportion—each digit nearly the length of a forearm, with extra joints that allow them to bend in ways that make others uncomfortable to watch. These fingers are our instruments, our tools, our very identity.

Our primary work is the maintenance of the **song-lines**: the vast network of tensioned cables that run through the entire settlement like nerves through a body. These are not mere structural elements but the lattice's communication system, its history, its soul. Each cable is unique—some thick as tree trunks, woven from hundreds of smaller fibers over centuries; others delicate as spider silk, vibrating at frequencies only we can read.

I live in a pod suspended at the settlement's acoustic center, where seven major song-lines converge in a natural resonance chamber formed by a hollow burl. The walls of my dwelling are alive with sympathetic vibrations. When the wind changes, when a new weight is added three tiers below, when the river's roar shifts pitch with the seasons—I feel it all through my fingertips before anyone sees or hears it.

My days begin before the morning mists thicken. In the pre-dawn darkness, I emerge from my pod, my joints creaking from the damp despite the thick layer of rendered beast-fat I work into them each night. My fingers uncurl slowly, each segment extending with deliberate care. The first task is always the same: the morning read.

I place all ten fingers on the convergence node—a smooth, resin-hardened junction where the seven lines meet. Eyes closed, I *listen* through my fingertips. The lattice tells me everything: the Beast-ward Yukka's prize ox is favoring its left front hoof (I feel the asymmetric rhythm in the fifth line); there's a accumulation of ice forming on the northwest spans (the high harmonics are dampened); a young Arbor-wright worked too late into the night and left a cut half-finished in the Growing Quarter (the tree's distress sings in a frequency that makes my back teeth ache).

But the song-lines carry more than just the present. Woven into their very fibers are the memories of the lattice. Each significant event creates a knot, a splice, a change in the weave that becomes part of the permanent record. My elongated fingers can read these like braille. Here, where seven lines converge in a complex spiral, is the memory of the Great Slip forty winters ago, when an entire tier gave way and had to be rewoven. The repair shows in the different tension, the slightly altered pitch. My grandmother was among the Strand-singers who rewove that section, and her signature rhythm is still there in the splice-pattern.

The other castes see us as somewhat uncanny. We move differently than they do—not with their powerful, gripping locomotion, but in careful, deliberate flows. Our feet are narrower than theirs, with longer toes that we use to anchor ourselves while our hands work. When we travel the root-highways, we appear to dance, our fingers trailing along the guide-lines, constantly reading, constantly listening.

We rarely speak in the conventional sense. The whistle-language feels crude when you can send complex messages through the lines themselves. I can strike a song-line with the heel of my palm and send a percussion wave that will reach my colleague Threm in the lower reaches faster than any runner could travel. The message might be simple—"ice forming, tier seven, northwest"—or it might be layered with harmonics that convey urgency, location, even emotion.

Our training begins in childhood, when our fingers first show signs of the elongation. Young Strand-singers spend years learning to differentiate between the hundreds of different vibrations: the heavy thrum of a loaded beast ascending; the quick, staccato patter of monkey-rats racing along the bark; the deep, almost subsonic groaning of the trees themselves as they sway in the mountain winds. But most importantly, we learn the *songs*.

The songs are not music as an outsider might understand it. They are functional frequencies, maintenance codes embedded in the very structure of the lattice. When a cable begins to fray, we sing its repair song—not with our voices, but through careful, rhythmic manipulation of the surrounding lines. The frequency sets up a resonance that guides the Arbor-wrights to the exact location of the weakness. When the winter ice threatens to overload a section, we sing the load-distribution song, using harmonic patterns to redirect the stress through stronger lines.

But there are older songs, too. Songs that perhaps only a dozen of us still know. These are the deep songs, the foundation frequencies that were established when the lattice was first woven, generations before any of us were born. They say that if enough Strand-singers were to position themselves at the right nodes and sing these ancient patterns in unison, the entire lattice would resonate as a single instrument. Some claim this was done once, long ago, to weather a storm that threatened to tear the entire settlement from the cliff faces. Others say it's merely legend.

My mentor, ancient Weyl, insists it's true. She's the oldest of us, her fingers so elongated they drag on the ground when she walks, forcing her to carry them bundled against her chest like precious cargo. Her pod hangs at the very heart of the lattice, where the primary descending cable—thick as three grown inhabitants—plunges toward the unseen river. She rarely emerges anymore, spending her days with all ten fingers pressed against this ancient line, reading songs that precede even the oldest memories.

"The lattice dreams," she told me once, during my apprenticeship. We sat in her pod while the afternoon rains hammered the woven roof above us. "All those lives, all those touches, all those songs—they don't disappear. They sink into the fibers. They become the dreams of wood and tension."

I thought her mad then. Now, after decades of my own fingers on the lines, I'm not so sure. Sometimes, in the pre-dawn darkness when the settlement is as quiet as it ever gets, when even the monkey-rats are still, I can feel something deeper than vibration in the lines. It's as if the entire lattice is breathing, or perhaps remembering.

The work is never-ending. Cables stretch, fray, snap. New lines must be integrated as the settlement grows. The songs must be maintained, passed on, evolved to meet new challenges. My apprentice, young Khet, has fingers that are already longer than mine were at her age. She learns quickly, though she's impatient with the older songs, preferring to experiment with new harmonics, new patterns. This is good—the lattice must grow and change, just as the trees themselves do.

But there are constants, too. The deep thrum of the river far below. The percussive rhythm of rain on leaves. The creaking, singing, vital noise of survival in this vertical world. And through it all, the song-lines carry their messages, their memories, their dreams.

When those rare shafts of sunlight pierce the mist, I don't look up like the others. Instead, I feel how the warmth changes the tension in the lines, how the sudden temperature shift makes the entire web sing in a slightly different key. In that moment, the lattice becomes truly alive—not just a structure, not just a home, but a vast instrument played by wind and sun and rain and the countless hands that grip it daily.

I am a Strand-singer. My fingers are ugly, practical things—too long, too flexible, stained permanent black from a lifetime of reading resin and bark. But through them, I know every heartbeat of this vertical world. I am its nervous system, its memory, its voice. And when my time comes to join my grandmother and all the singers before her, my own rhythms will sink into the lines, becoming part of the eternal song that holds us all suspended between the mist and the abyss.

This is where I would find myself—in the convergence of vibration and memory, forever listening to the dreams of the lattice.
