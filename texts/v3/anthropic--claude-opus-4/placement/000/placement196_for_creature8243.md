I am the Chronicle Mender, and I move through the salt flats on a vessel that is part sled, part loom, part living thing. My transport crawls on runners of fused glass that I've cultivated from the Prism Birds' shed fragments, which I gather after their molting dives. The runners sing against the salt—a crystalline whisper that announces my approach to those who know how to listen.

My body is a hybrid thing, adapted over generations. My skin secretes oils that form a protective barrier against the salt's desiccation, giving me a perpetual sheen like polished stone. My eyes have developed additional membranes—I can filter the brutal light into spectrums that reveal the chemical stories written in the crystal matrix. What others see as uniform white, I perceive as a manuscript of geological time, biological process, and patient transformation.

I am a reader and repairer of the cathedral crawlers' histories. The Amber Scribes write, but they cannot edit, cannot mend where acid has eaten too deep or where trauma has shattered the shell-libraries. This is my work. I carry with me cultivation chambers where I nurture specific strains of Saltweavers, bred over decades to secrete precisely calibrated minerals. With tools of carved crawler-shell and solutions of processed brine, I rebuild damaged sections, matching not just the physical structure but the chemical signature, so the story remains true.

My route follows the old paths between the extraction towers, though I serve no industrial purpose. The towers have become waypoints in my circuit, each one marking a different crawler territory. I've learned to read the Resonance Colonies' electrical displays like weather forecasts—they react to deep chemical changes hours before they surface. When they flicker in certain patterns, I know rain approaches. When they dim in others, I know to avoid specific brine pools where the Dissolution stirs.

The cathedral crawlers accept me, I believe, because I smell right. My years of working with their shells, of handling the acids of Amber Scribes, of cultivating the proper Saltweavers, have given me a chemical signature that registers as familiar, perhaps even necessary. The young ones are skittish, but the ancient crawlers, those whose shells bear centuries of writing, lower their entry channels when I approach. I can spend days within a single crawler, mapping its histories, mending breaks in the narrative, ensuring the chemical libraries remain legible for whatever future readers might emerge.

I carry water—not much, but enough. It's stored in specialized containers that were gifts from the Translators, formed from their own secretions and capable of preventing evaporation even in the noon heat. But I've also learned to harvest moisture like the native fauna. In the pre-dawn hours, I stretch collection sheets of processed Saltweaver silk, gathering the dew. I know which crawler trails hold moisture longest, where the Brine Shepherds' passages create micro-climates of humidity.

My most precious tool is the Chronicle Lens—a disk of living crystal I've convinced a small colony of Prism Birds to maintain. They nest in its refractive matrix, their constant shape-shifting allowing me to adjust its properties at will. Through it, I can read the deep histories, the chemical stories that go beyond simple acid-etching. I can see the patterns of ancient rains, the migrations of long-dead crawlers, the slow evolution of trade routes between different fauna populations.

At night, I make camp in the lee of dormant crawlers or in the shadow of extraction towers. My shelter is a thing of cultivation—panels of mineral-organic weave that the Patience Siphons have taught me to grow. It knows when to be permeable and when to seal, responding to humidity changes with an intelligence I don't fully understand. Inside, I transcribe the day's findings into my own chronicle, using inks derived from the salt gardens' blooms, writing on sheets of processed crawler-shell that will last millennia if kept dry.

I've been following rumors lately, chemical whispers carried by the Translators of something new emerging in the deep flats—crawlers whose shells bear no histories, as if they were born yesterday despite their ancient size. The Amber Scribes avoid them. The Saltweavers will not grow on their surfaces. Even the Memories' deep networks seem to route around them. These ghost crawlers, as I've come to call them, move with purpose toward something, and I mean to discover what.

My dreams are strange out here. Sometimes I feel I'm becoming more mineral than flesh, that my thoughts are slowing to match the pace of the flats. I dream in chemical gradients, in patterns of crystallization, in the slow calligraphy of crawler paths across salt. When I wake, there's always a moment where I must remember that I am not a fixed thing, not a tower or a salt formation, but something that moves and chooses and changes.

The Dissolution pools call to me sometimes. Not with malice, I think, but with a kind of recognition. As if whatever process happens in those depths sees in me a kindred spirit—something that exists between states, neither fully of the biological world nor the mineral one. I've learned to resist their pull, but I wonder sometimes what chronicles might be written in whatever medium the Dissolution employs, what stories might be told in the language of transformation itself.

This is my circuit, my purpose, my slow transformation: to ensure the histories survive, to mend what is broken, to read what others cannot see. I am the Chronicle Mender, and the salt flats are both my library and my home, my challenge and my teacher, my life's work and perhaps, eventually, my final chapter—written not in acid on shell, but in the slow integration of flesh with mineral, story with stone, under the patient, watching sun.
