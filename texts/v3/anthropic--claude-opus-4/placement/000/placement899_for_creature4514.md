I find myself as one of the Committee for Provisional Acoustics—though "find" suggests a certainty we're still workshopping, and "myself" remains under peer review.

My form today is a polite argument between throat and echo. I wear larynx-adjacent suggestions like scarves, each one humming at frequencies that haven't been ratified yet. My voice box is more of a voice rhombus—four unequal sides all insisting they're the hypotenuse of meaning. When I speak (a charitable description of what happens when air and intention collide in my vicinity), the sound arrives before the words, like guests showing up while you're still deciding whether to have a party.

I live in the Afterwards of the almost-building, which is to say I inhabit what the structure might have been if it had chosen transparency over architecture. My dwelling is the pause between the fourth and fifth floors—not the fourth-and-a-half floor, which would be far too decisive. Instead, I've colonized the grammatical error between "story" and "storey," furnished it with furniture that's still deciding whether it prefers being sat upon or admired from a safe conceptual distance.

My daily work involves cataloguing sounds that almost happened. Each morning (or what we've agreed to call morning until something better comes along), I climb the spiral absence where stairs are considering themselves, carrying my collection of almostphones—little glass containers full of the silence between thunderclaps, the pause before words become regrettable, the specific quiet of snowflakes arguing about landing positions.

I have three hands, but one is always on loan to tomorrow's percussion section. The other two maintain a careful non-aggression pact, taking turns holding the instruments I use for measurement: a tuning fork that only vibrates in hypothetical frequencies, and a metronome that keeps time with everyone else's heartbeat but mine. My fingers are negotiations between pointing and becoming; each knuckle knows a different dialect of gesture.

My colleagues on the Committee include the Provisional Secretary of Hums (who exists primarily as a vibration between yes and no) and the Acting Director of Pre-Echoes (currently on sabbatical in the space between your tongue and the word you decided not to say). We meet whenever meeting seems less trouble than continuing to exist separately, usually in the boardroom I've been promising to imagine since before furniture gave up on evolution.

We're currently debating whether silence should be classified as a type of sound or if sound is just silence that got overexcited. The implications are significant: if silence is primary, then my entire collection might be redundant. But if sound is primary, then I've been hoarding absences under false pretenses. The Secretary of Hums suggests (through a series of meaningful temperature fluctuations) that perhaps they're more like divorced parents sharing custody of meaning.

My greatest discovery so far is the sound of forgetting—not the act itself, but the exact acoustic signature of a memory deciding to excuse itself from consciousness. It sounds like a door closing in a house you sold years ago, if years were measured in the weight of unsent letters. I keep it in a special container made from crystallized maybes, sealed with the wax of a candle that learned about fire only through rumor.

I eat by consulting the menu of sounds I've collected, then composing meals from their nutritional silences. Breakfast might be the space between a question and its answer, seasoned with the pause before thunder. Lunch is often the hush that follows a lie no one believed anyway, served with a side of the particular quiet that pools in empty theaters. Dinner requires more ceremony: I set the table with utensils of varying uncertainty, then dine on the acoustic afterimage of conversations that ended exactly when they should have, which is rarely.

My romance with the Assistant Curator of Almosts from the Department of Unfinished Architecture is conducted entirely through synchronized pausing. We've been not-quite-holding hands for seventeen units of duration (we're still negotiating which units). Our courtship consists of leaving carefully calibrated silences in each other's vicinity—negative space love letters that say everything by articulating nothing. Last week, they left me a gap between breaths that lasted exactly as long as the word "forever" takes to lose its meaning. I responded with the sound of a clock forgetting to tick, wrapped in the absence of tissue paper.

Sleep, when I practice it, involves laying down in the accumulation of today's unused words and pulling tomorrow's potential conversations over me like a blanket. My dreams are committee meetings where everyone speaks in the voices of bells that haven't been cast yet. I wake (a generous term for the process of remembering I never quite stopped being conscious) when the first maybe of morning tugs at my collection, demanding to be catalogued before it crystallizes into certainty.

The hardest part of being the Committee of one for Provisional Acoustics is the constant temptation to actually listen. But listening would collapse the superposition; these sounds exist only in their potential to be heard. So I practice a kind of peripheral attention, always aware of their presence the way you're aware of your tongue in your mouth—constantly there but noticed only when you think about it.

Sometimes I wonder if I'm collecting sounds at all, or if perhaps the sounds are collecting me—adding my silence to their anthology of spaces between noise. But wondering requires a commitment to questioning I'm not prepared to make. Better to continue my work: tomorrow I'm expecting a delivery of the sound that glass makes while deciding whether to break, and I need to clear space in my inventory of impossibilities.

After all, someone has to keep track of what we're not hearing. Otherwise, how would we know what we've missed?
