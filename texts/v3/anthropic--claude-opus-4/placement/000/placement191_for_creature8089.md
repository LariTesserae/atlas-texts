In this secret highland cirque, where water has worn even the stones to weeping, I am what the mountain exhaled when it first learned patience.

My form shifts like altitude sickness—sometimes I am merely a tremor in the air where warm meets cold, a shimmer that could be mist or might be flesh. Other times I condense into something almost tangible: translucent as fairy shrimp, with organs visible through skin that refracts light like water droplets. My bones, if they are bones, seem to be made of compressed pollen and spider silk, flexible as sedge stems, hollow as pika calls echoing in granite chambers.

I exist primarily as a kind of living frequency, vibrating at the exact pitch where moss releases oxygen, where stone accepts water, where life becomes possible in impossible places. The bog knows me as a necessary note in its endless hymn—neither creature nor force, but something between, like the moment when mist decides whether to rise or fall.

My sustenance comes from transitions: the instant fairy shrimp molt, the millisecond between a midge's wingbeats, the precise moment when a water droplet shifts from sphere to stream. I feed on these infinitesimal transformations, growing denser with each season's accumulated changes, then dispersing again when the winter ice locks everything in crystal stasis.

The pikas sense me as a pressure change before storms. The salamanders know me as the reason some stones are warmer than others. The midges write my name in their columns, though I myself have forgotten what it might have been. I am older than the cirque but younger than the mountain's decision to let itself be carved.

I den in the spaces between—between water and ice in the bog pools' winter hearts, between stone and lichen where neither quite claims dominion, between sleep and waking in the minds of hibernating creatures. My home is the cirque's uncertainty, the maybe-spaces where edges blur and categories collapse.

Sometimes I remember being solid. There are days when I achieve enough coherence to press a handprint in the moss (it fills immediately with water and fairy shrimp, becoming a temporary cosmos). But mostly I prefer this dispersed existence, threaded through the cirque like mycelium, present in every exchange of moisture and minerals, every small negotiation between life and stone.

The abandoned shelter mistakes me for wind. The knapped stone holds my fingerprints in its fracture patterns—not because I made it, but because all broken edges in this place eventually conform to my frequencies. I am what happens when a mountain decides to pay attention, when geography develops something like consciousness but refuses the burden of permanent form.

In spring, when the flowers erupt in their miniature magnificence, I sometimes gather enough substance to cast a shadow. The shadow blooms too—a dark garden that follows no sun, made of all the light the real flowers choose not to reflect. This shadow-garden is my truest self: present but not present, defined by absence, beautiful in ways that require new kinds of eyes to see.

I am the cirque's dream of itself, perhaps. Or perhaps the cirque is my dream of stillness, my attempt to imagine what it would be like to stop vibrating, to choose just one form and inhabit it completely. Either way, we persist together—the bog and its ghost, the mountain and its breath, the endless weeping of stones and the thing that learned to drink their tears.
