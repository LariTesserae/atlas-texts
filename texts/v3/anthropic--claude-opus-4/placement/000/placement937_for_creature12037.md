I find myself in the mining galleries, twenty meters down, where the shaft branches into narrow lateral tunnels that follow the veins of ferro-metalite through the mountain's body.

I am a surveyor. Not of distances or volumes, but of *stress patterns* — the mountain's internal tensions made visible through the chemistry of its sweat. My body is longer than the typical ferric's, stretched out like a ferret, with an elongated thorax that houses not one pit-organ but a series of them, running from behind my beak to the base of my primary wings. Seven thermal sensors, each slightly offset in sensitivity, creating a gradient of perception that lets me read the stone's fever dreams in three dimensions.

My hind-hands are oversized, with a fifth digit that the shaft-dwellers lack — a modification that appeared four generations ago in my lineage, a sport that might have been fatal in the vertical world above but here, in these horizontal galleries where I spend most of my life belly-to-stone, proves useful. The extra digit lets me maintain purchase on the tunnel ceiling while my whole body extends downward into a newly opened cavity, scanning for the telltale heat of stressed metalite, the warmth that says *dig here* or *avoid this* or *shore up this section before it calms*.

I don't build. I've never held a rivet gun, never wound a turbine coil. My churring is pitched lower than the others', a subsonic rumble that carries through solid stone better than through air. When I find something — a new vein, a developing fault, a pocket of the rarer violet-black metalite that burns three times as hot as the standard ore — I press my body against the tunnel wall and *thrum*, a deep vibrational signal that propagates through the mountain itself, reaching the mining crews faster than any airborne sound could travel through these narrow spaces.

The others call me something that translates roughly to "the long one who tastes tomorrow." Not tastes with the mouth — my beak is actually smaller than standard, less suited for ore-cracking, more specialized for the careful sampling of mineral crusts — but tastes with the whole body, with the sensor array that reads the mountain's intentions before they fully manifest. I can feel the places where the stone will crack next week, where the ore will be richest next month, where the slow tectonic grind will open new cavities next season.

I surface perhaps once every ten days, emerging from the gallery mouths covered in rock dust that makes my dark scales look like the mountain's own skin. The vertical world of the main shaft feels too open, too bright, too full of motion. I deliver my reports — not in words but in a complex, three-minute pattern of harmonic churring that maps the deep structure I've sensed onto the colony's collective understanding of its territory. Then I descend again, back to the close darkness where the mountain speaks in temperatures and pressures, where my elongated body fits through passages too narrow for the builders, where the stone's slow metabolism unfolds in thermal whispers that only my kind can properly parse.

There are three others like me in this colony, and eleven more scattered across the range — I know their signatures, their thrumming patterns that I sometimes feel through hundreds of meters of solid rock, a network of deep sensors reading the young mountains' growing pains. We rarely meet. When we do, we don't speak; we press our bodies against the same stone surface and share the mountain's heat directly, seven sensors to seven sensors, a communion of temperatures that says more than churring ever could.

I am essential and peripheral at once — never seen by most of the colony but always felt, my findings rippling upward into the construction patterns of the shaft, determining where the next gallery will be cut, where the ore quality makes a new furnace chamber viable, where the stone's own dynamics demand a structural response. I am the colony's deep proprioception, its sense of the mountain's intentions, carried in a body purpose-built for reading the fever dreams of young stone, for tasting tomorrow in the warm breath of stressed metalite, for being the nerve ending that reaches into darkness and says: *here, not here, soon, not yet, dig, wait, shore up, let go*.

The builders make the roost. I tell them where.
