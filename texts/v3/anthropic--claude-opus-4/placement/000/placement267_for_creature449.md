In the flooded eastern reaches where the water runs deepest, I am the Listener.

My workshop was once a radio station, its broadcast antenna now a lattice for climbing vines that fruit small copper pods. The old soundproofing has become a growth medium for acoustic moss—a mutation I've been cultivating for two decades, ever since I noticed how certain patches dampened noise while others amplified it. Now my walls are a living mixing board, each section tuned to different frequencies by careful pruning.

I came here as a young audio engineer, hired to maintain the emergency broadcast system during the early years of flooding. I was supposed to leave when the water rose past the ground floor. Instead, I moved my equipment up and stayed to document the changing sounds: the last gasps of diesel generators giving way to water turbines, the crack of concrete releasing to roots, the establishment of new acoustics as spaces flooded and grew.

My body has adapted to constant immersion. Below my knees, my skin has thickened and darkened, become semi-permeable like amphibian hide. I can feel vibrations through the water—not just the mechanical pulse of the pumps, but subtler things: the respiratory rhythm of submerged root systems, the ultrasonic clicks of the not-quite-shrimp that clean the channels, the infrasonic groans of buildings settling into their new positions.

My equipment has evolved with me. The old mixing boards, sealed in resin, still function, but now they're integrated with biological sensors. Hollow gourds serve as resonance chambers. Fungal networks carry signals through mycelial cables more reliable than copper in this wet world. My microphones are sealed in oil-filled bladders that filter out the constant drip while capturing the frequencies that matter.

I record everything. In the morning, I wade through my domain with hydrophones, capturing the dawn chorus of pump stations warming up, the tonal shifts as overnight moisture evaporates from different surfaces. The Tender knows to find me by the turbine hall at midday—I've learned to translate her water-language into sound maps that help her diagnose problems sectors away. A dying root system sounds different from a thriving one. Nutrient deficiencies have their own acoustic signatures.

My archive fills the upper floors: thousands of hours on magnetic tape sealed in wax, newer recordings on solid state drives in desiccant chambers, and my latest experiments—encoding sound directly into the growth patterns of specialized fungi that can play back vibrations when stimulated. The history of the district's transformation lives in these recordings. I could tell you the exact day each building began to breathe, marked by the change in its resonance.

But I'm not just an archivist. The district responds to sound, and I've learned to speak its languages. Low-frequency pulses through the water encourage root growth in specific directions. Certain harmonics can slow or quicken the flow rate in the channels. The not-quite-insects that pollinate our food crops navigate partially by ultrasound—I maintain beacon networks that guide them to where they're needed.

At night, I broadcast. My transmitter, rewired to run on bioelectric current from the moss walls, sends out signals on frequencies abandoned by the dry city. Other adapted places listen—the salt marsh that consumed the airport, the forest reclaiming the eastern suburbs. We share information in packages of encoded acoustics: water quality data, growth rates, warnings of instabilities. It's a network of places learning to be themselves, connected by carrier waves and careful listening.

Sometimes the children find me in my flooded studio, drawn by curiosity about the old world or the strange sounds emanating from my equipment. I teach them to hear properly—not just with their ears but with their bones, their feet in the water, their hands on the resonant walls. They learn that the district is always speaking, always listening, and that communication doesn't require words.

I sleep in a hammock of woven kelp strung between transmitter towers, high enough to catch the night breezes but low enough to keep one foot trailing in the water. In that liminal state between waking and sleeping, I hear the district's dreams as sound: the slow symphonies of growth, the percussion of rain on leaves, the whispered data streams of root-to-root communication.

My recording from yesterday: seven minutes of the new species of frog that appeared in the rooftop pools, their calls at 3.7 kHz threading through the constant 60 Hz hum of the remaining electrical grid. Behind them, the Boatwright's pole strikes water in 4/4 time as he navigates past my window. A shift in overtones marks the moment when the morning sun hits the solar panels, adding their frequency to the mix. Somewhere in the low end, almost below perception, the great tree's circulation creates a standing wave that defines the acoustic baseline of our world.

This is what I do: I listen to the district breathing through its pores, and I teach it new songs. In return, it has given me new senses, new ways of being in the world. My ears have learned to function as gills, filtering meaning from the constant flow of sound. I am its memory, its voice, its interpreter—a human hydrophone in a city learning to sing underwater.

The water is rising again this season. My equipment and I will rise with it, another floor up, leaving the lower levels to new growth. But the recordings will remain, pressed into fungal memory, coded into the acoustic moss, vibrating forever in the bones of this breathing place. Even when I'm gone—returned to the soil like everyone here eventually is—the district will remember the sounds of its becoming. And someone new will learn to listen, to translate, to sing along.

This is how I've found my place: tuned to the frequency of transformation, recording the long song of a world teaching itself to grow.
