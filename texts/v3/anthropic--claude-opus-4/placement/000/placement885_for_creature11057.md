# The Filtration Cyst of the Lower Tangles

Between the seventh and eighth sub-platforms of Station Ondine's kelp vaults, where the maintenance passages narrow to crawlways barely wide enough for a child, the infrastructure takes on a different character. Here, the brutal geometry of human engineering gives way to something more organic—not by design, but by decades of slow accumulation and adaptive repurposing.

The Filtration Cyst (though no schematic would name it thus) began as a simple confluence of pipes—overflow drainage from multiple vaults, nutrient return lines, and the complex plumbing that once connected the cultivation chambers to their processing facilities above. But time and blockage and the patient work of small lives have transformed it into something else entirely.

The space is roughly spherical, perhaps four meters in diameter, though its exact dimensions shift with the tides of debris that wash through it. The walls are no longer visible—every surface has been colonized by a living mat of filter-feeders. Barnacles in impossible profusion, their white cones opening and closing in waves that follow no pattern you could predict. Between them, forests of hydroids wave their polyps in the gentle current, and bryozoans spread in lace-like sheets that look almost decorative until you see them feeding, each tiny mouth grasping at invisible morsels in the water.

The water here is different from the vaults—cooler, richer, carrying the mingled outflow of a dozen cultivation chambers. Nutrients meant for kelp drift through in clouds visible in the shaft of light from a single functioning lamp (installed for maintenance, now serving as an artificial sun for this unintended ecosystem). The light catches on particles—fragments of kelp tissue, the shells of dead crustaceans, unidentifiable organic matter that swirls in endless, hypnotic patterns.

And through this rich soup, the current flows. Always flowing. The Cyst sits at a crucial junction where the pressure differentials between vaults create a constant circulation. Water enters through three main pipes—you can see their openings, each one fringed with growth like a mouth—and exits through a dozen smaller outlets. The flow is gentle but relentless, and everything here has adapted to feed from it, to live in it, to be shaped by it.

---

**The Siphonophore**

It has no name for itself. It has no need for names.

What it has is length—extraordinary length, even for its kind. If you could stretch it out fully (though it never extends completely in the confined space), it would measure perhaps twelve meters from its leading bell to the last of its trailing tentacles. But it does not think in measurements. It thinks, if thinking is the right word, in terms of flow and resistance, food and void, the endless calculus of positioning itself in the current to feed.

It is not one creature but a colony, though this distinction means nothing to it. The pneumatophore at its apex—a gas-filled float the size of a child's fist—pulses with bioluminescent traceries that serve no purpose in this darkness except perhaps as a ghost of genetic memory from ancestors that lived in open water. Below this, the swimming bells contract in sequence, propelling the colony through the water with a motion like a silk scarf in a breeze. Then come the feeding polyps, hundreds of them, each one a transparent mouth ringed with stinging cells. The reproductive medusae cluster in groups along the stem. The defensive polyps, modified into long, trailing tentacles, drift behind like a bridal veil made of glass and venom.

---

It has been here for seven years—or rather, the colony has existed in this space for that long. Individual polyps die and are replaced constantly. The creature it is today shares no physical matter with the creature it was when it first arrived, carried through the maintenance port as a tiny budding colony no larger than a finger. But continuity persists in the arrangement, in the pattern, in the genetic instructions that tell each new polyp what to become and where to position itself along the stem.

It arrived by accident—swept in with a current during one of the rare moments when the maintenance tunnels flooded with outside water. It should have died. The water here is too warm, too rich, too confined for a creature designed for the open ocean. But it adapted, or rather, it generated polyps that could tolerate the conditions, and these survived while others died, and slowly the colony rebuilt itself for this new world.

---

Its days (though it does not experience days as such) are defined by the rhythm of flow through the Cyst.

When the vaults above cycle their heating systems, warm water rises through the drainage pipes, carrying with it a feast of particles. The colony positions itself in the strongest current, its feeding polyps extended like a net made of glass. Each polyp is a specialist—some catch the larger fragments of kelp tissue, others filter the smallest bacteria from the water, still others wait with infinite patience for the occasional crustacean to blunder into their stinging embrace.

It feeds constantly but never enough. The colony exists in a state of perpetual slow starvation, growing only slightly faster than its oldest parts die. This balance has shaped it into something different from its open-ocean kin—longer, thinner, more spread out to maximize its filtering surface. Its swimming bells have grown larger to maintain position against the current. Its tentacles have developed a different arrangement of stinging cells, optimized for the small, soft-bodied creatures that live among the barnacles.

---

It is not alone in the Cyst, but it is the largest predator, the apex of this confined ecosystem.

Below it, smaller colonies of hydroids and bryozoans compete for space on the walls. Barnacles filter the same water, but they cannot move, cannot position themselves in the best currents. Small crustaceans—amphipods and isopods and creatures that belong to no classification—graze on the bacterial mats and occasionally become food themselves. Once, another siphonophore arrived through the pipes, but the colony stung it repeatedly until it died, then fed on its dissolving tissues for days.

The colony does not think of this as competition. It simply responds—to chemical signals in the water, to the touch of other organisms, to the changing patterns of flow that indicate food or threat or opportunity. Its responses are perfect because they have been refined by millions of years of evolution, even if they are now applied to an environment that never existed in nature.

---

Sometimes Yuen passes through the Cyst.

She does not know the colony exists—the space is dark except for the single lamp, and she moves quickly through the crawlways, focused on reaching whatever repair demands her attention. But the colony knows her. It tastes her passage in the water—the oils from her skin, the exhaled air that rises as bubbles, the disturbance in the current as she pulls herself through the narrow space.

Once, her hand brushed against the trailing tentacles. The stinging cells fired automatically, injecting their venom, and she jerked away with a gasp that sent more bubbles rising. She assumed she had touched some mechanical hazard—exposed wiring perhaps—and was more careful afterward. The colony fed for hours on the few skin cells she left behind.

---

The colony has rhythms beyond feeding.

Every few weeks, the reproductive medusae mature and release their gametes into the water—eggs and sperm in clouds that catch the light like smoke. Most die immediately, poisoned by the warm water or eaten by the filter-feeders. But occasionally, fertilization occurs, and a tiny larva develops, and sometimes—rarely—it survives long enough to settle and begin budding into a new colony.

These offspring never survive long. The Cyst is already at capacity; there is no room for another apex predator. The parent colony encounters its own children in the current and stings them reflexively, then feeds on their dissolving tissues. There is no recognition, no exemption from the simple rule that governs here: anything small enough to eat is food.

---

The colony's relationship with the Cyst is one of perfect mutual shaping.

Its waste products feed the bacterial mats that feed the smaller creatures that occasionally feed the colony itself. Its movements stir the water in patterns that prevent the total stagnation that would kill everything here. Its feeding keeps the populations of small organisms in check, preventing them from overwhelming the filtration capacity of the barnacles and bryozoans.

And the Cyst shapes the colony in return. The confined space has compressed its usual form into something more like a living web, spread across the entire upper portion of the sphere. The constant current has strengthened its swimming bells and refined its ability to hold position. The diet of kelp fragments and cultivation-system bacteria has changed its biochemistry in subtle ways—its bioluminescence now pulses in different spectrums, useless in the darkness but beautiful if anyone could see it.

---

The colony does not think about the future. It exists in an eternal present of flow and feeding, growth and death, the endless maintenance of its pattern against the forces of entropy.

But changes are coming that it cannot anticipate.

The pumps that drive the circulation are failing, one by one, despite Yuen's best efforts. The flow through the Cyst will slow, and when it does, the rich water will stagnate. The filter-feeders will die first, then the grazers, then the colony itself, starving in water that no longer brings food.

Or perhaps the maintenance port will finally corrode through, and cold ocean water will flood the entire system. The colony might survive this—its genetic heritage includes instructions for cold-water adaptation—but it would have to rebuild itself so completely that it would no longer be the same creature.

Or perhaps Yuen will discover it one day, will see its glass-thread beauty in the light of her lamp and recognize it as something that doesn't belong. She might try to remove it, or poison it, or simply seal off the Cyst to protect the kelp vaults from contamination. The colony would die slowly in the darkness, still pulsing its useless bioluminescence, still trying to position itself in a current that no longer flows.

---

For now, it continues.

The pneumatophore pulses with its ghost-light. The swimming bells contract in their ancient rhythm. The feeding polyps strain the rich water for particles of kelp and bacteria and the occasional gift of living prey. The colony exists as it has for seven years, perfectly adapted to an impossible niche, beautiful in its functionless beauty, successful by the only measure that matters—it is here, it persists, it feeds.

The water flows through the Cyst, and the colony spreads itself across the current like a prayer made of glass and venom, and in the darkness, its bioluminescence draws patterns that no eye will ever see.
