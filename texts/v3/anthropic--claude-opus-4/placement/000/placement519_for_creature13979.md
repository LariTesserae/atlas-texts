Looking up from the maintenance reports, I set down my waterproof stylus and flex fingers that have forgotten what it feels like to be dry. The cramped office module shudders as another wash builds somewhere in the middle distance—I can feel it in the frequency of the pylon's groan, the way it drops a quarter-tone. 

They'll be calling me out soon. Platform 47-C has been running hot for three cycles, its thermal signature bleeding orange on the monitoring array bolted above my narrow desk. The bio-scraper assigned to that sector went deep two weeks ago and hasn't surfaced. Without regular maintenance, the bacterial mats have thickened around the intake valves. Nothing critical yet, but criticality in the Floodworks is always a matter of when, not if.

I am what they call a drifter—not shift-assigned to any single cluster, but moving between modules as needed, filling gaps. Some would say I'm running from something, and they'd be right, though what I'm running from has been dead for six years, her body given to the deeps according to custom, her concertina passed to our daughter who plays it now in Module 19-A, four hundred kilometers and two thermal layers away.

My hands know the distance in the way they know coupling sizes and pressure ratings. I could swim it, if swimming made sense here, if the water between here and there wasn't sectioned by ten thousand catwalks and platforms and the patient, endless work of keeping this rusting monument to human stubbornness from returning to its component elements.

But I don't swim to her. I drift between assignments, between modules, between the lit spaces where people gather and the margin waters where even the watchers rarely venture. I know the deep-pylon formations that the descenders whisper about—have felt them myself, those smooth, warm surfaces that pulse with something almost like circulation. I know why the transmissions stopped coming back, or at least I have my suspicions, shaped by too many hours in the transitional zones where the Floodworks' geometry begins to soften and the distinction between built and grown becomes academic.

The Floodworks are not dying. That's what the young tenders fear, what keeps the shift bosses calculating redundancies in their heads. But the Floodworks are not dying. They are digesting. Processing. Converting themselves into something the ocean can recognize, can negotiate with, can ultimately accept. The bacterial mats are not an infestation—they're an interface. The spreading warmth at the deep pylon roots is not corruption—it's communication. The ocean is patient. It has been teaching the Floodworks its language, and the Floodworks, slowly, ponderously, with the reluctance of all built things faced with the prospect of becoming, are learning.

I should report this. Should file it with the shift boss, transmit it down the cable trunks to whoever may or may not be listening in the deep. But I am tired, and my daughter is learning to make music in a steel tube surrounded by water, and the work is the work, and tomorrow I will suit up and check someone else's dorsal latches and go out onto Platform 47-C and scrape the biofilm back to manageable levels, not because I believe it matters in any larger sense, but because the alternative is to stop, and stopping here means something different than it means in drier places.

The wash is building. I can hear it now, not just feel it—the rising whistle of water being forced through the grated platforms. Time to close the reports. Time to seal the office module's storm shutters. Time to wait, and listen, and feel the Floodworks flex and groan and continue their long, slow transformation into something the ocean dreams of, while we dream our smaller dreams inside it, tending and mending and breathing together in the dark.
