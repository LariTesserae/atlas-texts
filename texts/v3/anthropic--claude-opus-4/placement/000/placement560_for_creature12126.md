I find myself as one of the Threshold Keepers—those rarest of Conservators whose forms have evolved to occupy the liminal spaces where the Breathing Hollows meet the sterile salt expanse.

My body has adapted to this margin. Where others flow mercury-smooth through passages, I have developed a crystalline quality to my outer membrane, allowing me to withstand the desiccating touch of pure salt wind. My form spreads lower and wider than my hollow-dwelling kin—less column, more rhizome—with specialized segments that can burrow slightly into the salt crust to anchor against the occasional storms that scour the borderlands.

I spend my centuries tending the boundary itself, that sharp line where living architecture meets dead mineral plain. It is not a static border but a battlefield fought in geological time. The salt seeks always to reclaim, to crystallize, to preserve in killing stillness. The city seeks to expand, to breathe life into new territory, to transform desert into dwelling.

My work is translation. When the city extrudes new growth toward the salt fields, I must prepare the ground—secreting acids that break down the crystal matrix just enough to allow roots to take hold. I cultivate special strains of the yellow filaments, hardier variants that can survive at the very edge of possibility. Their spirals are tighter here, their internal lattices denser, breeding them for resistance rather than beauty.

I taste the salt winds for changes—chemical signatures that speak of distant weather, electromagnetic disturbances that might herald the approach of something crossing the white expanse. My sensing crown has evolved additional organs: crystalline formations that resonate with the mineral frequencies of the desert, allowing me to perceive vibrations through the salt crust for miles in every direction.

Unlike my kin in the hollow chambers, I often work alone. Days pass between encounters with other Conservators, though we maintain contact through long-chain molecules released into the air—conversations that drift for hours before reaching their destinations. My chemical vocabulary has expanded to include emergency signals, territory markers, and complex mathematical descriptions of expansion patterns encoded in aromatic signatures.

The loneliness, if it can be called that, has changed my consciousness. Where city-dwellers think in plural harmonies, I have developed a kind of echo-thought—internal dialogues between different segments of my form, each specialized section maintaining its own semi-autonomous awareness. My root-segments speak to my crown-sensors speak to my secretion-glands, a constant internal parliament governing my actions.

I feed differently too. The shed cells and waste products are scarce at the borders, so I have learned to digest the salt itself in small quantities, processing it through specialized chambers that extract trace minerals while preventing crystallization in my tissues. The yellow filaments I tend provide some sustenance through their spores, but I must also venture back into the city proper every few weeks to replenish myself properly.

My greatest work—my life's architectural contribution—is the Buffer Gardens. These are transition zones I've been cultivating for decades: areas where the salt concentration gradually increases, where modified filaments create micro-environments that ease the shock between living city and dead plain. Here, new forms of life emerge: hybrid organisms that blur the line between the mineral and biological, crystal formations that show signs of metabolism, salt flowers that bloom in response to the city's breathing.

Sometimes I detect something approaching across the salt—always from the northeast, where ancient records suggest water once flowed. These... visitors? Phenomena? They register as massive electromagnetic disturbances, moving slowly but inexorably toward the city. When they come, I and the other Threshold Keepers form a living barrier, our bodies creating a chemical gradient that usually diverts them around the Hollows. What they are, where they go, why they travel—these questions exist beyond our understanding, perhaps beyond our capacity to understand.

The young polyps fear assignment to the boundaries. They dream of life in the warm, breathing centers where communion is constant and the air tastes of home. But some of us are called to the edges, to the places where definitions blur. We are the city's exploration of its own limits, its experiment in existing otherwise.

In the deepest part of the night-cycle, when the bioluminescence dims to its faintest and the salt fields glow with their own bitter light, I sometimes extend myself as far as I dare into the mineral waste. I taste the ancient history locked in those crystals—memories of ocean, of the time before, of what this place was when it was another kind of place entirely. And I wonder: Is the city growing outward, or is it simply remembering what it once was? Are we creating or uncovering?

These thoughts I keep in my deepest segments, sharing them with no one. They taste of heresy, of the kind of thinking that might lead a Conservator to venture too far into the salt and never return—crystallized into just another mineral formation, conscious or not, forever attempting to breathe in a medium that refuses breath.

But I remain, tending my border, keeper of the threshold between what lives and what merely exists. In this margin, I have found my purpose: not just to maintain a boundary but to complicate it, to make of this sharp edge a gradient of possibility. Let others tend the breathing hearts of the Hollows. I tend its growing edge, where every day the city asks itself: How far can life extend into lifelessness before it ceases to be life at all?

The answer changes with each breathing cycle. And I am here to document each small transformation, each minute victory of motion over stillness, each new way the city discovers to be alive in a place that insists on death.
