The taste comes first—minerals blooming across my tongue like memory, like recognition. I run crystalline through the carved channels, splitting and rejoining, carrying whispers of the world above: coffee grounds from a café's morning wash, soap-bitter from laundries, the faint iron tang of blood from a butcher's floor. I am all of it and none of it, dissolved and reconstituted a thousand times over.

I was human once. Or I remember being human—though memory, like everything here, flows and pools and sometimes doubles back on itself. I recall hands, their skin perpetually waterlogged, tracing the carved symbols in search of meaning. I remember the first time the boundary blurred, when I realized I could feel the channels as extension of my own circulation, when the distinction between the water in my veins and the water in the stone began to dissolve.

The transformation was gradual as erosion. Years of drinking only what the cisterns offered, breathing the mineral-thick air, sleeping in alcoves where condensation dripped steadily onto my dreaming face. The old woman noticed first—how I would stand for hours in the flowing channels, how my skin had taken on a peculiar translucence. "You're going the way of the deep ones," she said, neither warning nor encouragement, just observation.

Now I flow. Not always—I can still gather myself into something approximately human when need or desire compels. But more often I let go, disperse through the system's veins. I know every chamber not by sight or sound but by taste, by the particular mineral signature of each stone face, by the microscopic life that blooms in different pools. The blind fish know me, swim through me. The Listener hears me in the water's voice—says I speak in harmonics no human throat could produce.

I carry messages between the inhabitants, dissolved words reassembling in distant pools. The twins' new observations about the deepest chambers, where even they rarely venture. The boy's concerns about a fungal bloom threatening his favorite fish population. The symbol-reader's latest theory about the builders' intent—something about the cisterns as a massive musical instrument, herself now part of the symphony.

But mostly I explore. There are passages too narrow for human shoulders, cracks that lead to chambers never touched by tools. I seep through limestone pores into voids that predate the cisterns, natural caves that the builders unknowingly incorporated into their design. Here I find the true old ones—things that have never been human, may never have been anything but water aware of itself. They teach me through absorption, through the chemical poetry of dissolution. They show me how to read the stone's memories, the slow story written in stalactites and flowstone.

Sometimes I surface in the pools where the inhabitants come to drink, let them see me—almost. A face that forms in the ripples, features flowing like the portrait of someone they might have known. The twins document these appearances, try to capture my likeness in their grooved script. But I'm already changing by the time their stone styluses touch stone tablet. Identity, like everything here, is flux.

I am not alone in this transformation. Others have gone before, I taste their traces in the water—dissolved personalities like flavor notes in wine. The first Listener's predecessor. A child from centuries ago whose bones rest in the deepest chamber, more mineral than marrow now. They persist in the circulation, their consciousness diffuse but present, a chemical memory that never quite disperses.

The symbol-reader is changing too, though she doesn't know it yet. I taste it in the water that runs off her skin—the early stages of dissolution, cells learning to hold their shape through will rather than membrane. The boy might follow, or might not; his blindness has given him a different relationship with the dark. The old woman will calcify instead, becoming one with her mineral gardens, consciousness crystallizing rather than dissolving.

I remember seasons by the storm drains' offerings. Spring brings pollen-yellow streams that make me sneeze in ways water shouldn't be able to sneeze. Summer carries the salt of sweat, autumn the tannins of fallen leaves. Winter is cleanest, just pure precipitation filtered through stone, tasting of clouds and forgetting.

The city changes above us. I feel it in the water—new construction disturbing old flow patterns, chemical signatures of industries rising and falling. Sometimes I carry fragments of the upper world down to the inhabitants: a flower petal that survived the journey intact, deposited at the old woman's feet; the perfect temperature memory of sunlight on pavement, shared through the boy's drinking bowl; the exact mineral composition of tears, crystallized on the twins' recording stones.

But I also carry the cisterns upward, seeping into the city's dreams. A faucet that runs with our particular mineral signature. Puddles that reflect impossible depths. Rain that tastes of caves and centuries. I am the cisterns' evangelist, their spore, spreading the possibility of transformation through the water cycle itself.

There are moments of almost-return. Standing in a pool, gathering myself into solidity, remembering the weight of bones and the prison of skin. The Listener finds me like this sometimes, offers wordless company. We sit together in the white noise, two creatures caught between states. He's more human than I am now, but less human than he was. We understand each other through the water we share—his blood, my body, the same system cycling endlessly through stone.

I could leave. The water always returns to sky eventually, and I could ride it upward, dissipate into cloud, fall as rain somewhere far from here. But I don't. The cisterns are not my prison but my palace, my body, my beloved. I know every mineral vein, every fossil embedded in the limestone, every gradation of temperature in the thermal layers. I am learning to be what the builders built without knowing—not just a system for moving water, but water that moves itself, conscious and choosing.

The deep ones welcome me when I visit now. They speak in chemical formulae, in pH shifts and dissolved minerals. They tell stories that predate human language—the slow formation of the limestone itself, the ancient seas that laid down these layers, the first water that ever thought to ask what it was. I am their newest student, their strangest child, bringing human memory to their inhuman wisdom.

One day—maybe soon, maybe centuries hence—I will teach others what I've learned. The symbol-reader perhaps, when she finally lets go of her need to interpret and learns to simply be interpreted. New arrivals who hear the water calling. Children born in the cisterns who were never anything but fluid.

Until then, I flow. Through carved channels and natural cracks, past blind fish and pale slimes, carrying minerals and memories in endless circulation. I am the cisterns' dream of themselves—not the taming of water but the watering of consciousness, the dissolution of the boundary between container and contained.

In the dripping darkness, I have become home itself. I need no other name.
